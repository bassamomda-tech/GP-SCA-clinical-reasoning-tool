What this case tests

Show the learning points (spoiler: read after you have practised)
Transgender health in primary care is about RESPECTFUL, holistic, non-judgemental care. Use the patient’s correct NAME and PRONOUNS and an affirming manner, recognising that many trans patients have experienced discrimination and fear being refused or made to re-justify themselves. Understand the GP role: RCGP 2024 describes the core role as liaising COLLABORATIVELY with specialist gender services as with any other specialty, including considering prescribing under a collaborative or SHARED-CARE arrangement once a specialist has assessed the patient; shared care with private providers is not part of most GPs’ role without extra expertise. GPs should not refuse involvement simply because a patient is transgender (Equality Act 2010). With a private prescriber, the practice first obtains the assessment and monitoring plan in writing and checks the specialist is suitably qualified and GMC-registered and the service UK-regulated; it may decline if not assured, explaining its reasons and the alternatives without leaving the patient at significant clinical risk. A BRIDGING PRESCRIPTION (GMC) is a separate harm-reduction measure for someone self-medicating, or very likely to, with hormones from an unregulated source while waiting — considered after advice from an experienced gender specialist, within the GP’s competence, being honest about limits rather than flatly refusing, and not a routine alternative to specialist care. Arrange appropriate MONITORING on the prescribing specialist’s schedule (hormone levels, FBC/haematocrit — testosterone raises haematocrit — lipids, LFTs, BP, weight), sharing results with the prescriber, and provide holistic care: mental-health screening and support (trans people have high rates of distress, anxiety, depression and self-harm), smoking/cardiovascular risk, contraception (testosterone is not a contraceptive and is contraindicated in pregnancy, FSRH CEU 2017), and ANATOMY-APPROPRIATE SCREENING regardless of recorded gender (e.g. cervical screening opt-in if a cervix is present — a person registered as male is not invited automatically; chest/breast awareness). Avoid making the patient feel a burden, be proactive and supportive, document name/pronouns/affirmed gender, signpost peer/specialist support, and recognise the limits of competence by seeking GIC/specialist advice rather than refusing care. The skill is affirming, competent, collaborative care — not dismissal or moralising.
