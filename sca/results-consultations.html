Results consultations, explained so they land

Results cases are among the most common in the SCA: a blood test, an X-ray report, a screening result, a positive swab. The skill is less about the number and more about what it means for this person. This page covers the domains, a structure, pitfalls and cases.

Last checked: October 2026

What makes a results case

A results case gives you information the patient does not yet have, or has half-understood from a letter or an app. Your job is to find out what they already know and fear, explain the result in plain English, say what it means for them, and agree what happens next. Often there is a second layer: a result that changes their plans, a lifestyle conversation, or a worry that has been building since they saw the word on the screen.

Some results are reassuring, some uncertain, and some serious. For serious results, see breaking bad news in the SCA.

How the RCGP domains apply

Data gathering and diagnosis

Check what the test was for, what symptoms there are now, and what has changed since. Ask the questions that interpret the result: medicines, alcohol, family history, red flags. Read every value the brief gives you.

Clinical management and medical complexity

Interpret the result for this patient, decide whether it needs action, repeat testing, treatment or referral, and how urgently. Handle uncertainty openly. Safety-net and plan follow-up.

Relating to others

Find out what they understand and fear before explaining. Use plain words, chunk the information, and check understanding. Respond to the worry, not only the number.

A practical structure

  1. Check what they know. “What did you understand from the message you got?” or “What were you hoping this test would show?”
  2. Clarify the context. Symptoms now, anything new, medicines, and the questions that change how you read the result.
  3. Give the headline. One sentence: normal, not quite normal, or needs action. Then the detail they need, not every number.
  4. Explain what it means for them. Link the result to their life, their symptoms and their worries.
  5. Agree the next step. Repeat test, treatment, lifestyle change or referral, with timescales. Share the decision where there are options.
  6. Check and safety-net. Ask them to tell you what they will do next, and what would make them get in touch sooner.

Common pitfalls

  • Reading numbers aloud. A list of values without meaning helps nobody. Lead with what the result means.
  • Missing a second abnormality. Read the whole brief. A mildly raised value alongside the one you were asked about may matter more.
  • Over-reassuring. “It’s nothing to worry about” can be wrong when the next step is uncertain.
  • Under-explaining uncertainty. Say what the result can and cannot tell you, and what will settle the question.
  • No follow-up plan. If a repeat test is needed, say when and who will arrange it.

Natural phrases

  • “Before I go through it, what have you been told so far?”
  • “The short version is that it’s mostly reassuring, but there’s one thing I want to look at again.”
  • “Your kidneys are working a bit less well than they should for your age.”
  • “This test can’t tell us what’s causing it. What it does tell us is that we need to look a little further.”
  • “Just so I know I’ve explained it well, how would you describe it to your partner?”

Frequently asked questions

How do I explain a test result in the SCA?
Find out what the patient already knows and fears, give a one-sentence headline, then explain what the result means for them in plain words. Agree the next step and timescale, check understanding and safety-net.
Should I tell the patient the actual numbers?
Only if it helps. Lead with what the result means. Some patients want the figures, especially for long-term monitoring; offer them, but do not let a list of values replace an explanation.
What if the result is borderline or uncertain?
Say so. Explain what the result can and cannot tell you, what will clarify it (a repeat test, another investigation or review), and when. Uncertainty handled openly is good clinical management.
How do I avoid running out of time in a results case?
Give the headline early, keep the explanation to what matters for this patient, and leave at least three minutes for the plan, shared decision and safety-netting.
Are results cases usually by telephone?
Many are, because that reflects practice, but they can also be video consultations. On the phone, check understanding more often because you cannot see their reaction.
