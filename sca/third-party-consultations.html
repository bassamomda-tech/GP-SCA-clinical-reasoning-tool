Third-party consultations, with the patient still at the centre

Some SCA cases put you in conversation with someone other than the patient: a parent, a carer, a worried husband, a care-home nurse. This page explains how the domains apply, how to think about consent and confidentiality in the moment, and which of our cases to practise.

Last checked: October 2026

What a third-party case looks like

The RCGP's reasonable adjustments policy notes that while most SCA cases involve the patient, some use a role player as a carer, parent or other health or social care worker. In general practice this is everyday work: a mother with a toddler, a daughter ringing about her father's confusion, a husband worried about his wife's mood, a teenager who does not want a parent told.

Two questions run through every one of these cases. Who is my patient? And what can I share, with whom, and on what basis? The caller's worries are real and often part of the case, but they do not override the patient's right to confidentiality or, where they have capacity, to make their own decisions.

How the RCGP domains apply

Data gathering and diagnosis

Establish who you are speaking to, their relationship to the patient, and whether the patient is present, aware and agreeing. Gather what the third party has seen; it may be the best history you will get. Think about capacity where it is relevant.

Clinical management and medical complexity

Make a plan that works for the patient, not only the caller. Decide whether you need to speak to or see the patient. Know when confidentiality can be overridden, for example to protect someone from serious harm, and when it cannot.

Relating to others

Respect the caller and their concern while being honest about what you can share. With children and young people, involve the young person directly. Acknowledge carer strain; the carer may need support in their own right.

Consent and confidentiality in practice

You can always listen. Sharing is a separate decision. The GMC's confidentiality guidance sets out the principles: use the minimum information necessary, share with consent where you can, and disclose without consent only where the law requires it or where the risk of serious harm justifies it. In an SCA case, that usually means:

  • Ask the patient if they are there. If they are with the caller and able to talk, speak to them directly and ask what they are happy to share.
  • If they are not there, take the history the caller can give, explain kindly that you cannot discuss details without the patient’s agreement, and plan how to reach the patient.
  • Children and young people. Think about age and understanding. Young people who can understand a decision can consent to their own care and expect confidentiality, with limits where there is risk. Encourage them to involve a trusted adult.
  • Adults who may lack capacity. Capacity is decision-specific. If someone holds a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, check what it covers. Decisions should be in the person’s best interests and involve them as far as possible.

A practical structure

  1. Who is there? Introduce yourself, confirm who you are speaking to and whether the patient is present, aware and happy to go ahead.
  2. Hear the concern. Let the caller explain what they are worried about and why now. Ask what they have noticed.
  3. Bring the patient in. Speak to the patient directly if possible, including children at an appropriate level. Ask their view.
  4. Be clear about sharing. Say what you can and cannot discuss, and why, without sounding obstructive.
  5. Plan for the patient. Agree whether they need to be seen, by whom and when. Consider the carer’s own needs and any safeguarding concern.
  6. Safety-net both people. Make sure the caller and the patient know what to look out for and how to get help.

Common pitfalls

  • Talking about the patient as if they were absent when they are sitting next to the caller.
  • Sharing results or history with a relative without checking the patient agrees. This is a professionalism problem, not a communication slip.
  • Shutting the caller out completely. “I can’t tell you anything” ends a useful conversation. You can still listen, give general information and explain how to help.
  • Assuming a parent speaks for a teenager. Ask to speak to the young person alone where appropriate.
  • Ignoring carer strain. An exhausted carer is often part of the risk.

Natural phrases

  • “Before we start, can I check who I’m speaking to, and is your dad with you at the moment?”
  • “I’m really glad you rang. Tell me what you’ve noticed.”
  • “I can listen to everything you want to tell me. What I can share back depends on what your mum is happy with.”
  • “Would it be all right if I had a word with Josh on his own for a few minutes?”
  • “And how are you managing with all this yourself?”

Frequently asked questions

What is a third-party consultation in the SCA?
A case where you speak with someone other than the patient, or as well as the patient: a parent, carer, relative or another professional. The RCGP's reasonable adjustments policy notes that some cases use a role player in this kind of role.
Can I speak to a relative without the patient's consent?
You can listen to a relative's concerns. Sharing information about an adult patient normally needs their consent, unless there is a legal requirement or a risk of serious harm that justifies disclosure. Check the GMC confidentiality guidance.
How do I handle a parent and a teenager together?
Involve the young person directly and, where appropriate, offer to see them alone for part of the consultation. Young people who understand a decision can consent to their own care, and confidentiality applies, with limits where there is risk.
What if the carer seems to be struggling?
Ask. Carer strain is often part of the case. Acknowledge it, consider a carer's assessment or support, and think about whether it affects the patient's safety.
Does a lasting power of attorney let a relative make every decision?
No. It depends on the type of LPA and what it covers, and it only applies to decisions the patient lacks capacity to make. Check what has been registered and involve the patient as far as possible.
