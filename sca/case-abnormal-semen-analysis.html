What this case tests

Show the learning points (spoiler: read after you have practised)
An abnormal semen analysis must be interpreted and communicated carefully. A single abnormal sample should be CONFIRMED with a REPEAT before drawing conclusions — sperm parameters vary widely and are affected by recent illness/fever, abstinence period and collection — and because spermatogenesis takes about 72 days, NICE NG257 advises the repeat ideally 3 months after the first (as soon as possible if grossly abnormal/azoospermic). Take a male-factor history and address MODIFIABLE contributors (smoking, alcohol, recreational drugs — notably anabolic steroids, which suppress sperm production — obesity, medications, occupational exposures, and intercourse timing/frequency; raised scrotal temperature is associated with poorer semen quality, though benefit from looser clothing is uncertain), which can meaningfully improve borderline results. EXAMINE face to face (not on video; chaperone offered) testicular volume, varicocele, absent vas and hypogonadism signs, and — critically — PALPATE FOR A TESTICULAR MASS, because subfertile men have a somewhat increased testicular-cancer risk and NICE NG12 (Suspected cancer: recognition and referral, last updated April 2026) advises considering a suspected cancer pathway referral for a non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of the testis (and direct-access ultrasound for unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms); do not miss this while focused on fertility. Arrange hormonal tests (FSH, LH, testosterone ± prolactin; genetic/specialist tests if severe) and refer to fertility/andrology services now (NICE NG257: after 1 year of trying the couple is assessed together — at 14 months they qualify), framing fertility as a COUPLE issue — the partner must be assessed in parallel and a male abnormality does not mean the cause is solely his. Avoid false despair: abnormal results do not mean "never", many men with low counts conceive (especially after optimisation), and assisted options (IVF, and IVF with ICSI — which can fertilise with very few sperm) exist, with counselling offered (NICE QS73; HFEA Code of Practice). The hidden agenda is self-blame and guilt, with the patient choosing to hear it alone; address the blame compassionately (fertility issues are common, multifactorial and not a personal failing or matter of fault), correct the catastrophe, manage the emotional/relationship impact, encourage involving the partner, and deliver accurate information — neither cold facts nor false reassurance.
