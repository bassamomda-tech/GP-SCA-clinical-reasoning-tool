What this case tests

Show the learning points (spoiler: read after you have practised)
THREADWORM (Enterobius vermicularis/pinworm) is the commonest worm infection in UK children, presenting with intense PERIANAL (and vulval in girls) ITCH worse at NIGHT, disturbed sleep, and visible tiny white THREADS in the perianal area or stool — a clinical diagnosis (an early-morning adhesive-tape test only if uncertain; stool testing is usually not useful). Spread is FAECO-ORAL via eggs: scratching transfers eggs to the fingers/under the nails and then to the mouth (re-infection), other people and surfaces/bedding, which is why it spreads through households and recurs — and it is common and NOT a sign of poor hygiene, which the parent should be reassured about. The core treatment principle (Greater Manchester antimicrobial guidelines v16 2024; BIA 2024; BNFC) is to treat the affected child and appropriate HOUSEHOLD contacts on the same day (even asymptomatic members) with MEBENDAZOLE 100 mg as a single dose, repeated once after 2 weeks because it does not reliably kill eggs — for those aged 6 months and over, off-label from 6 months to 2 years — because treating only the index child leads to re-infection. Combine this with practical HYGIENE for at least 2 weeks: washing nightwear, towels and bed linen on the day of treatment (no need to hot-wash everything daily); a morning wash around the anus to remove overnight eggs; thorough hand-washing and nail-scrubbing (especially after toileting and before eating); keeping nails short; discouraging scratching and nail-biting; wearing close-fitting underwear at night; and avoiding towel-sharing. Under 6 months, hygiene alone for 6 weeks; in pregnancy, hygiene first for 6 weeks with medicines advice if severe or persistent; in breastfeeding, check product information rather than withholding automatically — so check age/pregnancy suitability for each person before prescribing. No school or nursery exclusion is needed (UKHSA). Persistent vulval symptoms need a face-to-face examination for other causes (including safeguarding where clinically indicated), and secondary infection needs prompt review. Reassure, treat the household plus hygiene, advise the caveats, and safety-net for persistence.
