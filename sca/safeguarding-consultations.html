Safeguarding consultations, calm, clear and on the record

Safeguarding concerns in the SCA can be the whole case or a detail you have to notice. Either way, the examiner wants to see you recognise risk, act proportionately and keep the person on side. This page covers the domains, a structure, pitfalls and cases.

Last checked: October 2026

What safeguarding looks like in the SCA

Safeguarding cases include a school calling about a child, a teenager asking for confidential contraception or testing, a carer who is struggling, a disclosure of female genital mutilation, or an older person whose care at home is slipping. Some are explicit from the brief. Others are hidden inside a different presenting problem, which is why it is worth asking yourself in every case: is anyone here at risk?

The GMC's guidance on protecting children and young people is clear that doctors must act on concerns about a child's safety or welfare, and that confidentiality does not stop you sharing information needed to protect a child from abuse or neglect. Local procedures decide who you contact; in practice that is usually the practice safeguarding lead and children's or adult social care.

How the RCGP domains apply

Data gathering and diagnosis

Recognise the signs and ask the questions that clarify risk: who lives at home, who else is affected, what has happened before, and whether there is immediate danger. For young people, assess understanding and consider whether the relationship or situation is exploitative.

Clinical management and medical complexity

Act proportionately: same-day action when there is immediate risk, a referral or discussion with the safeguarding lead when there is concern. Look after the presenting problem too. Document clearly and plan follow-up.

Relating to others

Be honest about what you will do with information and why. Do not promise secrecy you cannot keep. Stay non-judgemental, keep the person engaged, and explain the process so it feels less frightening.

A practical structure

  1. Notice and make space. If something does not fit, ask gently and give the person time. On video, check they are somewhere private and safe to talk.
  2. Listen and clarify. Let them tell you in their words. Ask open questions; avoid leading ones.
  3. Assess immediate risk. Is anyone in danger today? Children in the home? Thoughts of self-harm?
  4. Explain confidentiality honestly. Say that you keep things private unless someone is at risk of serious harm, and what would happen then.
  5. Agree the action. Who you will contact and when, what the person can expect, and how you will support them.
  6. Safety-plan and follow up. How to get help urgently, who they can tell, and when you will next speak.

Common pitfalls

  • Promising to keep a secret. If you later have to share, trust breaks. Explain the limits at the start.
  • Investigating instead of referring. Your job is to recognise and act, not to establish exactly what happened.
  • Forgetting other people at risk. Ask about other children in the household, or other adults who depend on the person.
  • Dropping the presenting problem. The contraception, the test or the headache still needs a plan.
  • Delay without a reason. “Let’s see how things go” is not enough when there is a clear concern.

Natural phrases

  • “Are you somewhere you can talk freely at the moment?”
  • “What you tell me stays between us, unless I’m worried someone could be seriously hurt. If that happens, I’ll talk to you first.”
  • “You’ve done the right thing by telling me.”
  • “Is there anyone else at home who could be affected?”
  • “I’d like to talk to our safeguarding lead today. Here’s what that means for you.”

Frequently asked questions

What do examiners look for in an SCA safeguarding case?
That you recognise risk, ask the questions that clarify it, are honest about confidentiality, act proportionately (including the same day if needed) and still manage the presenting problem. Our summary; see the RCGP marking descriptors.
Can I promise a teenager confidentiality?
You can offer confidentiality, but not unconditionally. Explain that you keep things private unless you are worried someone is at risk of serious harm, and that you would talk to them first if you needed to share.
Do I have to report a safeguarding concern in the SCA?
If there is a concern, you need a plan to act on it, usually involving the practice safeguarding lead and social care according to local procedures. The GMC says confidentiality does not stop you sharing information needed to protect a child.
What about adults at risk?
The same principles apply: notice, ask, assess immediate risk, respect the person's views and capacity, and act proportionately. A carer who is struggling may also need support in their own right.
How do I raise a safeguarding concern without upsetting the patient?
Be honest and calm, explain why you are worried and what will happen next, and keep the focus on support. Most people cope better with a clear process than with uncertainty.
