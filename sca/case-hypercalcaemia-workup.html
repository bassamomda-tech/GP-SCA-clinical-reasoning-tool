What this case tests

Show the learning points (spoiler: read after you have practised)
A mild, confirmed hypercalcaemia in a WELL person with no cancer history most commonly reflects PRIMARY HYPERPARATHYROIDISM (NICE NG132, 2019), not malignancy — so the patient’s "high calcium means cancer" certainty must be defused honestly and calmly (cancer is one cause, not the likeliest here), and the result worked up rather than guessed. The pivotal next test is PTH (with renal function, vitamin D, phosphate, ALP): a raised or inappropriately-normal PTH indicates primary hyperparathyroidism; a suppressed PTH points toward malignancy/other causes needing further work-up (including a myeloma screen if indicated). Crucially, take a full history INCLUDING self-bought items — high-dose vitamin D/calcium supplements and thiazide diuretics are common, reversible contributors that are easily missed — stop/adjust and recheck. Screen severity ("bones, stones, groans, moans"; Society for Endocrinology 2016: 3.0–3.5 mmol/L usually needs prompt treatment, over 3.5 urgent correction, and symptomatic hypercalcaemia same-day assessment whatever the level). The hidden agenda is catastrophic fear from a friend’s claim amplified by a mother’s cancer death; address it with empathy and accurate information, avoiding both false reassurance and feeding the catastrophe, and safety-net symptoms of severe hypercalcaemia.
