Breaking bad news in the SCA, at a human pace

Some SCA cases ask you to give news the patient does not want: a cancer result, a serious diagnosis, a death. This page sets out how the three marking domains apply, a practical structure for 12 minutes, the pitfalls we see most, and cases to practise on.

Last checked: October 2026

What makes these cases hard

Breaking bad news tests everything at once. You have to be clinically accurate, you have to be honest, and you have to do it in a way the patient can take in. Twelve minutes is not long. Most candidates either rush to the plan because silence feels uncomfortable, or spend so long on feelings that the patient leaves without knowing what happens next.

The task is not to deliver a speech. It is to find out what the patient already knows and wants to know, give the news clearly, stay with the reaction, and then agree the next step together. In general practice that next step is often a referral, a further test or a follow-up call, and the patient needs to leave knowing who will contact them and when.

How the RCGP domains apply

Data gathering and diagnosis

Check what has already happened: what tests, what the hospital has said, who is at home. Find out what the patient suspects before you speak. Ask about the things that will shape the plan, such as work, caring duties and how they usually cope.

Clinical management and medical complexity

Give accurate information without guessing beyond the evidence. Say what happens next, how urgently and who arranges it. Manage symptoms now if needed, and safety-net for the days before the next appointment.

Relating to others

Warn before the news, use plain words, then stop and listen. Acknowledge the emotion before moving on, check what has been understood, and offer support: a follow-up, someone to be with them, written information.

A practical structure

This is our suggested shape, not an RCGP template. Real cases rarely split so neatly.

  1. Set up (about 1 minute). Introduce yourself, check identity, and on video or phone check they can talk privately. Ask whether they would like anyone with them.
  2. Find out what they know (1 to 2 minutes). “What has the hospital told you so far?” and “What have you been thinking it might be?” Their answer tells you where to start and how far apart you are.
  3. Warning shot, then the news (1 minute). A short warning, then the news in one or two plain sentences. Avoid jargon and avoid hedging so heavily that the message is lost.
  4. Silence and emotion (2 to 3 minutes). Stop talking. Let the reaction happen and name it. Answer the question they ask, not the one you prepared for.
  5. What happens next (3 to 4 minutes). Explain the next step, the timescale and who will be in touch. Check their priorities and worries, including practical ones.
  6. Support and safety-netting (1 to 2 minutes). Agree a follow-up, give a way back in, and check what they will tell family. Summarise in a sentence.

Common pitfalls

  • Filling the silence. A pause after bad news is not a gap to fix. Talking straight into it tells the patient you are uncomfortable, and they stop asking questions.
  • Leading with statistics. Survival figures and staging detail rarely help in the first conversation, and you often do not have them. Say what you know and what will be found out next.
  • False reassurance. “I’m sure it’ll be fine” is not honest when you do not know. Hope can sit with honesty: “There are treatments, and the team will talk you through them.”
  • Running out of time for the plan. Good rapport with no clear next step fails clinical management. Keep an eye on the clock from the halfway point.
  • Ignoring the person in the room. If a relative is present, acknowledge them, but keep the patient at the centre and check what they want shared.

Natural phrases

  • “Before I go through the results, can I check what you’ve been told so far?”
  • “I’m afraid it isn’t the news we were hoping for.”
  • “The scan shows a growth in the bowel that looks like it could be a cancer.”
  • “That’s a lot to take in. Take your time.”
  • “What’s going through your mind right now?”
  • “What matters most to you over the next few weeks?”

Frequently asked questions

How do you break bad news in the SCA?
Find out what the patient already knows, give a short warning, say the news in plain words, then stop and let them react. Acknowledge the emotion, then agree what happens next, who will be in touch and when, and offer follow-up and support.
Should I use a model like SPIKES in the SCA?
Frameworks such as SPIKES are a useful reminder of the steps, but examiners mark what you do for this patient, not whether you follow a named model. Use the steps naturally and adapt them to the person in front of you.
How long should I leave for silence after bad news?
Longer than feels comfortable. A few seconds of silence after the news, followed by an open question about how they are feeling, is usually better than continuing to explain.
What if the patient asks something I do not know, such as their prognosis?
Be honest that you do not know yet, explain what will help answer it (for example the specialist appointment or further scans) and when they can expect to hear. Do not guess.
Is breaking bad news only in face-to-face style cases?
No. It can come up on video or by telephone. On the phone you cannot see the reaction, so check in more often and ask directly how they are taking it. See our telephone consultation guide.
