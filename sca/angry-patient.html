Angry patients and complaints in the SCA, without losing the plan

Challenging conversations are a regular part of the SCA: a patient who wants a colleague reported, a missed result, a demand you cannot meet. This page covers how the domains apply, a structure that keeps you calm and safe, pitfalls and cases to practise.

Last checked: October 2026

What these cases test

An angry patient is usually a worried or let-down patient. The case tests whether you can stay calm, find out what actually happened and what the person needs, and still reach a safe, honest plan. It is not a test of whether you can make them stop being angry, and it is not a test of how quickly you can apologise.

Many of these cases have a real clinical question underneath: a result that was not acted on, a symptom that has not been explained, a request that may or may not be appropriate. If you spend all 12 minutes on the emotion, you may miss the clinical problem the examiner is also marking.

How the RCGP domains apply

Data gathering and diagnosis

Let them tell the story first, then clarify the facts: what happened, when and who was involved. Find out what they want from today, and check for the clinical issue underneath the complaint.

Clinical management and medical complexity

Deal with any clinical risk first. Be honest about what went wrong if something did, explain what you will do and when, and tell them how to take a complaint further through the practice complaints process.

Relating to others

Listen without defending. Acknowledge the feeling and the impact, apologise where that is right, and avoid blaming colleagues. Keep your tone level and your boundaries clear if you cannot meet a demand.

A practical structure

Our suggested shape. Adapt the timing to the case.

  1. Let them speak (2 minutes). Open the door and then listen. Do not interrupt to correct details yet. Short acknowledgements show you are following.
  2. Acknowledge (1 minute). Name the feeling and the impact: “You’ve been waiting weeks and nobody called you back. I can hear how upsetting that’s been.”
  3. Clarify the facts (2 minutes). Ask the questions you need, and check the record if the brief gives you information. Separate what happened from what they fear happened.
  4. Find the need (1 to 2 minutes). “What would you like to come out of today?” An apology, an explanation, a referral, a change in process, or simply to be heard.
  5. Respond honestly (3 to 4 minutes). Apologise for what went wrong, explain what you can and cannot do, and deal with the clinical issue. If you have to say no, give the reason and an alternative.
  6. Agree next steps (1 to 2 minutes). Who will do what, by when, and how they can take it further. Summarise and check they feel heard.

Common pitfalls

  • Defending the practice or a colleague. Explaining why something went wrong before acknowledging that it did sounds like an excuse.
  • Agreeing to anything to calm things down. Giving an inappropriate prescription or referral to end the conflict is a clinical management failure.
  • Criticising a colleague. Avoid speculating about another doctor’s decisions. Say you will look into it, and explain how.
  • Repeating “I understand”. Show understanding by reflecting what they said instead.
  • Missing the safety issue. A complaint about a delayed result can hide a result that still needs action today.

Natural phrases

  • “I can see you’re really frustrated. Tell me what happened.”
  • “That shouldn’t have happened, and I’m sorry it did.”
  • “I don’t want to guess about what went on, so I’ll find out and come back to you by Friday.”
  • “What would you like to come out of today?”
  • “I’m not able to do that, and I want to explain why.”
  • “If you’d like to make a formal complaint, I can tell you how, and it won’t affect your care.”

Frequently asked questions

How do I deal with an angry patient in the SCA?
Let them speak without interrupting, acknowledge the feeling and its impact, then clarify the facts and ask what they want from today. Respond honestly, deal with any clinical issue, and agree clear next steps.
Should I apologise if it was not my fault?
You can say sorry for what the patient has experienced without accepting blame for something you do not know about. If something clearly went wrong, a direct apology and an honest explanation are appropriate.
What if the patient wants to complain about another doctor?
Listen, acknowledge, and avoid commenting on a colleague's decisions without the facts. Explain that you will look into it and how they can make a formal complaint through the practice, and that doing so will not affect their care.
Can I refuse a demand from an angry patient?
Yes, if it is not clinically appropriate. Explain why, keep your tone calm, offer an alternative that does meet their need, and safety-net. Giving in to end the conflict is not safe practice.
Do examiners expect me to calm the patient down?
No. They look at how you handle the conversation and whether you reach a safe, honest plan. Some patients stay upset, and that is realistic.
