What this case tests

Show the learning points (spoiler: read after you have practised)
POST-INTENSIVE-CARE SYNDROME (PICS) is the recognised cluster of new or worsening impairments persisting after critical illness/ICU across three domains: PHYSICAL (ICU-acquired weakness, fatigue, breathlessness, reduced exercise tolerance, weight loss, post-intubation voice/swallow problems), COGNITIVE (impaired memory, attention and processing — "brain fog"), and PSYCHIATRIC (depression, anxiety and POST-TRAUMATIC STRESS DISORDER with flashbacks, nightmares and hyperarousal). The first therapeutic act is to RECOGNISE and NAME it — legitimising the survivor’s experience — because PICS is frequently missed when patients are discharged without structured follow-up (NICE CG83 expects a functional reassessment 2 to 3 months after critical care discharge — missed here), and survivors often feel guilt and are told they "should just be grateful". Screen all three domains systematically: physical function and breathlessness while acting on physical red flags (worsening breathlessness, chest pain, haemoptysis, weight loss, fevers); depression/anxiety with validated tools and PTSD symptoms, plus suicidality risk; and cognition and capacity to work. Institute a coordinated MULTI-DOMAIN rehabilitation plan — physiotherapy and graded reconditioning, with pulmonary rehabilitation only where a local programme accepts post-critical-illness patients; treatment of mood/anxiety (NG222) and trauma-focused psychological therapy (trauma-focused CBT, or EMDR, per NICE NG116) for PTSD; cognitive strategies and graded return-to-work/occupational support; sleep; and social/family support — referring to an ICU-recovery clinic where available. Address the guilt and the "be grateful" narrative directly and compassionately: gratitude for survival and genuine suffering coexist, PICS is common and treatable, and struggling is neither weakness nor ingratitude; involve and educate the family. Close the follow-up gap proactively and track the referrals. Physical red flags are screened and safety-netted.
