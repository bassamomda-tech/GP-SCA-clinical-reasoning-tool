What this case tests

Show the learning points (spoiler: read after you have practised)
An asymptomatic man asking for a PSA test is entitled to one after informed discussion (Prostate Cancer Risk Management Programme), and his risk changes the balance: a first-degree relative with prostate cancer, Black ethnicity and BRCA2 variants all increase risk, and BRCA2 is linked to earlier, more aggressive disease. The most valuable question in this consultation is about genetic testing in the family. A pathogenic BRCA2 variant in a brother, with maternal breast cancer and possible ovarian cancer, means Rhys has a 50% chance of carrying it; predictive testing through clinical genetics is straightforward when the familial variant is known. A positive result would make him eligible for the targeted screening recommended by the UK National Screening Committee in May 2026 (2-yearly PSA for men aged 45–61 with BRCA2 and a relevant family history), and has implications for male breast and pancreatic cancer and for his children, who can choose testing from 18 with counselling. The GP role is to recognise the pattern, refer, support decisions at the patient’s pace, and avoid either refusing the PSA or ordering it without discussion.
