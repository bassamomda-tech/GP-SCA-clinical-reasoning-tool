Safety

Red Flags — Subacute Combined Degeneration & Severe Neurological B12 Deficiency

B12 deficiency + progressive spastic paraparesis + loss of vibration/proprioception + positive Romberg test Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (SACD). Posterior and lateral column demyelination. → Neurological emergency. Do not delay parenteral B12 replacement while awaiting test results or MRI. Arrange urgent medical/neurological assessment. MRI spine may support the diagnosis and exclude structural or alternative pathology, but a normal MRI does not exclude B12-related neurological disease. Neurological impairment may become permanent if treatment is delayed; early treatment improves the likelihood of recovery.
B12 deficiency + acute confusion or psychosis in elderly patient B12 deficiency can cause psychiatric and cognitive manifestations before haematological changes. → Urgent medical assessment for delirium, infection, medication toxicity, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, renal or hepatic failure, stroke, electrolyte disturbance, dementia or psychiatric disease. Start B12 replacement promptly where deficiency is suspected, but do not attribute acute confusion to B12 deficiency without a broader medical assessment.
Macrocytosis + severe anaemia (Hb <70 g/L) + pancytopenia + hypersegmented neutrophils on film Severe megaloblastic anaemia. → Severe symptomatic megaloblastic anaemia requires hospital assessment. Transfusion is an individualised decision (symptoms, cardiovascular or respiratory compromise, active bleeding, haemodynamic status, speed of onset, comorbidities, Hb and local policy). Monitor potassium and cardiac status in severe cases with rapid marrow recovery, significant anaemia, cardiac disease or inpatient treatment; treat hypokalaemia per local acute-care protocol.
B12 deficiency + angular cheilitis + glossitis (smooth, beefy-red tongue) + jaundice Severe B12 deficiency (e.g. autoimmune gastritis) with haematological and mucosal manifestations. Haemolysis from ineffective erythropoiesis causes mild jaundice. Urgent IM hydroxocobalamin.
B12 deficiency in pregnancy (vegan mother) + neonatal neurological deterioration Neonatal B12 deficiency from exclusively breastfed infant of severely deficient vegan mother. Neurological damage in infant can be irreversible. → Urgent paediatric/obstetric assessment. Treat the mother promptly; assess and treat the infant with specialist paediatric guidance (depends on clinical findings, results, age, feeding and severity).
B12 deficiency + nitrous oxide (N2O) abuse N2O oxidises cobalamin irreversibly — inactivates methionine synthase. Causes acute-onset severe B12 functional deficiency even with normal serum B12 levels. Can precipitate SACD in days. Ask specifically and non-judgementally about N2O use. Serum B12 may be normal or misleading — use MMA and/or homocysteine as initial tests (NICE NG239). → Urgent medical/neurological assessment if neurological symptoms; start IM hydroxocobalamin without waiting for results. Cessation of N2O is essential; assess for other neurological and toxicological complications.
Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (SACD) is the most severe neurological complication of B12 deficiency and the one where the speed of diagnosis determines the outcome — the pathology is demyelination of: (1) the posterior columns (dorsal columns — responsible for vibration, proprioception, and joint position sense), causing sensory ataxia, positive Romberg test, and falls; and (2) the corticospinal tracts (lateral columns), causing progressive spastic paraparesis with upper motor neurone signs (hyper-reflexia, Babinski positive, clonus). The critical clinical principle: SACD can occur WITHOUT macrocytic anaemia — neurological manifestations of B12 deficiency can precede or occur entirely independently of haematological changes. This means that a patient with a normal full blood count, and even a normal-range serum B12 (if borderline), can still be developing SACD. MRI spine may show T2 hyperintensity in the dorsal columns, but it may be normal, the finding is not pathognomonic (similar appearances occur in other metabolic, inflammatory, infective or structural disorders), and diagnosis remains clinical and biochemical. Nitrous oxide (laughing gas) abuse is an increasingly common cause of acute SACD in young people — N2O irreversibly oxidises the cobalt in cobalamin from the Co+ active form to the inactive Co3+ form, causing acute functional B12 deficiency that can cause SACD within days to weeks of heavy use. Serum B12 levels may be misleadingly normal — homocysteine and methylmalonic acid are the sensitive functional markers.

B12 Physiology, Causes of Deficiency & Clinical Spectrum

Physiology and absorption
Dietary B12 (cobalamin) sources: meat, fish, dairy, eggs. Absorption pathway: (1) Gastric acid and pepsin release B12 from food protein. (2) B12 initially binds haptocorrin. (3) Pancreatic enzymes release B12 in the duodenum. (4) B12 then binds intrinsic factor (IF, secreted by gastric parietal cells). (5) The B12–IF complex is absorbed in the terminal ileum via the cubilin receptor. (6) Transported in blood by transcobalamin II. Active form: methylcobalamin (CNS) + adenosylcobalamin (mitochondria). Functions: DNA synthesis (with folate — impaired in deficiency causes megaloblastosis), myelin synthesis, homocysteine remethylation to methionine.
Causes of deficiency
Reduced intake: vegans (no animal products), strict vegetarians. Autoimmune gastritis (historically termed pernicious anaemia, PA): autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells → absent intrinsic factor → B12 malabsorption. An important irreversible cause — consider particularly with severe deficiency, macrocytic anaemia, other autoimmune disease, family history or no dietary/medicine-related explanation. Anti-intrinsic factor antibody is the initial test. Gastric surgery: total/partial gastrectomy; bariatric (sleeve/bypass). Terminal ileum disease: Crohn disease (ileal involvement), ileal resection, tropical sprue. Drugs: metformin (reduces IF binding — affects 10-30% long-term users; test if symptomatic and consider periodic monitoring if risk factors — MHRA June 2022). PPIs (reduce gastric acid needed for B12 release from food protein — long-term high-dose use).
Clinical spectrum of B12 deficiency
Haematological: macrocytosis (MCV >100 fl), megaloblastic anaemia, hypersegmented neutrophils (>5 lobes in >5% neutrophils), pancytopenia in severe deficiency. Neurological: peripheral sensorimotor neuropathy (glove-and-stocking), SACD (posterior + lateral columns), optic neuropathy, cognitive impairment, psychiatric (depression, psychosis). Mucosal: glossitis, angular cheilitis, atrophic gastritis. Homocysteine may be raised in B12 or folate deficiency, but do not base thrombosis risk management on homocysteine alone — treat the deficiency and assess thrombosis using standard clinical pathways.
Autoimmune gastritis (historically termed pernicious anaemia) is an important irreversible cause of B12 deficiency — an autoimmune condition in which antibodies destroy gastric parietal cells and/or block intrinsic factor (antibody positivity rates vary by assay and population). The condition disproportionately affects: women (2:1 female:male ratio), Northern European populations (particularly Scandinavian), elderly patients (peak incidence in the 6th-7th decade), and patients with other autoimmune conditions (thyroid disease, vitiligo, Addison's disease, type 1 diabetes — polyglandular autoimmune syndrome). The lifelong requirement for B12 injections is the most important management principle — PA patients who stop their B12 injections will develop deficiency again within months because the underlying absorption defect is permanent. GPs who conduct annual medication reviews and consider stopping 'unnecessary' B12 injections in PA patients are at risk of causing iatrogenic SACD. The injection should never be stopped once diagnosed in PA.

Interpreting Serum B12 Levels & Additional Tests

Serum B12 — interpretation
Normal range: 180-900 ng/L (laboratory-variable). Deficient: <180 ng/L — treat. Indeterminate: 180–350 ng/L (active B12 25–70 pmol/L) — NICE NG239: consider MMA if symptoms/signs; interpret with clinical context + functional markers. >350 ng/L — deficiency less likely but not excluded if clinical suspicion is strong (neurological symptoms, recent nitrous oxide exposure, anti-intrinsic factor antibody assay interference, pregnancy). Interpret B12 results alongside symptoms, risk factors, FBC and the laboratory reference range; total or active B12 may be used. Serum B12 measures total B12 — approximately 80% is bound to haptocorrin (biologically inactive transport protein); only 20% is bioavailable (transcobalamin II-bound). This means serum B12 can be borderline-normal while functional intracellular B12 deficiency exists.
Functional markers (when borderline B12)
Homocysteine (raised in B12 and folate deficiency — also in CKD, hypothyroidism, smoking): >15 micromol/L = functional B12/folate deficiency. Methylmalonic acid (MMA) (raised specifically in B12 deficiency — not folate): >0.4 micromol/L = functional B12 deficiency. Sensitive markers when serum B12 is borderline (180–350 ng/L, NICE NG239 indeterminate range) or in suspected N2O-related deficiency (serum B12 may appear normal). Not routinely available in all labs — specialist/haematology request.
Antibody tests for autoimmune gastritis (pernicious anaemia)
Anti-intrinsic factor antibody (first test) — if autoimmune gastritis is suspected (NICE NG239). Highly specific but limited sensitivity (BSH): a positive result strongly supports autoimmune gastritis; a negative result does not exclude it. If negative but suspicion remains, consider anti-parietal cell antibody, gastrin, other investigations or specialist gastroscopy with gastric-body biopsy. Anti-parietal cell antibodies are relatively non-specific — they may support the diagnosis in the appropriate context but are not diagnostic alone.
Additional investigations
FBC + blood film (macrocytosis, hypersegmented neutrophils, pancytopenia) · Folate (concurrent deficiency — both treated) · Reticulocyte count (rises dramatically 5-7 days after B12 injection — confirms diagnosis) · LFTs (mild elevated bilirubin from ineffective erythropoiesis/haemolysis) · Renal function · TFTs (if clinically indicated — hypothyroidism may co-exist) · anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (when there are relevant gastrointestinal, nutritional or malabsorptive features)
The borderline serum B12 grey zone (180–350 ng/L in NICE NG239) is one of the most clinically challenging areas in routine blood test interpretation — approximately 50% of patients with serum B12 in this range have functional intracellular B12 deficiency (demonstrable by elevated homocysteine and/or MMA), while the other 50% have adequate B12 stores. The practical approach for primary care: (1) treat the clinical picture — if the patient has symptoms consistent with B12 deficiency (fatigue, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive change) with borderline B12, treat empirically with IM hydroxocobalamin; (2) arrange functional markers (MMA + homocysteine) if available — elevated levels in a borderline-B12 patient confirm functional deficiency; (3) check folate — isolated folate deficiency also causes elevated homocysteine; (4) the reticulocyte response to the first B12 injection is a useful diagnostic test — a peak reticulocyte count at days 5-7 post-injection confirms that the anaemia was indeed B12-responsive. The maxim in UK practice: 'If in doubt, treat — the treatment is safe and inexpensive.'

Autoimmune Gastritis (Pernicious Anaemia) Workup & Gastroscopy

Confirming autoimmune gastritis
A positive anti-intrinsic factor antibody strongly supports autoimmune gastritis. A negative result does not exclude it. Anti-parietal cell antibodies may support the diagnosis in the appropriate context but are not diagnostic alone. Serum gastrin (markedly elevated in PA due to achlorhydria and loss of acid-secreting parietal cells — sensitivity 80-90%, less commonly measured in primary care). Schilling test: historical (not available in most centres).
Gastroscopy in autoimmune gastritis
Autoimmune gastritis is associated with increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma and type I gastric neuroendocrine tumours, but routine gastroscopic surveillance is not recommended by NICE NG239. Routine gastroscopy is not required for every person with B12 deficiency or autoimmune gastritis. Consider specialist gastroscopy with gastric-body biopsies where there are new or worsening upper-GI symptoms (dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting — suspected-cancer pathway where appropriate), suspected gastric malignancy, or where the patient meets local gastroenterology criteria for baseline assessment (BSG 2019: baseline endoscopy with biopsies may be considered at age 50 or over with laboratory evidence of pernicious anaemia). Any subsequent surveillance interval is determined by the endoscopic and histological findings. Routine surveillance colonoscopy not required (PA does not increase CRC risk directly).
Metformin-induced B12 deficiency
Metformin reduces B12 absorption via IF-cubilin pathway interference. Affects approximately 10-30% of long-term metformin users. MHRA Drug Safety Update (June 2022): check serum B12 in patients on metformin with symptoms of deficiency, and consider periodic monitoring in those with other risk factors (intervals vary in practice). If deficient: oral or IM replacement by clinical judgement and patient preference while metformin continues (NICE NG239); continue metformin unless otherwise contraindicated (MHRA).
Family history and cascade testing
First-degree relatives of PA patients have 5-fold increased risk. Consider PA in any first-degree relative of a PA patient with: unexplained macrocytosis, B12 deficiency, fatigue, or peripheral neuropathy. Screen with serum B12 + IF-Ab. No formal NHS cascade screening programme for PA (unlike FH) — clinical judgement.
The gastric carcinoid risk in pernicious anaemia is clinically important but often omitted from patient education at diagnosis — PA causes chronic achlorhydria (absence of gastric acid due to parietal cell destruction). The loss of acid causes: (1) reflex hypergastrinaemia (antral G cells secrete gastrin attempting to stimulate acid production from the absent parietal cells); (2) sustained hypergastrinaemia is a trophic stimulus for enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells in the gastric fundus, leading over many years to ECL cell hyperplasia and eventually type I gastric carcinoid tumours. Type I gastric carcinoids are the most benign of the three gastric carcinoid types — they are almost exclusively associated with hypergastrinaemia from PA (or autoimmune atrophic gastritis), are usually small (<1 cm), multiple, and have an extremely low risk of metastasis (<5%). Patients should be reassured that this is not the aggressive gastric cancer that most people fear. Routine population-style surveillance is not currently recommended by NICE; management is symptom- and histology-led, with any surveillance interval set by gastroenterology from the endoscopic and histological findings.

Referral Pathways

Urgent medical / neurological assessment
Progressive weakness · Ataxia or loss of proprioception · Suspected SACD · Sphincter symptoms or severe neuropathy · Severe psychiatric/cognitive deterioration with possible B12 deficiency · Severe anaemia or pancytopenia · Neurological symptoms after nitrous oxide use · Diagnostic uncertainty. Mild paraesthesia with low B12 does not need same-day neurology — start treatment and review.
Haematology (urgent 2 weeks)
Severe megaloblastic anaemia (Hb <80 g/L or pancytopenia) · Suspicion of haematological malignancy (MDS, leukaemia) causing macrocytosis in addition to B12 deficiency
Gastroenterology
New or worsening upper-GI symptoms in suspected or confirmed autoimmune gastritis (NICE NG239, 2024) · Suspected gastric malignancy · Specialist need for confirmation or assessment of autoimmune gastritis · Histological atrophy or intestinal metaplasia requiring surveillance. Routine surveillance gastroscopy is not recommended · Ileal Crohn disease (ileal B12 malabsorption) + ongoing B12 management
Neurology
Peripheral neuropathy not improving with B12 replacement after 3 months · Cognitive decline disproportionate to B12 deficiency severity
GP management (majority of cases)
IM hydroxocobalamin loading + maintenance in autoimmune gastritis (PA). Oral supplementation in dietary deficiency; oral or IM in metformin-induced deficiency. Symptom-based follow-up (initial review ~3 months). Co-management of associated autoimmune conditions. Metformin B12 monitoring.
A single gastroscopy at PA diagnosis was historically common practice — it was intended to serve two purposes: exclusion of gastric malignancy (atrophic gastritis from PA carries approximately 1-3% lifetime gastric cancer risk — significantly higher than the general population but still modest) and exclusion of type I gastric carcinoid. NICE NG239 (2024) changed this: routine gastroscopic surveillance is not recommended for everyone with autoimmune gastritis, and gastroscopy should be offered only where there is a clinical reason — in particular new or worsening upper GI symptoms such as dyspepsia, nausea or vomiting, which are then managed through NICE NG12 (updated April 2026). Diagnose autoimmune gastritis on the anti-intrinsic factor antibody with a low B12 (NG239 advises against using anti-gastric parietal cell antibody testing to make the diagnosis). So the initial management plan is B12 replacement plus a clear symptom-based safety net for endoscopy, not an automatic endoscopy referral.

Treat

Hydroxocobalamin — Loading, Maintenance & Special Circumstances

Autoimmune gastritis (PA) (no neurological features)
IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg loading
6 injections over 2 weeks (1 mg three times a week for 2 weeks, BNF). Then maintenance: 1 mg IM every 2–3 months lifelong (BNF). Use the local protocol and current product information. Lifelong IM B12 replacement is the standard treatment when autoimmune gastritis is confirmed or strongly suspected; high-dose oral treatment may be considered only in selected circumstances with specialist/local-protocol support, reliable adherence and appropriate follow-up (oral therapy may be inadequate or unreliable, especially in severe neurological deficiency). Never stop injections in PA.
Autoimmune gastritis (PA) + neurological features (SACD / neuropathy)
IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg every other day loading
Alternate-day dosing until no further improvement (BNF), with urgent medical/neurological assessment; prolonged alternate-day treatment requires specialist direction. Then 1 mg every 2 months maintenance (more frequent than standard PA — higher neurological demand). The BNF advises more frequent (every 2 months) maintenance dosing when there is neurological involvement. Neurological impairment may become permanent if treatment is delayed; early treatment improves the likelihood of recovery.
Dietary deficiency (vegans, vegetarians)
Oral cyanocobalamin 50–150 micrograms daily (BNF, diet-related deficiency)
Oral B12 is effective when the intrinsic factor mechanism is intact: cyanocobalamin 50–150 micrograms daily between meals (BNF). If symptoms have not improved, increase the oral dose or switch to IM (NICE NG239). Also dietary modification: fortified nutritional yeast, fortified plant milks, B12-fortified cereals. Use a treatment dose appropriate to the severity and cause; review symptoms and laboratory response at ~3 months. IM remains standard for autoimmune gastritis.
Metformin-induced deficiency
IM hydroxocobalamin or oral cyanocobalamin 1 mg (1,000 micrograms) daily while on metformin (medicine-induced; NICE NG239)
Metformin impairs (but does not abolish) IF-mediated B12 absorption. Oral or IM by clinical judgement and patient preference while metformin continues (NICE NG239); continue metformin unless otherwise contraindicated (MHRA). If symptoms do not improve on oral: increase the dose or switch to IM. Follow up symptoms and response.
Patients requesting more frequent injections (PA)
Maintain 3-monthly schedule; if neurological: every 2 months
Some PA patients request more frequent injections (every 4-8 weeks) reporting return of symptoms before next scheduled injection. NICE NG239 advises people to return if symptoms reappear, because the dose and frequency of injections may need to be reassessed. Clinical judgement: if genuine neurological symptoms recur before 3 months, specialist review. No evidence that serum B12 between injections correlates with symptoms — levels naturally fluctuate.
The debate about injection frequency in pernicious anaemia is one of the most contested areas in UK primary care — the NHS protocol (1 mg IM hydroxocobalamin every 3 months) is based on pharmacokinetic data showing that the body can store 2-5 mg of B12, that a 1 mg injection repletes stores, and that stores last approximately 3 months in a healthy person. However, many PA patients and patient advocacy groups (Pernicious Anaemia Society) argue that individual variation in B12 metabolism means that some patients experience return of symptoms (fatigue, neurological symptoms) before the 3-month injection is due. The biochemical argument is sound: serum B12 levels after injection peak at approximately 1 week and fall back to baseline-or-below by 4-8 weeks in many patients, because the excess B12 from the injection is excreted in urine within days — only the B12 that enters cells and is stored there contributes to long-term function. NICE NG239 (2024) acknowledges that patients with neurological manifestations of PA should receive more frequent injections (every 2 months). For non-neurological PA where patients report early symptom recurrence, a trial of more frequent dosing (every 2 months) with reassessment is reasonable — there is no toxicity risk from more frequent hydroxocobalamin.

Oral Supplementation, Folate Co-Treatment & Monitoring

Oral B12 (when appropriate)
Oral cyanocobalamin dose depends on the cause: diet-related deficiency (intact IF) 50–150 micrograms daily (BNF); malabsorption treated orally at least 1 mg daily (NICE NG239); metformin-associated deficiency 1 mg daily or IM (NICE NG239: IM or oral by clinical judgement). Lifelong IM is standard for autoimmune gastritis (PA), total gastrectomy or complete terminal ileal resection (high-dose oral only in selected circumstances with specialist/local-protocol support). Mechanism: passive diffusion of approximately 1% of ingested B12 across the gut mucosa (independent of IF) — 1% of 1,000 mcg = 10 mcg absorbed = sufficient for daily requirement (approximately 1-2 mcg). In autoimmune gastritis oral therapy may be inadequate or unreliable (no IF = no active absorption), especially in severe neurological deficiency. Available as cyanocobalamin 50 microgram tablets (1–3 daily = BNF dose for diet-related deficiency; 1 mg tablets for malabsorption or other non-dietary causes, NICE NG239).
Concurrent folate deficiency
Folate deficiency commonly co-exists with B12 deficiency (both cause macrocytosis + megaloblastosis — impaired DNA synthesis). Treat both simultaneously. Important: NEVER give folate alone to a B12-deficient patient — folate can partially correct megaloblastic anaemia, masking the haematological signs of B12 deficiency while allowing neurological B12 deficiency to progress undetected (SACD). Always check and treat B12 first (or simultaneously).
Hypokalaemia during treatment
Hypokalaemia can occur during brisk erythropoiesis in severe megaloblastic anaemia, but the evidence and clinical relevance are variable; this is primarily an inpatient or specialist-monitoring issue. Severe symptomatic megaloblastic anaemia requires hospital assessment. Monitor potassium and cardiac status in severe cases where there is rapid marrow recovery, significant anaemia, cardiac disease or inpatient treatment. Treat hypokalaemia according to local acute-care protocol.
Response monitoring
Reticulocyte count peaks at days 5-7 after IM B12 — confirms diagnosis. Hb should rise 10-20 g/L per week. MCV normalises slowly (2-3 months — reflects the lifespan of current macrocytic RBCs). Serum B12: very high immediately post-injection, then falls — do not use as monitoring tool within 3 months of injection. Initial follow-up at ~3 months (earlier where severity warrants, NICE NG239): assess neurological, haematological and functional symptoms, adherence and adverse effects. Do not routinely use serum B12 to monitor patients established on regular IM treatment unless there is diagnostic uncertainty, poor response or a change in clinical status.
The folate-B12 interaction is one of the most important prescribing safety principles in haematology — giving folate alone to a patient with undiagnosed B12 deficiency causing megaloblastic anaemia will partially correct the macrocytic anaemia (because both B12 and folate are needed for the same DNA synthesis pathway, and supplementing one partially compensates for the deficiency of the other), but will not address the neurological B12 deficiency. The result: the macrocytic anaemia improves, the clinician believes the condition is treated, but neurological B12 deficiency progresses unchecked to SACD. This scenario is called 'subacute combined degeneration unmasked by folate treatment' and represents a clinical error. The safe practice: always check serum B12 and folate simultaneously in any patient with macrocytosis or unexplained neurological symptoms. If both are deficient: treat B12 first (or simultaneously with folate) — never treat folate alone without addressing B12 status. The BNF warns that folic acid should never be given alone in B12 deficiency states because it may precipitate subacute combined degeneration — so check B12 before starting folic acid.

Lifestyle

Dietary Sources, Vegan B12 & Patient Education

Dietary sources of B12 Plant foods do not naturally provide reliable B12; animal-derived foods (B12 is synthesised by bacteria and concentrated in animal tissues), fortified foods and supplements are the main sources. Richest sources: clams and oysters (98 mcg/100g), liver (83 mcg/100g), fish (trout 7 mcg/100g, sardines 9 mcg/100g), beef (2.5 mcg/100g), eggs (1.3 mcg/100g per egg), milk (0.9 mcg/100ml), cheese (0.8 mcg/100g). Daily requirement: 1.5-2 mcg (UK RNI). Fortified foods and supplements are important sources for people avoiding animal products.
Vegan B12 — prevention All vegans need a reliable B12 source: regular fortified foods and/or an appropriate OTC B12 supplement. Reliable fortified-food intake can be an alternative to a tablet if total intake is adequate. Fortified nutritional yeast provides B12 from bacterial fermentation — 3 tsp provides approximately 3 mcg. Fortified plant milks (fortification varies by brand and serving). Fortified breakfast cereals. Oral B12 supplement (dose depends on product, frequency and dietary pattern). Confirmed deficiency is treated separately with a treatment dose appropriate to the severity and cause. GPs advising patients transitioning to veganism must mention B12 supplementation.
Breastfeeding vegan mothers Breast milk B12 content mirrors maternal B12 status. Severely deficient vegan mother produces B12-deficient breast milk. Exclusively breastfed infants of vegan mothers are at high risk of neonatal B12 deficiency — neurological damage can be irreversible. A severely B12-deficient breastfeeding mother and her infant require urgent paediatric/obstetric assessment. Treat the mother promptly and assess the infant with specialist paediatric guidance. Infant treatment depends on clinical findings, laboratory results, age, feeding and severity; it should not be prescribed as a universal GP routine. Same-day referral is based on actual neurological or systemic features, not vegan diet alone.
Autoimmune gastritis (PA) patient education — why injections cannot stop Explain clearly: pernicious anaemia means the body permanently lacks the ability to absorb B12 from food. This cannot be cured, but it can be managed completely with regular injections. Stopping injections will cause B12 deficiency to return, risking permanent nerve damage. The injections are a lifelong treatment — like insulin for type 1 diabetes. Pernicious Anaemia Society (pernicious-anaemia-society.org) — excellent patient resource.
Associated autoimmune conditions Ask about symptoms and personal/family history of autoimmune disease. Check TFTs if clinically indicated or as part of a locally agreed autoimmune review. Do not routinely screen all patients with B12 deficiency for Addison's disease or type 1 diabetes. Test for coeliac disease when there are relevant gastrointestinal, nutritional or malabsorptive features. Consider associated autoimmune disease when the clinical context supports it; routine annual anti-TPO, anti-GAD and cortisol testing is not recommended.
Metformin users — monitoring and awareness MHRA Drug Safety Update (June 2022): check B12 in patients on metformin with symptoms of deficiency, and consider periodic monitoring in those with risk factors. Symptoms of B12 deficiency in metformin users are often subtle: peripheral tingling, fatigue, cognitive slowing. The B12 deficiency from metformin is reversible with replacement (oral or IM by clinical judgement) — continue metformin while replacing B12 unless otherwise contraindicated; if allowed to progress undetected, SACD risk. Medication review at annual diabetic review: check metformin dose and duration; test B12 where deficiency is suspected and consider periodic monitoring in those with risk factors (MHRA).
Nitrous oxide (N2O) harm reduction Recreational N2O (laughing gas, whippits) use has increased significantly in the UK — it is now commonly used by young people. N2O irreversibly inactivates cobalamin and can cause SACD after intensive use. Ask specifically and non-judgementally about N2O use. Key GP message to young patients who use N2O: this drug can cause permanent nerve damage to the spinal cord, particularly if you are already low in B12. Any tingling in the hands or feet, weakness in the legs, or balance problems after N2O use should be investigated urgently.
PA and travel Patients on 3-monthly IM B12 injections should ensure they have access to B12 injections when travelling abroad. Pre-travel prescription for hydroxocobalamin ampoules (to take to a local clinic for administration). Some countries have hydroxocobalamin available OTC. PA Society has a travel card explaining the condition in multiple languages (pernicious-anaemia-society.org). For extended travel (>3 months): plan injection schedule before departure.
The Pernicious Anaemia Society is the most important patient resource for PA management in the UK — it was founded in 2006 by Martyn Hooper after his own delayed PA diagnosis and provides: patient information leaflets, a helpline staffed by trained advisors, support groups across the UK, advocacy for improved diagnostic criteria (the society campaigns for testing functional B12 markers and broadening diagnostic criteria), and a travel card in multiple languages. The society has been instrumental in highlighting the problem of doctors discontinuing B12 injections after patients lose blood test abnormalities — once B12 injections normalise the MCV and the haemoglobin, some doctors query whether injections are still needed. They are — the underlying IF deficiency is permanent and will cause re-depletion within months of stopping. The society's leaflet 'What PA patients wish their GPs knew' is a valuable educational resource. GPs should provide the PA Society website (pernicious-anaemia-society.org) to every newly diagnosed PA patient.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Long-Term Surveillance

Autoimmune gastritis (PA) — follow-up
Initial follow-up at ~3 months, earlier where severity warrants (NICE NG239). At follow-up, assess neurological, haematological and functional symptoms, adherence and adverse effects. Do not routinely use serum B12 to monitor patients established on regular IM treatment unless there is diagnostic uncertainty, poor response or a change in clinical status. TFTs if clinically indicated. Gastroscopy only for new or worsening upper-GI symptoms or per gastroenterology, with any surveillance interval set by endoscopic and histological findings.
Dietary B12 deficiency — monitoring
Review at ~3 months: symptoms, adherence and laboratory response. If not improving: reassess cause (consider autoimmune gastritis or malabsorption), increase the oral dose or switch to IM (NICE NG239).
Metformin-induced B12 deficiency
B12 check if symptomatic, and periodic monitoring if risk factors (MHRA Drug Safety Update, June 2022). If deficient: oral cyanocobalamin 1 mg daily or IM by clinical judgement and patient preference (NICE NG239). Review symptoms and response at ~3 months. If not responding to oral: IM injections. Does not require stopping metformin — supplement alongside.
Neurological PA — enhanced follow-up
Every 2-month injections (NICE NG239). Neurological review at 3 and 6 months (document improvement or plateau). If SACD: physiotherapy + occupational therapy for balance, falls prevention, functional rehabilitation. No routine repeat MRI to monitor response — reserve repeat imaging for diagnostic uncertainty, deterioration, failure to improve or concern about an alternative or additional neurological diagnosis.
Same-day referral
Progressive weakness + loss of proprioception + B12 deficiency → urgent medical/neurological assessment (suspected SACD) · Severe symptomatic anaemia or pancytopenia → hospital assessment · N2O use + acute neurological symptoms → urgent medical/neurological assessment · Acute confusion or psychosis → urgent medical assessment for delirium and other causes
Prompt (start treatment now)
B12 <180 ng/L + peripheral neuropathy → start IM hydroxocobalamin immediately; urgent medical/neurological assessment if progressive, severe or diagnostically uncertain · Borderline B12 (180–350, NICE NG239) + symptoms → empirical IM treatment + MMA/homocysteine if available · PA confirmed plus new or worsening upper GI symptoms (dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting) → consider gastroscopy referral and follow NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) for upper GI cancer
The NICE NG239 (2024) guideline on vitamin B12 deficiency in over 16s provides updated guidance that significantly affects primary care practice — key updates include: (1) urgent treatment and specialist advice for neurological features, with alternate-day IM hydroxocobalamin until no further improvement per the BNF; (2) recommendation for MMA testing when total or active B12 is in the indeterminate range, rather than watchful waiting; (3) acknowledgement that some patients may require more frequent B12 injections based on individual clinical response; (4) specific guidance on oral versus IM treatment for different causes. GPs should be aware that NG239 updates the earlier BSH (BCSH) cobalamin and folate guideline (2014) for adults and represents a significant update in diagnostic criteria and management recommendations. The key clinical message from NG239: treat based on clinical picture + serum B12 + functional markers where available; do not withhold treatment in symptomatic patients with borderline-low B12; ensure patients with neurological manifestations receive more frequent injections.
