Anaemia in adults — triage and diagnostic approach

The MCV and other indices narrow the cause, but the question that changes outcomes is where is the blood going. Iron deficiency in a man or postmenopausal woman is a GI cancer question until answered.
 Page 1 / 2
AdultsLab resultHb below rangev1.0 · Sep 2026
Core rule. Anaemia is Hb below 130 g/L in men, below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women (WHO adult thresholds; use local laboratory reference ranges). Then three questions. 1. Is the patient compromised? Collapse, shock, ongoing major haemorrhage, severe symptomatic anaemia, chest pain or possible ACS, severe hypoxia or altered consciousness needs emergency assessment at any haemoglobin — interpret pulse and BP in clinical context. 2. What do the MCV and the rest of the FBC show? Read MCV with MCH, reticulocytes, ferritin and the other cell lines — mixed deficiencies can give a normal MCV. Microcytic = iron deficiency or thalassaemia; normocytic = acute bleed, renal, chronic disease or haemolysis; macrocytic = B12, folate, alcohol, hypothyroidism, myelodysplasia. 3. Where is the blood or the nutrient going? Iron deficiency is not a diagnosis — it is a finding that demands a cause. Never prescribe iron and re-test without answering it. Take the ferritin and haematinics before treatment or transfusion whenever clinically safe; transfusion is guided by symptoms, cardiovascular status, bleeding and local transfusion guidance, not Hb alone.
0
On seeing the result: is this an emergency now? Arrange emergency admission — 999 or immediate discussion with the acute team
Emergency assessment at any haemoglobin: collapse, shock, ongoing major haemorrhage, severe symptomatic anaemia, chest pain or possible ACS (manage as ACS irrespective of the Hb), severe hypoxia, altered consciousness or heart failure — interpret tachycardia and hypotension with age, baseline observations, sepsis, bleeding, medication and comorbidity · Hb below 70 g/L with dyspnoea at rest, syncope, angina, heart failure, confusion, haemodynamic compromise or suspected ongoing bleeding — same-day hospital assessment, with emergency transfer according to clinical condition and local transfusion/acute medicine protocol · a rapid fall from a recent normal value · active bleeding — haematemesis, melaena, large-volume rectal bleeding, or heavy menstrual bleeding with postural symptoms · pancytopenia with fever, bleeding, blasts or severe cytopenia, or anaemia with severe neutropenia, severe thrombocytopenia, blasts on the film or a very high white count · anaemia with fever, bruising, petechiae or bone pain · suspected haemolysis with a rapid Hb fall, haemoglobinuria, renal impairment, thrombocytopenia, neurological features or haemodynamic compromise · anaemia in pregnancy with breathlessness, or ongoing postpartum haemorrhage or haemodynamic instability. Hand over the FBC with the MCV, white count, platelets and film, previous values and dates, the bleeding history, and the drug list.
1
MCV and indices first, then the two questions they cannot answer Take the haematinics before treating — iron starts to change the picture within days

Microcytic (below 80), normocytic (80–100) and macrocytic (above 100)

Microcytic: iron deficiency until proved otherwise — ferritin below 15 µg/L strongly supports absent iron stores; below 30 µg/L generally supports iron deficiency. A normal or raised ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency in inflammation, liver disease, CKD or malignancy — in inflammatory disease a ferritin up to 100 µg/L with transferrin saturation below 20% can represent iron deficiency (BSG 2021), so send CRP alongside and consider transferrin saturation, reticulocyte Hb or therapeutic response when ferritin is hard to interpret. Also thalassaemia trait: a long-standing low MCV with a normal or raised red cell count and normal ferritin — haemoglobinopathy screening, not iron. Normocytic: acute blood loss (the MCV has not had time to change), chronic disease, CKD (check eGFR), hypothyroidism, myeloma, haemolysis, or a mixed deficiency where a low and high MCV cancel out — so a normal MCV does not exclude iron or B12 deficiency. Macrocytic: B12 or folate deficiency — send both, and B12 with neurological symptoms is urgent (ask about nitrous oxide use) · alcohol and liver disease · hypothyroidism · drugs (methotrexate, hydroxycarbamide, anticonvulsants) · reticulocytosis or haemolysis · marrow disease including myelodysplasia, considered when macrocytosis is unexplained, persistent or progressive, or with other cytopenias or an abnormal film.

Then the two real questions

Where is the blood going? Ask directly about melaena, rectal bleeding, bowel-habit change, dyspepsia, dysphagia, weight loss and — in women — menstrual volume, flooding and clots. What is on the rest of the FBC? Anaemia is only "simple" when the white count, platelets and film are normal.

Transfusion is guided by symptoms, cardiovascular status, bleeding and local transfusion guidance, not Hb alone. Chest pain with ECG changes is managed as possible ACS irrespective of the Hb. Observations with postural BP. Stop NSAIDs, but discuss anticoagulation with the receiving team rather than stopping it unilaterally. Do not start a PPI and review after melaena, or prescribe oral iron and review a compromised patient. This is not "anaemia" — it is a marrow problem, and it does not go in a routine iron pathway. Speak to haematology the same day; a patient with blasts, neutropenic fever or rapidly falling counts needs admission now. Do not start iron, B12 or folate while awaiting that discussion, and do not repeat the FBC in a month.
Suspected haemolysis — jaundice with dark urine, a rapid fall in Hb, splenomegaly, high reticulocytes, raised LDH and bilirubin with low haptoglobin; a new drug or known haemoglobinopathy. Or B12 deficiency with neurological features — paraesthesiae, numbness, unsteady gait, cognitive change or sore tongue, with or without anaemia or a macrocytosis — ask about nitrous oxide exposure, especially in younger adults with a normal or borderline B12. Haemolytic anaemia · subacute combined degeneration of the cord — irreversible if untreated
Urgent assessment; same-day haematology if the Hb fall is rapid, haemolysis is severe, or with haemoglobinuria, renal impairment, thrombocytopenia, neurological features or compromise		 Haemolysis: send reticulocytes, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, a film, renal function and urinalysis, and a direct antiglobulin test where immune haemolysis is suspected, stop the suspected drug, and judge urgency by the tempo of the fall rather than the absolute value. B12 with neurology: do not wait for the results or the intrinsic factor antibody, and never give folate first — folate alone can worsen the neurological damage. Give hydroxocobalamin by the neurological regimen (1 mg IM on alternate days until no further improvement, then 1 mg every 2 months, BNF), taking B12, folate and intrinsic factor antibody before the first dose.
Pregnancy — Hb below 110 g/L in the first trimester, below 105 in the second and third (BSH 2020); postpartum thresholds vary by guideline and clinical context. Or a cause on the drug chart or history: NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants, steroids, PPIs and metformin (B12), methotrexate; coeliac disease; bariatric or bowel surgery; dialysis; unsupplemented vegan diet. Anaemia of pregnancy · drug-related loss or malabsorption · coeliac disease · CKD anaemia
999 if bleeding or compromised Same-day maternity or drug review		 Pregnancy: speak to maternity rather than managing alone — there is fetal and delivery risk, and ongoing postpartum haemorrhage or haemodynamic instability is an emergency; otherwise urgency depends on bleeding, symptoms, haemodynamics, timing, comorbidity and the Hb trend. Start oral iron where deficiency is confirmed and refer for IV iron if oral is not tolerated, absorption is poor or delivery is near; a normal ferritin does not exclude deficiency late on. Otherwise: send coeliac serology in every unexplained iron deficiency — common, treatable and frequently missed — check eGFR, since CKD anaemia needs iron repletion before an erythropoietin-stimulating agent (NICE NG203, replaced NG8), and stop or review the culprit drug.
NG12 lower GI: iron-deficiency anaemia at any age → FIT; FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → urgent suspected cancer referral · anaemia at 60+, even without iron deficiency → FIT · rectal bleeding under 50 with abdominal pain or weight loss; at 50+ rectal bleeding, abdominal pain or weight loss; 40+ with unexplained weight loss and abdominal pain; bowel-habit change or abdominal mass → FIT · rectal mass, anal mass or anal ulceration → refer without waiting for FIT · dysphagia at any age · 55 or over with upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia plus weight loss · 60 or over with weight loss and new diabetes, nausea or bowel change · anaemia with bruising, petechiae or bone pain. Colorectal · upper GI · urological · haematological cancer
Urgent suspected-cancer referral or direct-access test within 2 weeks · NICE NG12		 Match the test to the criterion (NG12): iron-deficiency anaemia at any age → FIT; FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g is a colorectal urgent suspected-cancer referral — FIT is a triage test, not an exclusion test: a result below 10 does not exclude colorectal cancer, and persistent symptoms, recurrent IDA, a rectal or abdominal mass or strong clinical concern still warrant referral (ACPGBI/BSG 2022); actively follow up unreturned FIT kits · suspected cancer pathway referral for oesophageal or stomach cancer for dysphagia, or at 55 and over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)) · very urgent FBC within 48 hours if leukaemia is possible · protein electrophoresis with serum free light chains at 60 and over · consider a renal tract cause. Men and postmenopausal women with confirmed IDA generally need upper and lower GI investigation via the local pathway unless there is a clear alternative explanation.
Safety rule. Iron deficiency is a finding, not a diagnosis — prescribing iron without asking where the blood is going is the commonest serious error in this pathway, and it delays cancer diagnosis. Take ferritin, B12 and folate before treating or transfusing whenever clinically safe. A normal ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency in inflammation, and a normal MCV does not exclude either deficiency. Anaemia with an abnormal white cell or platelet count is a haematology problem, not an iron one.

3
Assessment and investigation Send the panel in one go, before any treatment

Assess, then send the panel in one go — and read it together

Pulse, BP with a postural drop, RR, temperature; conjunctival and palmar pallor; jaundice; koilonychia, glossitis; abdominal examination where GI loss is possible, and rectal examination when clinically indicated; nodes, spleen, liver; bruising and petechiae; neurology and gait where B12 deficiency is possible; menstrual history. Then, before treating: FBC with indices (MCV, MCH, white count, platelets) · a blood film where there are unexplained cytopenias, abnormal white cell or platelet counts, unexplained macrocytosis, suspected haemolysis, abnormal morphology, or severe or rapidly progressive anaemia · ferritin with CRP · B12 and folate where macrocytosis, neurological symptoms or risk factors are present · U&E and eGFR · LFTs · TFTs · coeliac serology (tTG-IgA with total IgA) · reticulocytes where haemolysis, blood loss, marrow failure or treatment response is in question · LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin and DAT if haemolysis is possible · protein electrophoresis with serum free light chains at 60 and over · haemoglobinopathy screen where thalassaemia is likely · urine dipstick for haematuria where clinically indicated. Then: low MCV with low ferritin = iron deficiency, find the source · low MCV, normal ferritin, normal or raised red cell count = thalassaemia trait, screen rather than treat · normocytic with low eGFR = CKD · normocytic with high reticulocytes = bleeding or haemolysis · macrocytic with low B12 = treat and check neurology · any abnormal white count, platelet count or film = haematology, not iron.
4
Classify: three exits, not two "Anaemia — start iron, repeat in 3 months" is not one of them

Emergency or urgent

Compromise, symptomatic Hb below 70 or falling fast, active bleeding, an abnormal second cell line, haemolysis, B12 with neurology, pregnancy, or an NG12 criterion. Output: destination, FBC with all three lines, previous values, drugs.

Cause identified, managed in primary care

Stable, panel complete, a clear non-sinister cause — menstrual loss, dietary, coeliac, CKD — and NG12 criteria absent. Output: cause, treatment, recheck date, thresholds.

Unexplained or not responding

No source found, no measurable Hb response within 2–4 weeks of iron, recurrence, unexplained, persistent or progressive macrocytosis, or an abnormal film. Output: state the uncertainty, take the §6 route with the panel in the letter.
5
Cause classifier: MCV, then the source Only once the urgency is settled
Iron deficiency
low MCV, low ferritin		The commonest anaemia, and always a question about a source. Premenopausal women: menstrual loss is the usual cause — quantify it (flooding, clots, duration) and treat, but still send coeliac serology and investigate the GI tract if there are alarm symptoms, disproportionate or recurrent IDA, a poor response, a strong family history or other concerning features. Men and postmenopausal women: generally need GI investigation unless there is a clear alternative explanation — FIT (NG12) and upper and lower GI investigation via the local suspected colorectal and upper-GI pathways (BSG). Also coeliac disease, NSAIDs, bariatric surgery, poor diet and urinary loss (check for haematuria where indicated). But a low MCV with a normal ferritin and a normal or raised red cell count is thalassaemia trait — often strikingly low, with family origin from the Mediterranean, Middle East, South Asia, Africa or South-East Asia — needing a haemoglobinopathy screen and genetic counselling, not iron, which risks overload.
Chronic disease and CKD (normocytic), B12 and folate (macrocytic)Chronic disease: inflammation, infection, malignancy or autoimmune disease with a normal or raised ferritin and a low transferrin saturation — treat the underlying disease, and never accept the label without excluding deficiency and myeloma. CKD: with eGFR above 60 anaemia is unlikely to be due to CKD — investigate other causes; at 30–60 investigate other causes using judgement on the extent; below 30 CKD is more likely to contribute, but still consider other causes. Consider investigation and management when Hb is 110 g/L or lower or symptoms are attributable to anaemia; iron stores are repleted before an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent, which renal services start and monitor (NICE NG203). B12: pernicious anaemia, gastric or ileal surgery, Crohn's, metformin and long-term PPIs, vegan diet, nitrous oxide — neurological features mean treat today and never give folate first. Folate: investigate the cause rather than assuming diet — alcohol, malabsorption including coeliac disease, pregnancy, haemolysis, medications such as methotrexate. Check both together.
Marrow and haemolytic causes, and what not to concludeMyelodysplasia — macrocytosis that stays unexplained after review of alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, medications, reticulocytosis, B12/folate and haemolysis, or that is persistent, progressive or with other cytopenias; myeloma — anaemia with renal impairment, bone pain and a raised ESR; leukaemia or infiltration — blasts or pancytopenia; haemolysis — high reticulocytes, raised LDH and bilirubin, low haptoglobin. All are haematology routes, and none responds to iron. Do not conclude: "iron deficiency — start iron" with no source sought in a man or postmenopausal woman · "chronic disease" without excluding deficiency and myeloma · "dietary" over 50 · "menorrhagia" without quantifying the loss or checking coeliac serology · or iron for a low MCV with a normal ferritin, which is probably thalassaemia trait.
6
Refer routinely from this consultation Not same-day — the cases that leave by letter; §7 is what primary care does today
Iron deficiency with no source found after the panel and NG12 assessment → gastroenterology, for both upper and lower GI tract · no measurable response within 2–4 weeks of adequate oral iron, or recurring → gastroenterology, reviewing adherence, diagnosis, ongoing loss and absorption first · oral iron contraindicated, ineffective, not tolerated, unlikely to be absorbed, or too slow when rapid correction is needed → IV iron via the local infusion pathway (not limited to IBD or CKD) · confirmed coeliac disease → gastroenterology, without a gluten-free diet first (NG20) · haemoglobinopathy → haematology with genetic counselling and family screening · macrocytosis that is unexplained (normal B12, folate, TFTs and LFTs; alcohol and drugs reviewed), persistent, progressive, or with other cytopenias or an abnormal film → haematology · CKD anaemia needing ESA treatment → renal services (NICE NG203) · uncontrolled heavy menstrual bleeding → gynaecology (NG88).
7
The only decisions that belong in this consultation What primary care manages today — a stable patient with a cause identified
Answer "where is the blood going", then prescribeSend the whole panel and apply the NG12 criteria before starting iron. In a man or a postmenopausal woman, send the FIT and GI referral today and start the iron alongside, not instead: iron prescribed without a source sought is how GI cancer is missed here. Then the iron: one standard oral iron preparation once daily, giving about 50–100 mg elemental iron (ferrous sulfate 200 mg ≈ 65 mg; choose products from the local formulary); if not tolerated, consider alternate-day dosing, a different preparation or IV iron when clinically indicated. Take away from tea, coffee, calcium, antacids and levothyroxine — routine vitamin C is not needed, and do not stop an essential PPI without review; warn about black stools and constipation. Expect a measurable Hb response within 2–4 weeks — if not, review adherence, diagnosis, ongoing blood loss, malabsorption and concurrent disease, and escalate. Continue about 3 months after the Hb normalises, longer where loss, malabsorption or chronic disease persists. IV ferric carboxymaltose: MHRA warning of symptomatic hypophosphataemia.
B12 and folate (order matters), and heavy menstrual bleedingNever give folate alone until B12 deficiency is excluded or B12 treatment has started — it can worsen neurological damage. Treat B12 first: hydroxocobalamin IM per the BNF, using the neurological regimen if there are neurological features, with lifelong IM maintenance in autoimmune gastritis (pernicious anaemia), total gastrectomy or complete terminal ileal resection (other malabsorptive causes IM or oral at least 1 mg daily, by cause); then folate for a duration set by the cause, and review metformin and PPIs. Heavy menstrual bleeding: treat the loss as well as the anaemia — tranexamic acid, an NSAID, a hormonal option or the levonorgestrel intrauterine system (NG88) alongside iron — but still send coeliac serology and reconsider GI loss if there are GI symptoms, a poor response, or she is over 50.
Recheck, the escalation rule, and what not to doRecheck: FBC at about 4 weeks (earlier if severe or the response is uncertain), FBC and ferritin at 3 months, then 3 months after stopping; for B12, review at about 3 months (1 month in pregnancy or breastfeeding) and do not routinely recheck serum B12 on IM treatment — with the triggers written down: chest pain, breathlessness at rest, syncope, melaena or heavy bleeding → emergency admission; Hb falling, no measurable response at 2–4 weeks, or a new white-count or platelet abnormality → urgent specialist route. Do not: start iron before the haematinics are taken · give iron for a low MCV with a normal ferritin (screen for thalassaemia) · treat an abnormal white count or platelet count with iron · give folate before B12 · accept a normal MCV or a normal ferritin in inflammation as excluding deficiency · stop at a normal colonoscopy with the upper GI tract unexamined · or file iron deficiency in a man with no referral.
8
Endpoint and documentation Finish with one of four conclusions

1 · Emergency

Admission arranged; Hb with previous values, MCV, white count, platelets, film, observations with postural BP, bleeding history, drugs, and haematinics taken before treatment.

2 · Urgent or same-day

Same-day or NG12 route taken, with date; the question being excluded; panel sent; culprit drug stopped.

3 · Cause identified, managed

MCV, ferritin and cause recorded; FIT below 10 µg Hb/g, no other NG12 criterion and no persisting clinical concern; treatment with dose and duration; recheck date and thresholds written down.

4 · Unexplained or not responding

Uncertainty stated; §6 referral sent with the panel, with date; adherence and absorption reviewed; review owner.
Why primary-care management is safe today, in one line: no row on the page-1 screen applies · no chest pain, breathlessness at rest or syncope, observations normal with no postural drop · white count and platelets normal (and the film, where sent), so not a marrow problem · MCV and ferritin read with the CRP · B12, folate, eGFR, TFTs and coeliac serology sent before treatment · FIT sent (NG12) and below 10 µg Hb/g, with no other NG12 criterion and no persistent symptoms or clinical concern (FIT does not exclude cancer) · a clear non-sinister cause treated · recheck at 4 weeks with a named reviewer and written thresholds. Safety-net: contact us or 111 the same day for black or bloody stools, vomited blood, heavy bleeding, or breathlessness at rest, dizziness or faints; 999 for chest pain or collapse.
Result-handling limitation. An anaemia result needs the MCV, white count, platelets and any film read alongside it — filing a haemoglobin alone is how leukaemia and myeloma are missed. Convert to face-to-face or emergency assessment for any compromise, active bleeding, an abnormal second cell line, neurology with a macrocytosis, pregnancy or a rapid fall — and a Hb below 70, or blasts on the film, must never sit in an inbox overnight.
