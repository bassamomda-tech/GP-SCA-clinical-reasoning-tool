Abnormal liver blood tests in adults — triage and diagnostic approach

Decide the pattern, then investigate the cause — never re-test and wait. A mildly raised ALT is the commonest finding and fatty liver the commonest cause; the risk is fibrosis, which the enzymes do not show. Adults only — children, adolescents, pregnancy and the post-partum period need separate interpretation and local pathways; do not apply adult FIB-4, NFS or ELF thresholds or adult referral criteria to them.

Core rule. Three steps. 1. Is this acute liver failure? Acute liver injury without established cirrhosis, with coagulopathy (typically INR ≥1.5) and new hepatic encephalopathy, is acute liver failure; hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, renal failure, jaundice or rapid deterioration indicate severe disease — the ALT level alone does not define it, and an isolated raised INR is not diagnostic. 2. What is the pattern? Hepatocellular (ALT-dominant), cholestatic (ALP and GGT — confirm hepatic, not bone), or isolated bilirubin. 3. Investigate, do not repeat and wait: the degree of abnormality does not predict the severity of liver disease and normal enzymes do not exclude cirrhosis, so an abnormal ALT with risk factors needs a fibrosis assessment, not a repeat in 3 months.

1
Name the pattern, then ask what the numbers are actually telling you The pattern narrows the causes; albumin, INR and platelets tell you about function and fibrosis

Hepatocellular — ALT-dominant

Alcohol, fatty liver (MASLD, formerly NAFLD), drugs, hepatitis B and C, autoimmune hepatitis, haemochromatosis, coeliac and thyroid disease, Wilson's in the young. The AST:ALT ratio is supportive only — above 2 can occur in advanced alcohol-related liver disease but is not diagnostic; never use it alone to separate alcohol, fatty liver or viral hepatitis.

Cholestatic — ALP and GGT-dominant

Confirm the ALP is hepatic: a raised GGT supports liver origin; an isolated ALP with a normal GGT points to a non-hepatic cause — bone (Paget's, metastases), vitamin D deficiency/osteomalacia or pregnancy. Then gallstones, drugs, primary biliary cholangitis (AMA), malignant obstruction. Isolated GGT: review alcohol, obesity/metabolic risk, smoking, medication and enzyme-inducing drugs — often non-specific, but persistent or unexplained elevation should not simply be dismissed.

Isolated bilirubin

Unconjugated with normal enzymes and FBC in a well young adult, often after fasting: Gilbert's — benign, no follow-up. Exclude haemolysis (FBC, reticulocytes, LDH, haptoglobin ± blood film) first, and do not diagnose Gilbert's from a single bilirubin result. Conjugated bilirubin means hepatic or biliary disease, never Gilbert's.

Function and fibrosis — what matters more

A low albumin, raised INR, low platelets or raised bilirubin may indicate impaired synthetic function or portal hypertension, and are far more serious than a modestly raised ALT — but interpret albumin and INR with drugs (warfarin, anticoagulants), nutrition, inflammation, renal disease and vitamin K status. Normal enzymes do not exclude cirrhosis. Assess fibrosis using the local pathway (ELF per NICE NG49, or FIB-4 triage).

999 or admission if jaundiced, encephalopathic or withdrawing Same-day assessment; urgent alcohol service referral		 Severe alcohol-related hepatitis has a high mortality and needs specialist care. Never advise abrupt cessation in dependence without support — unplanned withdrawal risks seizures and delirium tremens (CG100, CG115). Prescribe oral thiamine; admit for parenteral thiamine if Wernicke's is suspected. Refer to the alcohol service at this visit. Abnormal LFTs in pregnancy: raised transaminases or bilirubin with itching (especially palms and soles), hypertension, headache, epigastric pain, vomiting or malaise — particularly after 20 weeks. Pre-eclampsia and HELLP · obstetric cholestasis · acute fatty liver of pregnancy
999 if hypertensive, unwell or vomiting Same-day maternity assessment		 Abnormal LFTs in pregnancy are an obstetric problem: check the BP and send urine protein immediately, and speak directly to the maternity unit. Itching of the palms and soles with raised bile acids is obstetric cholestasis. Acute fatty liver of pregnancy presents after 20 weeks with vomiting and malaise, and is life-threatening.
Cancer features with abnormal LFTs: jaundice at 40 or over · an upper abdominal mass consistent with an enlarged liver or gallbladder · 60 or over with weight loss plus new diabetes, back pain, nausea, vomiting or bowel change · dysphagia at any age · 55 or over with weight loss and upper abdominal pain. Pancreatic · hepatobiliary · upper GI cancer
Urgent suspected-cancer referral within 2 weeks for jaundice at 40+ · urgent direct-access ultrasound for an upper abdominal mass consistent with an enlarged liver or gallbladder · urgent direct-access CT (or ultrasound if CT unavailable) at 60+ · suspected cancer pathway referral for oesophageal or stomach cancer for dysphagia, or 55+ with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia · NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)		 Match the route to the criterion (NG12) and state the LFT pattern in the request. Painless jaundice at 40 or over is an urgent suspected-cancer referral, not a routine hepatology letter — and not an automatic 999 transfer unless emergency features are present. Cirrhosis needs hepatology referral and 6-monthly HCC ultrasound surveillance ± AFP per cause and hepatology protocol (NG50); chronic hepatitis B with significant fibrosis, and selected non-cirrhotic patients by age, family history and HBV DNA, also qualify (CG165); not routine in advanced fibrosis without cirrhosis; not for end-of-life care or those unfit for HCC treatment. A normal ultrasound with persisting jaundice does not close the question.
Safety rule. The degree of enzyme abnormality does not predict the severity of liver disease, and normal LFTs do not exclude cirrhosis — so "mildly abnormal, repeat in 3 months" is not a plan. Look at albumin, INR, platelets and bilirubin, and assess fibrosis via the local pathway. A raised ALP with a normal GGT is probably non-hepatic (bone, vitamin D deficiency, pregnancy). An isolated bilirubin is only Gilbert's once haemolysis is excluded.

Assessment and the non-invasive liver screen Send it in one go, not in stages over months

History

Alcohol units per week, asked specifically (AUDIT-C) · all drugs including over-the-counter, herbal, recreational and gym products, and anything started or stopped in the preceding 6 months · anticoagulants and antiplatelets · paracetamol · BMI, diabetes, hypertension, lipids · blood-borne virus risk: injecting drug use, tattoos, transfusion before 1991, ethnicity/country of birth, household or sexual exposure, immunosuppression · pregnancy status · previous results, imaging and platelet trend · decompensation symptoms (ascites, oedema, confusion, bleeding, muscle wasting) · family and autoimmune history.

Examine

Jaundice; mental state and asterixis (supports encephalopathy but is not required for it); stigmata of chronic liver disease — spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, Dupuytren's (supportive, not diagnostic); hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, ascites; an upper abdominal or gallbladder mass; bruising; BP and urine protein in pregnancy.

The liver screen, then stage the fibrosis

Hepatitis B surface antigen and C antibody · ferritin and transferrin saturation · autoantibodies (ANA, AMA, SMA) with immunoglobulins · caeruloplasmin · coeliac serology · TFTs · HbA1c, lipids · FBC · INR and albumin · liver ultrasound. Then the step most often missed: assess fibrosis using the locally commissioned pathway. NICE NG49 supports ELF (≥10.51 = advanced fibrosis → hepatology); some UK pathways and the BSG guideline triage with FIB-4 first (<1.3 generally low risk under 65; ≥1.3 → second-line test), then ELF or elastography. Do not combine thresholds from different pathways; a low FIB-4 does not exclude advanced fibrosis. Fibrosis, not the ALT, determines prognosis and who needs hepatology.
4
Classify: three exits, not two "Mildly deranged, repeat in 3 months" is not one of them

Emergency or urgent

Acute liver failure, cholangitis, decompensation, new jaundice, marked or rapidly rising transaminases, alcohol-related hepatitis, pregnancy, or an NG12 criterion. Output: destination, pattern, albumin, INR, platelets, drugs and alcohol.

Cause identified, managed in primary care

Mild abnormality, no red flag, the screen negative apart from a modifiable cause — usually fatty liver or alcohol — and a low fibrosis score. Output: cause, score, intervention, recheck date.

Unexplained or fibrosis-positive

Screen negative but abnormal beyond 3–6 months, a raised or indeterminate FIB-4, or ELF ≥10.51, positive autoantibody or virology, or abnormal synthetic function. Output: state the uncertainty, take the §6 route with the screen in the letter.

Pattern classifier: the enzyme profile, then the cause Only once the urgency is settled
Fatty liver and alcohol
ALT-dominant		Fatty liver (MASLD, formerly NAFLD) — the commonest cause of a mildly raised ALT: obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, a bright liver on ultrasound, negative screen. The ALT does not indicate severity and may be normal in cirrhosis, so stage the fibrosis via the local pathway (ELF per NG49, or FIB-4/NFS triage then ELF or elastography), not the AST:ALT ratio. Alcohol — an AST:ALT above 2 (supportive, not diagnostic) with a raised GGT, macrocytosis and thrombocytopenia; quantify with AUDIT-C. Abstinence reverses much, but abrupt cessation in dependence is dangerous without support (CG100, CG115).
Drug-induced liver injury
either pattern		Any drug or product started or stopped in the preceding 6 months (latency days to months): antibiotics (co-amoxiclav, flucloxacillin, macrolides), methotrexate, azathioprine, statins, NSAIDs, amiodarone, herbal and bodybuilding supplements. Stop a suspected non-essential drug where clinically safe (essential treatment only with advice), recheck at 4–6 weeks, record it as a suspected reaction; the decision depends on symptoms, bilirubin, INR, competing diagnoses and the medicine. A statin need not stop for stable transaminases below 3 times normal; a persistent unexplained rise above 3 times normal needs investigation and review, not automatic permanent discontinuation (NG238).
Viral, autoimmune, inherited and cholestatic — the specialist limbHepatitis B or C — refer all HBsAg-positive adults and confirmed HCV RNA (or core antigen); antiviral choice is specialist-led; check HBV status before HCV treatment (reactivation risk). Autoimmune hepatitis — raised IgG with positive ANA or SMA; hepatology before steroids. Haemochromatosis — transferrin saturation above 45% with a raised ferritin, needing HFE genotyping. Wilson's — under 40, low caeruloplasmin. Cholestatic — confirm hepatic origin with the GGT first, then gallstones and biliary obstruction (dilated ducts), drugs, primary biliary cholangitis (AMA-positive, itch and fatigue), sclerosing cholangitis (with IBD, needing MRCP), and malignant obstruction — the NG12 route, not a routine referral.
Gilbert's, bone ALP, and what not to concludeGilbert's: isolated unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, normal enzymes, no haemolysis — benign, no follow-up. Isolated ALP with a normal GGT: bone, vitamin D deficiency or pregnancy, not liver. Do not conclude: "fatty liver" without a fibrosis score · "alcohol" without asking the units · "repeat in 3 months" as an endpoint · "normal LFTs, so no cirrhosis".

Refer routinely from this consultation Not same-day — the cases that leave by letter; §7 is what primary care does today
Any positive hepatitis B surface antigen, or hepatitis C antibody confirmed by HCV RNA or core antigen → hepatology or the viral hepatitis service — both treatable and hepatitis C curable, so never simply monitored · ELF ≥10.51 (NG49), or a raised or indeterminate FIB-4 or elastography per the local pathway → hepatology for fibrosis staging · positive autoantibodies with raised IgG → hepatology before any steroid · AMA-positive cholestasis, or cholestasis with IBD (for MRCP) → hepatology · transferrin saturation above 45% with a raised ferritin → HFE genotyping · abnormal beyond 3–6 months with a negative screen → hepatology with the full screen in the letter · confirmed cirrhosis → hepatology, with 6-monthly HCC ultrasound surveillance and variceal screening (NG50) · alcohol dependence → the alcohol service, today.

The only decisions that belong in this consultation What primary care manages today — mild abnormality with a modifiable cause and a low fibrosis score
Send the whole screen and stage the fibrosis, then address the drug and the alcoholScreen: do it in one go — virology, iron studies, autoantibodies with immunoglobulins, caeruloplasmin, coeliac serology, TFTs, HbA1c, lipids, FBC, INR, albumin, ultrasound — and assess fibrosis (local ELF/FIB-4/elastography pathway) at the same time, because staging the fibrosis changes outcomes and re-testing the ALT is not a substitute. Drug: stop the suspect and recheck at 4–6 weeks, recording it as a suspected adverse reaction; ask specifically about herbal, gym and weight-loss supplements, which patients do not report as medicines. Do not withhold a statin solely for stable transaminases below 3 times the upper limit or for fatty liver; check transaminases at baseline, within 3 months of starting or changing treatment and at 12 months, then only if clinically indicated. Persistent unexplained ALT/AST above 3 times → investigate, and usually do not start, or withhold if already taking, pending review — not automatic permanent discontinuation; consider cautious reintroduction once resolved (NG238). Alcohol: quantify with AUDIT-C and advise today, but in dependence do not advise abrupt cessation without support — withdrawal risks seizures and delirium tremens. Prescribe oral thiamine where indicated, aim for abstinence with alcohol-service support (after withdrawal, acamprosate or oral naltrexone per CG115 — avoid disulfiram, and avoid naltrexone in advanced liver disease), refer to the alcohol service now, and admit if there is jaundice, encephalopathy or established withdrawal (CG100, CG115).
Fatty liver, recheck, and what not to doFatty liver with a low fibrosis score: treat it as cardiometabolic disease, because that is what kills these patients — 7–10% weight loss, a Mediterranean-style diet, 150 minutes of activity weekly, alcohol reduction, optimised diabetes, BP and lipids, and a statin, which is not contraindicated and is usually indicated (NG49). Review: weight, metabolic risk, alcohol and lifestyle at about 3–6 months; repeat LFTs with albumin, INR and platelets at 4–6 weeks after a drug or alcohol change, or if clinically useful, persisting or worsening; repeat fibrosis assessment per the local pathway (NG49: 3-yearly if ELF <10.51), with the triggers written down — jaundice, confusion, drowsiness, bruising, vomiting blood or black stools, or abdominal swelling → emergency admission; marked or rapidly rising transaminases, a rising bilirubin, a falling albumin or platelets → urgent specialist advice. Do not: repeat the LFTs for months instead of investigating · reassure on normal enzymes, which do not exclude cirrhosis · investigate a raised ALP without a GGT · call an isolated bilirubin Gilbert's without excluding haemolysis · refer painless jaundice at 40 or over routinely (an NG12 route) · give NSAIDs in ascites or decompensated cirrhosis, or duplicate paracetamol-containing products · or advise abrupt alcohol cessation in dependence.

Endpoint and documentation Finish with one of four conclusions

1 · Emergency

Admission arranged; pattern, ALT, bilirubin, albumin, INR, platelets, glucose, mental state, paracetamol and alcohol history.

2 · Urgent or same-day

Assessment or urgent imaging arranged; the question being excluded; NG12 route where it applies; screen sent; drug stopped.

3 · Cause identified, managed

Pattern, cause and fibrosis score recorded; drug or alcohol addressed; cardiometabolic risk treated; recheck date and thresholds written.

4 · Unexplained or fibrosis-positive

Uncertainty stated; §6 referral sent with the screen and fibrosis score; surveillance where cirrhosis is confirmed.
Why primary-care management is safe today, in one line: no row on the page-1 screen applies · not jaundiced, no encephalopathy or ascites · albumin, INR and platelets normal · the pattern is named and a raised ALP confirmed hepatic or bone · the full screen is sent and negative apart from a modifiable cause · fibrosis assessment is low risk · alcohol quantified, drugs reviewed · NG12 considered · recheck booked with a named reviewer and written thresholds. Safety-net: contact us or 111 the same day if your skin or eyes turn yellow, you become confused or unusually sleepy, bruise or bleed easily, or your abdomen swells; call 999 for confusion or marked drowsiness, vomiting blood or black stools. Seek urgent help for fever with jaundice or upper abdominal pain, rapidly increasing abdominal swelling, severe vomiting or rapid deterioration. Result-handling limitation. An abnormal LFT is a result to act on, not to file: a jaundiced or coagulopathic patient must not sit in an inbox overnight. Convert to face-to-face or emergency assessment where there is jaundice, reported confusion or drowsiness, a paracetamol history, pregnancy, known cirrhosis, or marked or rapidly rising transaminases.
