Hyperkalaemia in adults — triage and diagnostic approach

A result on a screen, usually in a well patient. Decide the urgency from the level and the ECG together, exclude a spurious sample, find the cause, then protect the drugs that are protecting the kidney and heart.

AdultsLab resultK⁺ above 5.5v1.0 · Sep 2026
Core rule. K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L is a medical emergency at any level of wellness, and any ECG change potentially attributable to hyperkalaemia is an emergency at any potassium — arrange transfer, do not repeat the sample first. One classification throughout: mild 5.5–5.9 · moderate 6.0–6.4 · severe ≥6.5; 5.1–5.4 is above the local reference range, not hyperkalaemia. 6.0–6.4: ECG and same-day clinical assessment, with admission according to the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state. Two questions follow every raised result: is it real (haemolysis, a fist-clenched or delayed sample) and is there AKI. A normal ECG does not exclude severe hyperkalaemia. Act first on a critical value and confirm in parallel — never instead. Hospital treatment appears on this sheet only so you know what is coming and what to hand over; it is not for primary-care administration.
0
On seeing the result: is this an emergency now? Arrange emergency admission — 999 or immediate discussion with the acute team
K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L, however well the patient seems · any ECG change at any potassium — peaked or tented T waves, flattened or absent P waves, broad QRS, bradycardia, sine-wave pattern · palpitations, syncope, chest pain or new arrhythmia · muscle weakness, flaccid paralysis or paraesthesiae · features of AKI — oliguria, new confusion, volume depletion, rapidly rising creatinine · suspected rhabdomyolysis, crush injury, tumour lysis or massive haemolysis · known dialysis patient with a missed session. Do the ECG if it does not delay transfer, stop the culprit drugs, and hand over the potassium, the eGFR and the drug list.
1
Is the result real, and how urgent? A critical value is acted on first and confirmed in parallel
Could it be spurious? Haemolysis, fist clenching or tourniquet, delay or cold in transit, thrombocytosis or leucocytosis, sampling above a drip — ask the lab. A clean repeat is reasonable only below 6.5 in a well patient with no AKI and a normal ECG. Point-of-care or blood-gas potassium is usable for rapid decisions, confirmed by laboratory testing when safe; gas analysers may not flag haemolysis, so a discrepancy needs clinical correlation, not automatic dismissal. Urgency: severe ≥6.5, or any ECG change, significant symptoms, AKI or oliguria, missed dialysis, rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis or a rapid rise → emergency transfer, whatever the number; moderate 6.0–6.4 → ECG and same-day assessment, admission on the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state; mild 5.5–5.9 → primary care if asymptomatic, well and no AKI. A normal ECG never excludes severe hyperkalaemia. Is the kidney failing? Compare creatinine and eGFR with previous values — oliguria, volume depletion, sepsis, obstruction, or recent NSAID or contrast exposure makes this AKI, which changes the destination regardless of the potassium. What is the patient on? ACE inhibitor or ARB, spironolactone or eplerenone, amiloride, sacubitril–valsartan, potassium supplements, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, ciclosporin or tacrolimus, beta-blockers, digoxin — and potassium-containing salt substitutes.

K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L however well the patient looks · any ECG change potentially attributable to hyperkalaemia at any level · AKI — oliguria or anuria, rising creatinine, volume depletion, sepsis, obstruction, recent contrast or NSAID; or CKD 4–5 with a new rise · missed dialysis session · rhabdomyolysis, crush injury, burns, massive haemolysis or tumour lysis · a rapidly rising potassium. Risk of fatal arrhythmia · hyperkalaemic AKI · massive potassium release
Emergency transfer now		 Do not repeat the sample first — a repeat or point-of-care potassium runs in parallel only where it does not delay transfer. 12-lead ECG only if immediately available and it does not delay transfer; a normal ECG does not exclude severe hyperkalaemia. Do not attempt IV calcium in routine primary care unless an explicit local protocol, equipment and trained staff are available — that decision belongs to the receiving team. Withhold ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs, amiloride, potassium supplements, salt substitutes, trimethoprim and NSAIDs, and say what was withheld. Assess volume status and examine for a palpable bladder. With suspected muscle injury or tumour lysis the potassium keeps rising — say "query rhabdomyolysis" or "query tumour lysis" so fluids start early. Send the previous potassium and eGFR so the trend travels with the patient.
K⁺ 6.0–6.4 mmol/L with a normal ECG in a patient who is well, or a rise on repeat testing. Moderate hyperkalaemia — needs assessment today
ECG today and same-day clinical assessment		 ECG the same day, not "at the next appointment". Stop or hold the contributing drugs and review the whole list including over-the-counter NSAIDs and salt substitutes. Repeat U&E within 24 hours with a clean, promptly delivered sample. Dietary potassium restriction with written advice. If the repeat is still ≥6.0, or the ECG is abnormal, it becomes an admission.
Adrenal or tubular cause suspected: hyperkalaemia with hyponatraemia, postural hypotension, fatigue, weight loss, pigmentation, hypoglycaemia, or after stopping long-term steroids · or hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate and normal anion gap in diabetic CKD, or on trimethoprim, heparin, a calcineurin inhibitor or high-dose NSAID. Addison's disease · adrenal crisis · hypoaldosteronism · type 4 RTA · drug-induced tubular defect
999 if hypotensive, vomiting or acutely unwell Otherwise same-day drug review and endocrine or renal advice		 A high potassium with a low sodium in an unwell patient is an adrenal crisis until excluded — each number looks mild alone. Treat the crisis; do not wait for a short Synacthen test, and take cortisol only if it does not delay treatment. Never stop long-term steroids abruptly. Otherwise check bicarbonate and glucose: trimethoprim is a common reversible culprit — switch the antibiotic. Persistent hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate needs renal assessment, not repeated rechecks; aldosterone and renin are specialist-directed.
Heart-failure or CKD patient in whom the drugs are the treatment: a rise after starting or up-titrating an ACE inhibitor, ARB, sacubitril–valsartan or an MRA. Expected drug effect versus unsafe level — the decision is which drug, at what dose
Same-day review of drug and dose; specialist advice before abandoning an MRA		 A modest, stable rise after starting a renin–angiotensin blocker is expected and does not automatically mean stopping the drug — these agents reduce mortality, and blanket withdrawal causes harm. Follow the CKD/heart-failure monitoring rules, recheck within 1–2 weeks, and seek cardiology or renal advice about dose reduction or a potassium binder before stopping outright.
Unexplained renal impairment with systemic features: weight loss, bone pain, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, night sweats or lymphadenopathy behind the raised potassium. Myeloma · malignancy presenting through renal impairment
Very urgent myeloma screen — FBC, calcium, ESR/plasma viscosity, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains, sent together (urine Bence Jones if free light chains unavailable) · NICE NG12		 Hyperkalaemia is not itself an NG12 criterion, but the renal impairment behind it may be the presentation of myeloma. Send the myeloma screen as a very urgent investigation and refer urgently if abnormal (NG12). Do not stop at correcting the potassium.
Safety rule. Wellness is not reassurance: patients with K⁺ of 7 often feel normal until the arrhythmia. A normal ECG does not make a high potassium safe, and a normal potassium on a haemolysed repeat does not undo a genuine previous result. If a row applies and the safe destination is unavailable, escalate rather than recheck.

3
Assessment and investigation Above 6.0 this needs an ECG and a face-to-face assessment
ECG: peaked T waves → flattened or absent P waves and PR prolongation → broad QRS → sine wave and asystole; also bradycardia and new arrhythmia. A new broad QRS may equally be bundle branch block, pacing or sodium-channel blockade — compare with a previous ECG, and read peaked T waves in multiple leads and in context. Clinical: volume status and BP with postural drop, pulse and rhythm, palpable bladder, muscle power and reflexes, capillary glucose, Addisonian picture. Bloods — conditional, not a panel. All confirmed hyperkalaemia: U&E and eGFR on a clean unclenched sample delivered promptly, bicarbonate, glucose, FBC, medication review, ECG by severity. Only on trigger: muscle injury or tumour lysis → CK, urate, phosphate, calcium, LDH via acute services · low sodium with hypotension → emergency adrenal assessment; cortisol only if it does not delay treatment · persistent hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate → renal assessment. Do not delay transfer to repeat a critical value, use a binder as emergency treatment, recheck at 2 weeks at ≥6.0, stop a mortality-reducing drug on one mild rise, or order aldosterone and renin routinely.
4
Classify: three exits, not two "Repeat in a week" is only an answer for the mild, well, no-AKI group
Emergency or same-day — K⁺ ≥6.5, any ECG change, AKI or oliguria, missed dialysis, rhabdomyolysis or tumour lysis, adrenal crisis, a rapid rise, or 6.0–6.4 needing assessment today. Output: destination, potassium and eGFR with previous values, ECG findings, drugs withheld with the time. · Mild, manageable in primary care — 5.5–5.9, asymptomatic, well, no AKI, normal ECG, cause identified and modifiable. Output: cause, drugs and diet changed, repeat date, escalation trigger. · Unclassified or recurrent — persistent despite drug and diet changes, a level limiting a needed ACE inhibitor or MRA, or no cause found. Output: state the uncertainty, take the §6 route, name the reviewer and timeframe.
5
Cause classifier: spurious, shifted, retained, or loaded Only once the urgency is settled
Spurious, then drugsPseudohyperkalaemia: haemolysis, fist clenching or tourniquet, delay or cold in transit, thrombocytosis or leucocytosis, sampling above a drip. A clean repeat is acceptable only below 6.5 with no AKI and a normal ECG. Reduced excretion — drugs, the commonest real cause in primary care: ACE inhibitor, ARB, sacubitril–valsartan, spironolactone or eplerenone, amiloride, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, ciclosporin or tacrolimus, potassium supplements — often two or three together, plus a salt substitute the patient does not count as a medicine.
Kidney, endocrine, shift, load or releaseKidney: AKI, CKD 4–5 or obstruction — the question is always what changed (a new drug, dehydration, sepsis, obstruction) rather than the CKD itself. Endocrine: Addison's or hypoaldosteronism — hyperkalaemia with hyponatraemia, postural hypotension, fatigue, weight loss, pigmentation; type 4 RTA in diabetic CKD gives hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate and a normal anion gap. Shift: metabolic acidosis, insulin deficiency or DKA, beta-blockers, digoxin toxicity, severe hyperglycaemia — a high potassium in a hyperglycaemic unwell patient is a DKA question first. Load or release: rhabdomyolysis, crush injury, burns, haemolysis, tumour lysis, massive transfusion; dietary excess or supplements where excretion is already impaired. Potassium-based salt substitutes in CKD are a frequent and invisible cause. Do not conclude by default: "probably haemolysis" without asking the lab or at ≥6.5 · "chronic and stable" without comparing the trend · "diet" with a normal eGFR and no drugs — look again for adrenal or tubular causes; never treat the number alone when hyponatraemia or a low bicarbonate sits beside it.
6
Refer or seek advice routinely from this consultation Not same-day — the cases that leave by letter or advice-and-guidance; §7 is what primary care does today
Persistent or recurrent hyperkalaemia despite removing reversible contributors → nephrology (NG203) · hyperkalaemia limiting a needed ACE inhibitor, ARB or MRA → renal or cardiology advice on dose and on a potassium binder. The NICE criteria are specific: CKD 3b–5 or heart failure · confirmed K⁺ ≥6.0 mmol/L · not on dialysis · RAAS inhibition not optimised because of hyperkalaemia (TA623, TA599) — a potassium above 5.5 is not itself a reason to start one; initiation and prescribing follow local renal/cardiology arrangements and the Greater Manchester formulary or shared-care protocol · declining eGFR or proteinuria → nephrology · stable suspected Addison's or hypoaldosteronism → endocrinology with a 9 am cortisol (a crisis is an emergency, not a test) · suspected type 4 RTA → nephrology — fludrocortisone is a specialist decision, not a primary-care option · heart failure needing an MRA → the heart-failure team, not unilateral withdrawal.
7
The only decisions that belong in this consultation What primary care manages today — mild hyperkalaemia in a well patient
Review every drug — including the invisible ones — before changing a prognostic oneStop potassium supplements and potassium-containing salt substitutes (LoSalt and similar) — patients do not report these as medicines. Stop or switch trimethoprim, NSAIDs including over-the-counter ibuprofen, and heparin where possible; review amiloride and any MRA dose. ACE inhibitors, ARBs and MRAs reduce mortality in heart failure and slow CKD progression, so think before stopping: on the emergency or same-day pathway, withhold contributors pending assessment; for a stable mild rise, remove the reversible contributors first, assess AKI, diet and volume status, then consider dose adjustment or specialist advice. Do not make eGFR-based stop decisions in isolation — follow CKD and heart-failure guidance, seek renal or cardiology advice where the drug matters, and in heart failure or albuminuric CKD plan reintroduction rather than indefinite discontinuation. Give a personalised sick-day plan with explicit restart advice, not a blanket instruction; NSAIDs are generally avoided altogether rather than paused.
Diet, the driver, and the recheck rule — write it downDiet — individualised, substitution not exclusion. Start with potassium-containing salt substitutes (avoid entirely), potassium supplements and the concentrated sources the history identifies; do not reflexively restrict milk, pulses, nuts and vegetables. Boiling and discarding the water is a practical technique whose effect varies, not a fixed percentage; numeric targets and advanced-CKD advice come from the renal dietitian. Never rely on diet alone at ≥6.0. Driver: correct dehydration and constipation, treat infection, relieve obstruction, optimise glycaemic control. Assess hydration individually — no standard fluid target in CKD, heart failure, oliguria or suspected obstruction. Recheck: U&E within 3–5 days for a mild rise, sooner if a drug was changed, on a clean unclenched sample delivered promptly. Record and tell the patient what triggers what: ≥6.5, any ECG change, symptoms, AKI or a rapid rise → emergency transfer; 6.0–6.4 → ECG and same-day assessment. After hospital treatment rebound is expected — those treatments shift potassium rather than remove it, so check the discharge plan names a recheck interval. Name who is checking the result.
What not to doDo not repeat a K⁺ ≥6.5 instead of transferring. Do not start a potassium binder outside the NICE criteria and local shared care. Calcium resonium is not acute treatment — UKKA removed it from the acute protocol in 2023 (slow, unpredictable, risk of intestinal injury), and it is never a community substitute for admission. Do not give sodium bicarbonate routinely for hyperkalaemia. Do not initiate fludrocortisone. Do not stop long-term steroids abruptly. Do not file a raised potassium as "borderline" without a plan and a named reviewer — and a critical result must never sit in an inbox or a routine repeat queue overnight.
8
Endpoint and documentation Finish with one of four conclusions
Why primary-care management is safe today: K⁺ 5.5–5.9 · asymptomatic and well · no AKI · ECG normal · a modifiable cause identified and acted on · sodium, bicarbonate and glucose reviewed · repeat booked within 3–5 days with a named reviewer and written escalation thresholds. Safety-net wording: contact us or 111 the same day for palpitations, feeling faint or blacking out, new muscle weakness or numbness, being unable to keep fluids down, or passing much less urine; come for the repeat even if you feel well — a high potassium causes no symptoms until it affects the heart. Remote and out-of-hours rule. 6.0 or above cannot be managed by telephone — it needs an ECG and face-to-face assessment the same day; convert to emergency assessment where an ECG cannot be obtained today or the patient cannot attend. A critical result must never sit in an inbox or a routine repeat queue overnight — name who acts on it out of hours.
