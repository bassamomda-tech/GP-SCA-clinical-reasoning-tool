Safety

Red Flags — Dehydration, Sepsis and the Surgical Mimic First

Acute diarrhoea is usually self-limiting infective gastroenteritis. The work of this consultation is to find the patient who is dehydrated, septic, bleeding, has C. difficile or STEC, or has a surgical or metabolic cause wearing a diarrhoea label. Chronic (4 weeks or more) diarrhoea follows the chronic diarrhoea algorithm.

Shock or high-risk sepsis Hypotension, new confusion, mottled skin, RR ≥25, HR >130, no urine for 18 h, NEWS2 ≥7 → 999 Do not wait for stool results; follow NICE NG253 sepsis criteria
Severe dehydration or unable to keep fluids down Postural dizziness or syncope, oliguria, dry mucosae, tachycardia, persistent vomiting preventing oral rehydration, frail or pregnant → Same-day assessment ± admission for IV fluids and U&E
Bloody diarrhoea with severe abdominal pain Possible STEC (E. coli O157) → Same-day discussion; stool labelled "suspected STEC". No loperamide and no antibiotic without microbiology or Health Protection Team advice
Possible haemolytic uraemic syndrome Reduced urine output, pallor, bruising, oedema, confusion after bloody diarrhoea → Same-day hospital assessment; FBC, platelets, renal function, notify HPT on suspicion
Severe or rapidly worsening C. difficile Recent antibiotics or hospital stay with fever, marked abdominal pain or distension, rising WCC, AKI, or ileus with few stools → Same-day admission (NICE NG199)
Acute abdomen Severe or localised pain, guarding, distension, bilious vomiting, pain out of proportion in an older patient (mesenteric ischaemia) → Same-day surgical assessment — 999 if peritonitic or shocked
Diabetes and vomiting or diarrhoea Check capillary glucose and blood ketones; DKA, including euglycaemic DKA on an SGLT2 inhibitor → Same-day per sick-day/DKA pathway (999 if drowsy or Kussmaul breathing)
Fever after travel to a malaria area Diarrhoea is a common presentation of malaria and enteric fever → Same-day malaria films; enteric fever needs hospital assessment
Immunocompromised or neutropenic Recent chemotherapy (within 6 weeks) with fever or feeling unwell → Immediate neutropenic sepsis pathway (acute oncology hotline / 999; do not wait for FBC). HIV, transplant, biologics → same-day specialist advice
Adrenal insufficiency or on long-term steroids Diarrhoea and vomiting risk adrenal crisis → Same-day — follow steroid sick-day rules; IM hydrocortisone and 999 if unable to take oral steroid and unwell

Most deaths from gastroenteritis in adults are from dehydration and its complications in older, frail or comorbid people — not from the organism itself. Tachycardia can reflect fever, pain or anxiety, and skin turgor is unreliable in older adults, so judge the whole picture, including urine output and the ability to drink.

Loperamide and antibiotics in STEC may increase the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome, so bloody diarrhoea with severe abdominal pain is managed on specialist or Health Protection Team advice (UKHSA). C. difficile can be severe with few stools when ileus develops — a quiet bowel in an ill patient is not reassuring. In diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitors can cause DKA with near-normal glucose (MHRA), so check ketones rather than relying on the glucose.

2
Diagnose

Focused History — Onset, Exposures and Organism Clues

Duration & pattern
Acute: under 14 days. Persistent: 14 days to 4 weeks. Chronic: 4 weeks or more (see the chronic diarrhoea algorithm). Stool frequency, volume, watery or bloody, nocturnal stools, urgency, tenesmus
Blood & fever
Bloody diarrhoea + fever → invasive organism (Campylobacter, Shigella, Salmonella, STEC) or first presentation of IBD; bloody without fever + severe pain → think STEC or ischaemic colitis
Vomiting first
Vomiting-predominant, short incubation (1–6 h) → preformed toxin (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus). Vomiting and diarrhoea in an outbreak (care home, cruise, ward) → norovirus
Food & contacts
Suspect meal, barbecue, poultry, eggs, shellfish, unpasteurised milk; others ill after the same food → suspected food poisoning is notifiable; household or institutional contacts
Travel
Where, when, food and water exposure, fever. Travellers' diarrhoea is usually self-limiting; persisting diarrhoea after travel → Giardia, Cryptosporidium, amoebiasis; fever → malaria and enteric fever first
Antibiotics & hospital
Antibiotics in the last 12 weeks (clindamycin, cephalosporins, co-amoxiclav, quinolones), recent admission or care-home residence, PPI use → C. difficile until the stool test says otherwise
Medication review
New or increased metformin, magnesium-containing antacids, laxatives (including covert or overuse), PPI, SSRIs, colchicine, NSAIDs, mycophenolate, chemotherapy, orlistat; also drugs that become dangerous with dehydration (see the sick-day rules in step 7)
Host factors
Age, frailty, pregnancy, diabetes, CKD, heart failure, IBD, immunosuppression (HIV, transplant, biologics, chemotherapy, long-term steroids), adrenal insufficiency
Occupation & setting
Food handler, healthcare or social-care worker, nursery or school staff, or a child under 5 at nursery — these change exclusion and clearance advice (UKHSA risk groups)
Sexual history
Men who have sex with men: Shigella and other sexually transmitted enteric infections — ask sensitively; test and advise on transmission

Incubation and the dominant symptom narrow the organism: vomiting within hours of a meal suggests a toxin; bloody diarrhoea with fever suggests an invasive bacterium; prolonged watery diarrhoea after travel suggests a parasite. Most of the time the exact organism does not change management, so the history matters most for spotting the patient who needs a stool sample, a public-health action or a different diagnosis.

C. difficile risk persists for weeks after an antibiotic course and is increased by PPIs. A new diarrhoea in an older patient recently started on metformin, or after a laxative dose increase, needs a medication fix, not a stool sample.

3
Diagnose

Examination — Hydration, Sepsis and the Abdomen

Observations
Temperature, pulse, BP (lying and standing if dizzy), respiratory rate, SpO₂, capillary refill, mental state — calculate NEWS2 where the local pathway uses it
Hydration
Mucous membranes, urine output in the last 24 h, postural symptoms, weight change. In older adults skin turgor and sunken eyes are unreliable — urine output and postural BP matter more
Abdomen
Tenderness (localised or diffuse), guarding, distension, bowel sounds, masses, hernial orifices. Right iliac fossa pain → appendicitis can present with diarrhoea; left iliac fossa → diverticulitis
Rectal examination
When the history suggests: overflow in an older or immobile patient (faecal loading), rectal mass, or blood not yet seen
Extra-intestinal clues
Rash or jaundice (hepatitis A, enteric fever), joint swelling or red eye (reactive arthritis, IBD), mouth ulcers or perianal disease (Crohn's), pallor and bruising (HUS)
Capillary glucose
In anyone with diabetes, and blood ketones if on insulin or an SGLT2 inhibitor, or unwell

The examination answers two questions: how dry and how septic is this person, and is there something in the abdomen that is not gastroenteritis? Diarrhoea is common in appendicitis, pelvic sepsis and mesenteric ischaemia, and "gastroenteritis" is a frequent label on delayed surgical diagnoses — particularly in older adults with pain out of proportion to the signs.

Overflow diarrhoea around impacted stool is a common and easily missed cause in frail, immobile or opioid-treated older people; giving loperamide makes it worse.

4
Diagnose

Investigations — Stool Only When the Result Changes Something

Send a stool sample if
Blood or pus in the stool · systemically unwell or fever · immunocompromised · recent antibiotics or hospital admission (request C. difficile testing) · diarrhoea persisting beyond 7 days · recent foreign travel · suspected food poisoning or outbreak · risk-group occupation or setting (food handler, health or social care, nursery) where clearance may be needed
How to request
One sample for MC&S / PCR; add ova, cysts and parasites after travel or diarrhoea beyond 14 days; label "suspected STEC" if bloody; give travel and antibiotic history on the form. Do not send formed stool for C. difficile
Bloods if
U&E and FBC when dehydration is more than mild, vomiting is persistent, the patient is older, frail, on diuretics, ACE inhibitor/ARB, lithium or with CKD; CRP if bloody or systemic; FBC, platelets and renal function if HUS is possible; malaria films if fever after travel to a malaria area (same day)
Blood cultures
Septic, febrile after travel (enteric fever) or immunocompromised — usually in hospital
Not routinely
Stool testing for short, non-bloody diarrhoea in a well adult with no risk factor; imaging (unless an acute abdomen); faecal calprotectin during an acute infection (falsely raised)
Persistent or bloody after infection excluded
Consider first presentation of IBD — calprotectin once the infection has settled, and same-day advice if acute severe colitis (6 or more bloody stools a day with tachycardia, fever, anaemia or raised CRP). Persistent change in bowel habit or rectal bleeding meeting NICE NG12 → FIT and the colorectal suspected cancer pathway (see chronic diarrhoea)

Most acute diarrhoea does not need a stool sample, but the indications above identify the patients in whom the result changes management (antibiotic choice, C. difficile treatment, IBD work-up) or public-health action (outbreak control, exclusion and clearance of food handlers and care workers). Positive laboratory results for notifiable organisms are reported by the laboratory, but clinicians must still notify suspected cases (UKHSA).

Calprotectin is raised by infective colitis, so testing during the acute episode generates false positives; test once the infection has settled if symptoms persist. Severe bloody diarrhoea meeting Truelove and Witts criteria is a medical emergency whatever the eventual cause.

5
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis — Infective, Drug, Surgical and Inflammatory

Viral gastroenteritis
Norovirus, rotavirus, adenovirus
Vomiting prominent, outbreaks, watery stools, settles in 1–3 days. Commonest cause
Bacterial
Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, STEC
Fever, abdominal pain, blood or mucus; poultry, eggs, barbecues, unpasteurised milk, contacts
Toxin-mediated food poisoning
S. aureus, B. cereus, C. perfringens
Onset within 1–16 h of a meal, others affected, short illness
C. difficile
Antibiotic-associated
Recent antibiotics, hospital or care home, PPI, older age; watery diarrhoea ± fever, pain, raised WCC
Travel-related
ETEC, Giardia, amoebiasis
Persistent watery diarrhoea, bloating (Giardia); bloody with liver abscess risk (amoebiasis); exclude malaria and enteric fever
Drug-induced
Metformin, laxatives, PPI, magnesium
Timing with a new drug or dose change; also SSRIs, colchicine, NSAIDs, mycophenolate, chemotherapy
Inflammatory
IBD first presentation
Bloody diarrhoea beyond 7 days, nocturnal stools, mouth ulcers, perianal disease, weight loss, family history
Older adults
Overflow · ischaemic colitis
Faecal loading with leakage; sudden left-sided pain then bloody diarrhoea in a vascular patient; also diverticulitis, microscopic colitis
Surgical & systemic mimics
Appendicitis, pelvic abscess, sepsis
Also thyrotoxicosis, adrenal crisis, DKA, toxic shock, early hepatitis — look for the feature that does not fit

Classifying by mechanism keeps the dangerous categories in view: the infective causes usually need fluids and time, the drug causes need a medication change, and the surgical, ischaemic and inflammatory causes need a different pathway altogether. In older adults the combination of sudden abdominal pain followed by bloody diarrhoea should prompt thought of ischaemic colitis, and leakage around impacted stool is the commonest cause of "diarrhoea" in the frail and immobile.

6
Refer

Referral, Notification and Public Health

999
Shock, high-risk sepsis, peritonitis, reduced consciousness, DKA with drowsiness, adrenal crisis
Same-day
Severe dehydration or unable to retain fluids · AKI or significant electrolyte disturbance · suspected HUS · severe or worsening C. difficile · acute severe colitis · acute abdomen · fever after travel to a malaria area · neutropenic sepsis · unsafe home circumstances, especially in older or frail adults
Health Protection Team
Notify on clinical suspicion — do not wait for results: food poisoning, infectious bloody diarrhoea, HUS, cholera, typhoid and paratyphoid, and any outbreak or cluster (for example two or more linked cases, or cases in a care home). Phone the local HPT for urgent cases; the proper officer form for the rest (UKHSA)
Microbiology / ID advice
Confirmed organism in an unwell, immunocompromised or older patient; suspected STEC; enteric fever; persistent traveller's diarrhoea with negative results
Gastroenterology
Suspected first presentation of IBD — Urgent (same-day if acute severe colitis); persisting diarrhoea with negative stool tests → follow the chronic diarrhoea algorithm
Suspected cancer · NICE NG12
Not for a short infective illness — but a persistent change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss or an abdominal or rectal mass → FIT and the colorectal suspected cancer pathway per NG12

Registered medical practitioners have a statutory duty to notify suspected food poisoning and other notifiable diseases to the proper officer, on clinical suspicion and without waiting for laboratory confirmation (Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010). Early notification lets the HPT identify outbreaks, trace contaminated food and arrange clearance testing for people in risk groups.

7
Treat

Management at This Consultation

Everyone
Fluids first First-line
Small frequent drinks; ordinary fluids if not dehydrated. Oral rehydration salts if dehydration risk is higher (older, frail, comorbid, pregnant, high stool output): usual adult use is 200–400 mL after each loose stool, per the product SPC. Eat normally as tolerated
Symptom relief (selected adults)
Loperamide Only if no blood or fever
Usual adult dose: 4 mg first, then 2 mg after each loose stool, maximum 16 mg in 24 h, for up to 5 days (SPC). Not with blood in the stool, fever, suspected C. difficile or STEC, or IBD flare
Suspected C. difficile
Stop the trigger, send stool
Review and stop the causative antibiotic if possible; review PPI. Avoid loperamide and opioids. Confirmed: oral vancomycin first-line per NICE NG199 — usual adult dose 125 mg four times a day for 10 days (SPC); severe → same-day
Antibiotics
Not routine
Do not prescribe empirically for acute infective diarrhoea. Septic, severely immunocompromised, suspected enteric fever or cholera, or a confirmed organism that needs treatment → discuss with microbiology and follow local antimicrobial guidance
Step 1Assess and document hydration — decide home, supervised oral rehydration, or same-day assessment. Give the patient a clear drinking plan
Step 2Sick-day rules — while unable to keep fluids down or with significant diarrhoea, temporarily pause metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, diuretics, NSAIDs and (on the local plan) sulfonylureas; restart 24–48 h after eating and drinking normally. Insulin: never stop — check glucose and ketones. Steroid-dependent: double the oral glucocorticoid dose per steroid sick-day rules. Lithium, DOACs and other renally cleared drugs: consider checking U&E
Step 3Medication cause — reduce or stop the culprit (metformin dose reduction or modified-release; stop or reduce laxatives; review PPI, magnesium, colchicine)
Step 4Exclusion and notification — stay off work, school or care settings until 48 hours after the last episode of diarrhoea or vomiting; longer or with clearance samples for risk-group occupations and some organisms (STEC, typhoid, Shigella) on HPT advice. Notify suspected food poisoning
Step 5Contraception — severe diarrhoea or vomiting can reduce oral contraceptive absorption: follow CoSRH missed-pill advice and use condoms

Next steps: see Gastroenteritis · Sick day rules · Irritable bowel syndrome · Ulcerative colitis · Crohn's disease

Rehydration is the treatment; feeding should continue. Loperamide shortens watery diarrhoea in adults but should be avoided when there is blood, fever or a possible C. difficile or STEC infection, where slowing the bowel may worsen toxin-mediated disease. Antibiotics shorten some bacterial infections by about a day but drive resistance and C. difficile and may increase HUS risk in STEC, so they are kept for specific indications on microbiology advice.

Dehydration plus metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, diuretics and NSAIDs is a common route to AKI, lactic acidosis or DKA; a temporary pause with a clear restart instruction prevents most of these admissions. The 48-hour exclusion rule reflects continued shedding after symptoms stop, especially with norovirus.

8
Lifestyle

Self-Care, Hygiene and Preventing Spread

Hand hygiene Wash hands with soap and water after the toilet and before preparing food — alcohol gel does not kill norovirus or C. difficile spores.
Household Do not share towels; clean toilet seats, flush handles and taps daily with bleach-based products; wash soiled laundry on a hot cycle.
Food handling Do not prepare food for others until 48 hours symptom-free (longer if HPT advises for a food handler).
Swimming No swimming for 48 hours after symptoms stop, and 14 days after Cryptosporidium.
Diet Eat normally as tolerated; small, plain meals help. Avoid alcohol until recovered. Temporary lactose intolerance after infection can prolong loose stools.
Travel Before future travel: food and water hygiene, ORS sachets in the travel kit; standby antibiotics are not routinely advised.

Norovirus is highly infectious, survives on surfaces and is not killed by alcohol gel; soap and water and bleach-based cleaning are the effective measures. Cryptosporidium oocysts resist chlorine, hence the longer swimming restriction (UKHSA).

9
Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Safety-net 999
Collapse, confusion or drowsiness, cold mottled skin, severe abdominal pain, vomiting blood or passing large amounts of blood
Safety-net same-day
Unable to keep fluids down for 24 h (sooner if older, frail, pregnant or diabetic), passing very little urine, dizziness on standing, blood in the stool, high fever, worsening abdominal pain, blood glucose or ketones rising, or after bloody diarrhoea: passing less urine, pallor, bruising or swelling
Review
If not settling in 7 days, or sooner if risk factors — send stool if not already done. Persisting beyond 14 days → persistent diarrhoea work-up; 4 weeks or more → chronic diarrhoea algorithm
Results
Act on stool results (C. difficile, notifiable organisms, parasites); check U&E if drugs were paused or AKI risk; confirm restart of paused medicines
Remote consultations
Convert to face-to-face or same-day assessment if hydration cannot be judged, the patient is older, frail or has red flags, or a second contact for the same illness

Most acute infective diarrhoea settles within 7 days. Persistence beyond this prompts stool testing and reconsideration of C. difficile, parasites, IBD and drug causes. A clear, specific safety-net — especially about urine output and blood in the stool — is the main protection against delayed recognition of dehydration, HUS and colitis.
