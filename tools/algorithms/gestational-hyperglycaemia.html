1
Safety

Red Flags — Overt Diabetes, DKA, Pre-eclampsia & Fetal Compromise

Overt (pre-existing) diabetes An HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (at booking where measured, or at diagnosis of GDM — NICE NG3 advises measuring HbA1c at diagnosis to identify pre-existing type 2 diabetes), or a fasting/random glucose in the diabetic range, means diabetes that predates the pregnancy — not GDM. Manage as diabetes in pregnancy: tighter surveillance, folic acid 5 mg until 12 weeks, retinal and renal assessment, and aspirin from 12 weeks for pre-eclampsia.
Diabetic ketoacidosis Can occur in type 1 (or rarely type 2) at lower glucose levels in pregnancy ("euglycaemic DKA"). Vomiting, abdominal pain, thirst, breathlessness, ketones → same-day emergency obstetric/medical admission. Check blood ketones in any unwell pregnant woman with diabetes, even if the glucose looks acceptable.
Pre-eclampsia Diabetes raises the risk. Watch BP, proteinuria, headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain, brisk reflexes — urgent assessment. Aspirin 75–150 mg daily from 12 weeks until birth for women at high risk — type 1 or type 2 diabetes is a high-risk factor (NICE NG133, 2019); gestational diabetes alone is not.
Reduced fetal movements / polyhydramnios / macrosomia Markers of fetal compromise and of poor glycaemic control; diabetes carries a higher stillbirth risk. Reduced movements → same-day maternity assessment. Polyhydramnios or accelerating growth → review control and scan.
Severe maternal hypoglycaemia In women on insulin or a sulfonylurea — risk rises in the first trimester and with tight targets. Counsel on recognition, treatment, and DVLA/driving rules where relevant.
Shoulder dystocia risk at delivery Macrosomia from poor control predisposes to shoulder dystocia and neonatal injury — informs the timing and mode-of-birth discussion with obstetrics.
Possible new type 1 diabetes Weight loss, marked osmotic symptoms, ketonuria or very high glucose at any gestation — same-day specialist discussion; do not book a routine OGTT.
No NICE NG12 criterion Hyperglycaemia in pregnancy maps to no suspected-cancer criterion; none applies here.
The first safety job is to separate true gestational diabetes (glucose intolerance that first appears in pregnancy) from pre-existing diabetes that has only now been detected, because the latter needs more intensive care, earlier retinal and renal assessment and pre-eclampsia prophylaxis — NICE NG133 lists type 1 and type 2 diabetes among the high-risk factors for which aspirin is advised, but not gestational diabetes on its own. The acute emergencies — DKA, which can be euglycaemic in pregnancy, and pre-eclampsia — threaten both mother and baby. And throughout, the unspoken red flag is the raised stillbirth risk of poorly controlled diabetes, which is precisely why glucose targets in pregnancy are so tight and fetal surveillance so structured.

Diagnose

Who to Screen — Risk Factors & Timing

Risk factors (any one → OGTT)
BMI above 30 kg/m²; previous macrosomic baby weighing 4.5 kg or more; previous gestational diabetes; first-degree relative with diabetes; a minority ethnic family origin with a high prevalence of diabetes (for example South Asian, Black Caribbean, Middle Eastern) — NICE NG3.
Previous GDM
Offer early self-monitoring of blood glucose or a 75 g OGTT as soon as possible after booking, and a further OGTT at 24–28 weeks if the first is normal.
All other risk factors
75 g OGTT at 24–28 weeks.
Glycosuria in pregnancy
2+ on one occasion or 1+ on two or more occasions on routine testing → consider further testing for GDM.
Not for risk assessment
NICE NG3 advises against using fasting or random glucose, HbA1c, a glucose challenge test or urinalysis to assess risk of GDM — risk is assessed by the factors above, and diagnosis is by OGTT.
Timing trap
An early OGTT that is normal in a woman with previous GDM does not end the story — NICE NG3 repeats it at 24–28 weeks, because insulin resistance rises through the second and third trimesters.
Symptoms
Most GDM is asymptomatic and found on screening. Thirst, polyuria, fatigue or recurrent thrush should prompt glucose testing at any gestation.
When the OGTT is unsuitable
After bariatric surgery (risk of dumping) or with severe vomiting, discuss an alternative such as capillary glucose profiles with the antenatal diabetes team rather than omitting testing.
NICE NG3 uses risk-factor-based screening rather than universal OGTT. Identifying any single risk factor triggers a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, timed at 24–28 weeks for most women but brought forward for those with previous gestational diabetes, who have the highest recurrence rate. Because the condition is usually silent, the screening question — "does this woman have a risk factor?" — is what makes the diagnosis, not symptoms. The GP often holds the information that triggers testing (previous GDM, birthweights, family history), so it is worth checking the record when a pregnancy is first registered.

Diagnose

Diagnostic Thresholds — the 75 g OGTT

Diagnose GDM if either
Fasting plasma glucose ≥5.6 mmol/L  OR  2-hour plasma glucose ≥7.8 mmol/L (NICE NG3 thresholds).
Overt diabetes (not GDM)
HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol, or fasting ≥7.0 / random ≥11.1 with symptoms — manage as pre-existing diabetes.
HbA1c in pregnancy
Not used to diagnose GDM (red-cell turnover changes make it unreliable). Measure it at diagnosis of GDM to pick out pre-existing type 2 diabetes (NICE NG3).
Near-miss results
Values just below the thresholds do not make the diagnosis, but repeat testing is reasonable if symptoms, glycosuria or fetal overgrowth develop later in pregnancy.
Communicate the diagnosis well
Explain that good control greatly reduces risks, that it often resolves after birth, and that it is not caused by anything she did wrong — addressing guilt and engaging her early improves outcomes.
The two diagnostic numbers to commit to memory are a fasting glucose of 5.6 mmol/L or above and a two-hour glucose of 7.8 mmol/L or above on the 75 g OGTT — either one alone makes the diagnosis (NICE NG3). HbA1c does not diagnose gestational diabetes because pregnancy alters red-cell turnover, but an HbA1c of 48 or more identifies overt diabetes that predates the pregnancy, which is why NG3 asks for it at diagnosis. How the diagnosis is delivered matters clinically: a woman who understands the rationale and feels supported is far more likely to achieve the tight targets that protect her baby.

Diagnose

Differential — GDM vs Pre-existing vs Transient

Gestational diabetes
Glucose intolerance first recognised in pregnancy, typically 2nd/3rd trimester, resolving after delivery. The default when OGTT thresholds are met and HbA1c is below 48.
Pre-existing type 2 diabetes
Often undiagnosed before pregnancy; suggested by HbA1c ≥48, obesity, strong risk factors, or hyperglycaemia in the first trimester.
Type 1 diabetes (new)
Younger, lean, rapid osmotic symptoms, weight loss, ketosis-prone — needs urgent specialist care; do not mislabel as GDM.
Monogenic diabetes (e.g. glucokinase MODY)
Stable, mildly raised fasting glucose with a small rise on the OGTT and a parent with lifelong "borderline" glucose — worth raising with the specialist team, because it changes fetal management and needs genetic testing.
Transient stress / steroid hyperglycaemia
Antenatal corticosteroids (for fetal lung maturity) transiently raise glucose and may need short-term insulin cover — not a new diagnosis of GDM.
Renal glycosuria
Glucose in the urine with normal blood glucose — a lowered renal threshold, common in pregnancy and benign; confirm with blood glucose/OGTT before labelling.
Drug-induced
Systemic corticosteroids for other conditions and some antipsychotics raise glucose — review the medication list.
Most hyperglycaemia detected in pregnancy is gestational diabetes, but the differential matters because pre-existing type 2 diabetes needs more intensive management and a different risk conversation, and new type 1 diabetes is a specialist emergency. Monogenic diabetes is uncommon but easily missed: the persistent, modest fasting rise with a flat OGTT curve and a family pattern is the clue. Two common traps are steroid-induced hyperglycaemia after antenatal corticosteroids, which is transient, and pregnancy-related renal glycosuria, which reflects a lowered renal threshold rather than high blood glucose — both are confirmed by measuring blood glucose rather than relying on the dipstick.

Monitoring & Fetal Surveillance

Self-monitoring of blood glucose
Fasting and 1-hour post-meal capillary glucose. Targets: fasting <5.3, 1-hour post-meal <7.8 (or 2-hour <6.4) mmol/L; keep above 4 to avoid hypoglycaemia if on insulin or glibenclamide (NICE NG3).
Growth & liquor scans
Ultrasound for fetal growth and amniotic fluid volume every 4 weeks from 28 to 36 weeks (28, 32 and 36 weeks — NICE NG3); more often if concerns about macrosomia or polyhydramnios.
Pre-eclampsia surveillance
Regular BP and urinalysis; aspirin prophylaxis where NG133 criteria are met.
Glucose at antenatal contacts
Review self-monitoring records at each visit; escalate treatment promptly if targets are not met.
If she sees the GP
Ask for her glucose diary, check BP and urine, ask about fetal movements, and pass on any concern to the antenatal diabetes team the same day rather than changing treatment in isolation.
Management is driven by capillary glucose self-monitoring against deliberately tight targets — a fasting value below 5.3 and a one-hour post-meal value below 7.8 mmol/L — because maternal hyperglycaemia drives fetal overgrowth and its complications. Serial growth and liquor-volume scans at 28, 32 and 36 weeks detect macrosomia and polyhydramnios, which both signal suboptimal control and inform the timing and mode of birth. The specialist team owns the treatment plan; the GP's contribution during pregnancy is to spot the obstetric warning signs and route them quickly.

Refer

Referral & Shared Care

Joint diabetes-antenatal clinic
Refer all women with GDM to be seen within 1 week of diagnosis (NICE NG3) for combined obstetric and diabetes-team care — dietitian, diabetes specialist nurse/midwife.
Manage as pre-existing diabetes
HbA1c ≥48 or first-trimester hyperglycaemia → pre-existing diabetes pathway with tighter surveillance, 5 mg folic acid, retinal/renal assessment, and aspirin from 12 weeks.
Medication review (pre-existing diabetes)
Stop ACE inhibitors, ARBs and statins and review other diabetes drugs as soon as pregnancy is confirmed — agree replacements with the specialist team.
Same-day
Suspected DKA, severe pre-eclampsia, or reduced fetal movements → urgent obstetric assessment.
Neonatal planning
Deliver in a unit with neonatal facilities; alert the neonatal team — risk of neonatal hypoglycaemia, jaundice and respiratory distress.
Anaesthetic / obstetric
Macrosomia or other complications → consultant-led birth planning (timing, mode, intrapartum glucose control).
What to send
Gestation, both OGTT values, HbA1c, BMI, previous birthweights and any previous GDM, current medicines, and any symptoms — enough for the clinic to decide at triage whether the fasting value puts her straight onto insulin.

Treatment detail (targets, the diet → metformin → insulin ladder and doses) sits on the gestational diabetes protocol.

Gestational diabetes is managed in a joint diabetes-antenatal clinic, and prompt referral — within a week of diagnosis, as NICE NG3 recommends — gets dietary advice, monitoring and, where needed, medication started during the window when they most influence fetal growth. The general practitioner's role is to recognise the diagnosis, distinguish pre-existing diabetes, remove drugs that are unsafe in pregnancy for a woman with pre-existing diabetes, and act urgently on the obstetric and metabolic emergencies while specialist care is arranged.

Fasting <7 mmol/L, no complications
Diet & exercise trial
If glucose targets are not met within 1–2 weeks → offer metformin.
Targets not met after trial
Add metformin
First-line oral agent. Insulin instead if metformin is contraindicated or unacceptable to her.
Fasting ≥7, or 6.0–6.9 with macrosomia/hydramnios, or targets still unmet
Insulin (± metformin)
Offer insulin immediately if fasting ≥7 at diagnosis; consider it immediately at 6.0–6.9 with complications. Add to metformin when diet + metformin are insufficient.
Step 1Diet & exercise — low-glycaemic-index carbohydrates, portion control, and regular activity such as walking after meals. Refer to a dietitian. Review glucose in 1–2 weeks.
Step 2Metformin — first-line oral medication in pregnancy. Dose per protocol and BNF.
Step 3Insulin — added when targets remain unmet, or started first-line when fasting glucose is ≥7 mmol/L at diagnosis. Counsel on hypoglycaemia.
AlternativeGlibenclamide — considered when targets are not met on metformin but she declines insulin, or when she cannot tolerate metformin (NICE NG3).
Treatment escalates in steps but the starting rung depends on the fasting glucose (NICE NG3). A woman whose fasting glucose is below 7 with no fetal complications earns a one-to-two-week trial of diet and exercise; if that fails to reach target, metformin is the first-line oral drug and insulin is added next. A fasting glucose of 7 or more at diagnosis is too high for a lifestyle trial and warrants insulin straight away, and NG3 advises considering immediate insulin at 6.0–6.9 mmol/L when there is macrosomia or hydramnios. Glibenclamide is a narrow alternative for women who decline insulin after metformin fails, or who cannot tolerate metformin.

Lifestyle

Diet, Activity & Self-Management

Carbohydrate quality Choose low-glycaemic-index carbohydrates, control portions, and spread intake across meals and snacks to blunt post-meal peaks. Dietitian input is part of standard care.
Physical activity Regular moderate activity — a walk after meals is especially effective at lowering post-prandial glucose. Encourage unless an obstetric contraindication exists.
Weight & gestational gain Avoid excessive gestational weight gain with healthy-eating advice. Weight loss is not pursued during pregnancy.
Self-monitoring skills Teach reliable capillary testing, recognising and treating hypoglycaemia (if on insulin or glibenclamide), and keeping a glucose diary to guide reviews.
Breastfeeding Encourage — it benefits maternal glucose metabolism and reduces the child's later obesity/diabetes risk. Metformin and insulin are compatible with breastfeeding.
Future-pregnancy planning Explain the high recurrence rate, the value of pre-conception weight optimisation, and early testing in any future pregnancy.
Smoking Offer referral to stop-smoking support — it compounds fetal growth risk and long-term cardiovascular risk.
Lifestyle is genuinely therapeutic in gestational diabetes — many women reach target on diet and activity alone, which is why NICE NG3 starts there when the fasting glucose is below 7. The highest-yield measures are low-glycaemic-index carbohydrate choices, portion control, and a short walk after meals to flatten post-prandial peaks. Self-monitoring competence and hypoglycaemia awareness keep treatment safe, and breastfeeding plus pre-conception planning extend the benefit well beyond the index pregnancy for both mother and child.

Safety

Birth, Postnatal Care & Long-Term Follow-Up

Timing of birth
Advise birth no later than 40+6 weeks in GDM, offering elective birth if not delivered by then (NICE NG3); earlier if macrosomia, poor control, or other complications — agreed with obstetrics.
Immediately after delivery
Stop all glucose-lowering medication right after birth, and check blood glucose before transfer to community care to exclude persisting hyperglycaemia. Feed the baby early and monitor neonatal blood glucose — risk of neonatal hypoglycaemia, jaundice and respiratory distress.
Postnatal maternal testing
Fasting plasma glucose at 6–13 weeks postpartum. After 13 weeks, if not yet done: fasting plasma glucose, or HbA1c if a fasting test is not possible (NICE NG3). Then an annual HbA1c.
Reading the result
Fasting glucose below 6.0 mmol/L → low probability of diabetes now, but continuing risk. HbA1c 39–47 mmol/mol → high risk of type 2 diabetes: offer prevention advice and interventions (NICE NG3). A result in the diabetic range → confirm and manage as type 2 diabetes.
Coding and recall
Code "history of gestational diabetes" so the annual HbA1c recall and early testing in the next pregnancy are triggered automatically.
Lifestyle & prevention
Weight management, diet and activity advice reduce progression to type 2 diabetes. Address pre-conception care for future pregnancies.
Safety-net
Symptoms of hyperglycaemia (thirst, polyuria, weight loss) postnatally → test for diabetes. Reduced fetal movements antenatally → same-day assessment.
Recurrence
High recurrence in future pregnancies — arrange early self-monitoring or OGTT soon after the next booking.
Two safety priorities bracket the birth. Around delivery, glucose-lowering drugs are stopped immediately and the neonate is monitored for hypoglycaemia, the commonest neonatal complication. Then comes the most frequently neglected step in the whole pathway: the postnatal maternal glucose check at 6–13 weeks, followed by annual HbA1c (NICE NG3; also NICE QS109). Up to half of women with gestational diabetes develop type 2 diabetes within about five years, and a meta-analysis found roughly a ten-fold higher risk than in women without it (Vounzoulaki et al., BMJ 2020) — a powerful, actionable prevention opportunity. Recurrence in future pregnancies is high, so early retesting is planned in advance.
