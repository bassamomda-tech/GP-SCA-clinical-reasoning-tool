1
Safety

Red Flags — GI Malignancy, Significant Bleeding & Life-Threatening Anaemia

Low ferritin + rectal bleeding, melaena, weight loss or change in bowel habit Possible colorectal or upper GI cancer. Use the current NICE NG12/HTG690 criteria and local colorectal pathway (FIT criteria vary with age and symptoms; rectal, anal or abdominal masses bypass FIT). FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral; a negative FIT does not exclude significant GI pathology in confirmed IDA.
Low ferritin + severe symptomatic anaemia (dyspnoea at rest, chest pain, syncope, heart failure symptoms) or rapidly falling Hb Severe anaemia requiring urgent same-day clinical assessment; the transfusion decision is usually made in hospital. Transfusion decisions are based on symptoms, physiological compromise, active bleeding, comorbidity and the clinical setting — not Hb alone. In stable adults without active bleeding or acute coronary syndrome, consider a restrictive threshold around Hb 70 g/L; around 80 g/L in acute coronary syndrome. Give one unit and reassess clinically and with Hb (NICE NG24).
Low ferritin + post-menopausal woman or any man (no other explanation) Unexplained iron deficiency in post-menopausal women and men (any age) = GI blood loss until proved otherwise. Do NOT assume dietary. In men and post-menopausal women with newly diagnosed unexplained IDA, bidirectional GI investigation (gastroscopy + colonoscopy, or CT colonography if colonoscopy is unsuitable) is generally recommended via the urgent pathway, unless there is a clear reason why the risks outweigh the benefits. Iron deficiency without anaemia carries a lower risk of significant GI pathology — keep a lower threshold to investigate in men, post-menopausal women, GI symptoms or relevant family history.
Low ferritin + haematuria + dysuria + haemoptysis Multisource blood loss or malignancy. Investigate each source. Do not assume a single benign cause covers all features.
Significant or rapidly worsening IDA in pregnancy Discuss promptly with the maternity/obstetric team (gestation, symptoms, rate of fall, oral iron response, proximity to delivery, local protocol). Oral iron is usually first-line. Consider IV iron, generally from the second trimester onwards, when oral iron is not tolerated, ineffective, contraindicated, or when rapid correction is required. Neonatal iron deficiency risk if maternal severe IDA.
Low ferritin with normal or high ferritin in a patient with known chronic inflammatory disease Iron deficiency may coexist with anaemia of chronic disease (ACD) — ferritin is an acute phase reactant and may be falsely normal or high even with depleted iron stores. Interpret a normal or raised ferritin with CRP, transferrin saturation, red-cell indices, reticulocyte haemoglobin where available and the clinical context.
Iron deficiency anaemia in a post-menopausal woman or a man of any age is a red flag that generally warrants gastrointestinal investigation, not dietary counselling — the BSG guideline on iron deficiency anaemia (Snook et al, Gut 2021) recommends bidirectional investigation in men and post-menopausal women with unexplained IDA because of the risk of underlying GI malignancy, and states that FIT should not be used to exclude significant pathology in confirmed IDA. The investigation should be bidirectional (both upper and lower GI) because occult upper GI bleeding (gastric cancer, oesophageal cancer, gastric ulcer, oesophagitis with Cameron lesion in hiatus hernia) and lower GI bleeding (colorectal cancer, angiodysplasia, polyps) can both cause IDA. The practical guidance: OGD + colonoscopy or CT colonography should be arranged simultaneously (not sequentially) for these patients, as approximately 10% have both upper and lower GI pathology. If only one investigation is performed and normal, the other must still be arranged. Pre-menopausal women with IDA (the most common group) — investigation is guided by symptoms: clear heavy menstrual bleeding and no red flags = treat and monitor; GI symptoms, family history of CRC, age approaching menopause, or anaemia that is disproportionate, recurrent, unexplained or refractory = investigate.

Diagnose

Causes of Iron Deficiency — Classification

Blood loss (most common cause)
GI blood loss: most important cause in men and post-menopausal women. Sources: oesophagitis (GORD), peptic ulcer, gastric/oesophageal/colorectal cancer, angiodysplasia, coeliac, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticular disease, haemorrhoids (rarely causes significant IDA — diagnosis of exclusion). Menstrual loss: most common cause in pre-menopausal women — menorrhagia (IUD, fibroids, endometriosis, anovulatory cycles). Urinary: haematuria. Pulmonary: haemoptysis (diffuse alveolar haemorrhage — rare).
Reduced absorption
Coeliac disease: most important malabsorptive cause in UK (damages duodenal villi — site of iron absorption). Anti-tTG IgA + total IgA at IDA diagnosis, while the patient is consuming gluten. H. pylori infection: causes gastritis and reduces gastric acid (acid essential for non-haem iron solubilisation — Fe3+ to Fe2+). IBD (Crohn — duodenal involvement). Post-gastric surgery / bariatric. Achlorhydria (elderly, PPI use).
Increased demand
Pregnancy: maternal iron requirement triples in third trimester. Breastfeeding. Rapid growth (adolescents, premature infants). Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (EPO — used in CKD). Elite endurance athletes (intravascular haemolysis from foot strike, GI blood loss from exercise).
Inadequate dietary intake
Vegans and vegetarians (non-haem iron is less well absorbed than haem iron). Poverty/food insecurity. Exclusive milk diet in infants. Most common in developing countries — in UK, dietary deficiency alone rarely causes severe IDA without co-existing losses.
The coeliac disease-IDA connection is one of the most important and most frequently missed associations in primary care — coeliac disease damages the villous epithelium of the duodenum and proximal jejunum, which is the primary site of iron absorption (dietary iron is absorbed as Fe2+ in the duodenum via DMT1 transporter). In coeliac disease, villous atrophy dramatically reduces the absorptive surface area for iron, causing iron deficiency even when dietary iron intake is adequate. Approximately 5% of patients with IDA in primary care have coeliac disease as the cause. The critical clinical implication: anti-tTG IgA + total IgA should be checked in every patient with unexplained IDA, even without GI symptoms (silent coeliac is common). Starting a patient on iron supplementation without excluding coeliac disease means: (1) the iron may be poorly absorbed (wasted treatment); (2) the underlying cause remains undiagnosed; (3) if untreated coeliac disease is present, the patient continues to have GI inflammation and increased risk of complications (osteoporosis, lymphoma, infertility). The diagnosis is made on raised anti-tTG IgA + duodenal biopsy (Marsh 3 lesion) — GFD leads to villous recovery and iron absorption normalises within 3-6 months of strict adherence.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigation Strategy

History
Bleeding history: menstrual (heavy periods, flooding, clots, duration), rectal bleeding (fresh/altered), haematuria, haemoptysis, epistaxis. GI symptoms: heartburn, dyspepsia, dysphagia, change in bowel habit. Dietary history: vegetarian/vegan, dairy-heavy infant diet. Pregnancy/breastfeeding. Drug history: NSAIDs + aspirin (GI erosions/ulcers), PPIs (may reduce iron absorption), anticoagulants. Weight loss, night sweats (malignancy). Family history: CRC, coeliac. Previous IDA and response to iron.
Examination
Pallor (conjunctival, palmar — Hb <80 often visible). Angular cheilitis + koilonychia (spoon nails) + glossitis (classic iron deficiency signs — now less common with earlier diagnosis). Atrophic gastritis (epigastric tenderness). Splenomegaly (haemolytic anaemia or malignancy). Rectal examination (melaena, mass). Premenopausal: pelvic examination if menorrhagia (uterine fibroids).
Investigations — first line
FBC (Hb, MCV, MCH, RDW and platelet count; iron deficiency can occur with a normal MCV, particularly early or with mixed deficiency) · Serum ferritin (single most useful marker — a low ferritin strongly supports iron deficiency; cut-offs vary with laboratory method and context; a normal or raised ferritin does not reliably exclude deficiency in inflammation, liver disease, malignancy or CKD — BSG 2021) · Transferrin saturation (if ferritin may be falsely normal; serum iron alone is subject to diurnal and biological variation) · Urinalysis · Pregnancy status (where relevant) · Anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (coeliac) · FIT (per current NICE/local colorectal pathway — guides referral, does not exclude pathology) · CRP (if inflammation possible — elevated = ferritin less reliable)
Investigations — targeted
OGD (upper GI investigation) + colonoscopy or CT colonography (lower GI) — generally recommended in men and post-menopausal women with unexplained IDA · H. pylori (urea breath test or stool antigen — after negative or non-explanatory GI evaluation, or with dyspepsia/ulcer disease, per gastroenterology pathway) · Coeliac serology (if not done) · Renal function (CKD-related anaemia) · Blood film, reticulocytes, B12/folate (targeted — e.g. suspected mixed deficiency) · Haemoglobin electrophoresis (if thalassaemia trait suspected)
Ferritin interpretation in inflammatory disease is one of the most practically important clinical skills in interpreting iron studies — ferritin is an acute phase reactant that rises in response to inflammation, infection, malignancy, and liver disease. In a patient with rheumatoid arthritis, IBD, CKD, or any inflammatory condition, the ferritin may be 'normal' while the patient is actually iron-depleted (true iron stores are low, but the inflammatory stimulus has raised ferritin to a falsely reassuring level). In this context, the additional iron studies are essential for diagnosis: a low transferrin saturation supports iron deficiency even if ferritin appears normal (serum iron alone is subject to substantial diurnal and biological variation). The soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) is the most sensitive marker for iron-deficient erythropoiesis in inflammatory conditions — it is elevated in iron deficiency but not affected by inflammation. The sTfR/log ferritin ratio (Thomas plot) is the gold standard for distinguishing IDA from anaemia of chronic disease, but is not widely available in primary care. In practice: interpret a normal or raised ferritin with CRP, transferrin saturation, red-cell indices, reticulocyte haemoglobin where available and the clinical context — in inflammatory disease, iron deficiency may coexist with anaemia of inflammation despite a non-low ferritin. There is no universal ferritin diagnostic threshold.

Diagnose

FBC Indices, Blood Film & Differential Diagnosis

Typical IDA film findings
Hypochromic microcytic red cells (pale, small). Central pallor >1/3 of cell diameter. Poikilocytes: pencil cells (elongated hypochromic cells — characteristic of IDA), target cells. Thrombocytosis (reactive — platelet count often elevated 400-600 in IDA). A blood film and reticulocyte count are not routinely required to diagnose IDA, and the film does not reliably distinguish IDA from thalassaemia trait. Microcytosis is not required for IDA — MCV may be normal early or in mixed deficiency.
Distinguishing IDA from thalassaemia trait
Both: microcytic, hypochromic. IDA: MCV falls more than RBC count (disproportionate MCV reduction). Thalassaemia trait: RBC count often elevated or normal with very low MCV (Mentzer index = MCV/RBC count: <13 suggests thalassaemia, >13 suggests IDA — a screening aid, not a diagnostic test). Thalassaemia trait: ferritin normal/high. Haemoglobin electrophoresis when thalassaemia trait is suspected (beta-thal trait: raised HbA2 >3.5%; alpha-thal: normal electrophoresis — DNA testing needed). Interpret HbA2 cautiously if iron deficiency is present — iron deficiency can reduce HbA2.
Mixed deficiency (IDA + B12/folate)
Dimorphic picture: two populations on film (macrocytes from B12/folate deficiency + hypochromic microcytes from iron deficiency). MCV may be paradoxically normal (the two opposing effects cancel out). Treat both deficiencies. Coeliac disease is a common cause of combined iron + folate deficiency (both absorbed in proximal small intestine).
Anaemia of chronic disease vs IDA
ACD: ferritin normal or high, serum iron low, TIBC low or normal, transferrin saturation low but >15%. Marrow iron stores present. IDA: ferritin low, serum iron low, TIBC high, transferrin saturation <20%. Marrow iron stores absent. Both can coexist — CKD, IBD, RA with superimposed IDA.
The Mentzer index (MCV divided by RBC count) is a quick screening aid — not a diagnostic test — that may help distinguish iron deficiency anaemia from thalassaemia trait — both conditions cause microcytic hypochromic anaemia and can be confused without further testing. The calculation: MCV (fl) / RBC count (x10^12/L). Result below 13 = thalassaemia trait likely (the RBC count is maintained or elevated because the bone marrow compensates for small cells by making more of them). Result above 13 = iron deficiency anaemia likely (the RBC count falls in proportion to the MCV). This index has a sensitivity and specificity of approximately 75-85% for distinguishing the two conditions — it should trigger haemoglobin electrophoresis (for beta-thalassaemia trait: HbA2 >3.5%) or referral for haematology in cases where the distinction matters (particularly for reproductive counselling in patients of South/Southeast Asian, Middle Eastern, or Mediterranean origin where thalassaemia trait prevalence is high).

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day / urgent
Severe symptomatic anaemia (chest pain, syncope, severe dyspnoea or heart failure symptoms) or rapidly falling Hb → same-day hospital assessment (transfusion decided on symptoms and setting, not Hb alone) · Pregnancy with severe or rapidly worsening anaemia → maternity team same day · Active significant GI bleeding with haemodynamic instability → 999
Suspected cancer (colorectal)
Newly diagnosed unexplained IDA in a man or post-menopausal woman → gastroscopy + colonoscopy (CT colonography if colonoscopy unsuitable), generally via the urgent pathway · FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → colorectal suspected cancer referral; a negative or unavailable FIT does not exclude pathology or delay referral where concern is significant · Rectal bleeding and other symptoms → current NICE NG12/HTG690 criteria and local colorectal pathway
Gastroenterology (urgent)
IDA in any adult without clear explanation after basic investigations · Suspected coeliac disease + IDA (biopsy for confirmation) · H. pylori testing after negative or non-explanatory GI evaluation, or with dyspepsia/ulcer disease (per gastroenterology pathway) · Recurrent or refractory IDA despite confirmed adherence and adequate-quality bidirectional endoscopy → small-bowel assessment (usually capsule endoscopy) and consideration of renal-tract evaluation
Haematology
IDA not responding to oral iron after 4 months (suspected non-compliance or malabsorption) · Mixed deficiency + suspicion of malignancy · Suspected thalassaemia trait (haemoglobin electrophoresis)
Gynaecology
Menorrhagia causing recurrent IDA + failure of management per NICE NG88, or suspected structural cause
GP management
Pre-menopausal woman with clear heavy menstrual bleeding and no red flags: oral iron + manage bleeding (GI investigation if IDA is disproportionate, recurrent, unexplained, refractory or with GI symptoms). Confirmed coeliac + IDA: GFD + iron replacement via the coeliac pathway. H. pylori positive: eradication per current NICE/local antimicrobial formulary + iron.
The BSG guideline on iron deficiency anaemia (Snook et al, Gut 2021, replacing Goddard et al 2011) recommends bidirectional endoscopy (upper GI endoscopy plus colonoscopy or CT colonography) for men and post-menopausal women with IDA, and GI investigation in pre-menopausal women when there are GI symptoms, a strong family history of colorectal cancer, or IDA that persists despite treatment and correction of menstrual loss. The simultaneous bidirectional investigation principle: approximately 10% of patients with IDA who have a normal upper GI endoscopy have a lower GI lesion, and vice versa — performing only one investigation misses these patients. GPs who refer for OGD only (assuming upper GI cause) and do not arrange colonoscopy are following an incomplete investigation pathway.

Treat

Iron Replacement — Oral and IV

Oral iron — first line
Ferrous sulphate 200 mg once daily (65 mg elemental iron per tablet) — BSG 2021; alternate days if not tolerated
Start one standard oral iron tablet daily (choice per local formulary). Take on an empty stomach if tolerated; taking it with food is acceptable if this improves adherence. Vitamin C supplementation is not routinely required. Review whether PPIs are necessary. Side effects: constipation, dark stools, nausea — if adverse effects occur, use alternate-day dosing, a different preparation or IV iron where appropriate. Check Hb after approximately 2–4 weeks depending on severity: a rise of at least 10 g/L within 2 weeks strongly supports iron deficiency and treatment response. Continue for around 3 months after Hb normalises, or according to the cause and local protocol.
Alternative oral iron
Ferrous gluconate 300 mg BD (35 mg elemental iron)
Better tolerated than ferrous sulphate. Ferric maltol (Feraccru): licensed for IDA in IBD — subject to Greater Manchester formulary restrictions. Ferrous fumarate 210 mg BD (68 mg elemental iron). Liquid preparations for children (sodium feredetate/Sytron).
IV iron — second line
IV iron per local protocol/formulary (e.g. ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose)
Calculate the dose using the product-specific dosing method (Hb, body weight, iron deficit, maximum dose per administration, renal function) and local protocol — do not assume a single 1,000 mg dose is adequate for all patients. Indications: oral iron intolerant/failed, malabsorption (coeliac, IBD, post-bariatric), ongoing blood loss, need for rapid correction, IDA in pregnancy (generally from the second trimester), pre-operative anaemia, CKD, IBD active disease. Ferric carboxymaltose: hypophosphataemia can be severe and may cause osteomalacia and fractures (MHRA) — assess risk before treatment; check phosphate with repeated courses, high cumulative doses, risk factors for phosphate depletion, bone disease or symptoms; follow the current SPC. Transient flu-like reaction. Contraindicated: iron overload; avoid in first trimester unless specialist advice.
Blood transfusion
Individual decision (usually in hospital): symptoms, physiological compromise, active bleeding, comorbidity and setting — not Hb alone
Stable adults without active bleeding or acute coronary syndrome: restrictive threshold around Hb 70 g/L (post-transfusion target 70–90 g/L); around 80 g/L may be considered in acute coronary syndrome. Single-unit transfusion, then reassess clinically and with Hb before further units (NICE NG24). Hb recheck post-transfusion. Address underlying cause. Transfusion does not address iron deficiency — start iron supplementation after transfusion. Not first-line for stable mild-moderate IDA.
Ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject) IV iron has transformed the management of significant iron deficiency anaemia — IV iron can replete stores rapidly, but the dose must be calculated using the product-specific method and local protocol. The main complication to monitor is hypophosphataemia — ferric carboxymaltose stimulates FGF23 (fibroblast growth factor 23), which reduces renal phosphate reabsorption. Hypophosphataemia can be severe and may cause osteomalacia and fractures, particularly with repeated or high-dose treatment. MHRA: monitor serum phosphate in patients receiving multiple high-dose administrations or long-term treatment and in those with pre-existing risk factors; re-evaluate treatment in persistent hypophosphataemia. The NHS 'Getting It Right First Time' (GIRFT) programme has recommended expansion of IV iron use in primary care for IDA in IBD, CKD, and pre-operative patients — many ICBs now have pathways for GP-initiated IV iron infusion in day units without specialist referral. GPs should check local pathways.

Treat

H. pylori Eradication & Menorrhagia Management

H. pylori + IDA
H. pylori causes chronic active gastritis → reduced gastric acid secretion → reduced non-haem iron absorption. Also may cause occult gastric blood loss. Eradication per the current NICE/local antimicrobial formulary and product information (regimen depends on previous macrolide exposure, local resistance, penicillin allergy, previous eradication failure and recent antibiotic use) + iron replacement. Confirm eradication with stool antigen or urea breath test when clinically indicated, after the appropriate interval and after withholding PPIs as the test protocol requires (not serology — antibodies persist). Routine H. pylori testing in every IDA patient is not universally recommended. If eradication successful + IDA persists → GI investigation.
Menorrhagia + IDA
Treat simultaneously: iron replacement + menorrhagia management. Use the current NICE NG88 recommendations rather than a fixed medication list: options include LNG-IUS, tranexamic acid (after assessing thrombotic risk and contraindications), NSAIDs, combined hormonal contraception and cyclical oral progestogens (check regimens against current prescribing guidance). Choice depends on fibroids, uterine cavity distortion, adenomyosis, contraception requirements and patient preference — LNG-IUS is not suitable for every patient. Refer gynaecology if not responding or structural cause (fibroids) suspected.
NSAIDs + GI bleeding causing IDA
Stop NSAID where clinically possible (paracetamol may be an alternative). A COX-2 inhibitor is not automatically safe — it still carries GI, cardiovascular and renal risks. If an NSAID is essential, account for previous ulcer or bleeding, age, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, renal function, cardiovascular risk, H. pylori and the need for gastroprotection (PPI). Test for H. pylori and eradicate (reduces NSAID ulcer risk). Iron replacement while NSAID continues.
Dietary enhancement of iron absorption
Encourage a balanced diet containing iron-rich foods. Tea, coffee and calcium-containing products can reduce iron absorption, but advice should focus on practical adherence rather than rigid avoidance schedules.
The levonorgestrel-releasing IUS (Mirena) for menorrhagia is one of the most evidence-based interventions in primary care gynaecology — the NICE NG88 (Heavy Menstrual Bleeding) guideline recommends the LNG-IUS as the first-line pharmacological treatment for menorrhagia without structural pathology. It is not suitable for every patient — the choice depends on fibroids, uterine cavity distortion, adenomyosis, contraception requirements and patient preference. The mechanism: progestogen-mediated endometrial atrophy reduces endometrial proliferation and vascularity. The additional contraceptive benefit is relevant for women seeking both menorrhagia treatment and contraception. For women who decline IUS: tranexamic acid 1 g TDS for up to 4 days during the menstrual period (BNF) (combined with mefenamic acid 500 mg TDS if dysmenorrhoea coexists) is the most effective non-hormonal option. The GP should document: menorrhagia managed, iron supplementation prescribed, follow-up planned to confirm Hb response, and IUS/hormonal management initiated or referral made for refractory cases.

Lifestyle

Dietary Iron, Absorption Optimisation & Long-Term Prevention

Iron-rich foods Encourage a balanced diet containing iron-rich foods: red meat, dark poultry, fish, organ meats (liver — limit in pregnancy due to vitamin A), pulses, fortified cereals, tofu, nuts, seeds and green vegetables.
Absorption enhancement Vitamin C-containing foods eaten with meals can aid non-haem iron absorption, but vitamin C supplementation with iron tablets is not routinely required. Haem iron absorption is not significantly affected by dietary inhibitors or enhancers.
Absorption inhibitors Tea, coffee and calcium-containing products can reduce iron absorption — focus advice on practical adherence rather than rigid avoidance schedules. PPIs and antacids reduce gastric acid needed for iron solubilisation — review whether a PPI is necessary rather than stopping it automatically.
Iron supplementation timing Start one tablet daily (BSG 2021). Take on an empty stomach if tolerated; taking it with food is acceptable if this improves adherence. If not tolerated: one tablet on alternate days, an alternative preparation or IV iron (alternate-day dosing reduces the hepcidin rise that follows each dose — Stoffel et al, Lancet Haematology 2017).
Vegetarian and vegan iron intake Discuss pulses, fortified cereals, tofu, nuts, seeds and green vegetables, and consider dietetic advice if deficiency recurs. Do not recommend routine ferritin screening in healthy vegans without symptoms or risk factors; investigate deficiency for blood loss or malabsorption as in any patient.
Iron in pregnancy Routine iron supplementation in pregnancy is not recommended for all women (UK policy). Targeted supplementation for: Hb <110 g/L first trimester or <105 g/L second/third trimester, or ferritin <30 mcg/L. Dose: one tablet daily, e.g. ferrous sulphate 200 mg or ferrous fumarate 210 mg once daily (BSH 2020: 40–80 mg elemental iron daily; alternate days if not tolerated). Discuss significant or rapidly worsening IDA promptly with the maternity team; consider IV iron, generally from the second trimester onwards, when oral iron is not tolerated, ineffective, contraindicated, or rapid correction is required — Hb <85 g/L alone does not trigger automatic IV iron. Neonatal iron stores depend on maternal iron status — severe maternal IDA risks neonatal iron deficiency.
Post-treatment monitoring and recurrence prevention Hb check at ~2–4 weeks (a rise of at least 10 g/L within 2 weeks supports response). Continue iron for around 3 months after Hb normalises, or according to the cause and local protocol. Identify and treat the underlying cause (the single most important recurrence prevention measure). IDA from heavy menstrual bleeding: continue effective NICE NG88 treatment. IDA from NSAID: stop if possible. IDA from coeliac: strict GFD.
Exercise-related IDA in athletes Endurance athletes can develop iron deficiency (e.g. GI losses, increased demand). Investigate and treat as for any patient; do not recommend routine ferritin screening in healthy athletes without symptoms or risk factors.
The alternate-day iron dosing strategy (Stoffel et al, Lancet Haematology 2017) is an evidence-based approach that improves tolerability of oral iron supplementation without significantly compromising efficacy — the reason is hepcidin physiology. After each oral iron dose, the liver increases production of hepcidin (the master regulator of iron homeostasis), which blocks the ferroportin channel in duodenal enterocytes for approximately 24 hours, preventing further iron absorption. This means that twice-daily dosing results in partial inhibition of absorption for the second dose (taken when hepcidin is still elevated). By dosing every other day, hepcidin has time to fall back to baseline before the next dose, allowing maximum absorption. The practical advice: if a patient finds daily ferrous sulphate intolerable, alternating-day dosing is an evidence-based alternative that maintains adequate Hb response with significantly fewer GI side effects.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Hb and ferritin monitoring
Assess clinical response and repeat Hb after approximately 2–4 weeks; expect a meaningful rise, commonly at least 10 g/L within 2 weeks. Continue iron for around 3 months after Hb normalisation. Ferritin is not routinely needed at 3–4 months in every patient — consider it if the response is incomplete, recurrence occurs, there is ongoing inflammatory disease, malabsorption is suspected or the cause is unclear. No universal mandatory ferritin target.
Non-responders at 4 weeks
No Hb rise at 4 weeks: check compliance, absorption (coeliac re-check), ongoing blood loss, mixed deficiency (also B12/folate deficient). Consider IV iron if confirmed malabsorption. Reassess the diagnosis (thalassaemia trait?, ACD?) and ongoing blood loss.
After GI investigation
If IDA is recurrent or fails to respond despite confirmed adherence and adequate-quality bidirectional endoscopy: refer to gastroenterology for small-bowel assessment — usually capsule endoscopy (CT/MR enterography as an alternative) — and consider renal-tract evaluation. Long-term iron may be reasonable where the cause is irreversible or unknown, with an individualised, documented plan that is periodically reviewed.
Coeliac confirmed
Confirm that coeliac disease is being managed through an appropriate coeliac pathway. Review dietary adherence, symptoms and relevant deficiencies. Arrange bone-health assessment and specialist dietetic input according to age, fracture risk, nutritional status and local guidance. Do not repeat anti-tTG annually as an automatic test for every patient.
Return / refer urgently
Hb falling rapidly despite iron replacement + worsening symptoms → ongoing blood loss → identify source urgently · Severe symptomatic anaemia or rapidly falling Hb in treated patient → same-day hospital assessment · New macroscopic rectal bleeding or haematemesis at any point
Within 2 weeks
No Hb response at 4 weeks despite compliance → IV iron consideration · New upper or lower GI symptoms developing during treatment → OGD or colonoscopy · Man or post-menopausal woman with unexplained IDA and no prior GI investigation → urgent bidirectional GI investigation; FIT per NICE/local pathway (suspected cancer referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g; a negative FIT does not exclude pathology)
The recurrence of IDA as a safety-net principle is clinically important — a significant proportion of patients who are treated successfully for IDA relapse within 12-24 months, often because the underlying cause has not been adequately addressed. The most common reasons for IDA recurrence: ongoing unrecognised GI blood loss (particularly from angiodysplasia in elderly — missed on initial colonoscopy), suboptimal menorrhagia management (LNG-IUS removed, hormonal treatment discontinued), undiagnosed or inadequately treated coeliac disease (dietary compliance poor), and ongoing NSAID use. An individualised, documented follow-up plan allows early detection of recurrence. In recurrent or refractory IDA despite adequate-quality bidirectional endoscopy, refer to gastroenterology for small-bowel assessment (usually capsule endoscopy; detects small bowel angiodysplasia, Crohn, tumour) and consider renal-tract evaluation; any long-term iron should be documented and periodically reviewed.
