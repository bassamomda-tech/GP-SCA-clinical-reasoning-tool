1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Life-Threatening Bleeding and TTP

Screen for severe thrombocytopenia, active bleeding, thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP/HUS), and DIC. These require emergency or same-day haematology.

Platelets <20 × 10⁹/L or falling rapidly Urgent same-day haematology assessment, even without bleeding (especially below 10). Treatment depends on the cause; no routine prophylactic platelets in ITP, HIT or TTP (NICE NG24)
Active major bleeding GI bleed, haematuria, haemoptysis, ICH, retinal haemorrhage → 999 / emergency at any platelet count (bleeding risk is not set by the count alone)
TTP / TMA Thrombocytopenia with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes, raised LDH, low haptoglobin, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, reticulocytosis), particularly with neurological, renal, cardiac or GI features → emergency: immediate discussion with the regional TTP/haematology service. Do not wait for the full pentad or an ADAMTS13 result
Purpuric rash Non-blanching petechiae, extensive purpura, mucosal bleeding (gums, nose) → same-day FBC/film and haematology discussion
DIC features Bleeding from multiple sites, oozing IV sites, hypotension, confusion → 999 (e.g. sepsis-, trauma- or obstetric-related DIC)
Pregnancy Thrombocytopenia with hypertension, headache, visual changes or epigastric pain → emergency obstetric assessment (pre-eclampsia/HELLP — HELLP also requires haemolysis and elevated liver enzymes)
New drug or heparin exposure Recent heparin, quinine, antibiotics, chemotherapy + platelet fall → same-day haematology (drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia or HIT; emergency if HIT with thrombosis or severe concern). Suspected HIT: stop all heparin, 4Ts score, non-heparin anticoagulant per specialist protocol
Neurological symptoms Confusion, focal weakness, seizures + thrombocytopenia → 999 (TTP, ICH)

Very low platelets warrant same-day haematology assessment even without bleeding, but the count alone does not determine bleeding risk — interpret it with bleeding symptoms, the rate of fall, other cell lines, coagulation, medication, comorbidity and any planned procedure. Treatment is cause-specific and specialist-led; prophylactic platelet transfusion is not routinely used in ITP, HIT or TTP (NICE NG24).

TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura) is a life-threatening emergency caused by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (BSH 2023). It often presents without the historical pentad, so suspect it from thrombocytopenia plus MAHA. Discuss immediately with the regional TTP/haematology service; send ADAMTS13 before plasma therapy if this does not delay treatment, but do not wait for the result.

HELLP syndrome (Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) presents with epigastric pain, nausea and headache, often with hypertension or proteinuria. Low platelets alone in pregnancy are not HELLP — haemolysis and elevated liver enzymes are also required. It needs emergency obstetric assessment.

DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation) consumes platelets and clotting factors. Triggered by sepsis, trauma, obstetric catastrophe, malignancy. Prolonged APTT/PT, low fibrinogen, raised D-dimer. Treat underlying cause (sepsis source control, delivery in AFLP).

2
Diagnose

Confirm Thrombocytopenia — Repeat Count and Exclude Pseudothrombocytopenia

Confirm true thrombocytopenia and exclude laboratory artefact (pseudothrombocytopenia). A single low platelet count may be spurious due to clumping.

Repeat FBC
Review previous counts and the automated comment. Isolated, stable 100–150 × 10⁹/L with no atypical features: repeat and investigate according to the clinical context and local laboratory/haematology pathway. Below 100, new, falling or symptomatic: discuss or refer per local pathway. Bleeding, purpura or other cytopenias: same-day assessment.
Pseudothrombocytopenia
EDTA-induced platelet clumping falsely lowers the automated count. If thrombocytopenia is unexpected and the patient is clinically well with no bleeding, check the original result and request a repeat FBC with blood film; if clumping is reported or suspected, request a citrate sample (or other anticoagulant per laboratory advice). Do not delay urgent referral for symptoms, bleeding, other cytopenias or an abnormal film while excluding it.
Blood film
Request a blood film for unexplained thrombocytopenia, platelet clumping, other cytopenias, an abnormal differential, suspected blasts, schistocytes or dysplasia, or an unexpectedly rapid fall — clinical judgement, not a numerical threshold. Identifies platelet clumping (pseudothrombocytopenia), giant platelets (ITP), schistocytes (TTP/HUS/DIC), dysplasia (MDS).
Clinical context
Recent viral illness (EBV, dengue)? Medications (heparin, quinine, antibiotics)? Pregnancy? Alcohol excess? Family history of bleeding disorders?
Bleeding assessment
Interpret the platelet count alongside bleeding symptoms, the rate of fall, other cell lines, coagulation results, medication (anticoagulants/antiplatelets), platelet function, renal/liver disease, trauma, the planned procedure, pregnancy, age and comorbidity. Do not use the platelet count alone to determine bleeding risk or admission.
Persistent thrombocytopenia
Confirm with a repeat FBC and film interpreted against previous counts; use local laboratory/haematology guidance for repeat intervals.

Pseudothrombocytopenia is a laboratory artefact: EDTA (the anticoagulant in FBC tubes) causes platelet clumping in some patients, so automated counters undercount platelets. The film shows clumps, and a citrate (or lab-advised) sample gives the true count. No treatment is needed — but assume it only in a well patient without bleeding, other cytopenias or film abnormalities.

Blood film review is key in unexplained thrombocytopenia. It can show schistocytes (TTP/TMA/DIC), large young platelets (ITP), dysplasia or blasts (MDS, leukaemia), and platelet clumps (pseudothrombocytopenia) that automated counters cannot interpret.

Bleeding risk is not determined by the platelet count alone. Some patients with severe thrombocytopenia have little bleeding, whereas clinically important bleeding can occur at higher counts — depending on the cause and rate of fall, platelet function, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, coagulopathy, renal/liver disease, trauma and planned procedures, pregnancy, age and comorbidity.

Transient thrombocytopenia post-viral infection (EBV, dengue, COVID) is common. Counts usually recover as the infection settles; repeating the FBC per local pathway avoids unnecessary referral, but escalate if the count is falling, the patient is symptomatic or other cell lines are affected.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Identify Mechanism and Severity

Classify thrombocytopenia by severity, mechanism (decreased production vs increased destruction), and whether other cell lines are affected.

Mild thrombocytopenia
Platelets 100–150 × 10⁹/L. If isolated, stable and asymptomatic with no atypical features, often managed in primary care: repeat and investigate according to the clinical context. Causes: pregnancy (gestational), viral infection, liver disease/alcohol, drugs, mild ITP, incidental finding.
Moderate thrombocytopenia
Platelets 50–100 × 10⁹/L. Causes: ITP, drugs, liver disease, SLE. Investigate, and discuss or refer per the local haematology pathway — especially if new, falling, symptomatic or associated with other abnormalities.
Severe thrombocytopenia
Platelets below 50 × 10⁹/L. Causes: ITP, TTP, drugs, sepsis, marrow disease. Significant unexplained thrombocytopenia → same-day haematology; below 20 → urgent same-day assessment. Platelet transfusion only as clinically indicated (NICE NG24).
Critical thrombocytopenia
Platelets <10 × 10⁹/L. Urgent same-day haematology assessment, even without bleeding. Treatment depends on the cause; do not give prophylactic platelets routinely in ITP, HIT or TTP.
Isolated thrombocytopenia
Low platelets only. Hb and WCC normal. Differential: ITP, gestational, drug-induced, mild viral infection. Often peripheral destruction or consumption, but marrow disease is not excluded.
Pancytopenia
Low platelets + Hb + WCC. Bone marrow failure. Causes: Aplastic anaemia, MDS, acute leukaemia, B12 deficiency. Same-day haematology discussion.

Severity bands help prioritise but do not on their own define bleeding risk or the need for admission. Interpret the count with bleeding symptoms, rate of fall, other cell lines, coagulation, medication, comorbidity and any planned procedure; when platelets are falling rapidly, urgency is greater than the absolute count suggests.

Isolated thrombocytopenia (normal Hb/WCC) suggests peripheral destruction (ITP, TTP, drug-induced) rather than bone marrow failure. The marrow is producing platelets normally, but they're being destroyed in the spleen or by antibodies. Prognosis is better than pancytopenia.

Pancytopenia (low platelets + Hb + WCC) indicates bone marrow failure or infiltration. Urgent bone marrow biopsy differentiates aplastic anaemia (empty marrow, treat with immunosuppression), MDS (dysplastic marrow, supportive care ± chemotherapy), and acute leukaemia (blast infiltration, immediate chemotherapy).

Likely gestational thrombocytopenia is usually mild, isolated and late-onset, and resolves postpartum. New, falling or moderate thrombocytopenia in pregnancy requires obstetric/haematology assessment to exclude pre-eclampsia/HELLP, ITP, TTP, acute fatty liver and other secondary causes.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Identify Bleeding, Splenomegaly, and Systemic Disease

Examine for signs of bleeding, organomegaly, and underlying conditions causing thrombocytopenia.

Vital signs
Fever + thrombocytopenia → sepsis (DIC), TTP, viral infection. Hypotension + tachycardia → active bleeding (GI, retroperitoneal). BP >140/90 in pregnancy → pre-eclampsia/HELLP.
Skin and mucosa
Inspect skin for petechiae (pinpoint haemorrhages, non-blanching), purpura (larger bruises >3 mm), ecchymoses (large bruises >1 cm). Check oral mucosa, conjunctivae for bleeding.
Bleeding sites
Gum bleeding, epistaxis (mucosal), retinal haemorrhage (fundoscopy in severe thrombocytopenia), haematuria, PR bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding. Document location and severity.
Spleen
Percuss and palpate left upper quadrant. Splenomegaly → portal hypertension (cirrhosis), lymphoma, CML, myelofibrosis; significant splenomegaly argues against isolated ITP.
Liver
Palpate for hepatomegaly. Hepatosplenomegaly → chronic liver disease (portal hypertension, hypersplenism), lymphoma, storage disease. Jaundice + thrombocytopenia → MAHA (TTP/HUS).
Lymph nodes
Palpate cervical, axillary, inguinal chains. Lymphadenopathy → lymphoma, HIV, SLE, viral infection (EBV, CMV).
Joints and rash
Malar rash, photosensitivity, arthritis → SLE (antiphospholipid syndrome). Livedo reticularis → vasculitis, APS. Synovitis → RA (rare), SLE.

Petechiae vs purpura vs ecchymoses: petechiae (pinpoint, non-blanching), purpura (larger) and ecchymoses (bruises) document bleeding but do not map reliably to a specific platelet count. Widespread or mucosal bleeding is more concerning than a few dependent petechiae on the legs.

Splenomegaly in thrombocytopenia suggests splenic sequestration (hypersplenism) or lymphoproliferative disease. In ITP the spleen is usually not enlarged; significant splenomegaly points to CML, lymphoma, myelofibrosis or portal hypertension rather than isolated ITP.

Hepatosplenomegaly with thrombocytopenia suggests chronic liver disease. Portal hypertension causes splenomegaly (congestive) and hypersplenism (splenic sequestration of platelets).

Fundoscopy may identify retinal haemorrhage in severe thrombocytopenia. Bleeding at a critical site such as the eye or brain needs urgent haematology discussion; transfusion decisions follow NICE NG24 and specialist advice.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — Bloods, Clotting, and Targeted Tests

Investigate based on severity, clinical context, and blood film findings. Target investigations to the likely differential diagnosis.

Baseline bloods
FBC + blood film (for unexplained thrombocytopenia, clumping, other cytopenias or a rapid fall) Reticulocyte count (check marrow response) LDH (haemolysis, TTP) Haptoglobin (low in MAHA)
Clotting screen (if DIC, liver disease or bleeding possible)
PT/APTT (prolonged in DIC, liver disease, normal in ITP) Fibrinogen (low in DIC) D-dimer (elevated in DIC, TTP, VTE).
If suspected TTP/HUS
Repeat FBC and Blood film (schistocytes — judge morphology in clinical context, no standalone % cut-off), Reticulocytes, LDH, Bilirubin, Haptoglobin, U&Es, Urinalysis, coagulation screen and fibrinogen if DIC is possible. Direct antiglobulin test may help separate immune from non-immune haemolysis, but a negative result is not required to suspect TTP. ADAMTS13 via haematology — discuss immediately; do not wait for the result.
If suspected ITP
For suspected isolated ITP, investigate secondary causes according to clinical context — not mandatory for every low count: HIV test, Hep B/C serology, autoimmune disease (ANA), liver disease, medications and immunodeficiency (Immunoglobulins). ITP is generally a diagnosis of exclusion, normally confirmed or managed through haematology, particularly below 100 × 10⁹/L.
Liver function
LFTs (cirrhosis, portal hypertension), Albumin (low in liver disease), Bilirubin (haemolysis vs liver disease). If cirrhosis suspected, request ultrasound liver.
Drug review
Check all medications. High-risk drugs: heparin (HIT), quinine, antibiotics (linezolid, vancomycin), NSAIDs, thiazides, valproate, chemotherapy. Stopping a suspected culprit may be appropriate, but don't stop essential medicines without considering alternatives; HIT needs alternative anticoagulation, not simply cessation; recovery time varies; quinine, vancomycin, linezolid, valproate and chemotherapy need individual assessment.
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT perform bone marrow biopsy in primary care. Do NOT delay referral to "complete investigations" if platelets <20. Do NOT order antiplatelet antibody tests routinely. Do NOT delay urgent referral while excluding pseudothrombocytopenia.

The blood film differentiates ITP (large young platelets), TTP/TMA (schistocytes — judged on morphology and clinical context rather than a fixed percentage), MDS (dysplastic cells), and pseudothrombocytopenia (platelet clumps). Without a blood film, you cannot confidently diagnose ITP.

Clotting screen differentiates isolated thrombocytopenia (ITP, normal PT/APTT) from DIC (prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen) and liver disease (prolonged PT, normal APTT). In ITP, clotting is normal because clotting factors are unaffected; only platelet number is low.

TTP diagnosis: the full pentad is often absent, so do not wait for it. Suspect TTP/TMA with thrombocytopenia plus MAHA (schistocytes, raised LDH, low haptoglobin, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, reticulocytosis). Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency confirms TTP, but specialist treatment starts before the result — send ADAMTS13 before plasma therapy if this does not delay treatment (BSH 2023).

ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion. Secondary causes (HIV, hepatitis, autoimmune disease, liver disease, drugs, immunodeficiency, TTP, MDS) are sought according to clinical context. With isolated thrombocytopenia, a normal film (apart from large platelets) and no secondary cause, haematology confirms the diagnosis; bone marrow examination is a specialist decision for atypical features.

Refer

Referral Criteria — When to Involve Haematology or Obstetrics

Refer based on severity, bleeding risk, mechanism, and pregnancy status. Severe thrombocytopenia requires same-day haematology.

999 Emergency
Major bleeding (GI, ICH, haemoptysis). Suspected TTP/TMA (thrombocytopenia + MAHA, especially with neurological, renal, cardiac or GI features — do not wait for the full pentad). DIC with instability or significant bleeding. Suspected HIT with thrombosis or severe clinical concern. Pregnancy with suspected HELLP/eclampsia. Reduced consciousness or suspected intracranial haemorrhage.
Same-day haematology
Platelets <20 × 10⁹/L, particularly if falling. Clinically significant bleeding. Schistocytes or blasts on film. Pancytopenia. Significant unexplained thrombocytopenia <50. Suspected drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia or HIT. Concerning pregnancy-related thrombocytopenia. A rapid fall — urgency is greater than the absolute count suggests.
Discuss / refer (local pathway)
Platelets <100 × 10⁹/L — discuss or refer per the local haematology pathway, especially if new, falling, symptomatic or with other abnormalities. Splenomegaly + thrombocytopenia. Abnormal blood film (dysplasia, atypical cells). B symptoms (lymphoma concern).
Routine haematology
Suspected or known ITP (confirmation and management are haematology-led). Chronic ITP needing a treatment decision. Persistent unexplained thrombocytopenia after initial investigations. Use local laboratory/haematology thresholds and timings.
Primary care management
Isolated, asymptomatic, stable 100–150 × 10⁹/L with no atypical features: review medications, alcohol, liver disease and recent infection; examine for splenomegaly/lymphadenopathy; repeat FBC and film per local pathway; safety-net for bruising, petechiae or bleeding; discuss with haematology if persistent, falling or below 100. Likely gestational thrombocytopenia only after confirming late onset, isolated, no hypertension or proteinuria, normal liver enzymes and haemolysis markers, no bleeding, no previous ITP and no concerning trend. Transient post-viral thrombocytopenia that is recovering.
Obstetrics referral
New, falling or moderate thrombocytopenia in pregnancy: obstetric/haematology assessment — exclude pre-eclampsia/HELLP, ITP, TTP, acute fatty liver and other secondary causes. Pre-eclampsia/HELLP (HELLP needs haemolysis and elevated liver enzymes as well as low platelets): emergency obstetric assessment. Neuraxial anaesthesia: no universal platelet cut-off — decided by the obstetric anaesthetic and haematology/obstetric team according to cause, trend, coagulation, bleeding history, obstetric context and local policy.
Local pathway
Follow the current local haematology referral pathway and laboratory guidance for thresholds and repeat intervals.

Platelets <10 × 10⁹/L require urgent same-day haematology assessment even if asymptomatic. Treatment depends on the cause and is specialist-led; in ITP, prophylactic platelet transfusion is not routinely given (NICE NG24).

TTP is a medical emergency. Discuss immediately with the regional TTP/haematology service for plasma therapy; do not wait for ADAMTS13 results or for the full pentad.

HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) is paradoxically prothrombotic despite low platelets. If suspected, stop all heparin (including flushes) immediately, assess with the 4Ts score, contact haematology urgently and start an appropriate non-heparin anticoagulant per the specialist/local protocol (options include argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux with weight- and renal-function-dependent dosing, or a DOAC in selected patients). Do not wait for laboratory confirmation if probability is intermediate or high. Do not give platelet transfusions routinely — only for life-threatening bleeding or specialist-directed procedures (BSH 2023).

Gestational thrombocytopenia is usually mild, isolated and late-onset; new, falling or moderate thrombocytopenia needs obstetric/haematology assessment to exclude pre-eclampsia/HELLP, ITP, TTP and acute fatty liver. Suitability for neuraxial anaesthesia depends on the cause, platelet trend, coagulation, bleeding history, obstetric context and local anaesthetic policy, and is decided by the obstetric anaesthetic and haematology/obstetric team rather than a fixed threshold.

7
Treat

Treatment — Treat Underlying Cause and Raise Platelets if Severe

Treatment depends on cause and severity. Treatment of ITP is haematology-led and depends on bleeding, platelet count, comorbidity, planned procedures and patient preference. Primary care should arrange urgent referral, manage bleeding emergencies and avoid medicines that worsen bleeding unless essential.

Drug-induced thrombocytopenia
Review suspected drug Same-day
Stopping a suspected culprit may be appropriate, but don't stop essential medicines without considering alternatives; recovery time varies; quinine, vancomycin, linezolid, valproate and chemotherapy need individual assessment. Suspected HIT: stop all heparin immediately, assess the 4Ts score, contact haematology urgently and start a non-heparin anticoagulant per specialist/local protocol (dosing is weight- and renal-function dependent). Don't wait for laboratory confirmation if probability is intermediate or high; no routine platelet transfusion.
Suspected or known ITP, no bleeding
Haematology-led Refer
Whether to observe or treat is a haematology decision. Avoid NSAIDs/aspirin unless advised; individualise sport advice; don't stop anticoagulants without discussion. Monitoring interval per the haematology plan.
ITP with bleeding or severe thrombocytopenia
Haematology-led IVIg or steroids
Urgent haematology referral; treatment (e.g. corticosteroids or IVIg) is chosen and dosed by haematology. Primary care manages bleeding emergencies.

ITP treatment options (haematology-led — not a primary-care prescribing protocol):

Step 1Corticosteroids or IVIg — chosen, dosed and monitored by haematology (IVIg when a faster rise is needed, e.g. bleeding or an urgent procedure).
Step 2Second-line options (e.g. thrombopoietin receptor agonists, rituximab) for persistent, steroid-dependent or refractory ITP — specialist-initiated and specialist-monitored.
Step 3Primary-care role: urgent referral, managing bleeding emergencies, avoiding medicines that worsen bleeding unless essential, and prescribing only under agreed shared-care arrangements.
Step 4Splenectomy — a specialist decision, with timing set by haematology. Risk: lifelong infection risk (encapsulated bacteria). Vaccinate ideally at least 2 weeks before elective splenectomy per UKHSA Green Book chapters 7 and 25 (pneumococcal, MenACWY, MenB; annual influenza).
Platelet transfusion
Discuss urgently with haematology/transfusion medicine. For clinically significant bleeding with platelets <30 × 10⁹/L, platelet transfusion may be indicated; higher thresholds (up to 100 × 10⁹/L) for severe bleeding or bleeding at critical sites such as the brain or eyes (NICE NG24). Increments after transfusion are variable. In ITP, TTP and HIT, transfusion is generally reserved for life-threatening bleeding or urgent specialist-directed intervention.
Emergency ITP treatment
Platelets <10 or major bleeding: urgent same-day haematology/acute assessment; emergency treatment is specialist-directed and depends on the cause. Platelet transfusion is not given routinely as prophylaxis in ITP.
Monitoring on treatment
FBC frequency per the haematology plan. Watch for steroid side effects (hyperglycaemia, mood changes) and consider bone protection with prolonged courses.

Not every patient with ITP needs treatment. Haematology decides whether to observe or treat based on bleeding, platelet count, comorbidity, planned procedures and patient preference; treatments have side effects and often only temporarily raise platelets.

IVIg saturates Fc receptors on splenic macrophages, reducing destruction of antibody-coated platelets. Specialists use it when a rapid rise is needed (bleeding, urgent procedure) or steroids are unsuitable.

Corticosteroids suppress autoantibody-mediated destruction, but relapse on tapering is common and prolonged use brings osteoporosis, diabetes, weight gain and immunosuppression — so courses are kept as short as possible under haematology direction.

Platelet transfusion in ITP is generally reserved for life-threatening bleeding or urgent specialist-directed intervention, because transfused platelets are rapidly destroyed by the same antibodies (NICE NG24).

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Prevent Trauma and Recognise Bleeding Early

Lifestyle modifications reduce bleeding risk in patients with chronic thrombocytopenia. Education on recognizing serious bleeding is critical.

Avoid contact sports No universal platelet cut-off for sport. Tailor advice to the cause and stability of thrombocytopenia, bleeding history, contact and head-trauma risk, anticoagulation and the specific sport; wear a helmet where head injury is possible.
Avoid NSAIDs and anticoagulants Avoid NSAIDs and aspirin when significant thrombocytopenia or bleeding is present unless specifically advised by haematology. Use paracetamol where appropriate, with usual liver and dose precautions. Do not stop anticoagulants or antiplatelets automatically — discuss urgently with haematology/acute medicine, balancing bleeding and thrombosis risk (particularly with HIT, mechanical valves, recent VTE, atrial fibrillation and coronary disease).
Dental precautions Inform dentist of low platelets before any procedure. Agree procedure-specific platelet thresholds for invasive dental work (extraction, implants) with haematology and dentistry. Gentle brushing with soft toothbrush. Use waxed floss. Avoid mouthwash with alcohol (dries mucosa).
Alcohol moderation Alcohol suppresses platelet production and impairs function. Reduce intake; abstinence if alcohol-related thrombocytopenia or liver disease.
Menstrual management Heavy periods common in thrombocytopenia. Tranexamic acid can help menstrual or mucosal bleeding, but avoid or seek specialist advice with haematuria, DIC, significant thrombotic risk, certain renal impairment, or suspected TTP/HIT. Mirena coil or COCP suppresses menstruation. Iron supplementation if anaemic (ferrous sulfate 200 mg OD).
Shaving safety Use electric razor (not wet shave). Apply pressure if cut. Avoid waxing/threading (trauma). Epistaxis: pinch nose for 20 minutes, ice pack, lean forward (not back).
IM injections and procedures Agree thresholds for IM injections and other procedures with haematology (or dentistry, obstetrics, anaesthesia); NICE NG24 suggests considering platelets above 50 × 10⁹/L for invasive procedures and higher for high-risk or critical-site surgery, but the cause and procedure matter. Warn healthcare staff. Avoid acupuncture, tattoos, piercings. Blood draws: apply firm pressure for 10 minutes post-venepuncture.
Recognise serious bleeding Seek emergency help for: severe headache (ICH), vomiting blood, black stools (GI bleed), haematuria, breathlessness (pulmonary haemorrhage), sudden vision loss (retinal bleed). Teach patient warning signs.

NSAIDs and aspirin impair platelet function and may increase bleeding risk, but whether to avoid them depends on the platelet count and trend, bleeding symptoms, cause, concurrent anticoagulants and renal/GI/cardiovascular risk. Paracetamol has no antiplatelet effect; use usual liver and dose precautions.

Contact sports risk head trauma. Advice should reflect the cause and stability of thrombocytopenia, bleeding history, contact and head-trauma risk, anticoagulation and the specific sport, rather than a single platelet cut-off.

Heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia) is common in thrombocytopenia. Tranexamic acid (antifibrinolytic) can reduce menstrual bleeding where appropriate, and the levonorgestrel IUS or hormonal options can reduce or suppress menstruation — discuss with haematology/gynaecology.

Recognising ICH early saves lives. Sudden severe headache, vomiting, confusion, focal weakness and seizures are red flags needing 999 assessment; teach every patient with significant thrombocytopenia these warning signs.

9
Safety

Follow-Up — Monitor Platelet Count and Safety-Net for Bleeding

Monitor platelet trajectory, watch for bleeding, and re-refer if platelets falling or new symptoms. Chronic thrombocytopenia requires long-term surveillance.

Drug-induced
Repeat FBC per haematology/local advice after stopping a suspected drug (platelets should be rising; recovery time varies). If still falling, same-day haematology. Check for bleeding (petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, menorrhagia).
Post-viral
Repeat FBC per local pathway in transient viral thrombocytopenia (should normalise). If persistent, falling or below 100, discuss or refer per the local haematology pathway. New bleeding, significant unexplained thrombocytopenia (<50) or other cytopenias → same-day.
Known ITP
Monitor FBC at intervals set by the haematology plan; re-refer if falling or bleeding.
Chronic ITP
Long-term haematology follow-up; monitoring frequency and any change or step-down of therapy are haematology decisions.
Safety-net 999
Severe headache, vomiting, confusion (ICH). Vomiting blood, black stools (GI bleed). Haemoptysis (pulmonary haemorrhage). Sudden vision loss (retinal haemorrhage). Severe dyspnoea (pulmonary bleed).
Safety-net same-day
Extensive new purpura or petechiae. Uncontrolled epistaxis >20 min. Heavy vaginal bleeding (soaking pads hourly). Haematuria. Gum bleeding not stopping with pressure. Any bleeding in platelets <20.
Re-referral criteria
Platelets falling despite treatment. New bleeding on stable platelet count. Platelet count <20 × 10⁹/L (ICH risk). Patient requires a procedure (surgery, dental, neuraxial anaesthesia) — agree procedure-specific thresholds with haematology/anaesthesia. Pregnancy planning (pre-conception counselling).

Platelet trajectory matters as much as the absolute count: a steady rise suggests recovery, while fluctuating or falling counts need haematology review. When platelets fall rapidly, urgency is greater than the absolute count suggests.

Spontaneous remission can occur in ITP, especially after viral infection, which is why haematology may choose observation rather than treatment when there is no bleeding.

ICH risk is the primary concern in severe thrombocytopenia. Sudden severe headache in a thrombocytopenic patient is ICH until proven otherwise — call 999 for emergency assessment and imaging.

Chronic ITP (platelets <100 × 10⁹/L for >12 months) requires long-term haematology follow-up. Relapse after stopping treatment is common. Second-line therapies (rituximab, TPO agonists, splenectomy) have different risk-benefit profiles; shared decision-making with patient and haematologist is essential.

Pathway based on the International Consensus Report on ITP (Provan et al., Blood Advances 2019; international), ASH ITP guideline (2019; international), UKHSA Green Book chapters 7 and 25 (asplenia vaccination), NICE NG24 (blood transfusion — platelet transfusion thresholds), BSH guideline on TTP and other thrombotic microangiopathies (2023), BSH guideline on heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (3rd edition, 2023), current obstetric anaesthesia guidance, NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), and the local haematology referral pathway and laboratory guidance. Always adapt to individual patient context, local guidelines, and specialist advice. Thrombocytopenia is a laboratory finding — identify and treat the underlying cause while preventing bleeding complications.
