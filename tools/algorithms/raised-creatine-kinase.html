1
Safety

Red Flags — Rhabdomyolysis & Necrotising Myositis

Express CK as a multiple of the upper limit of normal (ULN) — it varies between laboratories and between the sexes. The danger is rhabdomyolysis: muscle breakdown releasing myoglobin, which causes acute kidney injury and hyperkalaemia.

CK >50× ULN (approx >5000 IU/L) Admit same day. Stop any causative drugs. Hospital rhabdomyolysis management — IV fluids, monitor renal function and potassium.
CK 10–50× ULN (approx 1000–5000) + AKI, oliguria or myoglobinuria Discuss with medics and admit. Myoglobinuria = dark urine, often ≥2+ blood on dipstick but no red cells on microscopy — do not wait for microscopy if suspected.
Severe / continuous muscle aches and progressive weakness even after stopping a statin Consider autoimmune statin-related necrotising myositis (anti-HMGCR antibody myopathy) — rare but very significant; weakness progresses despite drug cessation and CK stays high. Needs urgent specialist (rheumatology/neurology) assessment.
Crush injury, prolonged immobility, seizures, extreme exertion or hyperthermia High rhabdomyolysis risk — check CK, renal function and urine; treat the precipitant; admit if CK markedly raised or AKI present.
New muscle aches or raised CK after starting / up-titrating a statin Go to the statin-intolerance pathway. Stop the statin if symptoms are significant or CK is >5× ULN, and reassess.
Hyperkalaemia or AKI with a raised CK Potassium ≥6.0 mmol/L, a rising creatinine or ECG changes → same-day admission; treat as rhabdomyolysis until proven otherwise.
Tense, painful limb after crush, prolonged lie or injection-drug use Possible compartment syndrome — pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch → emergency surgical assessment.
Chest pain with a raised CK CK also rises with myocardial injury — if the story is cardiac, manage as acute coronary syndrome (troponin, not CK, is the diagnostic test).
Weakness with breathing or swallowing difficulty Inflammatory myopathy or neuromuscular disease involving respiratory or bulbar muscles → same-day specialist assessment.
Creatine kinase is released from damaged skeletal (and cardiac) muscle. The clinical risk in a very high CK is rhabdomyolysis — myoglobin released from breaking-down muscle is filtered by the kidney and is directly nephrotoxic, precipitates in tubules, and (with the volume depletion that often accompanies the precipitant) causes acute kidney injury. Hyperkalaemia from muscle breakdown can be life-threatening independently. Because microscopy takes time, a positive dipstick for blood with no red cells on microscopy is treated as myoglobinuria — but you act on the clinical suspicion (dark urine + very high CK + a precipitant) without waiting. Autoimmune necrotising myositis is a rare but important mimic of simple statin myalgia: the key discriminator is that weakness is severe and continues to progress after the statin is stopped, whereas ordinary statin myopathy improves on withdrawal. CK typically peaks one to three days after the muscle injury, so a moderately raised value early after a crush injury, prolonged lie or seizure may still be climbing — which is why a precipitant plus dark urine is acted on even before the number is very high.

Diagnose

When to check CK & how to interpret it

When to check
Check CK if any significant symptoms: muscle pain, tenderness or weakness. This pathway also applies if CK is found raised for any other reason (incidental). New muscle symptoms on a statin → use the statin-intolerance pathway.
Express as multiples of ULN
CK reference ranges vary lab to lab and between the sexes — always interpret as a multiple of the local ULN, not the absolute number. Approximate equivalents used here: <4× ≈ <500, <10× ≈ <1000, 10–50× ≈ 1000–5000, >50× ≈ >5000 IU/L.
Other tests at the same time
Check renal function and a urine sample (dipstick, and M,C&S). Look actively for precipitating causes — especially drugs (statins/fibrates).
Confirm & recheck
A transient rise (e.g. after exercise) settles. After high-intensity exercise CK usually normalises in 3–7 days — recheck in 1–2 weeks before extensive investigation if symptoms are mild and there is a clear precipitant.
Recheck conditions
Ask the patient to avoid strenuous exercise, intramuscular injections and heavy alcohol for about a week before the repeat test, so that the result reflects the resting level.
Statins — before starting
If a person has persistent generalised unexplained muscle pain before a statin is started, measure CK; if it is more than 5× ULN, re-measure after 7 days; if still more than 5× ULN, do not start a statin; if raised but below 5× ULN, start at a lower dose (NICE NG238, 2023).
Statins — asymptomatic
Do not measure CK routinely in people taking a statin who have no muscle symptoms (NICE NG238).
Unusual results
Isolated persistently raised CK with a normal examination may be macro-CK (a laboratory phenomenon) or idiopathic hyperCKaemia; the laboratory can advise on testing for macro-CK.
Because exercise is such a common and benign cause, a single mildly raised CK in a well person with a recent bout of exertion rarely needs a full myopathy work-up — the pragmatic step is to remove the precipitant (rest, hydration, hold any non-essential causative drug) and recheck after the muscle has recovered. Interpreting CK as a multiple of the ULN matters because reference ranges differ substantially between laboratories and are higher in men and in people of African ancestry, so an "absolute" number can mislead. The multiples used in this pathway follow a published phenotype classification for statin-related muscle toxicity (Alfirevic et al., Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics, 2014), adapted here as pragmatic triage bands.
3
Diagnose

Focused history & examination

Symptom pattern
Pain vs weakness; distribution (proximal weakness — difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, raising arms — suggests a myopathy); onset, duration, relation to exertion; dark urine; systemic features (rash, arthralgia, dysphagia).
Drug & substance history
Statins/fibrates and timing of any dose change; CYP3A4 interactors; antipsychotics, hydroxychloroquine, colchicine, retinoids, antiretrovirals; alcohol, cocaine, MDMA.
Precipitants
Recent unaccustomed/eccentric exercise, trauma, seizure, surgery, prolonged immobility, hyperthermia, intercurrent illness.
Background
Thyroid/endocrine symptoms; autoimmune features; family history of muscle disease; ethnicity (higher baseline CK in men and people of African ancestry).
Examination
Confirmed proximal weakness (e.g. unable to rise from squat), muscle tenderness/wasting, skin rash (dermatomyositis), thyroid status; check for signs of the precipitant.
Discriminating patterns
Exertional pain and cramps with dark urine after exercise, sometimes easing after a few minutes of rest ("second wind") → metabolic myopathy such as McArdle disease. Heliotrope rash, Gottron papules → dermatomyositis (screen for underlying cancer in adults). Calf hypertrophy, family history, young male → muscular dystrophy. Weight gain, cold intolerance, slow relaxing reflexes → hypothyroid myopathy. Breathlessness, arthritis, mechanic's hands → antisynthetase syndrome.
Features of rhabdomyolysis
Muscle pain and swelling, weakness, dark urine, reduced urine output, and systemic upset (fever, nausea, confusion) — ask about all of these at the first contact.
Ideas and concerns
Patients on statins often worry the drug is "damaging their muscles"; asking about this early helps keep the conversation on the statin-intolerance pathway rather than permanent discontinuation.
The single most useful clinical discriminator is whether there is true weakness (especially proximal) as opposed to pain alone: ache after exertion or with a statin is usually benign, whereas progressive proximal weakness points towards an inflammatory or hereditary myopathy and changes the threshold for referral and investigation. A careful drug and exertion history identifies the reversible drivers that account for most raised CK results, so they can be removed before any extensive work-up. Pattern recognition matters because some myopathies have their own associations: dermatomyositis in adults carries an increased risk of an underlying cancer, and antisynthetase syndrome brings interstitial lung disease, so these patients need specialist assessment rather than repeat CK checks.

Diagnose

Severity bands (multiples of ULN)

>50× ULN (≈ >5000)
Admit same day; stop causative drugs. Rhabdomyolysis management in hospital.
10–50× ULN (≈ 1000–5000)
Stop causative drugs. Discuss with medics / admit if AKI, oliguria or myoglobinuria. On a statin → statin-intolerance pathway. Severe continuous aches → consider necrotising myositis.
>4 to <10× ULN
More likely a secondary cause. Repeat CK; arrange the secondary work-up (step 4); urine dipstick.
<4× ULN (≈ <500)
Usually incidental / transient (e.g. exercise). Rest + hydration, recheck in 1–2 weeks; review lifestyle & drugs. A recurrently stable <500 with no symptoms/risk factors needs no further investigation.
What overrides the band
Weakness, dark urine, AKI, hyperkalaemia or systemic illness move a patient up a band regardless of the number. Conversely, a very high CK in a fit athlete 24 hours after an ultra-endurance event, with normal renal function and clear urine, may need only close review — but that judgement belongs to the admitting team, not a phone call.
The degree of CK elevation guides both urgency and the breadth of investigation. Very high values are about preventing kidney injury (rhabdomyolysis); intermediate values prompt a structured search for a secondary cause; modest values in a well person are most often physiological and self-limiting. The bands are pragmatic multiples of ULN rather than fixed numbers because of inter-laboratory and sex variation. In the SCA, state the multiple explicitly ("your CK is about six times the top of the normal range") — it shows the examiner you are interpreting, not just reading, the result.

Diagnose

Causes & secondary work-up

Physiological / muscle insult
Exercise (resistance or extreme) ± dehydration — commonest; trauma, seizures, recent surgery, systemic illness.
Substances
Alcohol excess (and associated thiamine deficiency); stimulants — cocaine, MDMA.
Drugs
Statins / fibrates (especially with other drugs affecting CYP3A4), antipsychotics, hydroxychloroquine, colchicine, systemic retinoids, antiretrovirals for HIV, some cancer drugs.
Endocrine
Thyroid disease, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing's, acromegaly.
Autoimmune
RA, SLE, coeliac disease, dermatomyositis, polymyositis. Commoner in those >50 with symptoms.
Hereditary myopathies
Glycogen storage disease (e.g. McArdle disease), muscular dystrophy, mitochondrial myopathy; carriers of Duchenne or Becker dystrophy (including some women) can have a raised CK with few symptoms.
Infection
Viral myositis (influenza, COVID-19 and others), especially in children with calf pain after a viral illness; severe sepsis.
Drug interactions to check
Simvastatin with clarithromycin or erythromycin, some azole antifungals and HIV protease inhibitors; dose limits for simvastatin with amlodipine, diltiazem, verapamil and amiodarone (MHRA Drug Safety Update, August 2012; BNF). Statin plus fibrate (especially gemfibrozil) or fusidic acid raises the risk further.
Secondary bloods (>4 to <10× ULN)
U&Es LFTs TFTs Corrected calcium Phosphate Magnesium Vitamin D + urine dipstick; consider inflammatory markers / other endocrine tests per history.
Raised CK is frequently multifactorial — for example a statin plus alcohol plus a recent bout of unaccustomed exercise. The secondary panel screens for the common reversible drivers (thyroid and other endocrine disease, electrolyte and vitamin D abnormalities, renal impairment) so that the elevation is not wrongly attributed to a primary muscle disease. Persisting, unexplained elevation — particularly with weakness in someone over 50 — raises the possibility of an inflammatory myopathy, where rheumatology assessment and consideration of myositis-specific antibodies and imaging is worthwhile. Drug interactions deserve a specific check because the same statin dose can become myotoxic when a short course of a macrolide or azole blocks its metabolism — a common, preventable cause of statin-related rhabdomyolysis.

Treat

Management in primary care

<4× ULN, well, recent exercise
Rest, hydrate, recheck 1–2 weeks
Advise rest and good hydration; recheck CK (usually normalises in 3–7 days). Review alcohol, medication and personal/family history of muscle disease.
Recurrently stable <500, no symptoms
No further investigation
A recurrently stable CK <500 IU/L with no symptoms or risk factors for an underlying cause needs no further work-up.
>4 to <10× ULN
Repeat CK + secondary bloods
Reassess for causative drugs; arrange the secondary panel (step 4) and urine dipstick. Treat any reversible cause found.
On a statin with muscle symptoms
Statin-intolerance pathway
Stop the statin if symptoms significant or CK >5× ULN; reassess and re-challenge / switch per the lipids & statins pathway.
Statin re-challenge
For people with muscle symptoms on a statin, NICE NG238 (2023) advises stopping it and trying again when symptoms have resolved, to check whether the statin caused them; if symptoms recur, reduce the dose or change to a different statin; seek specialist lipid advice if several statins are not tolerated. Other lipid-lowering options (for example ezetimibe) are considered per NG238 and the relevant technology appraisals.
Vitamin D and coenzyme Q10
Correct vitamin D deficiency if found; coenzyme Q10 is not recommended as a treatment for statin muscle symptoms (clinical practice — trial evidence is inconsistent).
Communicating the evidence
Blinded N-of-1 trials have shown that most muscle symptoms reported on statins are similar on placebo (Herrett et al., BMJ, 2021) — a useful, non-dismissive point when discussing a re-challenge.
SCA phrasing
"Your muscle enzyme is mildly raised, which is very common after exercise. I'd like you to rest from hard exercise for a week and repeat the test. If your urine goes dark like cola, you pass much less urine, or your muscles become very painful or weak, please get seen the same day."
Most management is conservative: remove the precipitant, hydrate, and recheck. The key judgement is distinguishing the well patient with a transient, exercise-related rise (who needs reassurance and a recheck) from the patient whose CK reflects a treatable secondary cause or an evolving myopathy. Statin-related symptoms have their own structured pathway because many patients tolerate a re-challenge, a lower dose or an alternative statin, and unnecessary permanent discontinuation removes important cardiovascular protection. The re-challenge sequence in NICE NG238 is designed to separate true statin myopathy from coincidental aches, which are common in the age group taking statins.
7
Refer

Referral

Admit same day
CK >50× ULN (≈ >5000), OR any CK with AKI, oliguria or myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis). Stop causative drugs.
Rheumatology
Suspected inflammatory myopathy — persistent unexplained elevation, or muscle weakness, especially in those >50 with symptoms.
Neurology
Suspected hereditary myopathy (e.g. young patient, family history, exertional symptoms) or unexplained progressive weakness.
Statin pathway
New muscle aches or raised CK on starting/up-titrating a statin → lipids & statins (statin-intolerance) pathway.
Urgency
Urgent (days): progressive proximal weakness, rash of dermatomyositis, weakness persisting after stopping a statin, dysphagia. Routine: persistent unexplained CK above 4× ULN with a negative secondary panel, or a suspected hereditary cause in a well patient.
What to include
Serial CK values with dates and the local ULN, exercise and drug history (with statin dates), secondary panel results, examination findings including a clear statement on weakness, and family history.
Occupational and sport
Athletes, military recruits and manual workers with recurrent exertional rhabdomyolysis need specialist assessment for an underlying metabolic myopathy before returning to full training.
Referral is reserved for the minority: those with a persistently unexplained or rising CK, with weakness suggesting an inflammatory or hereditary myopathy, where specialist investigation (antibodies, EMG, MRI, muscle biopsy or genetic testing) changes management. Most raised CK results are explained by a reversible cause found in primary care. A well-documented referral — serial values expressed as multiples of ULN, dated drug changes and an explicit strength examination — speeds up specialist triage considerably.

Lifestyle

Modifiable Factors — Remove the Muscle Insult

Rest & hydrate Avoid intense/eccentric exercise and stay well hydrated before rechecking — exercise is the commonest cause and rises settle in 3–7 days. Advise a gradual return once CK normalises.
Alcohol & stimulants Reduce alcohol (and address thiamine deficiency); avoid cocaine/MDMA.
Review drugs Reassess statins/fibrates and CYP3A4-interacting drugs; use the statin-intolerance pathway rather than abandoning cardiovascular protection.
Treat reversible causes Correct thyroid disease and vitamin D; manage other endocrine/electrolyte abnormalities found on the secondary panel.
Training sensibly Build up new or eccentric exercise gradually (e.g. spin classes, heavy lifting), stay hydrated, and avoid training in extreme heat or when unwell.
Supplements Some bodybuilding and weight-loss supplements contain stimulants or undeclared drugs — ask directly and advise stopping while CK is raised.
Because raised CK is usually multifactorial, the highest-yield intervention is simply removing the stacked muscle insults — recent unaccustomed exercise, alcohol, dehydration and a contributing drug — and then re-measuring once the muscle has recovered. Treating an under-recognised hypothyroidism or vitamin D deficiency can normalise a persistently raised CK without any need for myopathy investigation. Advice about graded return to exercise matters because exertional rhabdomyolysis tends to occur in people who are unaccustomed to a new, intense activity rather than in the habitually fit.

Follow-up

Monitoring & Safety-net

Recheck
Repeat CK in 1–2 weeks after rest (exercise-related rises settle in 3–7 days). A recurrently stable CK <500 with no symptoms/risk factors needs no further work-up.
If still raised
Persistent unexplained elevation → complete the secondary-cause panel ± rheumatology/neurology referral. Track the trend, not a single value.
Same-day / 999
Dark (cola/tea-coloured) urine, reduced urine output, severe muscle pain or weakness — rhabdomyolysis with AKI. Stop causative drugs and seek urgent care.
Progressive weakness
Weakness that worsens despite stopping a statin → urgent specialist review for autoimmune necrotising myositis.
After rhabdomyolysis
Check renal function has recovered after discharge; record the episode and any culprit drug in the notes; advise on the precipitant (drug, exertion, alcohol); refer if recurrent or unexplained.
Document
Record the CK as a multiple of ULN, the precipitant, drug changes made, the recheck date and the safety-net given.
Rhabdomyolysis can evolve after the initial test, so a patient sent home with a moderately raised CK must know the warning features — severe pain, dark urine, reduced urine output — that mandate urgent reassessment. Following the trend rather than a single number distinguishes the transient, resolving rise from the persistent elevation that warrants a full work-up, and the progressive-weakness rule is the safety-net for the rare necrotising myositis that does not improve on statin withdrawal. A clear recheck date, handed to the patient, stops a mildly raised CK from being forgotten in the results inbox.
