1
Safety

Red Flags — Cauda Equina, Cancer & Infection

Check red flags at every consultation — they can develop in a patient with established chronic back pain. Always ask about bladder, bowel, and saddle area.

Cauda equina syndrome Suspect with ANY of: new urinary retention, loss of urinary sensation, difficulty initiating micturition, new urinary incontinence, saddle/perineal sensory disturbance, bilateral sciatica, progressive bilateral leg weakness or new faecal incontinence — CES may be incomplete; not all features need be present → immediate emergency assessment and urgent MRI per the local spinal pathway. Chronic urinary frequency or stress incontinence is not equivalent to CES.
Back pain + cancer history / weight loss Spinal metastases (prostate, breast, lung, kidney, thyroid, myeloma) → imaging directed by the suspected pathology via the local cancer/MSCC pathway. No generic "cancer screen" — do not order PSA or CA125 routinely for undifferentiated back pain. Older adults with persistent unexplained back/bone pain, weight loss, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, renal impairment or other myeloma features → FBC, calcium, renal function, ESR/CRP, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains (per local laboratory availability); refer urgently if results or findings suggest myeloma or metastatic disease. NICE NG12: persistent unexplained back/bone pain 60+ → urgent FBC, calcium & ESR/PV; plus serum free light chains (urine Bence-Jones protein if free light chain testing is unavailable); results suggesting myeloma → suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12, updated April 2026).
Known cancer + back pain (?MSCC) Neurological symptoms/signs (radicular pain, weakness, sensory level, gait disturbance, bladder/bowel symptoms) → immediately contact the MSCC coordinator/acute oncology; whole-spine MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 h; dexamethasone 16 mg promptly for neurological symptoms/signs, in liaison with the MSCC service (NICE NG234). Suspected spinal metastases without neurological signs → MRI within 1 week; no routine corticosteroids unless severe pain or haematological malignancy. Low threshold to contact the MSCC pathway.
Back pain + fever / infection risk IV drug use, recent spinal procedure or surgery, bacteraemia, immunosuppression, diabetes, endocarditis, recent invasive infection → vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis. CRP/ESR are supportive, not diagnostic (normal early markers do not exclude infection); blood cultures if febrile or systemic infection suspected; MRI is the imaging test of choice. Same-day hospital if sepsis, neurological deficit, severe systemic illness or spinal instability suspected.
Severe unrelenting night pain (not positional) Cannot find a comfortable position, worse at night — malignancy, infection, or inflammatory (ankylosing spondylitis in younger patients). Investigations directed by the suspected pathology — no routine X-ray or PSA without a specific suspicion.
Back pain + steroid use / osteoporosis Consider vertebral fracture in sudden severe focal back pain, particularly after trauma or with osteoporosis, long-term corticosteroid use, frailty or previous fragility fracture. Imaging per clinical assessment and local pathway — X-ray may identify compression fracture; CT or MRI if neurological deficit, suspected malignancy, infection or diagnostic uncertainty.
Progressive neurological deficit Progressive leg weakness, increasing paraesthesia spreading up leg, loss of reflexes — cord or cauda equina compression → immediate emergency assessment (urgent MRI via the local spinal pathway). Do not wait for symptoms to stabilise.
Back pain after significant trauma Fall from height, RTA, direct spinal trauma → X-ray / CT spine. Fracture / cord injury risk — do not mobilise before imaging in major trauma or suspected unstable fracture (not required for every osteoporotic patient with new pain).
Back pain in patient on anticoagulant + neurological deficit Epidural haematoma — spinal cord compression → 999 emergency. Rare — emergency decompression may be needed.
Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is the most catastrophic missed diagnosis in back pain — it is a spinal surgical emergency. The cauda equina (horse's tail) is the bundle of nerve roots below L1 that innervates the lower limbs, bladder, bowel, and perineum. Compression (most commonly from a large central disc prolapse at L4/5 or L5/S1) causes rapidly progressive neurological deficit. Delay risks permanent bladder, bowel, sexual and lower-limb dysfunction; the timing of surgery depends on neurological status, MRI findings, specialist assessment and local pathways. Suspected CES needs immediate emergency assessment and urgent MRI via the local spinal pathway. The GP's clinical rule: ask every back pain patient about bladder (retention = most specific sign — painless inability to pass urine), bowel, and saddle area sensation at every consultation. A new positive answer to any of these → immediate emergency assessment. CES may be incomplete — not all features need be present; chronic urinary frequency or stress incontinence is not equivalent. Bilateral leg symptoms, even mild, in a back pain patient must also raise concern.

Diagnose

Classification — Non-Specific vs Specific

Non-specific CLBP
Most patients seen in primary care, after serious and specific causes have been considered — reassess if symptoms change. Chronic (>12 weeks). No neurological deficit. No red flags. Influenced by psychosocial factors (yellow flags). NICE NG59: do not routinely image in non-specialist settings (specialist imaging only if the result is likely to change management) — imaging rarely changes management and may be harmful (catastrophising, over-medicalisation of normal findings). Exercise is the treatment.
Radiculopathy (nerve root)
Radiation of pain below the knee following a dermatomal pattern. L4: medial calf, weakness of knee extension. L5: lateral calf + dorsum of foot, weakness of great toe extension (EHL). S1: posterior calf + sole + small toe, absent ankle jerk, weakness of plantar flexion. A positive straight leg raise supports nerve-root irritation but is not diagnostic alone.
Spinal stenosis
Neurogenic claudication — bilateral leg pain/heaviness/weakness brought on by walking, relieved by sitting or leaning forward (flexing the spine increases canal space). Age >60. Canal narrowing from OA, facet joint hypertrophy, thickened ligamentum flavum. Conservative first (exercise, physio); MSK/spinal referral for severe neurogenic claudication — routine MRI is not a primary-care requirement. Do not use epidural injections for neurogenic claudication due to central canal stenosis (NICE NG59).
Inflammatory back pain (spondyloarthropathy)
Use NICE NG65 referral criteria, not a simplified rule: onset before 45 (before 40 particularly suggestive), symptoms >3 months, improvement with movement, night waking, buttock pain, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, uveitis, enthesitis, family history, good response to NSAIDs. HLA-B27 and CRP may both be negative — do not use them to exclude. Ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, reactive, enteropathic. Rheumatology referral.
Chronic CLBP duration
Subacute: 6–12 weeks. Chronic: >12 weeks (this pathway). Reassess if symptoms change.
Distinguish pain types
Non-specific mechanical back pain, radicular pain (sciatica), clearly neuropathic pain (NICE CG173) and chronic primary pain (NICE NG193: >3 months with significant distress or functional disability not better explained by another condition) are managed differently — do not blur them.
NICE NG59 explicitly states: "Do not offer imaging for non-specific low back pain." This is a major departure from historical practice and is frequently misunderstood by patients and clinicians alike. The evidence base is clear: routine MRI in back pain patients without red flags increases catastrophising, disability, and healthcare utilisation without improving outcomes. Degenerative disc changes (disc bulges, facet joint OA, Modic changes) are common in pain-free adults on MRI — they are radiological variants of ageing, not pathological findings requiring treatment. Telling a patient they have a "bulging disc" or "wear and tear changes" significantly increases their pain beliefs, reduces physical activity, and promotes disability behaviour. The GP communication task is to explain that absence of imaging is the evidence-based approach, and that the spine is strong and movement is healing.
3
Diagnose

Yellow Flags — Psychosocial Barriers to Recovery

Yellow flags predict chronicity more reliably than any imaging finding. Consider risk stratification (e.g. STarT Back) at first contact for each new episode (NICE NG59) — it informs, not replaces, clinical judgement.

Beliefs (catastrophising)
"My back is damaged and fragile" · "Pain means harm" · "I'll never get better" · "Only surgery/injection will fix me." These beliefs are the strongest predictors of disability. Address with validating language: "The pain is real, but pain does not always indicate ongoing tissue damage."
Emotions
Distinguish psychological distress from a diagnosis of depression or anxiety; use PHQ-9/GAD-7 when depression or anxiety is clinically suspected, and treat it actively. Fear-avoidance behaviour: fear of movement (kinesiophobia) → reduced activity → deconditioning → more pain. Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia.
Behaviours
Prolonged rest (bed rest is harmful and counterproductive — NICE NG59 advises encouraging people to continue normal activities; movement is medicine). Passive coping (waiting for someone else to fix the pain). Repetitive healthcare seeking without self-management engagement. Medication over-reliance.
Work and social context
Work dissatisfaction → poor outcomes. Manual or heavy labour with no modified duties available → barrier to return to work. Low socioeconomic status. Social isolation. Medicolegal involvement (compensation claim) → prolongs disability even with treatment.
STarT Back Tool
9-question tool (0–9 score): Low risk (0–3) → reassurance + advice + remain active. Medium risk (4–8 + physical element) → physiotherapy. High risk (4–8 + psychological element) → physiotherapy with closer follow-up, addressing fear-avoidance and work barriers; consider a combined physical and psychological programme where there are significant psychosocial obstacles or previous treatment has not worked (NICE NG59). Available free at keele.ac.uk/startback.
The STarT Back Tool (Keele University) is the risk-stratification tool named as an example in NICE NG59 for stratified care in non-specific LBP. It identifies patients at high risk of chronicity due to psychosocial factors (the high-risk group), who warrant closer follow-up and active attention to catastrophising, fear-avoidance and work barriers. The large RCT by Hill et al. (2011, Lancet) demonstrated that STarT Back-stratified care produced better outcomes and was more cost-effective than undifferentiated usual care. NICE NG59 continues to recommend psychological therapy using a CBT approach as part of a treatment package including exercise, and a combined physical and psychological programme for persistent pain with significant psychosocial obstacles or when previous treatment has not worked — keep exercise, self-management and return-to-work central (ACT/CBT under NG193 applies to chronic primary pain). The tool takes 2 minutes to complete and is available online. NICE NG59 suggests considering it at first contact for each new episode.
4
Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Neurological examination
Essential when there are neurological symptoms, sciatica, progressive pain or red flags (not needed at every review for stable uncomplicated mechanical pain): power (hip flexion L2/3, knee extension L3/4, great toe extension L4/5, plantar flexion S1), sensation (dermatomes L3–S1), reflexes (knee jerk L3/4, ankle jerk S1). Also gait, SLR if sciatica suspected, and bladder/bowel/saddle symptoms when relevant. Document for deterioration tracking. New objective or progressive motor deficit → urgent assessment.
Straight leg raise (SLR)
Where sciatica is suspected: patient supine, raise straight leg — reproduction of radiating pain below the knee supports nerve-root irritation (L4/L5/S1) but is not diagnostic alone. Crossed SLR may be more specific but is not required at every consultation.
Spinal movement
Schober testing is not a routine primary-care test for axial spondyloarthritis — if suspected, use an inflammatory back-pain history and refer per the local rheumatology pathway. Assess flexion, extension and lateral flexion. Facet joint loading (pain on extension) = facet arthropathy.
Investigations (NICE NG59)
Do not routinely image in primary care; specialist imaging only when the result is likely to change management. No routine X-ray for night pain without a specific suspicion. MRI preferred for suspected infection, malignancy, MSCC, CES or significant neurological deficit (via emergency/specialist pathway); persistent radiculopathy → specialist/MSK interface arranges imaging (no universal 6-week MRI rule). X-ray may show compression fracture. CRP + ESR supportive (infection, inflammatory). Myeloma tests (FBC, calcium, renal function, ESR/CRP, Serum protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains) when clinically suspected. PSA only when guided by symptoms and prostate cancer risk, with shared decision-making.
PHQ-9 + GAD-7
Use when depression or anxiety is clinically suspected — not mandated annually for every patient. Distinguish psychological distress from a diagnosis of depression or anxiety. Treat diagnosed depression/anxiety actively in its own right.
Axial spondyloarthritis is identified mainly from an inflammatory back-pain history (onset before 45, >3 months, night waking, buttock pain, improvement with movement or NSAIDs, extra-spinal features) — Schober testing, CRP and HLA-B27 cannot exclude it, so refer using NICE NG65 criteria. It affects young adults of both sexes, and causes irreversible spinal fusion if untreated — but often responds to NSAIDs and specialist biologic therapy. Diagnostic delay is common and preventable.
5
Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / A&E same-day
Immediate emergency assessment: suspected CES (any of the features — not all need be present); progressive significant neurological deficit; suspected MSCC (contact MSCC coordinator immediately; whole-spine MRI within 24 h); spinal infection with systemic illness or neurological signs; suspected epidural haematoma (anticoagulated + new neurological deficit); unstable trauma or fracture.
Specialist spinal / MSK assessment
Most uncomplicated sciatica is managed conservatively first. Refer if progressive or significant motor deficit; severe persistent pain or functional limitation despite appropriate treatment; symptoms persist and surgery may be considered; or imaging is likely to alter management (usually arranged by the specialist/MSK interface) · Severe neurogenic claudication (spinal stenosis) · Urgent: progressive deficit, CES, suspected infection or malignancy · Known cancer + new back pain → low threshold to contact the MSCC pathway
Physiotherapy (primary care)
All non-specific CLBP (NICE NG59: offer exercise as first-line). STarT Back Low risk: advice + leaflet + encourage activity. Medium risk: physiotherapy. High risk: consider a combined physical and psychological programme where there are significant psychosocial obstacles or previous treatment has not worked (NICE NG59).
Rheumatology
Suspected axial spondyloarthritis per NICE NG65 referral criteria (onset before 45, >3 months, plus inflammatory features) — a negative HLA-B27 or normal CRP does not exclude · RA with spinal involvement · Psoriatic arthritis + axial disease
Pain clinic / MSK specialist
Individualised (no fixed 12-week rule) — based on diagnosis, functional impairment, failure of appropriate conservative management, medication complexity/dependence, suitability for a multidisciplinary pain programme and local MSK pathways. Radiofrequency denervation only in carefully selected patients (specialist). Spinal cord stimulation (NICE TA159, 2008: an option for chronic neuropathic pain, including failed back surgery syndrome, persisting at least 6 months despite conventional management, after a successful trial). Combined physical and psychological / multidisciplinary pain programme where indicated (NICE NG59).
Occupational health
Prolonged or at-risk work absence. Workplace assessment and reasonable adjustments. Phased return to work. Fit note guidance: GP should specify modifications not just "unfit for work" — per DWP fit note guidance.
Malignant cord compression (MSCC) from vertebral metastases is a spinal emergency that requires urgent MRI of the whole spine. NICE NG234 (Spinal metastases and MSCC, 2023) asks for MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 hours for suspected MSCC, and 16 mg dexamethasone promptly for people with neurological symptoms or signs of MSCC, continued while awaiting surgery or radiotherapy — in liaison with the MSCC service, not GP-initiated prolonged dexamethasone; no routine corticosteroids for suspected spinal metastases without neurological signs unless severe pain or haematological malignancy; spinal pain suggestive of metastases without suspected MSCC still needs MRI within 1 week via the acute oncology/MSCC service. GPs should have a low threshold for suspecting MSCC in any back pain patient with a history of cancer — the primary cancer may have been diagnosed years previously. Common primaries: prostate (sclerotic lesions), breast, lung, kidney, thyroid, myeloma. The outcome of MSCC depends critically on the patient's neurological status at the time of decompression — so act before deficits progress.

Treat

Pharmacological Treatment Ladder

Step 1Topical NSAID (clinical practice, not a NICE step) — evidence is stronger for localised superficial conditions and benefit for deep spinal pain is uncertain; if offered, explain this and assess NSAID contraindications. NICE NG59 itself recommends considering an oral NSAID (step 2) and advises against offering paracetamol alone for low back pain.
Step 2Oral NSAID (short trial if safe) — e.g. naproxen 500 mg BD may suit some adults (not a universal regimen). NICE NG59: lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time, with gastroprotection where appropriate. Assess age/frailty, renal function, hypertension/CV disease, heart failure, peptic ulcer/GI bleeding, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, pregnancy, concurrent corticosteroids or SSRIs. Paracetamol may sometimes be an adjunct, but benefit should not be assumed.
Step 3Amitriptyline 10–30 mg nocte (low-dose) — not for the back pain itself: NICE NG59 advises against SSRIs, SNRIs and tricyclics for low back pain. Consider only for a separate indication (e.g. chronic primary pain under NICE NG193 after shared decision-making, or a clearly neuropathic condition under CG173) — do not blur these with non-specific mechanical or radicular pain. If used: start at 10 mg, titrate to 30 mg; review at 6 weeks.
Step 4Duloxetine 30–60 mg OD — SNRI; not for the back pain itself (NICE NG59 advises against SSRIs, SNRIs and TCAs for low back pain). Use only for co-existing depression or anxiety, treated in its own right, or a neuropathic component (NICE CG173). Review at 4 weeks for efficacy and tolerability. Maximum 120 mg OD.
Step 5Gabapentin / pregabalin — not recommended for low back pain or sciatica (NICE NG59, 2020 update). If a patient is already taking gabapentin or pregabalin for this indication, plan an individualised withdrawal (particularly after long-term or high-dose use) following dependence/withdrawal guidance — no fixed schedule. NICE NG59: do not offer gabapentinoids or other antiepileptics for low back pain or sciatica. Both are Class C, Schedule 3 controlled drugs (since April 2019). Addiction potential — review regularly and do not prescribe indefinitely without specialist review.

NICE NG59: Do NOT use opioids for non-specific chronic low back pain. Weak opioids (codeine, tramadol) have no evidence of long-term benefit and significant harms (dependence, cognitive impairment, falls, hyperalgesia). If already prescribed, agree an individualised tapering plan — assess dependence, withdrawal, mental health, substance use and patient preference; seek specialist pain/addiction advice for complex cases. Strong opioids are not appropriate for CLBP.

The NICE NG59 recommendation against opioids for non-specific chronic low back pain is based on robust evidence that opioids produce minimal long-term pain reduction while causing significant harms: dependence, opioid-induced hyperalgesia (opioids paradoxically increase pain sensitivity with chronic use), constipation, cognitive impairment, falls (especially in elderly), sexual dysfunction (opioid-induced androgen deficiency), and overdose risk. Many patients on long-term opioids would benefit from an individualised tapering plan combined with exercise-based physiotherapy and support. Opioid tapering for CLBP is an active RCGP GP guidance priority and is specifically tested in SCA examination scenarios. A key phrase for the SCA: "I can help you come off these medications — they may actually be making your pain worse in the long run."
7
Treat

Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Exercise (NICE NG59 — FIRST LINE)
Any form of exercise is beneficial — there is no evidence that one type is superior. Aerobic exercise (walking, swimming, cycling), strengthening (core stabilisation, resistance training), yoga, Pilates, tai chi. Choose sustainable activity that is acceptable and achievable; progress gradually and focus on function rather than pain elimination. Consider physiotherapy for a tailored programme.
Physiotherapy
Manual therapy (manipulation, mobilisation, soft tissue) — consider only as part of a treatment package including exercise (NICE NG59), not standalone. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) — NICE NG59 advises not to offer it for low back pain, although it is used widely. Heat therapy — temporary relief, adjunct only.
Psychological interventions
NICE NG59: consider psychological therapy using a CBT approach as part of a treatment package including exercise, and a combined physical and psychological programme for persistent pain with significant psychosocial obstacles or when previous treatment has not worked. NHS Talking Therapies for co-existing depression or anxiety. ACT or pain-focused CBT remains an NG193 option where the pain meets criteria for chronic primary pain. Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR). Pain management programme (multidisciplinary — pain clinic).
Acupuncture
NICE NG59: do not offer acupuncture for low back pain. However, NICE NG193 allows consideration of a limited course of acupuncture or dry needling for chronic primary pain under specified conditions — explain the distinction to patients who request it.
Spinal injections
Epidural steroid injection (ESI): NICE NG59 — do not offer spinal injections for low back pain; consider epidural local anaesthetic and steroid only in acute and severe sciatica. Evidence: short-term benefit for radiculopathy only (not non-specific pain). Do not use epidural injections for neurogenic claudication due to central canal stenosis. Facet joint injections are not offered for low back pain. Radiofrequency denervation: consider only in carefully selected patients after a positive diagnostic medial branch block (NICE NG59) — specialist decision.
Exercise is a core NICE NG59 treatment for chronic low back pain. The type of exercise is less important than adherence and consistency — patients should be encouraged to choose an activity they enjoy (swimming, walking, yoga, tai chi) rather than a specific "back exercise" programme, because enjoyment predicts adherence and adherence predicts outcome. The neurobiological mechanisms by which exercise reduces chronic pain include: endogenous opioid release, central pain inhibition (reduced central sensitisation), anti-inflammatory cytokine production, improved sleep, and mood enhancement via serotonin/dopamine. The GP's most important CLBP communication task is to explain that movement does not damage the back — the spine is a strong structure designed for movement, and, in validating terms, the pain is real but does not always indicate ongoing tissue damage.
8
Lifestyle

Active Self-Management — The Core Message

Movement is medicine Key message, in validating language: "The pain is real, but pain does not always mean ongoing damage — movement is safe and helps." Choose sustainable activity that is acceptable and achievable, progress gradually, avoid prolonged bed rest, and focus on function rather than pain elimination.
Posture and ergonomics No single "correct" posture prevents CLBP — varying position and regular movement breaks matter more than any specific posture. Offer practical lifting, desk and driving advice as needed rather than fixed rules.
Sleep quality Poor sleep worsens pain perception (increases central sensitisation) — address sleep as part of self-management. Practical sleep-position advice as preferred (e.g. pillow between knees for side sleepers). Treat insomnia actively (CBT-I, sleep restriction therapy via NHS Talking Therapies).
Weight management General health advice — offer weight management support (exercise + dietary support) where appropriate. Tier 3 if BMI >35.
Smoking cessation General health advice — smoking cessation benefits overall health (no guaranteed reduction in back pain). Stop Smoking Service + pharmacotherapy (varenicline most effective).
Work and activity Staying at work or returning to usual roles where feasible is an important outcome in CLBP. Fit note: specify modifications ("can do light duties / avoid heavy lifting / standing breaks") rather than total incapacity where possible. Occupational health referral early.
Pacing and flare management Teach activity pacing: do activities in small chunks with scheduled rest — avoids boom-and-bust cycle (overdoing → crash → bed rest → deconditioning). During flares: short course NSAIDs + continue gentle movement + avoid prolonged bed rest. Written flare management plan.
Back pain resources NHS Pain Toolkit (paintoolkit.org): free self-management guide. Arthritis UK (arthritis-uk.org): back pain information. MIND back pain and mental health resources. Prescription for an online pain management programme (PMP): Pathway Through Pain (NHS digital). Refer to social prescriber for community activity.
The "boom-and-bust" cycle is one of the most important concepts in chronic pain self-management — patients with CLBP frequently overdo activities on "good days" (boom), experience a pain flare from overexertion (bust), then rest for days (which deconditions them further). This cycle gradually escalates disability. Pacing (doing a set quota of activity regardless of pain level — not stopping when pain increases, not overdoing when pain is low) breaks this cycle. The concept comes from operant pain management theory (Fordyce, 1976) and is the foundation of all evidence-based pain management programmes. Teaching pacing explicitly in a GP consultation is one of the highest-impact behavioural interventions available in primary care for CLBP. The pain toolkit (paintoolkit.org) is a free resource developed by Pete Moore (a chronic pain patient) that encapsulates all these principles in patient-friendly language.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Early review (individualised)
STarT Back stratification completed? Physiotherapy commenced and engaged? Medication reviewed — escalate or rationalise. Depression/anxiety treated? If opioids prescribed → review tapering plan. New neurological or systemic symptoms? Examine neurology if symptoms are present.
Later review
Exercise programme sustained? Functional improvement (optionally a patient-reported outcome measure, e.g. Roland Morris or Oswestry). Consider pain service referral based on functional impairment, failure of appropriate conservative management, medication complexity/dependence and suitability for a programme — not a fixed 12-week rule. Opioid tapering progress?
Ongoing review
Individualised by functional impact, treatment response, medication risks, work status, new neurological or systemic symptoms and patient goals. Assess function and activity, work participation, medication benefit and adverse effects, exercise adherence, mood/distress when clinically indicated, and new red flags. Consider stepping down medications if pain stable.
Fit note review
Review fit note at every issuing — avoid open-ended fit notes for CLBP. Specify modifications where possible. Describe functional limitations and workplace adjustments — do not promise a phased return by a particular time. Consider occupational health for prolonged absence (the national Fit for Work service closed in 2018).
Opioid review
Patients on long-term opioids for CLBP: review regularly. Assess efficacy (functional improvement — not just pain relief), side effects, dependence signs. Agree an individualised taper (no universal rate), assessing dependence, withdrawal, mental health, substance use and patient preference. Seek specialist pain/addiction advice for complex cases; buprenorphine patches are not a routine primary-care bridge.
Emergency safety-net
New urinary retention, altered urinary sensation, difficulty starting to pass urine, new incontinence, saddle numbness or bilateral leg symptoms → immediate emergency assessment (CES). Sudden severe back pain in known osteoporosis/cancer (vertebral fracture/cord compression). New rapidly progressive leg weakness.
Same-day GP
New or worsening neurological signs (leg weakness, new foot drop, spreading paraesthesia), new fever + back pain (vertebral osteomyelitis), sudden intractable pain in patient with known cancer
The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) is the gold standard patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) for low back pain — it is a 10-item questionnaire covering personal care, lifting, walking, sitting, standing, sleeping, social life, travelling, and pain intensity. Scores: 0–20% = minimal disability; 21–40% = moderate; 41–60% = severe; 61–80% = crippled; 81–100% = bedbound. Where useful, documenting ODI at baseline and at review provides an objective functional measure to guide escalation decisions, evidence treatment response, and support referral justification. Significant improvement is defined as ≥10-point reduction. For the SCA examination, being able to name a validated functional outcome measure for back pain and explain its clinical application demonstrates clinical knowledge at examiner-quality level. The Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (24-item) is an alternative PROM that is simpler to complete.
