Safety

Red Flags — Pituitary Macroadenoma & Visual Emergency

Elevated prolactin is common — but significant hyperprolactinaemia requires exclusion of a pituitary tumour. A new visual-field defect with raised prolactin = same-day specialist/emergency assessment.

Prolactin significantly elevated + visual field defect (bitemporal hemianopia) + headache Pituitary macroadenoma (>10 mm) compressing optic chiasm — visual emergency. Same-day specialist/emergency assessment with urgent MRI and formal visual fields. If prolactinoma is confirmed, cabergoline is specialist-initiated. Neurosurgery if non-prolactinoma macroadenoma causing compression.
Prolactin very high (>5000 mIU/L) + no drug cause identified + amenorrhoea Strongly suggestive of prolactinoma, but not by itself an absolute MRI threshold — clinical context and assay issues still matter. Urgent endocrinology referral ± pituitary MRI.
Patient on antipsychotics + prolactin >2000 + galactorrhoea + sexual dysfunction Antipsychotic-induced hyperprolactinaemia — dopamine receptor blockade. Risk of osteoporosis from long-term hypogonadism. >2000 mIU/L is a local (Greater Manchester shared-care) trigger for specialist discussion, not a universal threshold. Do not stop the antipsychotic without the mental-health team; aripiprazole switch or augmentation is a psychiatric decision; dopamine agonists can worsen psychosis or mania. Endocrinology co-management if needed.
Sudden severe headache (thunderclap) + visual disturbance + nausea in patient with known pituitary adenoma Pituitary apoplexy — haemorrhage or infarction into the pituitary tumour. Emergency assessment via 999/A&E; urgent endocrine and neurosurgical discussion; urgent MRI (or dedicated CT if MRI unavailable/contraindicated). If adrenal insufficiency is suspected or haemodynamically unstable, give hydrocortisone 100 mg parenterally per the emergency/adrenal-crisis protocol — do not delay transfer.
Raised prolactin + chest wall stimulation / nipple piercings + no other cause Physiological neurogenic hyperprolactinaemia — confirm history carefully. Exclude all pharmacological and structural causes before accepting neurogenic aetiology. Repeat prolactin under rested conditions after avoiding breast/nipple stimulation, using the interval advised by the local laboratory/endocrine pathway.
Nipple discharge, retraction or other change of concern in ONE nipple only, age 50 or over Galactorrhoea from hyperprolactinaemia is usually bilateral and milky, but this criterion is independent of the prolactin result. This is the breast cancer route → Suspected cancer · NICE NG12 suspected cancer pathway referral (rec 1.4.1, updated April 2026). Under 50: use clinical judgement and the local breast pathway.
Prolactin remaining elevated after an appropriate medication review Possible underlying pituitary pathology. Seek endocrine advice ± pituitary MRI — persistent elevation should not automatically be attributed to the drug.
Pituitary apoplexy is one of the most dramatic emergencies in endocrinology — it causes sudden severe headache (thunderclap onset, maximal at onset), often with diplopia (CN III, IV, VI — passing through the cavernous sinus immediately lateral to the pituitary), visual field defects, and profound adrenal insufficiency (the posterior pituitary is usually spared, but anterior pituitary destruction causes acute cortisol deficiency). The headache pattern can mimic subarachnoid haemorrhage. The key GP action is 999/A&E without delay; give hydrocortisone 100 mg parenterally per the emergency protocol if adrenal insufficiency is suspected or the patient is unstable — do not delay transfer. Any patient with a known pituitary adenoma who develops acute severe headache with or without visual symptoms should be treated as pituitary apoplexy until proved otherwise. The importance of prolactin sample conditions: prolactin is a stress-hormone that rises in response to venepuncture, exercise, sexual activity and breast/nipple stimulation; fasting is not routinely required. A single elevated prolactin result in a patient who was anxious, had a difficult venepuncture, or had a breast examination beforehand may be a false positive. Measure prolactin according to the local laboratory protocol: the patient should be rested and the sample is preferably taken at least 2 hours after waking. A single result above the reference range may establish hyperprolactinaemia, but a mildly elevated or unexpected result should be repeated under rested conditions, ideally via an indwelling cannula if venepuncture stress is suspected. Many apparent hyperprolactinaemia cases are artefactual. On NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): hyperprolactinaemia itself has no suspected-cancer criterion, and none should be invented — prolactinomas are benign, and the urgent routes here are visual and endocrine. The one genuine cancer adjacency is in the galactorrhoea assessment: bilateral milky discharge fits prolactin, but discharge, retraction or other change of concern in one nipple only in someone aged 50 or over meets the NG12 breast criterion for a suspected cancer pathway referral. A non-prolactinoma sellar mass found on imaging is managed as a brain tumour by neurosurgery and endocrinology, not on a primary care cancer route.

Causes of Hyperprolactinaemia — Classification

Physiological (normal)
Pregnancy (physiological rise) · Breastfeeding/lactation · Stress (venepuncture, physical examination) · Sleep (nocturnal prolactin surge) · Sexual intercourse · Nipple stimulation · Newborn period. Repeat a mildly raised or unexpected result under rested conditions (fasting not routinely required; preferably ≥2 h after waking).
Pharmacological (most common pathological cause)
Dopamine antagonists: antipsychotics (haloperidol, risperidone, amisulpride, olanzapine — all raise prolactin; aripiprazole rarely does), metoclopramide, domperidone, prochlorperazine, chlorpromazine · Antidepressants (SSRIs, TCAs — modest elevation) · Antihypertensives: methyldopa, verapamil · H2 blockers: cimetidine · Opioids (chronic use) · Oestrogens (OCP — mild elevation) · Recreational drugs. Prolactin level does not reliably predict symptom severity.
Pituitary causes
Prolactinoma (microprolactinoma <10 mm — most common pituitary tumour, macroprolactinoma >10 mm) · Stalk compression by any sellar/parasellar mass (craniopharyngioma, non-functioning adenoma, meningioma) — causes "disconnection hyperprolactinaemia" (removes dopamine inhibition) · Hook effect: a pituitary or parasellar mass with an unexpectedly modest prolactin — ask the laboratory to repeat with dilution
Systemic disease
Primary hypothyroidism (TRH stimulates prolactin release — ALWAYS check TSH first) · Chronic kidney disease (reduced renal clearance of prolactin — can raise prolactin) · Cirrhosis (reduced hepatic clearance) · PCOS (mild elevation — often co-exists) · Chest wall injury/surgery/herpes zoster (neurogenic — same reflex as nipple stimulation)
Macroprolactinaemia
Prolactin forms large immunoglobulin complexes (macroprolactin) that are biologically inactive but are measured by standard assays → falsely elevated total prolactin. A common cause of apparent hyperprolactinaemia. Request macroprolactin analysis, usually by polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation, particularly where prolactin is raised but symptoms are absent or disproportionate. Interpret using the reporting laboratory's criteria — the clinically relevant result is the post-PEG monomeric prolactin, not a universal percentage cut-off. Symptoms or persistently significant monomeric prolactin need further assessment.
The hypothyroidism-prolactin connection is one of the most commonly missed causes of apparent hyperprolactinaemia — TSH stimulates both TSH and prolactin release (via TRH — thyrotropin-releasing hormone), so primary hypothyroidism can cause clinically significant hyperprolactinaemia. Treatment of the hypothyroidism with levothyroxine normalises the prolactin completely. This is why TSH must always be checked before investigating hyperprolactinaemia further. Macroprolactinaemia is the most important cause of false-positive hyperprolactinaemia — it is a common finding in patients referred for hyperprolactinaemia investigation and is caused by immunoglobulin binding to prolactin molecules, creating large biologically inactive complexes that are measured by immunoassay but cannot bind to prolactin receptors in tissues (therefore no clinical effect). The macroprolactin complex is too large to be filtered by the kidney and accumulates in the serum. A patient with macroprolactinaemia has a high measured prolactin but no symptoms (no amenorrhoea, no galactorrhoea, no infertility, no headache). The PEG precipitation test identifies macroprolactin — it should be requested particularly where prolactin is raised but symptoms are absent or disproportionate, and interpreted by the laboratory's criteria (post-PEG monomeric prolactin).

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Symptoms of hyperprolactinaemia: amenorrhoea/oligomenorrhoea, galactorrhoea (spontaneous or expressed milk from non-puerperal women/men), loss of libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, headache, visual disturbance · Drug history (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, domperidone, SSRIs, methyldopa, cimetidine, opioids) · Pregnancy/breastfeeding status · Menstrual history · Recreational drugs · Renal, liver or thyroid disease · Diplopia · Stress around blood test? Recent exercise, sexual activity or breast stimulation?
Examination
Visual fields (confrontation is a screening test — arrange formal perimetry where indicated) · Neurological status · Breast discharge only if clinically appropriate — avoid repeated stimulation (bilateral non-bloody = prolactin picture; unilateral/bloody → consider breast pathology; use the local breast pathway) · Signs of pituitary hormone excess/deficiency · Signs of hypothyroidism (cold, dry skin, bradycardia, delayed reflexes) · Signs of acromegaly (if co-secreting adenoma) · BMI · Hirsutism/acne (PCOS)
Investigations — first line
Prolactin (per local lab protocol; repeat if mildly raised, unexpected or stressful sample; interpret against the lab's sex-specific range and units) · TSH ± free T4 (primary hypothyroidism) · HCG (exclude pregnancy where relevant) · Renal + liver function · Macroprolactin (PEG) (if asymptomatic or result disproportionate) · Pituitary function (if a pituitary lesion is suspected) · Assay dilution (large lesion with unexpectedly modest prolactin — hook effect)
MRI pituitary indications
Endocrine advice ± MRI pituitary — for: unexplained persistent hyperprolactinaemia after excluding pregnancy, hypothyroidism, renal/liver disease, medication and macroprolactin; any raised prolactin with visual or cranial nerve symptoms, significant/progressive headache, hypopituitarism or hypogonadism; markedly elevated prolactin (particularly >2000–2500 mIU/L) unless a convincing medication cause exists and local endocrine advice supports observation; prolactin still raised after appropriate medication review; a pituitary/parasellar mass with only mildly raised prolactin (request dilution). Thresholds vary with assay and local pathway — local endocrinology pathways take precedence.
The prolactin cannula technique is the gold standard for accurate prolactin measurement — a sample taken immediately after the stress of venepuncture will contain stress-released prolactin in addition to the baseline. The correct protocol: (1) insert cannula at the start of the consultation; (2) allow patient to sit quietly and rest quietly; (3) draw blood at the end of the rest period through the cannula without any new needle insertion. This eliminates the venepuncture stress response. A cannula is not needed routinely and fasting is not required, but the patient should be rested and the sample preferably taken at least 2 hours after waking; use a cannula when venepuncture stress is suspected. Request macroprolactin analysis where prolactin is raised but symptoms are absent or disproportionate. Interpret results against the laboratory's sex-specific reference range and units — do not use universal cut-offs without confirming the local assay. A prolactin above approximately 5000 mIU/L is strongly suggestive of a prolactinoma, but a symptomatic patient may need imaging at a much lower level; MRI thresholds follow the local endocrine/radiology pathway.

Prolactinoma Classification & Differential

Microprolactinoma (<10 mm)
Most common. Menstrual disturbance, galactorrhoea, infertility. No mass effect (no visual field defect, no headache from compression — though headache can occur). Treatment: specialist-led, usually cabergoline. Rarely requires surgery.
Macroprolactinoma (>10 mm)
Larger tumour — causes mass effect (visual field defect, headache, CN III/IV/VI palsy from cavernous sinus extension). Prolactin usually markedly raised (above approximately 5000 mIU/L is strongly suggestive), but interpret with the local assay. Treatment: specialist-led; cabergoline first-line (often shrinks the tumour). Surgery only if: resistant to or intolerant of dopamine agonist, or emergency (apoplexy with vision threatening).
Stalk/disconnection hyperprolactinaemia
Any sellar or suprasellar mass (non-functioning pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma, Rathke's cleft cyst, meningioma) compresses the pituitary stalk → removes dopamine (PIH) inhibition → prolactin rises. Prolactin often in the approximate range 400–2000 mIU/L, but this is not reliable enough to distinguish lesions in every patient — MRI and specialist assessment distinguish; request dilution if prolactin is unexpectedly modest (hook effect). DO NOT treat with dopamine agonist (the mass is not a prolactinoma — cabergoline will not shrink it). Surgical removal if mass effect.
Drug-induced differentiation
Onset correlates with drug start. If a medicine can be safely withheld, repeat prolactin after an interval appropriate to the drug and clinical situation, agreed with the relevant specialist or local pathway. Persistent elevation despite a convincing drug cause should not automatically be attributed to the drug — seek endocrine advice ± MRI. Prolactin level does not reliably predict symptom severity.
The prolactin level at which macroprolactinoma vs stalk compression can be distinguished is clinically important — a prolactin result above approximately 5000 mIU/L is strongly suggestive of a prolactinoma (the pituitary cells secreting prolactin, not just compression releasing the inhibition on normal cells). Stalk compression by non-prolactin-secreting tumours may produce prolactin in the approximate range 400–2000 mIU/L, but this does not reliably distinguish lesions in every patient. This matters for treatment: prolactinomas respond to cabergoline (which directly suppresses prolactin secretion from the tumour and causes tumour shrinkage); non-functioning adenomas with stalk compression do not respond to cabergoline and require surgical decompression. Getting the diagnosis right prevents both under-treatment (missing a cabergoline-responsive prolactinoma) and inappropriate treatment (giving cabergoline to a patient with a non-functioning adenoma requiring surgery).

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day
Pituitary apoplexy (sudden severe headache + visual disturbance + known pituitary adenoma) or sudden severe headache with visual loss, ophthalmoplegia, vomiting, collapse or altered consciousness → 999/A&E, urgent endocrine and neurosurgical discussion; hydrocortisone 100 mg parenterally per emergency protocol if adrenal insufficiency suspected or unstable — do not delay transfer · New visual-field defect with suspected pituitary disease → same-day specialist/emergency assessment
Endocrinology (urgent within 2 weeks)
Unexplained persistent elevation · Significant symptoms or hypogonadism · Markedly raised prolactin · Prolactin >2000 mIU/L in an antipsychotic-treated patient, particularly if symptomatic or rising (local pathway trigger) · Pregnancy with a macroprolactinoma or tumour near the chiasm · Raised prolactin persisting despite an appropriate medication review · Macroprolactinoma on MRI
Endocrinology (routine)
Confirmed microprolactinoma on MRI — management discussion (cabergoline vs expectant) · Drug-induced hyperprolactinaemia causing significant symptoms (amenorrhoea, hypogonadism, galactorrhoea) in patient where drug cannot be changed · Complex macroprolactin results or persistent monomeric hyperprolactinaemia
Suspected cancer · NICE NG12
Aged 50 and over with discharge, retraction or other changes of concern in one nipple only → suspected breast cancer pathway referral. Hyperprolactinaemia itself has no NG12 criterion; don't send a raised prolactin down a cancer route.
Psychiatry (for antipsychotic-related)
Significant antipsychotic-induced hyperprolactinaemia (amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea, sexual dysfunction; >2000 mIU/L is a Greater Manchester shared-care trigger, not a universal threshold) → discuss with the psychiatric team; aripiprazole switch or augmentation, or quetiapine, is a psychiatric decision. Dopamine agonists can worsen psychosis or mania. Do NOT adjust antipsychotics without psychiatric team agreement.
GP management
Macroprolactinaemia where the patient is asymptomatic, monomeric prolactin is normal, there are no visual/neurological/pituitary-function abnormalities and the laboratory interpretation is clear: reassure, no treatment or MRI; reassess if symptoms develop · Drug-induced (mild, asymptomatic, known cause): reassure, advise drug review with prescriber, recheck after an agreed interval if the drug is safely withheld
The antipsychotic switching dilemma for hyperprolactinaemia deserves specific attention — antipsychotic-induced hyperprolactinaemia is common, and the long-term consequences of sustained hyperprolactinaemia from antipsychotics include: osteoporosis (from hypogonadism), sexual dysfunction, infertility, and possible breast cancer risk (oestrogen-dependent; prolactin has growth-promoting effects on breast tissue). The evidence base: amisulpride and risperidone are the most potent prolactin-raising antipsychotics; aripiprazole is a partial D2 agonist that has negligible prolactin-raising effect and can even reduce prolactin when added to other antipsychotics (combination strategy without compromising antipsychotic efficacy). Quetiapine also has low prolactin-raising potential. GPs who identify patients on antipsychotics with symptomatic hyperprolactinaemia (amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea, sexual dysfunction, bone density concerns) should raise this with the prescribing psychiatrist — switching to aripiprazole or quetiapine is a clinically meaningful quality-of-life intervention that requires psychiatric team decision-making.

Drug-Induced & Prolactinoma Management

Drug-induced hyperprolactinaemia — management
(1) Where clinically safe, discuss stopping, reducing or switching the suspected medicine with the prescriber; do not stop antipsychotic treatment without agreement from the mental-health team. (2) If it can be safely withheld, repeat prolactin after an interval appropriate to the drug and clinical situation, agreed with the relevant specialist or local pathway. (3) If prolactin remains raised → endocrine advice ± MRI. (4) If the drug cannot be stopped, symptoms are significant, or prolactin is markedly raised: seek endocrine and/or psychiatric advice rather than treating empirically with a dopamine agonist. (5) For antipsychotics: aripiprazole switch or augmentation is a psychiatric decision; dopamine agonists can worsen psychosis or mania.
Microprolactinoma — cabergoline (first-line)
Specialist-initiated and titrated. Cabergoline 0.5 mg weekly (once weekly or as two divided doses) → increase by 0.5 mg a week at approximately monthly intervals, according to prolactin and tolerability, to achieve prolactin normalisation (usual dose 1 mg weekly, range 0.25–2 mg weekly; doses above 1 mg weekly given in divided doses — BNF/SmPC). Higher doses need specialist supervision. SmPC study data: prolactin normalised in about 84%, regular cycles restored in about 83%, ovulation in about 89%, galactorrhoea resolved in about 90% — not guaranteed outcomes. Baseline echocardiogram and assessment for cardiac or other fibrosis before long-term treatment. Adverse effects: nausea, dizziness, headache, postural hypotension; somnolence and rare sudden sleep onset (do not drive or operate machinery if affected); impulse-control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive spending, binge eating); psychiatric effects, particularly with a history of psychosis or mania; fibrotic disorders and valvulopathy. Interactions: dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, domperidone, antipsychotics).
Microprolactinoma — bromocriptine (second-line)
Bromocriptine 1.25 mg OD increasing to 2.5–5 mg BD. Less well tolerated than cabergoline (nausea, postural hypotension, nasal stuffiness). Longer safety record in pregnancy; dopamine agonist use around conception and pregnancy should be agreed with endocrinology.
Macroprolactinoma — cabergoline (first-line)
Specialist-led: start at low dose and titrate under endocrinology. MRI and formal visual-field follow-up individualised by endocrinology (tumour size, proximity to the chiasm, response, symptoms). Surgery reserved for: cabergoline-intolerant, cabergoline-resistant, or emergency (apoplexy).
Cabergoline vs bromocriptine — the evidence clearly favours cabergoline for prolactinoma management: cabergoline is more effective than bromocriptine at normalising prolactin and restoring ovulation, and has far better tolerability (once or twice weekly dosing vs BD/TDS for bromocriptine; much lower rate of nausea, postural hypotension, and gastrointestinal side effects). The cardiac valvulopathy risk from cabergoline is principally established at the much higher doses used for Parkinson's disease — at the lower doses used for prolactinoma (typically 0.5–2 mg/week), the risk is low but not zero, so a baseline echocardiogram is followed by monitoring per the SfE/BSE/BHVS position statement (repeat at 5 years if ≤2 mg/week; annually if >2 mg/week) or the more intensive SmPC schedule, according to the local endocrine/cardiology pathway. Bromocriptine remains the preferred choice in women planning pregnancy because it has a much longer safety record in early pregnancy (established since the 1970s) — cabergoline is usually stopped when pregnancy is confirmed (in microprolactinoma patients) and restarted postpartum if required. The decision to stop or continue dopamine agonist during pregnancy in macroprolactinoma requires specialist input.

Management in Special Situations

Pregnancy with prolactinoma
Pre-conception management with endocrinology, especially for a macroprolactinoma or tumour close to the optic chiasm. Do not routinely measure prolactin in pregnancy (physiological rise makes it unreliable). Small intrasellar microprolactinoma: dopamine agonist usually stopped once pregnancy is confirmed, with review if symptoms develop. Macroprolactinoma: management depends on tumour size, previous treatment and distance from the chiasm — specialist follow-up required. Symptomatic tumour enlargement: restart dopamine agonist under specialist advice; MRI without gadolinium when clinically indicated; visual assessment symptom-led. The SmPC advises stopping cabergoline and avoiding conception for one month after stopping, whereas specialist guidance commonly continues until pregnancy is confirmed — follow endocrine advice/local policy. Breastfeeding is often possible, but dopamine agonists suppress lactation and are generally incompatible with breastfeeding. Reassess prolactin and tumour status after pregnancy/breastfeeding before restarting long-term treatment.
Infertility due to hyperprolactinaemia
Normalise prolactin first → ovulation typically restores spontaneously once prolactin normal. Add ovulation induction (clomifene) only if cycles do not resume despite normal prolactin, with endocrine/fertility advice. Stop dopamine agonist when pregnancy confirmed (if microadenoma). NICE: measure prolactin in infertility when there is an ovulatory disorder, galactorrhoea or a pituitary tumour — not routinely with regular ovulatory cycles.
Bone density in hyperprolactinaemia
Long-standing hyperprolactinaemia causes hypogonadism → bone loss. Assess fracture risk according to standard osteoporosis guidance. Consider bone-density assessment where there has been prolonged hypogonadism or amenorrhoea, additional fracture risks, previous fragility fracture, or prolonged antipsychotic-associated hyperprolactinaemia. Do not request DEXA solely because prolactin is mildly raised. Correct vitamin D deficiency, ensure adequate dietary calcium, weight-bearing exercise, smoking cessation, and treat hypogonadism where appropriate. Cabergoline treatment restores oestrogen → improves bone density. HRT if needed (for premenopausal women with persistent hypogonadism).
Antipsychotic-induced — osteoporosis prevention
Long-term antipsychotic use with sustained hyperprolactinaemia → assess fracture risk; consider DEXA if prolonged hypogonadism or additional risk factors. If osteopenia/osteoporosis: calcium + vitamin D supplementation, weight-bearing exercise. Consider switching antipsychotic (psychiatric team) or adding oestrogen/testosterone replacement (endocrinology).
The fertility restoration rate with prolactin normalisation is one of medicine's most satisfying treatment outcomes — many women with hyperprolactinaemic amenorrhoea and infertility resume spontaneous ovulation once prolactin is normalised with cabergoline. This means that for most women with hyperprolactinaemia-related infertility, the treatment is cabergoline alone, not IVF. The sequencing is important: normalise prolactin first and allow time for ovulation to resume before adding ovulation induction (agreed with endocrinology/fertility services) — many women need only the prolactin-normalising treatment and nothing further. Adding clomifene or gonadotrophins while prolactin is still elevated will be ineffective because elevated prolactin suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis at a level upstream of where these drugs act.

Lifestyle

Lifestyle Support & Long-Term Monitoring

Preconception planning with prolactinoma Women who normalise prolactin with cabergoline and want to conceive: plan with endocrinology (microadenoma: usually stop when pregnancy test positive; SmPC and specialist advice differ on timing — follow local endocrine policy). Contraception: women with a microprolactinoma can generally use combined hormonal contraception if otherwise eligible (FSRH); agree with endocrinology for a macroprolactinoma or residual tumour. The cabergoline SmPC recommends mechanical contraception — this is product information, not evidence that hormonal contraception is unsafe; follow the local endocrine/sexual-health policy.
Bone health maintenance Adequate calcium intake (700 mg/day minimum — 1200 mg/day if osteoporosis risk): dairy, fortified plant milks, canned fish with bones, dark leafy greens. Vitamin D 10–25 mcg OD (supplementation throughout year in UK — inadequate sunshine for synthesis). Regular weight-bearing exercise (walking, jogging, dancing) — 150 min/week. Stop smoking (major bone loss accelerator).
Psychosocial impact of prolactinoma diagnosis Pituitary tumour diagnosis causes significant anxiety — patients often catastrophise about cancer or permanent disability. Explain clearly: "This is a benign tumour — not cancer. It is almost always curable or permanently controllable with medication. Most people never need surgery." The Pituitary Foundation (pituitary.org.uk) provides excellent patient information. Support group: Pituitary Network Association.
Medication compliance for cabergoline Twice-weekly dosing requires habit formation — advise linking doses to a specific activity (e.g., Monday/Thursday evenings with meal, as food improves tolerability and reduces nausea). Smartphone reminder. Do not skip doses — prolactin can rise quickly and menstruation stop within weeks of cessation. Do not alter the dose or stop cabergoline without specialist advice; withdrawal trials are specialist-led.
Driving and pituitary tumour Visual field defect from macroadenoma: must not drive until ophthalmology confirms visual fields are safe for driving (DVLA notification required for any visual field defect). Notify DVLA and document advice given. After treatment and visual field resolution: retest formally before resuming. Cabergoline: may cause somnolence and rare sudden sleep onset — advise not to drive or operate machinery if affected, and document advice.
Echocardiogram monitoring (long-term cabergoline) Before long-term cabergoline: baseline echocardiogram and assessment for clinical features of cardiac or other fibrosis. SfE/BSE/BHVS position statement (hyperprolactinaemia): repeat at 5 years for ≤2 mg/week, then every 5 years if unchanged; annually for >2 mg/week. The SmPC/MHRA wording is more intensive (echocardiogram within 3–6 months, then every 6–12 months). Follow the local endocrine/cardiology pathway, particularly for pre-existing valve disease, high-dose treatment or symptoms.
Prolactin and imaging monitoring On treatment: follow the endocrine team's agreed prolactin schedule. MRI and formal visual-field follow-up are arranged by endocrinology and individualised to tumour size, proximity to the optic chiasm, treatment response, residual tumour and symptoms. Cabergoline withdrawal after sustained normal prolactin and stable/absent tumour is a specialist decision.
Galactorrhoea management Physiological galactorrhoea: avoid breast stimulation, well-fitting supportive bra. Medical management: cabergoline normalises prolactin → galactorrhoea resolves in most (about 90% in SmPC study data). Reassure: galactorrhoea from prolactinoma does not cause cancer. Bilateral non-bloody discharge in a non-pregnant, non-breastfeeding woman = consistent with hyperprolactinaemia. Unilateral or bloody → consider breast pathology and use clinical judgement: aged 50 and over with change in one nipple only → suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12); younger → breast clinic referral on clinical judgement, where imaging is decided.
The cabergoline withdrawal trial (attempting to stop cabergoline after 2+ years of normalised prolactin and stable MRI) is an important management option that is underutilised — some microprolactinoma patients achieve sustained remission after cabergoline withdrawal (prolactin remains normal without treatment). The predictors of successful withdrawal include: normalised prolactin for at least 2 years on treatment, no visible tumour on MRI (or dramatic tumour size reduction), and low pre-treatment prolactin. The Endocrine Society guideline (Melmed et al., 2011; international) and the Pituitary Society consensus (2023; international) support discussing withdrawal after about 2 years of stability — no UK national guideline covers hyperprolactinaemia. The protocol: the dose is reduced gradually under specialist supervision while monitoring prolactin, aiming for eventual cessation. If prolactin rises to above normal on dose reduction → continue treatment. If prolactin remains normal off treatment on the specialist's agreed checks → remission likely. This approach prevents patients from taking a lifelong drug when they may not need it.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

On cabergoline — monitoring schedule
Follow the endocrine team's agreed prolactin, MRI, formal visual-field and echocardiographic schedule (individualised). Primary care: monitor symptoms, adherence, blood pressure, adverse effects (psychiatric symptoms, impulse-control disorders, somnolence and driving risk) and pregnancy plans; do not independently alter the dose or discontinue cabergoline without specialist advice.
Drug-induced hyperprolactinaemia monitoring
If the drug is safely withheld, repeat prolactin after the interval agreed with the specialist/local pathway. If normalised → no further investigation. If still elevated → endocrine advice ± MRI pituitary.
After cabergoline withdrawal
Prolactin checks per the endocrine team's agreed schedule (more frequent in the first year). If prolactin rises above normal → restart cabergoline. MRI if symptoms recur.
Macroprolactinaemia (PEG positive) — no treatment
No treatment or further investigation if the patient is asymptomatic, post-PEG monomeric prolactin is normal, there are no visual, neurological or pituitary-function abnormalities, and the laboratory interpretation is clear. Reassure: this is biologically inactive prolactin, no health risk. Explain to patient: "Your prolactin appears high but the type of prolactin causing this result does not have any effect on your body — it is a lab test phenomenon, not a real disease."
Return immediately
Sudden severe headache + visual disturbance in patient with known pituitary tumour → pituitary apoplexy → 999 (do not delay transfer) · Any new visual field defect → same-day specialist/emergency assessment
Urgent endocrinology review
Prolactin rising despite adequate cabergoline dose · New visual symptoms on treatment · Pregnancy with macroprolactinoma or tumour near the chiasm — specialist review throughout
Nipple discharge that changes
Safety-net in words: "If the discharge becomes one-sided or bloodstained, or the nipple pulls in, come back — that needs a different check." One-nipple change at 50 or over is an NG12 suspected cancer referral.
Discharge from follow-up
Macroprolactinaemia with normal monomeric prolactin and no symptoms: reassure, explain, and discharge. No MRI, no repeat prolactin unless symptoms appear.
SCA case
A 29-year-old woman has a prolactin of 780 mIU/L, taken for 4 months of irregular periods at the end of a busy consultation that included a breast examination. She takes sertraline; TSH has not been checked.
Examiner checkpoints
Treats the result as possibly artefactual and repeats it properly (rested, preferably at least 2 hours after waking, no breast examination first; cannula if venepuncture stress suspected); checks TSH and a pregnancy test before any imaging; asks for macroprolactin (PEG) because symptoms are equivocal; names sertraline as a modest contributor without stopping it reflexively; interprets 780 against the local reference range and knows a mildly raised result does not on its own justify an MRI; and — the step candidates miss — explains macroprolactin in plain language if it comes back positive, so she doesn't feel dismissed after being investigated.
The macroprolactinaemia explanation to patients requires care — patients who have been told their prolactin is 'high' and then are told 'actually it's a form of prolactin that doesn't matter' often feel confused or dismissed. The clear explanation is: 'There are different forms of prolactin in your blood. Standard blood tests measure all of them together, but one form — a larger version — attaches to other proteins and can't actually reach the cells that would respond to it. You have more of this inactive form, which makes the total reading look high. But your body is working perfectly normally — this is a characteristic of your blood chemistry, not a disease, and you don't need any treatment or follow-up unless you develop symptoms.' This explanation validates the patient's concern, explains why the test result is not as alarming as it appears, and prevents unnecessary anxiety about a finding that requires no action. It helps to acknowledge the oddness directly — "I know it's strange to be told this after being sent for tests" — and to invite questions. In the SCA the same skill is being marked: the candidate who gets the biochemistry right but leaves the patient confused has only done half the job.
