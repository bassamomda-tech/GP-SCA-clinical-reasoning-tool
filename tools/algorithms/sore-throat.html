Safety

Red Flags — Epiglottitis, Quinsy & Life-Threatening Complications

Suspected epiglottitis/supraglottitis is an airway emergency. Keep the patient calm and sitting upright. Do not lie the patient flat, use a tongue depressor, or attempt an uncomfortable oropharyngeal examination. Do not leave the patient unattended. Call 999 and transfer for expert airway management. Stridor is not required — adults may present with severe odynophagia, dysphagia, drooling, muffled voice, respiratory distress or a relatively normal oral examination, so the absence of stridor does not make epiglottitis unlikely. Examination may worsen distress and airway compromise and should only be undertaken by an appropriately equipped airway team.

Ask and examine for: stridor or respiratory difficulty · drooling or inability to swallow saliva · muffled/changed voice · tripod posture · rapidly progressive neck or floor-of-mouth swelling · trismus · dehydration · severe systemic illness or sepsis · unilateral tonsillar swelling and uvular deviation · neck swelling or pleuritic chest symptoms after recent pharyngitis.

Epiglottitis/supraglottitis — drooling, muffled voice, distress (stridor may be absent) → 999. Sit patient upright. Do NOT lie flat. Do NOT use a tongue depressor or attempt to examine the throat. Avoid giving anything by mouth if aspiration or urgent anaesthesia is likely, but do not let this delay emergency transfer. Call 999 and stay with patient. Most common in adults (H. influenzae type b, Streptococcus); childhood cases largely prevented by Hib vaccine.
Deep neck space infection / Ludwig's angina extension Parapharyngeal / retropharyngeal abscess — fever + neck stiffness + trismus + "hot potato" voice + toxic appearance → same-day hospital assessment. Call 999 if there is airway compromise, severe sepsis, stridor or rapidly progressive swelling. CT with contrast and IV antibiotics are hospital management, not arranged before transfer. Risk of mediastinitis via deep cervical fascial planes.
Quinsy (peritonsillar abscess) Unilateral tonsillar swelling + uvular deviation to opposite side + trismus + "hot potato" voice + drooling → same-day ENT. Most common deep neck infection in adults. Hospital treatment: drainage (aspiration or I&D), analgesia, hydration and antibiotics per local hospital protocol. Stridor, respiratory distress or inability to maintain the airway → 999.
Lemierre's syndrome Severe or recurrent pharyngitis (recent or resolving) + new fever, rigors, unilateral neck swelling or septic pulmonary features (pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis) → urgent hospital admission (999 if airway compromise, severe sepsis or respiratory distress). Internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis from oropharyngeal infection (Fusobacterium necrophorum). Hospital: blood cultures before antibiotics where this does not delay treatment, cross-sectional imaging and IV antibiotics with anaerobic cover. Discuss anticoagulation with the specialist team; not recommended routinely in primary care.
Persistent unexplained neck lump, hoarseness or sore throat Head and neck cancer (oropharyngeal / hypopharyngeal SCC) → ENT suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12, updated April 2026: 45 and over with an unexplained neck lump or persistent unexplained hoarseness). A persistent unexplained neck lump needs suspected cancer referral whether or not sore throat is present; HPV epidemiology does not change the referral threshold. Persistent unilateral tonsillar enlargement, ulceration, bleeding, firmness, unexplained cervical lymphadenopathy, referred otalgia, weight loss or persistent unilateral symptoms require urgent ENT assessment. Acute asymmetry during infection may be reactive, but persistence after recovery requires review.
Sore throat + rash + purpura / petechiae Streptococcal scarlet fever (sandpaper rash + strawberry tongue + palatal petechiae) — treat with penicillin. Notify the local UKHSA Health Protection Team promptly according to current local arrangements. Exclude from school or nursery until at least 24 hours after starting appropriate antibiotics and when clinically well. Rash with severe toxicity, meningism, hypotension, rapidly progressive disease or non-blanching purpura → emergency assessment for invasive bacterial infection.
Sore throat + severe trismus + unilateral fullness Parapharyngeal space abscess (medial to parapharynx — less visible than quinsy) — → same-day hospital assessment (999 if airway compromise, severe sepsis or rapidly progressive swelling). CT neck is a hospital investigation. More dangerous than quinsy due to proximity to carotid sheath and deep fascial planes.
Immunocompromised patient with sore throat Agranulocytosis from carbimazole / clozapine / methotrexate — neutropenia → catastrophic oropharyngeal infection. Carbimazole + sore throat, fever, mouth ulcers or other infection symptoms → stop carbimazole and urgent FBC with differential; if neutropenia/agranulocytosis, same-day specialist/hospital management. Clozapine + fever/sore throat → urgent assessment and immediate ANC/FBC via the clozapine monitoring pathway; contact the clozapine/mental-health team urgently and do not independently stop or restart clozapine unless directed. Methotrexate/other myelosuppressives → follow the shared-care protocol. Oral candidiasis (thrush) in HIV / immunosuppression.
Epiglottitis is a rapidly progressive airway emergency — the epiglottis and supraglottic structures become severely oedematous, and stimulation (tongue depressor, crying in a child, anxiety) may worsen distress and airway compromise. The classic presentation is the tripod position: patient sitting upright, leaning forward on hands, with neck extended, mouth open, drooling. This posture maximises the airway calibre — it is an involuntary survival response. Lying the patient flat on an examination couch may worsen airway compromise. The GP's role is entirely supportive: call 999, keep the patient calm and upright, administer high-flow oxygen via mask if available (do not use a tight-fitting mask), and do NOT attempt any examination or investigation. The decline of childhood epiglottitis from H. influenzae type b followed universal Hib vaccination (1992 in UK), but adult epiglottitis (GAS, Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae non-type b) has filled the epidemiological gap. Lemierre's syndrome deserves specific attention because it is a disease that affects young previously healthy adults (typically 16–30), occurs in the convalescent phase of pharyngitis (after the sore throat seems to be improving), and is frequently not considered. The incidence is approximately 3.6 per million per year — rare but rising. The Fusobacterium necrophorum pathogen is found in 80% of cases and is resistant to many standard antibiotics (notably macrolides). Any young adult with a two-week history of pharyngitis who now presents with fever, neck pain, and chest symptoms needs urgent hospital assessment for Lemierre's syndrome (blood cultures and cross-sectional imaging are hospital investigations).

FeverPAIN & Centor Scoring — Antibiotic Decision

Most sore throats are viral and self-limiting. Antibiotics should be targeted using FeverPAIN (NICE NG84) or Centor scoring — not prescribed routinely. Before scoring, first consider EBV, scarlet fever, influenza/COVID-19 or other viral infection, drug-induced agranulocytosis, STI-related infection, ANUG/dental disease, malignancy and reflux/postnasal symptoms.

FeverPAIN score (NICE NG84)
Score one point each: Fever ≥38°C in past 24 hrs · Purulence (pus on tonsils) · Attend rapidly (within 3 days of symptom onset) · severely Inflamed tonsils · No cough or coryza. Score 0–1 (estimated 13–18% streptococcal): do not offer antibiotics. Score 2–3 (estimated 34–40%): no antibiotic or a back-up prescription. Score 4–5 (estimated 62–65%): consider an immediate or back-up prescription. Probabilities are approximate estimates — neither score confirms GAS infection.
Centor / McIsaac score
Score one point each: Tonsillar exudate · Anterior cervical lymphadenopathy (tender) · Temperature >38°C · Absence of cough. McIsaac adds: age 3–14 (+1), age 15–44 (0), age ≥45 (−1). NICE NG84 uses the four-item Centor score: 0–2 (estimated 3–17% streptococcal) → do not offer antibiotics; 3–4 (estimated 32–56%) → consider an immediate or back-up antibiotic. McIsaac is a modification (international).
Delayed prescription strategy
Give patient a back-up prescription with instruction to use it ONLY if symptoms do not start to improve within 3–5 days, or worsen rapidly or significantly at any time. Inability to swallow fluids or systemic deterioration needs same-day clinical assessment (?dehydration, quinsy or another complication) — not delayed antibiotic collection. Evidence shows this approach reduces antibiotic use by 60–75% compared to immediate prescription, with equivalent patient satisfaction and outcomes (Little et al.).
Rapid antigen test (RADT)
Group A Streptococcus (GAS) antigen test from throat swab. NICE NG84 does not recommend routine RADT as a replacement for clinical assessment; RADT should not be presented as a routine requirement in general practice. Use only where supported by a local validated pathway (for example community pharmacy). Performance varies by platform, sampling technique and population; a positive result may represent carriage, and a negative result does not exclude other bacterial or non-GAS causes. A result should not override clinical assessment, red flags, immunocompromise or suspected suppurative complications.
Do NOT routinely throat swab
Routine throat culture for all sore throats is not needed (clinical practice) — results take 48 hrs, management should not be delayed awaiting swab results, most sore throats are viral and a culture cannot distinguish colonisation from infection. Consider targeted microbiology in: suspected diphtheria (unusual membrane + systemic toxicity), in conjunction with immediate UKHSA advice · outbreaks or public-health investigations · treatment failure or unusual organisms · immunocompromised patients, following specialist/microbiology advice · suspected gonorrhoea (NAAT and culture before treatment where possible). If diphtheria is suspected, do not wait for swab results — isolate and notify the UKHSA Health Protection Team urgently.
The FeverPAIN score was specifically developed and validated in UK primary care (Little et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases, 2013) — making it the most applicable scoring tool for UK GP practice. The key insight from this landmark study is that most sore throats are viral (70–80%), and even GAS pharyngitis is largely self-limiting — antibiotics reduce symptom duration by only 16 hours on average (from approximately 7 days to approximately 6.4 days). The modest symptom benefit must be weighed against antibiotic side effects (diarrhoea in 10%, rash in 5%, Clostridioides difficile risk), contribution to antibiotic resistance, and medicalisation of self-limiting illness. The primary indication for antibiotics in streptococcal pharyngitis is prevention of rheumatic fever (acute rheumatic heart disease) — a complication that has become extremely rare in the UK due to improved living conditions and historical antibiotic use, but remains important in high-incidence communities. Secondary indications include prevention of peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) and invasive GAS disease. The NNT for preventing quinsy with antibiotics in a high-risk sore throat is approximately 100 — meaning 100 patients must be treated to prevent one case. The delayed prescription strategy (back pocket prescription) is one of the most evidence-based antibiotic stewardship interventions in primary care — it empowers patients to self-assess, significantly reduces antibiotic consumption, and achieves equivalent clinical outcomes to immediate prescribing for uncomplicated sore throat.

Differential Diagnosis

Viral pharyngitis / tonsillitis
Most common cause (70–80%) — rhinovirus, coronavirus, adenovirus, parainfluenza, influenza. Gradual onset, coryza, mild fever, bilateral sore throat, no exudate (or diffuse). Resolves in 5–7 days without treatment. Manage with analgesia + hydration. No antibiotics. Reassure natural resolution.
Group A Streptococcus (GAS) tonsillitis
Acute onset, high fever (>38°C), severe sore throat, bilateral tonsillar erythema and exudate (white/yellow), anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, no cough or coryza. FeverPAIN score 4–5 or Centor score 3–4. Consider immediate or back-up phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg QDS (or 1 g BD) × 5–10 days (adult dose). Scarlet fever: add sandpaper rash — notifiable.
Infectious mononucleosis (EBV)
Teenagers / young adults. Triad: severe exudative tonsillitis + posterior cervical lymphadenopathy + splenomegaly. Fatigue out of proportion. Palatal petechiae (not specific — also GAS). Atypical lymphocytes on FBC film. In adolescents and young adults with prolonged fatigue, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, marked tonsillar inflammation or atypical lymphocytosis, consider FBC and EBV serology according to local laboratory practice; heterophile antibody (Monospot) tests may be falsely negative early in illness and are less reliable in younger children. Avoid amoxicillin or ampicillin if EBV is suspected — a characteristic maculopapular rash is common. Avoid contact sports, heavy lifting and activities with a risk of abdominal trauma for at least 3–4 weeks from symptom onset (splenic rupture risk), then an individualised gradual return when clinically recovered.
Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)
Unilateral tonsillar swelling pushing uvula to contralateral side + trismus (unable to fully open mouth) + "hot potato" voice (muffled due to pharyngeal swelling) + drooling + severe dysphagia. Same-day ENT/hospital assessment — drainage and antibiotics according to the local hospital protocol. Recurrent quinsy → ENT discussion; a single episode may be sufficient for referral depending on the local pathway, severity and history.
Gonococcal / chlamydial pharyngitis
Consider only with a relevant sexual history (oral sex with new/multiple partners) — not a routine cause of acute sore throat. Often asymptomatic or mild. Gonorrhoea pharyngitis: exudative pharyngitis, cervical lymphadenopathy → urgent GUM referral or discussion with sexual health; NAAT and culture from all relevant sites before treatment where possible; ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose (BASHH 2025); pharyngeal test of cure (generally at least 2 weeks after treatment); partner notification; full STI screen; abstain from sex until treatment and partner management are complete. Isolated pharyngeal chlamydia is uncommon — testing/treatment guided by a sexual-health service per the current BASHH chlamydia guideline, assessing for concurrent genital/rectal infection and other STIs.
Glandular fever complications
EBV complications: upper airway obstruction from massive tonsil enlargement (urgent hospital assessment; corticosteroids specialist-led), hepatitis (check LFTs if significant systemic illness, jaundice or abdominal symptoms), myocarditis (rare), haematological (thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anaemia), meningitis/encephalitis (rare). Reactivation → chronic active EBV (rare). Burkitt's lymphoma (EBV + malaria in sub-Saharan Africa).
Non-infective causes
GORD / laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) — chronic throat clearing, globus, hoarseness, morning worse. No acute fever. Multiple causes — do not start empirical PPI automatically; persistent hoarseness, dysphagia, weight loss, unilateral otalgia or neck lump → appropriate ENT/suspected cancer referral. Postnasal drip (chronic rhinosinusitis) — tickle/mucus sensation in throat, worse on waking. Aphthous ulcers — recurrent painful oral ulcers. Contact/irritant (dry air, smoking, chemical exposure). Agranulocytosis (drug-induced) — check FBC urgently.
Vincent's angina (ANUG)
Acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis — necrotising infection of gingivae, spreads to tonsils and pharynx. Fusobacterium + anaerobes. Poor oral hygiene, smoking, immunocompromised. Painful gum ulceration + halitosis + necrotic membrane. Urgent dental referral — debridement and dental treatment are central; antimicrobial (e.g. metronidazole) per the local dental antimicrobial pathway; chlorhexidine mouthwash is adjunctive, not definitive. Urgent assessment if rapidly spreading, unable to eat/drink, immunosuppressed or systemically toxic.
The amoxicillin rash in EBV is one of the most important pharmacological interactions to know — and one of the most easily forgotten in a busy acute consultation. When a patient with EBV (infectious mononucleosis) is given amoxicillin or ampicillin (including co-amoxiclav), a generalised pruritic maculopapular rash is common (estimates vary substantially), usually within 24–72 hours. This is not a true penicillin allergy — it is a specific interaction between EBV-infected B-lymphocytes and the aminopenicillin structure, producing an immune complex-mediated rash. The patient is not allergic to penicillin and can safely take other penicillins in future, but this must be explained carefully to avoid a permanent and incorrect "penicillin allergy" label being added to their records. The practical implication: if a patient presents with exudative tonsillitis in the 15–25 year age group and the diagnosis might be EBV, do NOT prescribe amoxicillin or co-amoxiclav. If antibiotics are warranted (FeverPAIN ≥4), use phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V). Splenomegaly in EBV is present in 50% of patients by the end of the second week — splenic rupture, although rare (0.1–0.5%), is potentially fatal and is precipitated by contact sport, heavy lifting, or trauma. Advise avoidance of contact sports, heavy lifting and activities with a risk of abdominal trauma for at least 3–4 weeks from symptom onset, followed by an individualised gradual return when clinically recovered. Routine ultrasound to document splenic resolution is not required for everyone; consider specialist assessment or imaging when the patient participates in high-risk sport, has persistent splenomegaly or the timing of return is uncertain.

Examination & Investigations

Throat examination
Inspect oropharynx with good light: tonsil size (grade 1–4: 1 = behind pillars, 4 = kissing), erythema, exudate (bilateral = GAS or viral; unilateral = quinsy, malignancy), membrane (grey pseudomembrane = ?diphtheria — isolate and notify the UKHSA Health Protection Team urgently; do not wait for swab results), uvula position (deviated = quinsy), posterior pharyngeal wall (cobblestoning = postnasal drip / viral). Palatal petechiae can occur with GAS or EBV but are not diagnostically specific.
Lymph node examination
Anterior cervical lymphadenopathy: tender = GAS / viral. Posterior cervical lymphadenopathy supports EBV (posterior chain) but is not diagnostic — feel behind sternomastoid. Persistent, hard, fixed, supraclavicular or generalised lymphadenopathy requires a broader differential (including lymphoma and other malignancy).
Airway and systemic assessment
Voice quality (muffled "hot potato" = quinsy / epiglottitis / parapharyngeal abscess). Mouth opening (trismus = abscess). Temperature and HR (septic screen if systemically unwell). Persistent unilateral tonsillar enlargement, ulceration, bleeding, firmness, unexplained cervical lymphadenopathy, referred otalgia, weight loss or persistent unilateral symptoms require urgent ENT assessment (local pathway; not an NG12 criterion — lymphoma or SCC). Acute asymmetry during infection may be reactive, but persistence after recovery requires review.
Investigations (targeted)
Rapid antigen test (RADT) (only within a local validated pathway; does not override clinical assessment) · EBV serology / heterophile test (EBV suspected — per local laboratory practice; heterophile may be falsely negative early and in younger children) · FBC + film (EBV — atypical lymphocytes; agranulocytosis in immunosuppressed; haematological malignancy) · LFTs (EBV — if significant systemic illness, jaundice or abdominal symptoms) · EBV IgM antibodies (Monospot negative <7 days) · NAAT + culture pharyngeal swab (relevant sexual history; via sexual health where possible) · Throat culture (targeted — suspected diphtheria with UKHSA advice, outbreaks, treatment failure, immunocompromised per microbiology advice) · CECT neck (suspected quinsy / deep space infection — hospital)
Carbimazole / clozapine alert
Carbimazole: sore throat, fever, mouth ulcers or other infection symptoms → stop carbimazole and urgent FBC with differential. If neutropenia or agranulocytosis is present, arrange same-day specialist/hospital management and do not restart antithyroid treatment unless directed by endocrinology. Clozapine: fever, sore throat or other infection symptoms → urgent clinical assessment and immediate ANC/FBC via the clozapine monitoring pathway; contact the clozapine/mental-health team urgently. Do not independently stop or restart clozapine unless advised through the specialist pathway (except where the monitoring service or specialist team directs immediate cessation for neutropenia/agranulocytosis); consider clozapine toxicity during serious infection. Methotrexate / other myelosuppressives: follow the shared-care protocol. Do NOT assume sore throat is viral in any patient on these medications without FBC.
Asymmetric tonsillar enlargement in an adult is one of the most important and most missed signs of oropharyngeal malignancy — lymphoma (particularly NHL) and SCC can both present as unilateral tonsillar enlargement without ulceration or an obvious mass. The key clinical question is: is one tonsil consistently and persistently larger than the other on separate examinations? Transient asymmetry during acute tonsillitis is common — but tonsillar asymmetry that persists weeks after an acute infection, or that is noted incidentally in a patient without acute infection, must be investigated. Unexplained unilateral tonsillar enlargement in an adult warrants urgent ENT referral (clinical practice; not a specific NICE NG12 criterion). The monospot (Paul-Bunnell / heterophile antibody) test has a known false-negative rate of 20% in the first week of EBV illness (before sufficient heterophile antibodies are produced) — a negative monospot in the first 4–5 days does not exclude EBV. EBV-specific antibodies (VCA-IgM) are more sensitive in early infection and should be requested when clinical suspicion remains high despite a negative monospot. LFTs are reasonable in EBV with significant systemic illness, jaundice or abdominal symptoms — EBV hepatitis (transaminitis, with ALT often elevated 3–10× ULN) is present in 80% of EBV cases but is usually subclinical and resolves spontaneously within 4–6 weeks. Severe jaundice or ALT >10× ULN warrants gastroenterology review. Avoid alcohol if there is hepatitis, abnormal LFTs or significant systemic illness; advise according to clinical findings.

Referral Pathways

999

Urgent referral (local pathway): ENT
Not NG12 criteria (clinical practice): Persistent unilateral tonsillar enlargement or unilateral symptoms (adult) · Unexplained bleeding from throat
ENT routine
Recurrent tonsillitis meeting the NHS England Evidence-Based Interventions / ENT UK (2021) criteria for tonsillectomy: ≥7 episodes in 1 year, or ≥5 episodes/year for 2 years, or ≥3 episodes/year for 3 years — with significant functional impact. Episodes must be clinically significant, attributable to acute tonsillitis (not nonspecific sore throat), adequately documented and treated, and disabling (substantial impact on work, education or caring). Use the ENT UK recurrent tonsillitis decision aid (2023) and local criteria. Recurrent quinsy → ENT discussion (a single episode may suffice per local pathway). Obstructive sleep apnoea, significant tonsillar hypertrophy, recurrent peritonsillar abscess or suspected malignancy may justify referral outside the numerical thresholds.
GUM clinic
STI-associated pharyngitis (gonococcal, chlamydial) — partner notification, test of cure (gonorrhoea). Any STI identified → GUM referral as per BASHH guidelines. NAAT positive pharynx: urgent GUM referral/discussion — gonorrhoea: culture before treatment where possible, ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose (BASHH 2025), pharyngeal test of cure (generally ≥2 weeks after treatment), partner notification, full STI screen, abstinence until treatment and partner management complete; chlamydia: per the current BASHH chlamydia guideline.
Haematology (same-day)
Neutropenia/agranulocytosis on FBC in a carbimazole patient (same-day specialist/hospital; no restart unless endocrinology directs) or clozapine patient (with the clozapine/mental-health team). Blast cells or pancytopenia on FBC (leukaemia presenting as sore throat).
Local pathways
Use local Bolton / Greater Manchester pathways for same-day ENT/OMFS access, acute airway pathways, GUM referral, UKHSA notification and clozapine/neutropenia management.
The tonsillectomy criteria (7-5-3 rule, adapted from the Paradise criteria — originally in SIGN 117, now withdrawn; for UK practice use the NHS England Evidence-Based Interventions programme, the ENT UK 2021 commissioning guide, the ENT UK decision aid and local criteria) provide the evidence-based threshold for tonsillectomy referral — ≥7 clinically significant episodes in 1 year, ≥5 per year for 2 consecutive years, or ≥3 per year for 3 consecutive years, where episodes must be documented and clinically significant (not just "a sore throat"). The evidence base for tonsillectomy in recurrent tonsillitis was established by the Burton and Paradise trials — tonsillectomy reduces the frequency of sore throats for 2 years post-procedure, but the benefit is modest (approximately 3 fewer throat infections per year) and most patients improve without surgery within 3–4 years. Therefore, tonsillectomy is reserved for patients meeting the frequency criteria AND experiencing significant functional impact (time off school/work, hospitalisation, analgesia dependence). The suspected cancer pathway referral for suspected head and neck cancer is one of the highest-yield cancer safety-net decisions in GP practice — oropharyngeal SCC (largely HPV-associated) is the most rapidly rising cancer in the UK, with incidence increasing 200% since the late 1990s. It typically affects non-smoking middle-aged adults (the "new" head and neck cancer profile) and presents as a unilateral tonsillar mass, sore throat with referred otalgia, or neck mass. The 5-year survival is 80% at Stage I–II but falls to 40% at Stage IV — early suspected cancer pathway referral is critical. Clinical red flags and the local pathway — not HPV epidemiology — determine the referral threshold.

Antibiotic Prescribing & Symptom Management

GAS tonsillitis (FeverPAIN 4–5 / Centor 3–4)
Phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg QDS (or 1 g BD) × 5–10 days (adult)
First-line (NICE NG84). 500 mg QDS (or 1,000 mg BD) for 5–10 days (NICE NG84) — 5 days may be sufficient for symptomatic cure; 10 days may improve microbiological cure. Distinguish true immediate/severe hypersensitivity, delayed non-severe rash and GI intolerance. Penicillin allergy/intolerance (not pregnant): clarithromycin 250–500 mg BD × 5 days. Pregnancy: erythromycin (GMMMG). Do not use cefalexin unless a local formulary and allergy pathway support it. Do NOT use amoxicillin if EBV clinically plausible (rash). Children: age/weight-based BNFC doses. Check pregnancy, renal impairment, anticoagulation/interactions and immunocompromise.
Analgesia (all sore throats)
Paracetamol, or ibuprofen if preferred and suitable
Advise paracetamol for pain or fever, or ibuprofen if preferred and suitable, with adequate fluids (NICE NG84). Use age-appropriate or weight-based dosing and do not exceed the maximum daily dose (watch combination OTC products). If both are used, provide a clear written schedule; do not advise alternating every 2–3 hours as a generic rule. NSAID cautions: pregnancy (particularly after 20 weeks and in the third trimester), renal impairment or dehydration, peptic ulcer disease, anticoagulation, heart failure or significant cardiovascular disease, NSAID-sensitive asthma, older/frail patients. Soluble formulations easier to swallow. Flurbiprofen and local anaesthetic lozenges can be mentioned for adults — medicated lozenges give only small pain benefits and are not equivalent to oral analgesia.
EBV (infectious mononucleosis)
Supportive — NO amoxicillin / ampicillin
Paracetamol or suitable ibuprofen. Rest and hydration. Avoid alcohol if there is hepatitis, abnormal LFTs or significant systemic illness. Avoid contact sports, heavy lifting and abdominal-trauma risk for at least 3–4 weeks from onset, then individualised gradual return (splenic rupture). Corticosteroids are specialist/hospital-led — only for airway obstruction from massive tonsils, severe thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anaemia or severe systemic illness. Do NOT give corticosteroids routinely for EBV fatigue.
Corticosteroids — not routineA single dose of dexamethasone may modestly improve pain resolution at 48 hours in selected patients — the UK TOAST trial (Hayward et al., JAMA 2017) found no significant difference at 24 hours but more complete resolution at 48 hours (35.4% vs 27.1%). NICE NG84 (2018) does not recommend corticosteroids for acute sore throat (self-limiting illness, safety concerns with repeated courses), so this is not routine primary care practice.
Scarlet feverPhenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg QDS × 10 days (adult dose; children per BNFC; longer course than uncomplicated GAS — rheumatic fever prevention). Notify the local UKHSA Health Protection Team promptly according to current local arrangements. Exclude from school or nursery until at least 24 hours after starting appropriate antibiotics and when clinically well. Severe toxicity, meningism, hypotension, rapidly progressive disease or non-blanching purpura → emergency assessment for invasive bacterial infection. Household contacts: no prophylaxis unless immunocompromised or outbreak setting. Advise parents to return if child develops worsening rash, fever, or joint pain (rheumatic fever / post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis).
Vincent's angina (ANUG)Urgent dental referral for debridement and dental treatment (central to management). Antimicrobial (e.g. metronidazole for Fusobacterium / anaerobes) per the local dental antimicrobial pathway. Chlorhexidine 0.2% mouthwash is adjunctive, not definitive. Urgent assessment if rapidly spreading, unable to eat/drink, immunosuppressed or systemically toxic. Address smoking and poor oral hygiene. Offer HIV testing according to risk, severity, recurrence and local testing policy. Vitamin C deficiency screen (scurvy → ANUG in malnourished patients).
The single-dose dexamethasone evidence shows only a modest benefit. The TOAST trial (Hayward et al., JAMA, 2017) — a UK primary care RCT in adults not given immediate antibiotics — found that a single dose of dexamethasone did not significantly increase complete symptom resolution at 24 hours, but did at 48 hours (35.4% vs 27.1% placebo, NNT approximately 12), with no significant increase in adverse effects. NICE NG84 (2018) nevertheless does not recommend corticosteroids for acute sore throat, citing its self-limiting course and the risks of repeated steroid courses. This is a clinical stewardship intervention — the steroid reduces the inflammatory pain without promoting bacterial proliferation (antibiotics decision remains based on FeverPAIN/Centor). The choice of phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) over amoxicillin as first-line for GAS tonsillitis is specifically because amoxicillin causes the florid rash in EBV — since EBV cannot be excluded clinically in every case, penicillin V is the safer default. Phenoxymethylpenicillin is narrow-spectrum, effective, cheap, and spares the broader spectrum for other indications. Clarithromycin and erythromycin (used for penicillin allergy) are associated with macrolide resistance in GAS and Streptococcus pneumoniae — they should only be used when genuine penicillin allergy exists.

Treat

Specific Conditions — Quinsy, Lemierre's & Recurrent Tonsillitis (hospital management is specialist-led)

Quinsy (peritonsillar abscess) — hospital
Suspected quinsy requires same-day ENT/hospital assessment. Treatment generally includes drainage by aspiration or incision and drainage, appropriate analgesia, hydration and antibiotics according to the local hospital protocol. Assess airway, ability to swallow and need for anaesthetic support. Call 999 if there is stridor, respiratory distress or inability to maintain the airway. Recurrent quinsy should prompt ENT discussion — a single episode may be sufficient for referral depending on the local pathway, clinical severity and history; do not require two episodes before referral.
Lemierre's syndrome — hospital
Suspected Lemierre's syndrome requires urgent hospital admission, blood cultures before antibiotics where this does not delay treatment, cross-sectional imaging and intravenous antibiotics with anaerobic cover; antibiotic choice and duration are specialist-led. Discuss anticoagulation with the relevant specialist team; do not recommend it routinely in primary care. 999 depends on airway, sepsis and respiratory status.
Recurrent tonsillitis — tonsillectomy criteria
NHS England Evidence-Based Interventions / ENT UK criteria (adapted from the Paradise criteria — use the ENT UK decision aid and local criteria): ≥7 documented clinically significant episodes in 1 year, OR ≥5/year for 2 consecutive years, OR ≥3/year for 3 consecutive years. Episodes must be clinically significant, attributable to acute tonsillitis rather than nonspecific sore throat, adequately documented and treated, and disabling (substantial impact on work, education or caring). OSA, significant tonsillar hypertrophy, recurrent peritonsillar abscess or suspected malignancy may justify ENT referral outside these thresholds. Benefit: reduces infections by ~3/year for 2 years post-op. Document episodes carefully in GP records (with objective findings) to support referral. Tonsillectomy is elective — avoid during acute illness.
Post-streptococcal complications
Acute rheumatic fever (ARF): 2–4 weeks post-GAS throat — carditis, migratory polyarthritis, Sydenham's chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules. Same-day paediatrics (or medical team if adult). Treatment and secondary penicillin prophylaxis are specialist-led (prevents recurrence and further cardiac damage). Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis: haematuria + hypertension 10–14 days post-GAS — refer nephrology.
Gonococcal pharyngitis
Urgent GUM referral or discussion with a sexual-health specialist. NAAT and culture from all relevant sites before treatment where possible. Ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose (BASHH 2025 — due to rising resistance). Pharyngeal test of cure, generally at least 2 weeks after treatment (BASHH 2025). Partner notification, full STI screen, and sexual abstinence until treatment and partner management are complete.
Acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is rare in the UK (approximately 100 cases/year) but is the historical reason why GAS tonsillitis is treated with antibiotics — the original therapeutic rationale was preventing ARF and its devastating valvular heart disease consequence (rheumatic heart disease). ARF still occurs in the UK and causes significant morbidity, particularly in children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds and immigrants from high-prevalence regions. GPs should be aware of the Jones Criteria for ARF diagnosis (major criteria: carditis, polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules) and should consider ARF in any child presenting with migratory joint pain, fever, or cardiac symptoms 2–4 weeks after a sore throat. Secondary prophylaxis with penicillin V for 5–10 years prevents further streptococcal infections and further cardiac damage in patients who have had ARF. The documentation of tonsillitis episodes for tonsillectomy criteria is a practical GP task — if patients are attending out-of-hours, walk-in centres, or A&E with tonsillitis, the GP records may not capture all episodes. Advising patients to keep a symptom diary and providing written documentation at each GP consultation (including objective findings — temperature, tonsillar appearance, investigation results) creates an accurate record. A referral without documented episodes with objective features is unlikely to be accepted for tonsillectomy. Patient self-reported frequency alone is insufficient — all episodes must be clinician-documented.

Lifestyle

Self-Care, Prevention & Antibiotic Stewardship

Symptom self-management Most sore throats are viral and self-limiting — 90% resolve in 7–10 days without antibiotics. Rest, adequate hydration (cold fluids, ice lollies, iced water), warm fluids (hot honey and lemon — not evidence-based but comforting). Analgesia: paracetamol, or ibuprofen if preferred and suitable — do not exceed the maximum daily dose; if both are used, follow a clear written schedule. Salt water gargling (warm saline) — mild mucous membrane soothing effect, no evidence of bacterial elimination but well-tolerated.
Over-the-counter preparations Flurbiprofen 8.75 mg lozenges (Strefen — OTC, adults) — topical NSAID; medicated lozenges give only small pain benefits (NICE NG84) and are not equivalent to oral analgesia. Benzydamine spray (Difflam 0.15%) — topical NSAID. No useful evidence for non-medicated lozenges, mouthwash or local anaesthetic spray alone. Anaesthetic lozenges (Strepsils with lignocaine) — temporary symptom relief. Hexetidine (Oraldene) — antiseptic mouthwash, limited clinical evidence. Avoid excessive OTC preparations in children under 12.
Infection control Respiratory and hand hygiene to reduce spread: cover coughs/sneezes, dispose of tissues immediately, wash hands 20 seconds. Avoid sharing cutlery, cups, and kissing (EBV — "kissing disease" — spread via saliva). Scarlet fever: exclude from school or nursery until at least 24 hours after starting antibiotics and clinically well. Avoid contact with vulnerable individuals (neonates, immunocompromised) during symptomatic period.
Smoking cessation Smoking is the single most important modifiable risk factor for recurrent tonsillitis, pharyngitis, and head and neck cancer. Tobacco smoke impairs mucociliary clearance, damages pharyngeal epithelium, and suppresses local immune function. Brief smoking cessation advice at every sore throat consultation — every contact counts. Refer to Stop Smoking Service. Passive smoke exposure in children increases tonsillitis risk — advise smoke-free home.
Antibiotic stewardship messaging Explain: most sore throats are caused by viruses — antibiotics do not work for viruses, do not shorten viral illness, and cause side effects (diarrhoea, thrush, rash) plus resistance. A delayed prescription gives control — "use this only if your symptoms don't start to improve within 3–5 days, or get rapidly or significantly worse." Written information (TARGET antibiotic leaflets from RCGP — free download). Empowering patients as partners in stewardship.
EBV recovery advice Fatigue in EBV can persist for 4–12 weeks after acute illness — reassure this is normal. Avoid alcohol if there is hepatitis, abnormal LFTs or significant systemic illness. No contact sport, heavy lifting or abdominal-trauma risk for at least 3–4 weeks from onset, then individualised return (splenic rupture risk). Graduated return to exercise — start with walking, increase gradually when symptoms allow. Most patients are fully recovered by 3 months. Persistent fatigue beyond 3 months → post-viral fatigue assessment (overlap with CFS/ME pathway).
HPV vaccination and oropharyngeal cancer HPV vaccination (NHS school programme for all Year 8 students, boys and girls, since September 2019) prevents HPV 16/18 — responsible for 70–80% of oropharyngeal SCCs. Check vaccination status opportunistically in adults who missed school-age vaccination (catch-up via GUM for MSM up to age 45). HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer is rising — vaccination is the primary prevention strategy.
Oral hygiene and dental health Good oral hygiene reduces bacterial load in oropharynx — twice-daily toothbrushing with fluoride toothpaste, daily flossing, regular dental checks. Dental disease (ANUG, dental abscess) can cause or worsen pharyngeal infection. Avoid mouthwash containing alcohol (dries mucosa, impairs mucosal immunity). Chlorhexidine mouthwash appropriate for Vincent's angina but not for routine sore throats — alters normal flora.
The RCGP TARGET (Treat Antibiotics Responsibly, Guidance, Education, Tools) toolkit provides excellent free patient-facing antibiotic stewardship resources — the "When should I worry?" and "Treating your infection" leaflets are validated tools that improve patient understanding and reduce antibiotic expectations. These should be available in every GP consulting room and given to patients who request antibiotics that are not clinically indicated. Multiple RCTs show that providing written patient information alongside an explanation of natural history reduces antibiotic prescribing for URTI by 20–30% without reducing patient satisfaction. The single most evidence-based message is: "Antibiotics will not make you better faster for a viral sore throat — your body will do that on its own in 7–10 days. Antibiotics have side effects and can cause resistant bacteria. Here's what to do to manage your symptoms, and here's exactly when you should come back or seek urgent care." Oral health and sore throat have a stronger connection than is often recognised — periodontal disease and poor oral hygiene increase the oropharyngeal bacterial load and are independent risk factors for recurrent pharyngitis and for Vincent's angina. The ANUG presentation specifically requires urgent dental referral for professional debridement — without this, the necrotising process continues despite antibiotic therapy. GPs managing ANUG should prioritise the dental referral (emergency dental, NHS 111) as the definitive treatment.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Viral / low-risk (FeverPAIN 0–1)
Safety-net written instructions: return if unable to swallow fluids (dehydration risk), develop neck stiffness or severe headache (meningitis), voice becomes muffled or mouth won't open (quinsy / deep neck infection), breathing becomes noisy or laboured (epiglottitis / airway oedema), new trismus, drooling or neck swelling, or rash develops. Expect resolution in about a week; return if no improvement after approximately one week, or for persistent hoarseness, sore throat, unilateral symptoms or a neck lump. Back-up prescription option if FeverPAIN 2–3 (use only if not starting to improve within 3–5 days or worsening rapidly/significantly).
Antibiotic course
Not improving after 5 days of phenoxymethylpenicillin → reconsider diagnosis (EBV? quinsy? resistant organism?). Return for assessment. Do not automatically repeat antibiotics without re-examination. Non-response to penicillin at 5 days → consider co-amoxiclav (covers H. influenzae and beta-lactamase-producing anaerobes).
EBV (confirmed) — 4–6 weeks
Review at 4–6 weeks: fatigue resolving? LFTs normalising (if abnormal)? Individualised gradual return to contact sport when clinically recovered — routine ultrasound not required; consider specialist assessment or imaging for high-risk sport, persistent splenomegaly or uncertain timing. Persistent fatigue beyond 3 months → post-viral fatigue pathway (CFS/ME assessment). Persistent lymphadenopathy beyond 6 weeks → lymphoma exclusion (FBC + film + USS lymph node + suspected cancer pathway referral if concerning features).
Recurrent tonsillitis
Document each episode with: date, temperature, examination findings (tonsillar erythema/exudate), treatment given, days off work/school. Build documented record toward tonsillectomy criteria. Advise: visit same GP for each episode to ensure consistent documentation. Refer to ENT when criteria are met (≥7 in 1 year, ≥5/year for 2 years or ≥3/year for 3 years) with significant impact.
Scarlet fever
Complete 10-day antibiotic course. Return if: new joint pain / swelling (ARF), dark urine / oedema 10–14 days post-illness (post-streptococcal GN), recurrence of fever after initial improvement (complications). UKHSA HPT notified promptly per local arrangements. Return to school/nursery at least 24 hrs after starting antibiotics and when clinically well.
999 safety-net
Breathing difficulty, stridor, drooling or muffled voice with distress at any time (?epiglottitis — stridor may be absent; keep upright, do NOT examine throat, call 999 immediately) · Inability to breathe comfortably or stridor (airway obstruction) · Neck stiffness + severe headache + photophobia (meningitis) · Rapidly spreading neck swelling (Ludwig's / Lemierre's)
Same-day GP
Trismus developing (unable to open mouth fully) · Voice becoming muffled / "hot potato" · Bulge behind one tonsil (quinsy forming) · Unable to swallow fluids · Neck swelling and pain (possible internal jugular thrombophlebitis — Lemierre's) · Dehydration or worsening rapidly/significantly · Fever or sore throat on carbimazole / clozapine / myelosuppressive drug · Immunosuppressed patient not responding to 48 hrs of antibiotics
Written safety-netting instructions for sore throat are particularly important because the most dangerous complications (epiglottitis, quinsy, Lemierre's syndrome) can develop or worsen days after the initial GP consultation, often at night or over a weekend when access to primary care is limited. Patients and parents need written, specific, unambiguous instructions about which symptoms require calling 999 immediately vs which require same-day GP contact. The TARGET toolkit provides validated safety-netting advice sheets for respiratory infections that are free to download and print. The key signs to include are: cannot open mouth, voice becoming muffled, can hear noisy breathing, neck swelling, unable to swallow water. The EBV follow-up at 4–6 weeks is particularly important because: (1) individualised advice on return to contact sport (imaging only if high-risk sport, persistent splenomegaly or uncertain timing); (2) persistent fatigue beyond 12 weeks requires formal CFS/ME assessment (overlap with post-viral fatigue is well-documented — 10–15% of EBV patients develop CFS/ME); (3) persistent cervical lymphadenopathy beyond 6 weeks requires lymphoma exclusion, as EBV is causally associated with Hodgkin's lymphoma and several non-Hodgkin lymphomas. GPs should schedule a proactive 4–6 week review for every confirmed EBV case — not leave it to the patient to return. This review prevents the "lost to follow-up" scenario that delays diagnosis of both post-viral fatigue and EBV-associated lymphoma.
