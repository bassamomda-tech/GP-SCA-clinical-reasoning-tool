Safety

Red Flags — Penetrating Injury, Chemical Burns & High-Velocity FB

High-velocity mechanism (grinding, drilling, hammering metal on metal, explosion) — even without visual loss or severe pain Possible intraocular foreign body (IOFB) / open globe. → Stop examination. Do NOT irrigate, apply pressure, perform tonometry or attempt FB removal. Rigid eye shield (not an eye pad). Avoid nose-blowing, bending and straining. Nil by mouth. Analgesia and antiemetic as required. Assess tetanus status per local guidance. Urgent emergency ophthalmology/ED transfer (999 if systemic instability, major trauma or no safe alternative transport). Thin-slice CT orbits if requested by the emergency/ophthalmology pathway; do not arrange MRI until a metallic IOFB has been excluded.
Chemical splash to eye (acid or alkali) + severe burning pain + whitened cornea + chemosis Ocular chemical burn — alkali burns (bleach, cement, ammonia) more dangerous than acid (continued penetration). → Start irrigation immediately with copious tap water or 0.9% saline. Do not delay irrigation to obtain history, visual acuity, fluorescein or pH. Remove contact lenses. Continue until ocular surface pH is neutral or near-neutral (usually approximately 6.5–8.5, per local ophthalmology guidance) — there is no fixed time endpoint; severe alkali injuries may need substantially longer irrigation and large fluid volumes. Recheck pH with pH paper after irrigation has stopped for several minutes. Remove retained particulate matter from the fornices, including by lid eversion where feasible. A topical anaesthetic may be used by a trained clinician to facilitate irrigation but must not delay it. Then arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment, particularly for alkali exposure, persistent abnormal pH, corneal haze, epithelial loss, limbal ischaemia or reduced vision. 999 only for severe exposure requiring immediate transfer, systemic exposure/instability or no safe alternative transport.
Significant blunt trauma + orbital haematoma + enophthalmos or proptosis + restricted eye movement + diplopia Orbital blow-out fracture with possible ruptured globe. → Emergency ophthalmology/ED + CT orbits (999 if major trauma or systemic instability). Do not apply pressure. No nose-blowing (orbital emphysema).
FB mechanism + worsening eye pain + increasing redness + mucopurulent discharge + photophobia 24-48h post-injury Corneal ulcer / bacterial keratitis complicating corneal abrasion or retained FB. → Same-day ophthalmology. Slit lamp examination. Topical antibiotic + urgent culture swab.
Metallic corneal FB + rust ring (rust can develop within hours) Corneal rust ring from oxidation of metallic FB. → Refer urgently for corneal rust-ring removal if central, deep, extensive, associated with reduced visual acuity, or if the clinician is not trained and equipped to remove it. Peripheral superficial rust rings may be managed according to the local ophthalmology pathway, but should not be repeatedly manipulated in primary care.
Differential diagnosis (not a retained FB) — welder's arc/UV exposure + bilateral burning eye pain + photophobia + blepharospasm hours after exposure Photokeratoconjunctivitis (arc eye / welder's flash). → Topical anaesthetic for examination only — never prescribe. Management is usually removal from UV exposure, oral analgesia and lubricant/antibiotic according to local policy; cyclopentolate is not routinely required in primary care. Review if symptoms persist. Same-day ophthalmology if reduced vision, corneal opacity, atypical features, severe unilateral disease or failure to improve.
The chemical eye burn is the true ophthalmic emergency in the foreign body context — alkali burns (from bleach, cement dust, ammonia, calcium hydroxide) cause progressive penetrating injury because hydroxide ions continue to saponify cell membranes and penetrate into the anterior chamber, potentially reaching the lens, trabecular meshwork, and ciliary body. The damage is time-dependent: any delay in irrigation increases the risk of permanent corneal scarring, limbal stem cell destruction, and potentially severe visual loss. The initial management priority is immediate, continuous, copious irrigation — before transport, before visual acuity measurement, before phone calls. Every GP surgery should have equipment for eye irrigation: a Morgan lens or standard IV giving set and normal saline. The target is a neutral or near-neutral ocular surface pH (usually approximately 6.5–8.5, per local ophthalmology guidance), measured with pH paper in the lower fornix after irrigation has stopped for several minutes — there is no fixed time endpoint. If the pH does not normalise: continue irrigation and look for retained particulate chemical (cement, lime) in the fornices — evert the lids where feasible and sweep the fornices with a moistened cotton bud. Then arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment.

Types of Ocular Foreign Body — Classification

Superficial corneal/conjunctival FB (most common)
Mechanism: dust, grit, metal particles, insects, plant material. Usually caught by blink reflex — may embed under upper eyelid (subtarsal FB) causing repeated linear corneal abrasions on vertical blinking. History: foreign body sensation, tearing, blepharospasm, photophobia. Examination: fluorescein + cobalt blue light (corneal staining pattern — linear vertical abrasion = subtarsal FB, not visible on corneal surface).
Subtarsal foreign body
Located under the upper eyelid (tarsal plate — inner surface). Not visible without upper lid eversion. Causes a characteristic vertical corneal abrasion pattern (the FB scratches the cornea with every blink — like a windscreen wiper). Upper lid eversion technique: patient looks down, apply cotton bud or fingers at base of upper lid, fold lid upward. The FB is often immediately visible — remove only a clearly visible superficial FB with a moistened cotton bud. Do not repeatedly manipulate the lid if the patient is distressed; in a child or anxious patient avoid forceful manoeuvres and use an appropriately trained clinician. Recheck the cornea with fluorescein after removal; refer if symptoms or staining persist.
Corneal abrasion (without retained FB)
Scratch to corneal epithelium from FB, fingernail, contact lens, plant material. Intense pain (corneal nerve density is highest in the body). Photophobia. Tearing. Fluorescein staining: epithelial defect (bright green patch under cobalt blue light). Management: oral analgesia, lubricant and topical antibiotic per local eye-injury protocol. Usually heals within days. Contact lens-related abrasion: risk of Pseudomonas infection — urgent assessment with a lower threshold for same-day ophthalmology (see Step 4).
Intraocular FB (IOFB)
High velocity mechanism. May be relatively pain-free initially (small entry wound seals). Seidel test (fluorescein streaming from the wound indicating aqueous leak) indicates penetrating injury; a negative Seidel test does not exclude an open globe, particularly where the wound is self-sealing. Risk: endophthalmitis within 24-72h (Bacillus cereus from metal — extremely destructive). Metallic IOFB causes siderosis bulbi (iron deposits gradually destroying retina) or chalcosis (copper). → Emergency ophthalmology/ED; thin-slice CT orbits (do not arrange MRI until a metallic IOFB has been excluded).
The subtarsal foreign body is one of the most frequently missed causes of persistent ocular foreign body sensation in primary care — a tiny particle of grit, metal, or plant material lodged under the upper eyelid causes relentless vertical corneal abrasions with every blink, producing a foreign body sensation that persists despite the patient believing they have 'got it out.' The characteristic clue is the vertical corneal abrasion pattern on fluorescein staining — multiple parallel vertical scratches on the superior cornea (where the tarsal plate sweeps across the cornea on blinking) rather than a single focal abrasion. Upper lid eversion is particularly important when there is persistent foreign-body sensation, vertical linear corneal staining, persisting symptoms after a visible FB has been removed, or suspected chemical or particulate exposure (do not evert if an open globe is suspected; in a child or anxious patient avoid forceful manoeuvres and use an appropriately trained clinician). The technique: ask the patient to look down (this relaxes the levator muscle and makes the lid more mobile), place a cotton bud or two fingers horizontally at the base (lower margin) of the upper lid at the tarsal plate, and fold the upper lid upward over the cotton bud. The inner surface of the lid is now visible and the subtarsal FB is often immediately apparent.

Assessment — History, Examination & Equipment

History
Mechanism: high-velocity (drill, grinder, hammer) vs low-velocity (dust, grit). Nature of FB: metallic (rust risk, IOFB risk) vs organic (higher infection risk) vs glass (not always visible on X-ray — CT preferred). Time since injury (rust ring forms within hours for metal). Eye protection worn? Contact lens wearer (increases infection risk from any corneal break). Chemical exposure (chemical name/concentration, duration of exposure)? Welding exposure. Estimated velocity of the FB. Tetanus immunisation status.
Examination (in order)
Chemical injury: begin irrigation immediately; obtain visual acuity once irrigation is underway or complete. 1. Visual acuity (unless chemical irrigation takes priority) — each eye separately, unaided and corrected where relevant; near vision if a distance chart is unavailable; count fingers or light perception if the eye cannot be opened. 2. Pupils and RAPD. 3. External/periocular injury and globe position. 4. Cornea, conjunctiva and anterior chamber: visible FB, laceration, hyphaema, irregular pupil. 5. Fluorescein and cobalt-blue examination. 6. Seidel test only if appropriate and without applying pressure — a negative Seidel test does not exclude an open globe. 7. Upper-lid eversion where there is foreign-body sensation or linear vertical staining (not if open globe suspected). 8. Check the fornices where chemical exposure or particulate contamination is suspected. Visual fields where relevant. Stop the examination if an open globe is suspected.
Equipment
Essential: VA chart (near-vision card or Snellen) · Pen torch + cobalt blue filter (or Wood's lamp) · Fluorescein strips (moisten with single drop of saline or tetracaine — do not use fluorescein + benzalkonium chloride drops with contact lenses still in) · Tetracaine 0.5% drops (diagnostic anaesthesia — never prescribe for home use) · Cotton buds (moist, for subtarsal FB removal) · Irrigation set + normal saline (chemical splash) · pH paper · Rigid eye shield. Any corneal FB removal beyond irrigation/cotton bud needs a slit lamp and a trained clinician.
Fluorescein dye is an essential diagnostic tool for corneal assessment — it stains damaged corneal epithelium bright green under cobalt blue light, making corneal abrasions, ulcers, and foreign body tracks visible that are invisible in white light. The technique: moisten a fluorescein strip with a drop of saline (or tetracaine — the anaesthetic facilitates patient cooperation by reducing blepharospasm), touch the lower conjunctival fornix with the wet strip, ask the patient to blink to distribute the dye, then examine with cobalt blue light (on a standard pen torch with a blue filter, or a dedicated fluorescein examination light). The interpretation: a single bright green patch = corneal abrasion or FB site; multiple parallel vertical lines on the superior cornea = subtarsal FB; dendritic (branching) pattern = herpes simplex keratitis (not a foreign body — antiviral urgently); large diffuse staining with ring lesion = Acanthamoeba keratitis (contact lens wearers — emergency ophthalmology). GPs should be proficient in fluorescein examination — it takes 2 minutes and provides definitive diagnosis in the majority of corneal presentations.

Intraocular FB — Identification Cues

Features suggesting penetrating injury
Mechanism: high-velocity (metallic shard from grinding, exploding machinery, nail gun, military). Wound that appears small or self-sealing. Reduced vision. Teardrop-shaped pupil (iris prolapse sealing a corneal/limbal wound). Peaked pupil pointing toward the wound. Hyphaema (blood in anterior chamber). Seidel test positive: fluorescein streaming from the entry wound (aqueous leaking under positive IOP). A negative Seidel test does not exclude an open-globe injury — if the mechanism or examination remains concerning, treat as a penetrating injury and refer urgently. High-velocity mechanisms should raise concern even without visual loss or severe pain. Any sub-conjunctival haemorrhage where the posterior border is not visible.
CT orbits — the correct imaging for IOFB
CT orbits (axial and coronal cuts, thin slices 1-2mm) is the standard imaging for suspected IOFB. Accuracy depends on FB composition, size, slice thickness, scanner protocol and radiology expertise. CT does not replace ophthalmology assessment and may not reliably exclude radiolucent organic material such as wood. Do not arrange MRI until a metallic intraocular foreign body has been excluded (magnetic force on an intraocular metal particle can cause catastrophic retinal/vitreous injury). CT is performed before any surgical exploration — identifies location, size, number, and track of FB for operative planning.
Corneal injury in contact lens wearers
Contact lens wearers are at particularly high risk of Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis after any corneal break. A contact-lens wearer with a corneal epithelial defect requires urgent assessment and a lower threshold for same-day ophthalmology review. Same-day review is required if there is infiltrate, ulcer, discharge, photophobia, reduced vision, significant pain, worsening symptoms or an incomplete examination. Follow the local pathway for uncomplicated small abrasions. Remove lenses immediately; do not resume until the cornea is fully healed and the eye is symptom-free. Do not use chloramphenicol if antimicrobial treatment is required for a contact-lens-related corneal defect (no reliable Pseudomonas cover); a topical fluoroquinolone may be required, following the local ophthalmology/antimicrobial pathway. Suspect microbial keratitis if there is an infiltrate, increasing pain, photophobia, discharge or reduced vision.
The Seidel test is a critical sign that every GP performing eye examinations should know — if fluorescein is applied to a corneal wound and the dye streams away from the wound site in a flowing rivulet (rather than remaining pooled), this indicates that aqueous humour is leaking from the anterior chamber through the wound (positive Seidel test). This is diagnostic of a penetrating corneal injury with an open globe — the intraocular pressure is maintaining a flow of aqueous outward through the wound. A positive Seidel test is an absolute indication for emergency ophthalmology referral: do not apply any pressure to the eye, do not irrigate (unless chemical injury — in which case gentle irrigation is still appropriate), apply a rigid eye shield (not a pad), keep nil by mouth and arrange emergency ophthalmology/ED transfer (999 if systemically unstable, major trauma or no safe alternative transport). A negative Seidel test does not exclude a penetrating injury, particularly where the wound is self-sealing. The Seidel test takes 30 seconds — applying fluorescein and examining for streaming flow under cobalt blue light is a simple but potentially sight-saving assessment.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Emergency ophthalmology / ED (999 if criteria below)
Suspected IOFB (high velocity + any eye signs) · Chemical burn (after irrigation) · Ruptured globe (Seidel positive, peaked pupil, hyphaema, reduced VA) · Orbital blow-out fracture with significant eye injury · Significant visual loss. 999: systemic instability, major trauma, severe chemical exposure requiring immediate transfer, severe pain with inability to open the eye, or no safe alternative transport. Use the local (Bolton/Greater Manchester) emergency eye care contact pathway.
Ophthalmology (same-day urgent)
Corneal rust ring that is central, deep, extensive, with reduced VA, or where the clinician is not trained/equipped to remove it · Contact lens wearer with corneal defect plus infiltrate, ulcer, discharge, photophobia, reduced vision, significant pain, worsening symptoms or incomplete examination · Corneal FB that is central, deep, not completely visualised or not removable by irrigation/cotton bud · Corneal abrasion failing to improve within 24–48h · Arc eye with reduced vision, corneal opacity, atypical features, severe unilateral disease or failure to improve · Organic FB that is deep, incompletely removed, central, with infiltrate or worsening symptoms
Ophthalmology (next available, within 24-48h)
Corneal FB successfully removed but residual staining or symptoms · Uncomplicated contact-lens abrasion per local pathway · Any concern about complete FB removal
GP management
Superficial conjunctival or very superficial corneal FB removed by irrigation or moistened cotton bud + fluorescein clear: topical antibiotic prophylaxis according to the local eye-injury protocol + oral analgesia + clear 24-hour safety-netting. Simple corneal abrasion (no FB): lubricant, antibiotic per local protocol, avoid contact lenses until fully healed and symptom-free, review according to risk. Chloramphenicol is not suitable for contact-lens-related injuries (no adequate Pseudomonas cover).
The rust ring removal decision is one where GPs must refer rather than attempt — an iron-containing metallic FB on the cornea begins to oxidise within hours, forming an iron-oxide ring in the surrounding corneal stroma. The rust ring is not inert — it continues to release iron ions that are directly toxic to keratocytes (corneal stromal cells), causing progressive corneal stromal damage. Rust ring removal requires: a slit lamp (for precise 3D visualisation), local anaesthesia (topical), and either a sterile hypodermic needle or an ophthalmic rotary drill (burr) to mechanically debride the ring. This procedure requires ophthalmic training and equipment that is not available in primary care. GPs who identify a rust ring should refer according to the local ophthalmology pathway (urgently if central, deep, extensive or with reduced visual acuity) — attempting to dig out a rust ring with a hypodermic needle in primary care under a hand torch risks corneal perforation and worsening the injury.

Treat

FB Removal — Primary Care Limits & Competence

Preparation
Instil tetracaine 0.5% eye drops (1 drop — topical anaesthetic). Wait 30-60 seconds for full effect. Warn patient: eye will feel numb for approximately 15-20 minutes — do not rub during this time (risk of corneal damage without protective sensation). NEVER prescribe tetracaine for home use (corneal anaesthesia prevents protective sensation → corneal damage undetected).
Conjunctival FB removal
Only if the globe is intact, the FB is clearly visible, visual acuity is preserved and the clinician is trained and confident. For visible conjunctival FB (not on cornea): using cobalt blue + fluorescein to identify exact location. Moisten cotton bud with saline. Gently sweep over the FB in one smooth motion — most conjunctival FBs dislodge easily. Irrigate with saline wash. Recheck with fluorescein: no residual staining = successful removal.
Superficial corneal FB removal (visible, no Seidel)
In primary care, limit removal to irrigation or a moistened cotton bud for a superficial conjunctival or very superficial corneal foreign body when the globe is intact, the foreign body is clearly visible, visual acuity is preserved and the clinician is trained and confident. Do not use a needle or burr unless specifically trained, equipped and authorised under the local ophthalmic pathway — needle removal requires slit-lamp magnification, good illumination, stable patient positioning and head support, appropriate supervision or local governance, and an immediate ophthalmology escalation pathway. Reassess visual acuity and fluorescein after removal. Refer if: central cornea, rust ring, unable to remove, any doubt.
Do NOT attempt
Central corneal FB (over visual axis) · Deep or stromal FB · Rust ring · FB associated with reduced VA or RAPD · Positive or uncertain Seidel test · Hypopyon, corneal infiltrate or anterior-chamber abnormality · FB not completely visualised · Intraocular FB (any mechanism with penetrating features) · Contact lens wearer, unless the local ophthalmic service specifically supports community management
Corneal foreign-body removal with a needle or burr is a slit-lamp procedure — the cornea is thin, and an instrument used without magnification, good illumination and stable head support risks iatrogenic perforation or a deeper epithelial defect. It should only be undertaken by an appropriately trained and experienced clinician working within a defined local pathway with clear escalation arrangements (RCOphth emergency eye care guidance). In general practice, limit removal to irrigation or a moistened cotton bud. GPs who are not trained, equipped and authorised should refer — a confident referral to ophthalmology or urgent care with a slit lamp is better than an inadvertent iatrogenic corneal injury.

Post-Removal Care & Corneal Abrasion Management

Topical antibiotic therapy
Do not prescribe antibiotics automatically for every trivial abrasion — distinguish uncomplicated minor abrasion, retained/removed FB, contact-lens-related abrasion, organic/contaminated injury, immunocompromised patient and suspected microbial keratitis. For a simple traumatic abrasion or superficial foreign body after complete removal, follow the local eye-injury protocol regarding topical antibiotic prophylaxis (chloramphenicol is commonly used in UK practice). Contact lens wearers: chloramphenicol is not suitable because it does not provide adequate Pseudomonas cover, and fusidic acid is not an alternative — a topical fluoroquinolone may be required, following the local ophthalmology/antimicrobial pathway. Do not prescribe topical steroid-containing eye drops without ophthalmology advice (risk of worsening infection, herpes simplex reactivation, glaucoma).
Pain management
Topical NSAID drops are an ophthalmology/local-protocol decision rather than a routine GP prescription. Systemic paracetamol 1g QDS + ibuprofen 400mg TDS (if no contraindication) for pain. Eye patch: no longer routinely recommended (does not speed healing + impairs binocular vision + increases bacterial contamination risk). Lubricant drops (hypromellose 0.3% or sodium hyaluronate): soothes and protects the exposed stroma.
Contact lens guidance post-abrasion
Remove lenses immediately. No contact lens wear until the cornea is fully healed (fluorescein clear) and the eye is symptom-free. Then recommence daily disposable lenses first. Extended-wear lenses: reconsider and discuss risk with optician. Any recurring abrasions from CL use: optician review for lens fitting assessment.
Review timeline
Arrange review or ophthalmology advice according to risk — size is only one factor; the visual axis, contact-lens use, depth, infection risk, visual acuity and symptom trajectory are more important. Urgent review is required for persistent pain, photophobia, reduced vision, infiltrate, discharge, worsening redness, incomplete foreign-body removal or failure to improve within 24–48 hours. Recurrent pain weeks after the injury may represent recurrent corneal erosion — refer to ophthalmology/optometry rather than making a fixed diagnosis.
Recurrent corneal erosion syndrome (RCES) is an important but frequently missed complication of corneal abrasion — it can occur weeks after a significant corneal abrasion. The mechanism: the new corneal epithelium that regrows over the abrasion does not form adequate hemidesmosome attachments to Bowman's membrane, making it prone to re-avulsion during sleep (particularly when the eyelid moves across the dry ocular surface during REM sleep). The patient wakes suddenly with severe eye pain — exactly reproducing the sensation of the original injury. The clue: pain awakening the patient from sleep, weeks after an original corneal abrasion, with fluorescein showing a recurrent abrasion in the same location. Treatment: preservative-free lubricant ointment (Lacri-Lube or VitA-POS) applied at bedtime for several months — this lubricates the eyelid-corneal interface during sleep and prevents the avulsing force. Ophthalmology referral for: recurrent episodes despite ointment (anterior stromal puncture, phototherapeutic keratectomy).

Lifestyle

Eye Protection, Occupational Safety & Prevention

Eye protection mandatory occupations and activities Legal requirement under COSHH and PPEWR (Personal Protective Equipment at Work Regulations 1992): grinding (BS EN 166 safety spectacles or full-face shield), welding (shade-rated welding visor), wood/metal machining, angle grinding, nail guns, chemical handling. DIY activities: drilling masonry, cutting metal, mowing grass near fences. Gardening: pruning, hedge cutting (plant material FBs are particularly infection-prone). Shooting sports: ballistic-rated eye protection.
Contact lens safety and infection prevention Never sleep in daily disposable or conventional monthly lenses (even one night increases the risk of microbial keratitis). Wash hands before handling lenses. Replace lens case monthly. Never top up solution — discard and refill. Never use tap water to rinse lenses or case (Acanthamoeba). Discard lenses if eye becomes red or uncomfortable. Attendance at annual optician contact lens check.
Chemical eye protection Safety goggles (not glasses — side exposure risk) for any chemical handling at work or at home (bleach, drain cleaner, oven cleaner, cement). Keep an eye wash station accessible in any workplace with chemical hazard. Train staff in first-aid eye irrigation technique. At home: keep a 500ml bottle of saline (or clean water) accessible in kitchens and bathrooms where chemicals are used.
Children and eye safety Children have a disproportionate rate of eye injuries from: toys with projectiles, aerosol products, plant material, batteries (button batteries particularly dangerous — caustic if lodged in eye or orbit). Age-appropriate toys (BSEN71 certification). Supervise children when using potentially hazardous items. Teach children never to point anything at another person's face.
Post-FB education for welders Arc eye (welder's flash / photokeratoconjunctivitis): wear appropriate shade-rated welding visor for all arc welding (shade rating appropriate to the welding process). UV radiation from welding arc does not require direct gaze to cause damage — even reflected UV causes arc eye. The delay of several hours between exposure and symptom onset means welders often do not connect the symptoms to the exposure from hours earlier. Education is key.
Farm and rural eye injuries Agricultural workers have high rates of ocular FB from grain dust, straw, plant material. Organic FBs are at higher risk of fungal keratitis (Fusarium, Aspergillus) — organic or soil-contaminated material increases the risk of microbial keratitis, including fungal infection: arrange a lower-threshold ophthalmology review, particularly if the FB is deep, incompletely removed, central, associated with an infiltrate, or symptoms worsen. A small superficial plant fragment completely removed with a clear fluorescein examination does not automatically need same-day ophthalmology, but give clear follow-up and safety-netting. Check tetanus status for soil-contaminated injuries. Any deteriorating corneal abrasion in a farm worker = suspect fungal keratitis.
Work-related eye injury reporting RIDDOR 2013 reporting: the employer (not the GP) must report to the HSE any work-related injury likely to cause permanent loss or reduction of sight, any burn (including a chemical burn) causing significant damage to the eyes, and any injury causing more than 7 consecutive days' incapacity for work. GP documentation of work-related eye injuries should include: mechanism, exposure details, whether PPE was worn, employer details. Occupational health assessment if recurrent work-related eye injuries.
Recovery and visual rehabilitation Most superficial corneal FBs and abrasions usually heal completely within days with no long-term visual effects. Significant corneal injury (deep stromal, central, or infection) may cause corneal scarring — reduced visual acuity + glare. Referral to optometry: new glasses/contact lens prescription after corneal healing. Corneal specialist (ophthalmology): corneal transplant (DALK or PK) for significant visually-impairing scar. Realistic counselling: most GP-managed FBs heal completely.
The fungal keratitis risk from organic foreign bodies (plant material, soil, grain) is a serious and time-sensitive condition that differentiates plant/agricultural FB from metallic FB management — fungal keratitis (caused by Fusarium solani, Aspergillus fumigatus, Candida, and other fungi) is typically preceded by a corneal injury with organic material. The clinical presentation differs from bacterial keratitis: slower onset (days rather than hours), grey-white corneal infiltrate with feathery borders, satellite lesions, immune ring, and hypopyon. The diagnosis is confirmed by corneal scraping + microscopy and culture (specialist procedure). Treatment requires topical antifungal (natamycin 5% eye drops — not widely available in UK; voriconazole 1% or itraconazole depending on organism). Standard chloramphenicol eye drops provide no antifungal coverage. GPs who treat a corneal abrasion from organic material and find the patient returning at 48-72h with worsening symptoms (instead of the expected improvement) should suspect fungal keratitis and refer urgently to ophthalmology.

Follow-Up, Documentation & Medicolegal Standards

Documentation standard for every FB consultation
Mechanism and estimated velocity. Eye protection used. Chemical name/concentration and duration of exposure. Initial and post-procedure visual acuity (each eye). Pupils/RAPD. Seidel result, if performed. FB site, depth, material and size. Fluorescein findings, including residual staining. Upper-lid eversion result. Contact-lens use. Tetanus status. Procedure performed. Referral discussion and destination. Plan: treatment prescribed, review arrangements, safety-netting given.
Review timeline
Review according to risk, not abrasion size alone. Urgent review or same-day ophthalmology for persistent pain, photophobia, reduced vision, infiltrate, discharge, worsening redness, incomplete FB removal or failure to improve within 24–48 hours. Return at any time: increased pain, visual change, photophobia worsening.
Occupational injury documentation
Record: mechanism, whether PPE was worn, nature of FB (metallic/organic/chemical). Advise patient to report to occupational health if work-related. RIDDOR notification if qualifying criteria met. Fitness for work: advise against driving until VA certified normal. Welders: no welding until ophthalmology clears if IOFB cannot be excluded.
Eye injury contact lens special note
Any corneal injury in a contact lens wearer: remove lenses immediately and advise NO contact lens use until the cornea is fully healed and the eye is symptom-free. Risk of Pseudomonas keratitis is substantially higher in CL wearers — routine GP antibiotic coverage (chloramphenicol) does not cover Pseudomonas adequately.
Tetanus
Check tetanus immunisation status in penetrating, contaminated, soil-related or significant periocular injuries and follow current UK immunisation guidance. Tetanus vaccination does not replace ophthalmology assessment or antimicrobial treatment where indicated.

IOFB suspected (high velocity + any eye signs) · Chemical burn (after immediate irrigation) · Positive Seidel test · Reduced VA after injury · RAPD present
Same-day ophthalmology
Rust ring (central, deep, extensive, reduced VA, or clinician not trained/equipped) · Contact lens wearer with corneal defect plus infiltrate, discharge, photophobia, reduced vision, significant pain, worsening or incomplete examination · Organic FB that is deep, incompletely removed, central, with infiltrate or worsening · Arc eye with reduced vision, corneal opacity, atypical features or failure to improve
The visual acuity documentation in eye injury consultations is a medicolegal standard that cannot be overlooked — a GP who treats an eye injury without measuring and documenting visual acuity has an incomplete clinical record. If the patient subsequently develops visual loss and claims it was caused by the injury (or by inadequate treatment at the GP consultation), the absence of a documented VA measurement makes it impossible to establish whether the vision was normal at the time of the GP assessment. The documentation should state: 'VA right: 6/6 (or specific near vision equivalent). VA left: 6/6 (or specific result). Measured with [near-vision card / Snellen chart / patient's reading glasses].' If the patient was unable to cooperate with VA testing (severe photophobia, blepharospasm from the injury), document: 'VA measurement attempted — patient unable to cooperate due to blepharospasm and photophobia. Referred to ophthalmology for formal assessment.' This protects the patient and the GP.

This guide separates immediate first aid and emergency transfer, superficial conjunctival FB removal, corneal FB removal by an experienced slit-lamp practitioner, and injuries requiring ophthalmology without attempted removal. Emergency eye care should be delivered by appropriately trained staff within a defined local pathway (Bolton/Greater Manchester emergency eye care) with clear escalation arrangements.
