1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude serious causes first

Haematospermia is usually benign and self-limiting, particularly in men under 40 with a single episode. Age over 40, persistence or recurrence, and blood in the urine as well are the features that move a man into a higher-risk group.

Prostate feels malignant — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Hard, nodular, irregular or asymmetric prostate on DRE → suspected cancer pathway referral for prostate cancer, whatever the PSA.
PSA above the age-specific range — NICE NG12 40–49 >2.5; 50–59 >3.5; 60–69 >4.5; 70–79 >6.5 ng/mL (µg/L) → suspected cancer pathway referral. Under 40 and 80 or over: clinical judgement.
Visible haematuria — NICE NG12 Aged 45 or over with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or visible haematuria persisting or recurring after a treated UTI → suspected cancer pathway referral (bladder/renal). Visible haematuria is also an NG12 reason to consider PSA and DRE.
Age over 40 + persistent or recurrent Repeated episodes, or blood persisting over several weeks → PSA, DRE and urology referral even if both are normal.
Testicular change — NICE NG12 Non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of a testis → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral; unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms → consider urgent direct-access ultrasound.
Fever, rigors, perineal pain Possible acute bacterial prostatitis or epididymo-orchitis with sepsis (temperature >38°C, rigors, tachycardia, hypotension) → same-day / emergency assessment.
Retention Painful inability to pass urine (prostatitis, clot) → same-day catheterisation and urology.
Weight loss, bone pain, night sweats Systemic features alongside haematospermia → examine thoroughly, PSA, and the relevant NG12 route.
Bleeding elsewhere Bruising, epistaxis, gum bleeding or haematuria on anticoagulants → check INR (warfarin) or FBC and clotting; think coagulopathy or liver disease.
Travel to endemic areas Fresh-water exposure in sub-Saharan Africa or the Nile valley — urogenital schistosomiasis (Schistosoma haematobium) is a recognised cause.
NG12 has no recommendation specific to haematospermia, so the safety step borrows the adjacent NG12 criteria: a prostate that feels malignant is a direct referral, a PSA above the age-specific range is a referral, and visible haematuria has its own bladder-cancer route. Beyond that, risk is stratified by age, persistence and associated haematuria — the approach taken in the international EAU guidelines on sexual and reproductive health (haemospermia section), which advise PSA and DRE in counselled men over 40. The absolute cancer risk is small but real: in a US screening cohort, 13.7% of men reporting haematospermia were diagnosed with prostate cancer versus 6.5% overall (Han et al, J Urol 2004), and a UK tertiary series of 393 men investigated for haematospermia found prostate cancer in 5.3% overall and 7.2% of those aged 40 or over (Current Urology 2022). Fever with perineal pain needs same-day assessment because acute bacterial prostatitis can progress to sepsis or retention.
2
Diagnose

History — Characterise the haematospermia

A systematic history sorts most men into the "reassure" or the "assess further" group.

Duration and frequency
First episode vs recurrent; how many ejaculations affected; how long it has persisted. Persistence or recurrence is the main driver of further investigation.
Is it really semen?
Blood seen only in a condom may be from the partner (e.g. vaginal or cervical bleeding); blood in urine as well as semen changes the pathway.
Colour description
Bright red (recent bleed) vs brown or dark (older blood); the amount is a poor guide to cause.
Associated pain
Dysuria, perineal, testicular or ejaculatory pain → infective or inflammatory cause.
Urinary symptoms
LUTS (frequency, nocturia, hesitancy, urgency, poor flow) or erectile dysfunction → NG12 prompts PSA and DRE; visible haematuria → bladder and renal pathway.
Sexual history
New or multiple partners, condom use, urethral discharge, symptoms in partners — STI risk and the need for testing and partner notification.
Recent procedures
Prostate biopsy, vasectomy, catheterisation, cystoscopy, radiotherapy or brachytherapy — iatrogenic cause, usually self-limiting.
Medical history and drugs
Hypertension, bleeding disorders, liver disease; warfarin, DOACs, antiplatelets.
Family history
Prostate cancer in a father or brother; BRCA2 mutation in the family; Black ethnicity — all raise prostate cancer risk.
Travel history
Fresh-water swimming or wading in schistosomiasis-endemic areas (sub-Saharan Africa, Nile valley).
Ideas, concerns, expectations
Most men fear cancer or an STI; some worry about fertility or partner safety. Name the concern early.
History does most of the risk stratification. Recent prostate biopsy or other instrumentation explains a large share of cases and needs no work-up beyond reassurance, while a young man with a single episode, no other symptoms and a normal examination is managed with explanation and review. Age over 40 with persistence or recurrence, urinary symptoms, haematuria, or a strong family history moves him towards PSA, DRE and a lower threshold for urology. Checking that the blood is truly from the ejaculate (not the partner, not the urine) prevents both false reassurance and needless investigation. Exploring the man's own fear — usually cancer, sometimes infection or fertility — shapes an explanation that he will actually accept.
3
Diagnose

Classification — Identify likely aetiology

Classify by most likely cause group to direct investigations efficiently.

Iatrogenic
Post-prostate biopsy (very common, can last several weeks), vasectomy, catheterisation, cystoscopy, radiotherapy → reassure; review if it persists beyond the expected period.
Infective / inflammatory
Prostatitis, epididymo-orchitis, urethritis (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, Mycoplasma genitalium), seminal vesiculitis, UTI → treat the cause.
Idiopathic
No identifiable cause after assessment — the commonest outcome in younger men; usually resolves spontaneously.
Vascular
Uncontrolled hypertension, prostatic varices, vascular malformations → control BP; imaging if persistent.
Structural / obstructive
Seminal vesicle or ejaculatory duct cysts and calculi, midline prostatic cysts, benign prostatic enlargement → urology-led imaging (transrectal ultrasound or MRI).
Systemic
Anticoagulation or antiplatelets, bleeding disorders, liver disease → review medicines and clotting.
Malignant
Prostate cancer (the main concern); rarely bladder, testicular or seminal vesicle tumours → NG12 routes.
Parasitic
Schistosomiasis after travel → urine microscopy and serology; infectious diseases referral.
Classification drives investigation and management. Idiopathic haematospermia (most common in young men) needs minimal work-up, and iatrogenic bleeding after biopsy is expected and self-limiting. Infective causes need targeted microbiological tests and, for STIs, partner management. Structural causes such as seminal vesicle or ejaculatory duct cysts are found on specialist imaging and are the usual explanation for persistent haematospermia in otherwise well younger men. The key decision in primary care is not to find the exact cause but to separate the benign, self-limiting group from the higher-risk group that needs PSA, DRE and urology.
4
Diagnose

Examination — Targeted genito-urinary assessment

Always examine (with a chaperone offered). Findings significantly alter the management pathway.

Vital signs
BP (uncontrolled hypertension can contribute); temperature, pulse (sepsis from prostatitis or epididymo-orchitis).
Abdomen
Palpable bladder (retention), suprapubic or renal angle tenderness, masses.
External genitalia
Urethral discharge (STI); meatal lesions; penile lesions.
Scrotal exam
Testicular size, consistency and masses (testicular cancer); epididymal tenderness or swelling (epididymo-orchitis); varicocele.
Digital rectal exam (DRE)
Offer in all men over 40, and in younger men with persistent symptoms: size, consistency, nodularity, tenderness. Hard, nodular or irregular → NG12 referral. Exquisitely tender, boggy and hot → acute prostatitis (examine gently; avoid prostatic massage).
Lymph nodes
Inguinal lymphadenopathy → STI, lymphoma, or penile or anal pathology.
Signs of bleeding
Bruising, petechiae, stigmata of chronic liver disease.
Examination is where the NG12 criteria are triggered or excluded. A prostate that feels malignant on DRE is a suspected cancer pathway referral in its own right under NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), regardless of the PSA value, so DRE is not optional in the older man. Testicular examination can reveal a painless mass, which carries its own NG12 route. A tender prostate with fever points to acute bacterial prostatitis, where the examination should be gentle and the priority is antibiotics and a sepsis assessment. In the SCA, explaining why an intimate examination is needed, offering a chaperone and seeking consent are marked just as much as the findings.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — Risk-stratified workup

Investigations guided by age and risk. Avoid over-investigating young men with single episodes.

All men
Urine dipstick and MSU — UTI and haematuria; BP.
Men over 40 (or persistent)
PSA after counselling — interpret against the NG12 age-specific ranges. Avoid testing within 48 hours of ejaculation or vigorous exercise, or within 6 weeks of a prostate biopsy or cystoscopy; if infection is present, treat first and repeat once it has settled.
STI screen
Chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT on first-void urine; add blood-borne virus testing and refer to sexual health if risk is high or tests are positive.
Bleeding tendency
FBC, clotting, INR if on warfarin, if there is bleeding elsewhere, or if liver disease suspected; LFTs.
Haematuria
Visible haematuria → NG12 bladder route (45+). Non-visible haematuria on dipstick → confirm, check renal function and ACR, and follow NG12 for 60+ with dysuria or raised white cell count.
Travel history
Terminal urine for schistosome ova, schistosomiasis serology (can take 2–3 months after exposure to become positive), eosinophil count.
Do NOT routinely order
Transrectal ultrasound, MRI or semen culture — these are urology-led investigations.
Men under 40, single episode
If no red flags, no haematuria, normal examination and a negative urine test: no further tests — reassure and safety-net.
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) sets age-specific PSA thresholds for referral: above 2.5 ng/mL at 40–49, 3.5 at 50–59, 4.5 at 60–69 and 6.5 at 70–79, with clinical judgement outside those ages. Government PSA-testing guidance for GPs advises avoiding ejaculation and vigorous exercise in the 48 hours before a test and avoiding testing within 6 weeks of a prostate biopsy or cystoscopy, because each can raise PSA; prostatitis and UTI raise it too, so a result taken during infection should be repeated after treatment. Blood tests for coagulation are targeted, not routine. Specialist imaging (transrectal ultrasound, MRI) belongs to urology, where it is used for persistent haematospermia and for older men. In the young man with a single episode and a normal assessment, extra tests add anxiety without benefit.
6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to escalate

Suspected cancer pathway — NG12
Prostate feels malignant on DRE; PSA above the age-specific range; aged 45+ with unexplained visible haematuria (or persisting after treated UTI); testicular enlargement or change in shape or texture.
Same-day
Fever with perineal pain and systemic features (acute prostatitis, epididymo-orchitis with sepsis); acute urinary retention.
Urology (non-cancer pathway)
Age over 40 with persistent or recurrent haematospermia despite a normal PSA and DRE; any age with haematospermia persisting despite treatment of an identified cause; suspected cyst or calculus.
Sexual health clinic
STI confirmed or strongly suspected; partner notification; Mycoplasma genitalium or resistant gonorrhoea.
Haematology / anticoagulation
Coagulopathy identified; INR out of range; bleeding on anticoagulation needing a risk–benefit review.
Infectious diseases
Suspected or confirmed schistosomiasis.
Primary care manage
Age under 40, single or short-lived episode, no red flags, no haematuria, normal examination → reassure and safety-net.
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) directs a suspected cancer pathway referral when the prostate feels malignant or the PSA is above the age-specific range, and has separate routes for visible haematuria and testicular change. Haematospermia alone in an older man with a normal PSA and DRE does not meet an NG12 criterion, but persistence or recurrence still warrants urology assessment — the EAU guidance (international) stratifies these men as higher risk and uses MRI and transrectal ultrasound to look for structural causes and cancer. Since October 2023, NG12's suspected cancer pathway is aligned to the Faster Diagnosis Standard (diagnosis or exclusion within 28 days). The GP's role is risk stratification, correct routing and safety-netting, not definitive investigation of complex haematospermia.
7
Treat

Treatment — Cause-directed management

Treatment is cause-specific. Most idiopathic cases require reassurance only.

Idiopathic / Age <40
Reassurance 1st line
Usually self-limiting. No medicine needed. Explain that it is not harmful to a partner and is not in itself a sign of infection.
Acute bacterial prostatitis
Antibiotic NICE NG110
Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 14 days then review, or trimethoprim 200 mg twice daily for 14 days then review if a fluoroquinolone is not appropriate. Send an MSU first. Fluoroquinolones only when other recommended antibiotics are inappropriate (MHRA Drug Safety Update, January 2024).
Chlamydia confirmed
Doxycycline Antibiotic
100 mg twice daily for 7 days (BASHH chlamydia treatment update, 2018). Partner notification and treatment; avoid sex until both treated.
Hypertension-related
Optimise BP control
NICE NG136 target under 80: clinic <140/90 mmHg (home or ambulatory <135/85). May resolve with adequate BP control.
Anticoagulant-related
Review anticoagulation
Check INR if on warfarin. DOAC: review dose, renal function and indication. Do not stop without weighing the thrombotic risk — seek advice if unsure.
Not settling at reviewRe-examine (including DRE), PSA if not done, repeat urine tests; consider urology referral
Persistent or recurrentUrology referral for specialist imaging (MRI ± transrectal ultrasound) — at any age if troublesome, and with a lower threshold over 40
Reassurance is the most powerful intervention for idiopathic haematospermia — anxiety about the symptom often exceeds clinical risk. Antibiotics are only for a demonstrated or strongly suspected infection. For acute bacterial prostatitis, NICE NG110 recommends a 14-day course then review (not the 28 days sometimes quoted), with ciprofloxacin or trimethoprim; since January 2024 the MHRA has restricted systemic fluoroquinolones to situations where other recommended antibiotics are inappropriate, because of disabling and potentially long-lasting adverse effects. Confirmed chlamydia is treated with doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days per BASHH, with partner notification. Blood pressure and anticoagulation are optimised, not simply stopped.
8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Patient education and self-care

Reassurance first Many men are frightened. Explain that most cases, especially in younger men, have no serious cause and settle on their own.
Partner communication Blood in semen is not harmful to a partner. If an STI is found, the partner needs testing and treatment too.
Blood pressure Home BP monitoring if hypertensive; NICE NG136 home target below 135/85 mmHg for adults under 80.
STI prevention Condoms with new partners. Signpost to local sexual health services or NHS online STI testing.
Alcohol Keep to 14 units a week or less, spread over several days — heavy drinking affects liver function and bleeding.
Prostate awareness For men 50 and over — and younger men at higher risk (Black ethnicity, family history of prostate cancer or BRCA2) — explain the PSA test's benefits and limitations; there is no national prostate screening programme.
After a biopsy Expect blood in semen for several weeks after prostate biopsy — no action needed unless urine bleeding, fever or retention.
Sexual activity No need to abstain for benign haematospermia; abstain only while an STI is being treated.
Anxiety about haematospermia is often disproportionate to clinical risk, and clear, confident reassurance with a concrete plan reduces re-attendance and repeated testing. Specific advice lands better than general statements: what is expected after a biopsy, when sex is fine, what the partner needs to know. STI prevention matters because infection is a treatable cause in younger, sexually active men. Blood pressure control and alcohol moderation address the vascular and bleeding contributors. The prostate-awareness conversation follows the government's PSA-testing guidance for GPs, which supports informed choice rather than routine screening.
9
Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Review
Arrange a review (for example at 4–6 weeks, or after a course of treatment): has it resolved? Check infection response; repeat MSU if haematuria; repeat PSA if it was taken during infection.
Persisting
If haematospermia continues or recurs despite treatment → urology referral (urgency according to age and findings).
999 / emergency
Fever with rigors, perineal pain and feeling very unwell, or unable to pass urine → sepsis from prostatitis or acute retention.
Same-day GP
New visible blood in urine; acute testicular or scrotal pain (exclude torsion); worsening systemic symptoms; unable to tolerate antibiotics.
Re-refer if
PSA rises on repeat; DRE becomes abnormal; haematuria develops; weight loss, bone pain or night sweats appear.
Document
Age, episodes, examination findings including DRE, PSA with the age-specific range, urine results, plan and safety-net advice.
SCA phrasing
"Blood in the semen is alarming, but in most men it settles by itself and isn't caused by anything serious. Because you're over 40, I'd like to check your prostate with an examination and a blood test so we can be sure. If you notice blood in your urine, get a fever or can't pass water, please don't wait — contact us the same day."
The review serves two functions: confirming resolution (the majority) and catching the minority where pathology is emerging. A PSA taken during active prostatitis or UTI is unreliable and should be repeated once the infection has settled rather than acted on in isolation. Safety-netting for retention and sepsis is critical because acute prostatitis can escalate quickly, and new visible haematuria converts the problem into an NG12 bladder pathway. In the SCA, the examiner is looking for the balance between reassurance and appropriate testing: acknowledging the man's fear, explaining why age changes the approach, offering DRE and PSA with consent, and giving specific return advice.
