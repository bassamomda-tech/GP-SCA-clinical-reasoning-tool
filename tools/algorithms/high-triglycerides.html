Safety

Red Flags — Acute Pancreatitis & Chylomicronaemia Syndrome

Raised triglycerides at any level (pancreatitis can occur below 10 mmol/L, especially after fasting or vomiting) + severe epigastric pain radiating to back + nausea/vomiting Possible hypertriglyceridaemia-induced acute pancreatitis. → Urgent hospital assessment (999 if unwell); do not wait for a repeat fasting lipid profile. Hospital-based supportive treatment: analgesia, IV fluids where indicated, monitoring and early oral/enteral nutrition as tolerated — no routine nil by mouth (NICE NG104). Hypertriglyceridaemia-specific treatment, including insulin, is directed by the hospital team (particularly with diabetes or DKA); plasma exchange is not routine — only after specialist discussion in selected severe or refractory cases. Very high TG can interfere with pancreatic enzyme assays, so a normal amylase/lipase does not completely exclude pancreatitis when the picture is strongly suggestive — discuss with the laboratory and hospital team.
Very severe fasting triglycerides (>20 mmol/L) + eruptive xanthomata (small yellow-white papules on buttocks/elbows) + lipaemia retinalis (creamy vessel appearance on fundoscopy) or recurrent pancreatitis Suggests chylomicronaemia — familial chylomicronaemia syndrome (FCS), multifactorial/polygenic chylomicronaemia or type V hyperlipoproteinaemia, often with uncontrolled diabetes, alcohol, renal disease, pregnancy or drugs superimposed on genetic susceptibility. It does not by itself establish FCS. → Urgent specialist lipid assessment. Very-low-fat diet only under a specialist metabolic/lipid dietitian (risk of essential fatty-acid and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency). Volanesorsen only for genetically confirmed FCS under a specialist centre (NICE HST13).
Severely lipaemic blood sample (plasma appears milky/creamy) in fasting patient Chylomicronaemia — TG almost certainly >10 mmol/L. The laboratory may be unable to run accurate biochemistry on a lipaemic sample. Repeat fasted and non-fasted TG. Same-day assessment if symptomatic.
Raised triglycerides + hyperglycaemic symptoms, vomiting, abdominal pain or acute illness Diabetic dyslipidaemia — hypertriglyceridaemia may be the first sign of undiagnosed T2DM or DKA. If hyperglycaemia, polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, vomiting, abdominal pain or acute illness is present, check capillary glucose and blood ketones urgently and assess for DKA/HHS. HbA1c is useful for chronic glycaemic assessment but does not exclude an acute metabolic emergency. DKA can cause marked TG elevation through insulin deficiency impairing LPL activity.
Triglycerides >5 mmol/L + nephrotic syndrome (heavy proteinuria + oedema + hypoalbuminaemia) Secondary hypertriglyceridaemia from nephrotic syndrome (reduced LPL activity + increased VLDL synthesis). Nephrology urgently. TG will not normalise until underlying nephrotic syndrome treated.
Hypertriglyceridaemia-induced acute pancreatitis accounts for approximately 2-4% of all cases of acute pancreatitis (after gallstones and alcohol) and is the most important life-threatening complication of very high triglycerides. The pathophysiology: hydrolysis of excess triglycerides in pancreatic capillaries by local lipases releases free fatty acids that are directly cytotoxic to pancreatic acinar cells. The clinical trap: amylase and lipase levels may be falsely low or normal in hypertriglyceridaemia-induced pancreatitis because lipaemia interferes with the enzyme assay — a normal result does not completely exclude pancreatitis when the clinical picture is strongly suggestive — discuss with the laboratory and hospital team, who may use imaging. Management is hospital-based: supportive care (analgesia, IV fluids where indicated, monitoring and early oral/enteral nutrition as tolerated — NICE NG104 does not support routine prolonged starvation). Insulin and other TG-lowering measures are directed by the hospital team using local protocols with glucose and potassium monitoring; plasma exchange is considered only after specialist discussion in selected severe or refractory cases. Acute pancreatitis can occur with TG below 10 mmol/L, particularly after levels fall with fasting or vomiting.

Diagnose

Causes of Hypertriglyceridaemia — Classification

Primary (genetic) causes
Familial hypertriglyceridaemia (FHTG): most common primary cause. Single gene variant in TG metabolism (VLDL overproduction). TG usually 3-10 mmol/L. Familial combined hyperlipidaemia (FCH): elevated TG + elevated LDL (mixed pattern). Most common familial dyslipidaemia — CVD risk predominantly from LDL. Familial chylomicronaemia syndrome (FCS): biallelic LPL mutations — TG >20 mmol/L, pancreatitis risk, rare (1 in 1 million).
Secondary causes (most common in UK primary care)
Uncontrolled T2DM (insulin resistance reduces LPL activity). Hypothyroidism (reduces LPL + increases VLDL). Obesity and metabolic syndrome. Chronic alcohol excess (stimulates hepatic VLDL synthesis + inhibits LPL). Chronic kidney disease (reduced LPL activity). Nephrotic syndrome. Pregnancy (physiological — TG normally doubles in third trimester). Drugs: corticosteroids, thiazides, beta-blockers, retinoids (isotretinoin), antiretrovirals (PI-based), oestrogen-containing OCP.
TG action thresholds (NICE NG238)
<4.5 mmol/L (assess overall CVD risk; manage LDL-C/non-HDL-C, usually with a statin where indicated) · 4.5–9.9 mmol/L (confirm, address secondary causes; risk tools may underestimate CVD risk; specialist advice if non-HDL-C >7.5 mmol/L) · 10–20 mmol/L (fasting repeat after 5 days but within 2 weeks; specialist advice if still >10) · >20 mmol/L (urgent specialist review if not explained by excess alcohol or poor glycaemic control) · Any level + severe abdominal pain/vomiting (emergency assessment for possible acute pancreatitis)
The secondary causes of hypertriglyceridaemia are extremely common and must be excluded before attributing elevated TG to a primary genetic cause — in UK primary care, the vast majority of patients with TG above 3 mmol/L have a secondary cause. The most important to identify and treat: (1) uncontrolled T2DM — insulin deficiency or resistance reduces lipoprotein lipase activity, allowing VLDL and chylomicrons to accumulate; HbA1c should be checked in every patient with TG above 3 mmol/L; (2) hypothyroidism — reduces both hepatic LDL receptor activity (raising LDL) and LPL activity (raising TG); TSH should be checked in all dyslipidaemia workups; (3) alcohol — even moderate-to-heavy drinking can raise TG significantly; enquire specifically about alcohol use in every patient with elevated TG; (4) medications — corticosteroids, thiazide diuretics, and oestrogen-containing preparations can all raise TG meaningfully. The practical rule: treat the secondary cause first; re-check TG after the secondary cause has been addressed before considering primary pharmacological treatment for TG.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigation

History
Dietary history: alcohol intake (specific units), high-fat meals, sugary drinks (fructose markedly raises TG), fast food. Weight history and BMI trend. Diabetes or insulin resistance symptoms (polydipsia, polyuria, fatigue). Drug history: corticosteroids, thiazides, beta-blockers, isotretinoin, antiretrovirals, oestrogen OCP/HRT. Family history: premature CVD, pancreatitis, xanthomata. Pancreatitis history (previous TG-related episodes). Thyroid symptoms.
Examination
BMI + waist circumference (central obesity = metabolic syndrome). BP (hypertension cluster). Xanthomata: eruptive (tiny papules on buttocks/extensor surfaces — very high TG), tendon (Achilles, extensor tendons — hypercholesterolaemia), tuberous (on pressure areas — mixed). Xanthelasma (periorbital — not specific to TG). Lipaemia retinalis (fundoscopy — creamy vessels in TG >20 mmol/L). Acanthosis nigricans (insulin resistance). Hepatomegaly (fatty liver).
Investigations
Lipid profile (non-fasting for routine CVD assessment; fasting when TG ≥4.5 mmol/L — especially a 10–20 mmol/L repeat — LDL-C unreliable, chylomicronaemia suspected or result unexpected/discordant) · HbA1c + fasting glucose · TSH · LFTs + GGT (alcohol, NAFLD) · Renal function + urinalysis (CKD, nephrotic) · Capillary glucose + blood ketones (urgently if hyperglycaemic symptoms, vomiting, abdominal pain or acute illness — DKA/HHS; not triggered by a TG threshold) · ApoB (not routine in primary care — specialist test in mixed dyslipidaemia, LDL-C/non-HDL-C discordance, suspected familial combined hyperlipidaemia or persistent severe TG) · Non-HDL cholesterol (total cholesterol minus HDL — particularly useful when TG is raised: captures cholesterol in TG-rich remnant particles and remains interpretable when calculated LDL-C is unreliable; use alongside overall clinical risk assessment)
The Friedewald equation for calculating LDL (LDL = total cholesterol - HDL - TG/2.2) is unreliable when triglycerides exceed 4.5 mmol/L — at very high TG concentrations, the TG/2.2 term (used to estimate VLDL) disproportionately underestimates VLDL, causing LDL to be significantly underestimated. This means that in a patient with TG of 8 mmol/L, the calculated LDL may appear reassuringly normal (or even low) while the actual LDL and atherogenic particle burden is substantially higher. The solution: use non-HDL cholesterol (total cholesterol minus HDL) alongside the overall clinical risk assessment when TG exceeds 4.5 mmol/L — it captures all atherogenic lipoproteins (LDL, VLDL, IDL, Lp(a)) without relying on the Friedewald calculation. NICE NG238 targets: in primary prevention aim for a reduction in non-HDL cholesterol of more than 40%; in secondary prevention aim for LDL cholesterol of 2.0 mmol/L or less, or non-HDL cholesterol of 2.6 mmol/L or less. Direct LDL measurement (by specific laboratory assay rather than calculation) is also available and accurate regardless of TG level — useful in high-TG patients.

Cardiovascular Risk Assessment in Hypertriglyceridaemia

TG as a CVD risk marker
Elevated TG is an independent cardiovascular risk factor — meta-analyses show approximately 30% increased CVD risk per 1 mmol/L TG increase, independent of LDL and HDL. However, TG is closely correlated with other metabolic risk factors (low HDL, insulin resistance, obesity, diabetes) — it is debated whether TG itself causes atherosclerosis or is a marker of the atherogenic lipoprotein environment. Remnant cholesterol (VLDL remnants) is the mechanistically plausible atherogenic particle.
QRISK3 and TG
QRISK3 incorporates HDL:total cholesterol ratio but not directly TG. In a patient with mixed dyslipidaemia (high TG + low HDL + borderline LDL), QRISK3 may underestimate CVD risk because TG and non-HDL cholesterol are not directly entered. Supplement QRISK3 with clinical judgement: metabolic syndrome + family history + non-HDL cholesterol >4 mmol/L = higher actual risk than QRISK3 score suggests. NICE NG238: at TG 4.5–9.9 mmol/L, CVD risk may be underestimated by risk tools — optimise other risk factors and seek specialist advice if non-HDL-C >7.5 mmol/L.
Metabolic syndrome criteria
3 of 5 required: Waist >94 cm men or >80 cm women · TG >1.7 mmol/L · HDL <1.03 mmol/L men or <1.3 mmol/L women · BP >130/85 mmHg · Fasting glucose >5.6 mmol/L. Metabolic syndrome = 2-4x increased CVD risk + 5x increased T2DM risk. Lifestyle-first approach (TG often responds to lifestyle modification, though effects vary).
The metabolic syndrome is a cluster of cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors that are physiologically linked through insulin resistance — it affects approximately 25-30% of UK adults and is strongly driven by central obesity. The mechanism: central adiposity (visceral fat) releases excess free fatty acids into the portal circulation, driving hepatic triglyceride synthesis (VLDL overproduction). Insulin resistance impairs lipoprotein lipase activity (the enzyme responsible for clearing TG from blood), causing TG to accumulate. The resulting lipoprotein profile (high TG + low HDL + small dense LDL) is the 'atherogenic dyslipidaemia' of metabolic syndrome. The good news: this pattern is highly responsive to lifestyle modification. Weight loss typically lowers TG and raises HDL, although the size of the effect varies with baseline TG, diabetes control, alcohol intake, diet and genetic susceptibility. This is why lifestyle intervention is the cornerstone of treatment for hypertriglyceridaemia in primary care — pharmacological therapy is an adjunct, not a substitute.

GP management

Any level + severe abdominal pain/vomiting: treat as possible acute pancreatitis — urgent hospital assessment.
The familial chylomicronaemia syndrome (FCS) referral is urgent and important — FCS is caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in LPL or its cofactors (apoC-II, apoA-V, LMF1, GPIHBP1), causing near-complete absence of functional lipoprotein lipase. TG levels are typically above 20 mmol/L (and can exceed 100 mmol/L). The condition is rare (1 in 1 million) but causes recurrent life-threatening pancreatitis from childhood. Standard lipid-lowering drugs (statins, fibrates, niacin, omega-3) are largely ineffective because the LPL enzyme itself is absent. The transformative new treatment is volanesorsen (Waylivra — ionis/Akcea) — an antisense oligonucleotide targeting apoC-III mRNA in the liver, reducing apoC-III production (apoC-III normally inhibits LPL activity; in FCS where LPL is absent, reducing apoC-III reduces TG via LPL-independent pathways). NICE HST13 reserves volanesorsen for genetically confirmed FCS with high pancreatitis risk and inadequate response to diet and TG-lowering therapy; it is initiated and monitored by a specialist centre.

Treat

Lifestyle — The Primary Intervention

Dietary modification
Low-sugar diet: refined carbohydrates and fructose (sugary drinks, fruit juices, sweets, white bread/rice) are the most potent dietary drivers of TG. Advice: stop sugary drinks and fruit juice and cut excess refined carbohydrate; reduce total calorie intake; replace saturated with unsaturated fats for general CVD risk reduction. TG >10 mmol/L: specialist-directed low-fat diet (dietitian) rather than a generic Mediterranean diet prescription. Oily fish is general healthy-eating advice, not treatment for severe hypertriglyceridaemia.
Alcohol reduction
Alcohol is a modifiable contributor to hypertriglyceridaemia and pancreatitis risk. Avoid or substantially reduce alcohol; complete abstinence is advisable while TG is severely elevated or there is a history of pancreatitis. Quantify intake explicitly at every review — use AUDIT-C.
Weight loss
Weight loss lowers TG, but the size of the effect varies with baseline TG, diabetes control, alcohol intake, diet and genetic susceptibility. Waist circumference reduction (visceral fat) is more important than total weight for TG reduction. Calorie restriction + increased physical activity. GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide — can reduce TG in addition to weight + glucose): consider in T2DM + high TG + obesity.
Physical activity
Encourage regular aerobic activity as tolerated (e.g. 150 min/week brisk walking, cycling, swimming), which helps lower TG. Mechanism: increases LPL activity in skeletal muscle (same enzyme that clears TG from blood). Effect is transient (returns to baseline within 48h of inactivity) — reinforces the need for habitual daily activity rather than occasional intense exercise.
The dietary sugar-TG connection is one of the most practically important and under-communicated nutritional facts in primary care — many patients who reduce dietary fat (as traditionally advised for dyslipidaemia) inadvertently increase their carbohydrate intake, particularly from refined sugars and fruit juices, which actually worsens hypertriglyceridaemia. The biochemical mechanism: fructose (the sugar in table sugar, high-fructose corn syrup, and fruit juice) is metabolised almost exclusively in the liver, where it bypasses the rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis (phosphofructokinase) and contributes to hepatic de novo lipogenesis and VLDL-TG production, although the size of the effect varies between individuals. The practical message for patients: 'For high triglycerides, the single most important dietary change is cutting out sugary drinks, fruit juices, and refined carbohydrates — these matter as much as cutting fat.' With TG >10 mmol/L a specialist-directed low-fat diet is also needed.

Pharmacological Management

Persistent or severe TG pancreatitis risk a concern
Fenofibrate — on specialist advice
Do not routinely prescribe a fibrate solely to reduce CVD risk (NICE NG238). For persistent or severe hypertriglyceridaemia, particularly where pancreatitis risk is a concern, seek specialist advice on fenofibrate or other TG-lowering therapy — the decision depends on fasting TG level and persistence, secondary causes, previous pancreatitis, diabetes control, renal function, current statin and local lipid-specialist advice. If prescribed: check renal function and liver transaminases before starting; use the formulation-specific renal dose (formulations are not interchangeable mg-for-mg — follow the individual SPC); avoid in severe renal impairment; be alert to gallstones, hepatic dysfunction and myopathy. A creatinine rise is not invariably benign — reassess if creatinine rises substantially or eGFR declines. Mechanism: PPAR-alpha agonist.
Raised TG + elevated CVD risk
Statin first — treat overall atherogenic risk
Usually prioritise an appropriate statin and non-HDL-C/LDL-C management (NICE NG238; ESC/EAS: statins first drug of choice in high-risk patients with hypertriglyceridaemia). TG-lowering treatment does not replace evidence-based LDL-C lowering. Adding fenofibrate to a statin needs individual risk–benefit assessment and monitoring (renal function, LFTs; CK if muscle symptoms). Do not combine gemfibrozil with a statin (MHRA).
Established CVD on a statin fasting TG ≥1.7 mmol/L + LDL-C >1.04 to ≤2.60 mmol/L
Icosapent ethyl 2 g twice daily (Vazkepa) NICE TA805
Consider only within NICE TA805/local formulary criteria: established CVD, raised fasting TG ≥1.7 mmol/L, LDL-C >1.04 to ≤2.60 mmol/L and concurrent statin therapy (not recommended for primary prevention). It is not a general treatment for severe hypertriglyceridaemia or a substitute for fibrate/specialist management of pancreatitis risk. Cautions: atrial fibrillation/flutter; bleeding, particularly with anticoagulants or antiplatelets; fish or shellfish hypersensitivity — see the Vazkepa SPC. Mixed EPA/DHA products lower TG but lack REDUCE-IT outcome evidence; over-the-counter fish oils are not interchangeable with either. Greater Manchester restricts omega-3-acid ethyl esters for hypertriglyceridaemia to lipid-specialist care (dose-dependent AF risk).
Genetically confirmed FCS high pancreatitis risk
Volanesorsen (Waylivra) Specialist only (FCS)
ApoC-III antisense inhibitor. Not indicated for generic TG >20 mmol/L. NICE HST13: genetically confirmed FCS, high pancreatitis risk and inadequate response to diet and TG-lowering therapy. Initiated and monitored by a specialist centre; thrombocytopenia monitoring is a major safety issue — follow the current SPC protocol.
The REDUCE-IT trial (2018) is one of the most important cardiovascular pharmacology trials of the last decade — it showed that high-dose icosapentaenoic acid (icosapent ethyl 2 g twice daily; UK product Vazkepa) reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE — MI, stroke, cardiovascular death) by 25% (relative risk reduction) in patients with established cardiovascular disease or diabetes who had elevated TG (above 1.5 mmol/L) despite statin therapy. The absolute risk reduction was approximately 4.8% over 4.9 years (NNT approximately 21 for 5 years). The mechanisms proposed: TG lowering; anti-inflammatory effects of EPA; anti-oxidant effects; membrane stabilisation effects on vulnerable plaques. The result was controversial because the control arm used mineral oil (which may have raised cardiovascular risk) rather than olive oil or corn oil, potentially inflating the treatment effect. Despite the controversy, NICE TA805 (2022) recommends icosapent ethyl (Vazkepa) for secondary cardiovascular prevention in people on a statin with fasting TG 1.7 mmol/L or above and LDL-C above 1.04 and up to 2.60 mmol/L (TA805). GPs may consider icosapent ethyl as an add-on only when all TA805/local formulary criteria are met (established CVD, fasting TG ≥1.7 mmol/L, LDL-C >1.04 to ≤2.60 mmol/L, on a statin), after reviewing AF and bleeding risk.

Lifestyle

Omega-3, Mediterranean Diet & Long-Term Management

Oily fish and omega-3 intake 2-3 portions of oily fish weekly (salmon, mackerel, sardines, herring, trout) is general healthy-eating advice — it is not equivalent to pharmacological-dose omega-3 therapy and is not treatment for severe hypertriglyceridaemia. Distinguish icosapent ethyl (purified EPA with cardiovascular outcome evidence in selected patients — NICE TA805), mixed EPA/DHA prescription products (lower TG but lack REDUCE-IT outcome evidence; Greater Manchester: lipid-specialist only) and over-the-counter fish-oil supplements (not interchangeable with either).
Mediterranean diet pattern Olive oil (monounsaturated fat) as primary fat source. High vegetable and legume intake. Moderate fish and poultry. Limited red meat. Moderate nuts and seeds. Very limited sugar and refined carbohydrate. Minimise alcohol. Suitable for general CVD risk reduction; with TG >10 mmol/L use a specialist-directed low-fat diet rather than a generic Mediterranean diet prescription.
Sugary drinks elimination Soft drinks, energy drinks, fruit juices, squash, and flavoured coffees are a major dietary TG driver to eliminate. Water, unsweetened tea, and coffee (without sugar) are the recommended replacements. Artificially sweetened drinks: not proven to raise TG directly, but do not help with insulin resistance.
Alcohol and pancreatitis risk communication In patients with TG >5 mmol/L, explain the pancreatitis risk directly: "Alcohol can dramatically raise your triglycerides — at your current level, you are at significant risk of acute pancreatitis, which requires hospital treatment and can be life-threatening. I strongly recommend you stop or dramatically reduce alcohol intake." AUDIT-C at every review. NHS Stop Drinking and Drugs services. Apps: Drinkaware, Try Dry.
Exercise prescription for TG Short bouts of aerobic exercise after meals (even 10-15 minute walks after each meal) are particularly effective at clearing postprandial TG from the blood — skeletal muscle LPL activity peaks within 30-60 minutes of moderate aerobic activity. Daily habitual activity is more effective than weekend-only exercise for TG control. Cycle commuting, taking stairs, lunchtime walks — all measurably reduce TG.
Weight management targets For TG >5 mmol/L: 5-10% weight loss target with 3-month timeframe. GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide — Ozempic/Wegovy): can reduce TG in addition to weight and HbA1c. Consider in patients with T2DM + obesity + high TG. VLCD (very-low-calorie diet, 800 kcal/day): produces rapid weight loss and can lower TG (effects vary; supervised programmes only). NHS Tier 2/3 weight management referral.
Pancreatitis prevention education for very-high-TG patients Patients with TG >10 mmol/L need explicit pancreatitis risk education: the symptoms of acute pancreatitis (severe central/upper abdominal pain, vomiting, fever), when to call 999 (severe abdominal pain or vomiting at any TG level = do not wait — go to A&E), what triggers pancreatitis (alcohol, high-fat meals, uncontrolled diabetes), and how to reduce risk (strict diet, medication adherence, alcohol avoidance).
Pregnancy and breastfeeding TG rises physiologically in pregnancy, and in women with pre-existing hypertriglyceridaemia it can reach pancreatitis-risk levels. Severe hypertriglyceridaemia in pregnancy requires obstetric and lipid-specialist co-management; dietetic management and control of secondary causes are central. Monitoring frequency is individualised (baseline TG, previous pancreatitis, gestational stage, specialist advice) — not routine monthly testing for all. Avoid fibrates and other lipid-modifying drugs unless specifically advised by specialists; do not assume high-dose omega-3 preparations are automatically safe or indicated — use product-specific pregnancy information. Review statins and other lipid-modifying treatment before conception: NICE advises stopping statins when pregnancy is possible and not restarting until breastfeeding has finished.
The postprandial TG response is an underappreciated cardiovascular risk factor — fasting TG measurement captures only a fraction of the TG burden in most people, as TG levels are highest postprandially (2-4 hours after a fat-containing meal). Studies consistently show that postprandial hypertriglyceridaemia is a cardiovascular risk factor independent of fasting TG, because the postprandial state (lasting approximately 16-18 hours in adults eating three meals per day) is when TG-rich lipoproteins are present in the circulation and driving atherosclerosis. The practical implication: postprandial TG can be considerably higher than the fasting value — the actual TG exposure over 24 hours is dramatically higher than the fasting measurement suggests. Non-fasting lipid measurement (Total cholesterol + HDL + non-HDL cholesterol) is now recommended as the preferred screening approach in many guidelines precisely because it captures part of the postprandial TG burden; NICE accepts non-fasting lipids for routine CVD risk assessment.

Follow-Up & Monitoring

After lifestyle + treatment initiation
Repeat lipid profile (fasting if TG markedly raised) to confirm response. No universal TG target: in severe hypertriglyceridaemia the immediate objective is to reduce pancreatitis risk and address the cause, using specialist-agreed targets (particularly after pancreatitis or with suspected chylomicronaemia). Manage LDL-C and non-HDL-C according to CVD-risk category. Repeat per the lipid-management pathway.
Fenofibrate monitoring
Baseline renal function/eGFR and liver transaminases; consider baseline CK if muscle symptoms or increased myopathy risk. Repeat renal function and LFTs per SPC/local protocol (commonly within the first few months and after dose changes). No routine CK in asymptomatic patients — check CK if muscle pain, weakness or tenderness; stop and urgently assess severe muscle symptoms or suspected rhabdomyolysis. Reassess if creatinine rises substantially or eGFR declines. Use the formulation-specific renal dose per the product SPC; avoid in severe renal impairment and in pregnancy.
Icosapent ethyl monitoring
Not monitoring-free: review atrial fibrillation symptoms and pulse/rhythm where clinically indicated; review bleeding risk and concomitant anticoagulants/antiplatelets. Repeat lipid profile according to the statin/lipid pathway.
Pancreatitis history
If prior TG-induced pancreatitis: specialist-agreed TG target and monitoring frequency. Alcohol abstinence non-negotiable. Avoid high-fat meals. Gastroenterology follow-up for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (faecal elastase if steatorrhoea).

TG 10–20 mmol/L without abdominal pain → dietary counselling, review secondary causes, and repeat a fasting TG after at least 5 days but within 2 weeks; seek specialist advice if it stays above 10 mmol/L (NICE NG238) · New TG 4.5–9.9 mmol/L without secondary cause explanation → workup, CVD risk and non-HDL-C review (specialist advice if non-HDL-C >7.5 mmol/L); no automatic fibrate for CVD prevention
The TG response to treatment is one of the most dramatic and rapid in lipidology — in contrast to LDL which takes weeks to respond to statins, triglycerides can fall quickly after stopping alcohol, eliminating sugary drinks, starting TG-lowering treatment or a very-low-calorie diet, although the size of the response varies. This rapid response has clinical utility: it allows early reassessment (3-4 weeks rather than 3 months) to confirm treatment response and reinforce lifestyle change. Sharing the TG result with the patient at each review — and pointing out the improvement — is one of the most motivating tools in metabolic medicine. A patient who sees their TG fall after stopping alcohol and soft drinks has powerful objective evidence of the impact of their lifestyle change.
