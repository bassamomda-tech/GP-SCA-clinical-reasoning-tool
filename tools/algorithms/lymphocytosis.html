Safety

Red Flags — Leukaemia, Lymphoma & High-Grade Disease

Rapidly rising lymphocyte count + cytopenias, abnormal film, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly or B symptoms Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) or lymphoma with leukaemic phase. → urgent haematology discussion / suspected-cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12). Do not use 20 × 10⁹/L as a standalone emergency threshold — serious disease can occur at lower counts, and an asymptomatic patient with a high stable CLL count may not need same-day referral.
Lymphocytosis + blast cells on blood film + rapid onset over days-weeks + bone marrow failure features Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) — paediatric and young adult. → Blasts or a blood-film report suggesting acute leukaemia require immediate discussion with haematology/acute oncology and hospital assessment (NICE NG12). Do not arrange or promise a bone-marrow biopsy from primary care. Highly time-sensitive.
Lymphocytosis + abnormal-appearing lymphocytes on film + mediastinal mass on CXR T-cell lymphoma in leukaemic phase (e.g., T-lymphoblastic lymphoma) or Sezary syndrome (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma). Haematology suspected cancer pathway referral; staging (CT, bone marrow) is arranged by haematology.
Persistent unexplained lymphocytosis (reactive causes excluded) Needs further assessment: repeat FBC + differential, blood film, examination for lymphadenopathy/hepatosplenomegaly, review of previous counts, and peripheral-blood flow cytometry via the local laboratory/haematology pathway. CLL = persistent clonal B cells ≥5 × 10⁹/L; MBL = clonal B cells <5 × 10⁹/L without lymphadenopathy, organomegaly or cytopenias — thresholds refer to the clonal B-cell count, not the total lymphocyte count. Smear cells (Gumprecht shadows) may support CLL in the right context but are not diagnostic. Route (advice and guidance, routine or urgent referral) depends on film, cytopenias, organomegaly, symptoms and rate of change.
Lymphocytosis + fever + pharyngitis + lymphadenopathy ± splenomegaly Infectious mononucleosis (EBV) likely — atypical lymphocytes on film support but are not specific for EBV. Self-limiting. Avoid contact sports during the acute phase. Diagnosis usually clinical; if confirmation is needed use the local laboratory EBV serology pathway (heterophile tests may be unreliable early in illness and in younger children). Treat complications if they arise (see glandular fever).
Infant with failure to thrive, persistent or severe infections, chronic diarrhoea, thrush or unusual organisms Same-day paediatric assessment. Do not attempt to diagnose SCID from the lymphocyte count (classic SCID usually presents with lymphopenia); interpret against age-specific lymphocyte ranges and arrange specialist immunology assessment.
CLL (Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia) is the most common adult leukaemia in the Western world — it presents as an incidental finding of lymphocytosis on routine FBC in approximately 70% of cases. The blood film helps distinguish reactive from suspicious morphology: smear cells (Gumprecht shadows or smudge cells — fragile lymphocytes that disintegrate when spread on the slide) may support CLL in the appropriate clinical and morphological context but are not diagnostic; confirmation requires immunophenotyping and specialist interpretation. CLL is diagnosed by persistent (at least 3 months) clonal B lymphocytes ≥5 × 10⁹/L with a characteristic immunophenotype (typically CD19+, CD5+, CD23+ with light-chain restriction), usually confirmed by peripheral-blood flow cytometry. A total lymphocyte count alone does not diagnose CLL, as it includes T cells and other lymphocytes. The majority of CLL patients are elderly (median age at diagnosis 72) and have indolent disease that requires no treatment initially — 'watchful waiting' (active monitoring without treatment) is appropriate for patients without active-disease criteria. The GP's role: arrange haematology advice or referral per the local pathway, provide reassurance that CLL is often slow-growing, and avoid over-alarming the patient.

Causes of Lymphocytosis — Classification

Reactive lymphocytosis (most common)
Viral infections: EBV (infectious mononucleosis — atypical lymphocytes/Downey cells on film, heterophile positive); CMV (similar picture, heterophile negative); acute HIV seroconversion; pertussis (Bordetella — often marked lymphocytosis, predominantly CD4+ T-cells; supports but does not alone establish the diagnosis); adenovirus; hepatitis A. Post-splenectomy: persistent mild lymphocytosis (loss of splenic sequestration). Childhood: lymphocyte counts are higher in young children — use the laboratory's age-specific reference range. Smoking: mild persistent reactive lymphocytosis (CD4 T-cells increase in smokers).
Clonal / malignant
Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL): most common. Persistent (≥3 months) clonal B cells ≥5 × 10⁹/L with a characteristic immunophenotype (CD19+/CD5+/CD23+, light-chain restricted) on flow cytometry — a total lymphocyte count alone does not diagnose CLL. Smear cells may be seen (supportive, not diagnostic). Age >60 usually. Indolent course in most. Monoclonal B-lymphocytosis (MBL): clonal B-cell population <5 × 10⁹/L without lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, cytopenias, disease-related symptoms or other evidence of a lymphoproliferative disorder; low-count (clonal B cells <0.5 × 10⁹/L) or high-count (0.5 to <5 × 10⁹/L). High-count MBL progresses to CLL requiring treatment at approximately 1–2% per year. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL): blast cells, rapid onset, bone marrow failure. Lymphoma with peripheral blood involvement: follicular lymphoma, mantle cell, marginal zone — circulating lymphoma cells (confirmed by flow cytometry ± cytogenetic/molecular testing). Large granular lymphocyte (LGL) leukaemia: T-LGL or NK-LGL — neutropenia + splenomegaly + rheumatoid arthritis-like picture; diagnosis needs persistent LGL expansion and specialist assessment, often including T-cell receptor clonality.
Other causes
Addison's disease (cortisol deficiency removes lymphocyte suppression). Hyperthyroidism. Drug reactions (some antiepileptics cause lymphocytosis).
Pertussis (whooping cough) classically causes a very pronounced lymphocytosis — absolute lymphocyte counts of 15-30 × 10⁹/L are characteristic and are caused by pertussis toxin inhibiting lymphocyte migration out of the blood into lymphoid tissue (the cells accumulate in peripheral blood). This lymphocytosis pattern in a child or unvaccinated adult with a prolonged cough (the catarrhal phase lasting 1-2 weeks, followed by the paroxysmal phase with inspiratory whoop) should prompt consideration of pertussis, although marked lymphocytosis alone does not establish the diagnosis — manage via the separate whooping cough pathway (notification, PCR timing, antibiotic indications, infectious-period advice and contact prophylaxis per current UKHSA guidance). The nasopharyngeal swab or PCR is the diagnostic test in the first 3 weeks; serology (pertussis IgG) after 3 weeks. Treatment: azithromycin 500 mg once daily for 3 days in adults (UKHSA pertussis guidance) (reduces transmission, minimal effect on illness duration if paroxysmal phase already begun). Pertussis is a notifiable disease — notify the UKHSA Health Protection Team. The whooping cough immunisation programme (6-in-1 at 8, 12, 16 weeks; a fourth 6-in-1 dose at 18 months for children born on or after 1 July 2024; pre-school booster at 3 years 4 months; maternal vaccination at 16-32 weeks of pregnancy — UKHSA routine schedule, Green Book ch. 11 and ch. 24) has significantly reduced but not eliminated pertussis. Large granular lymphocyte (LGL) leukaemia is another important and frequently delayed diagnosis — the triad of: neutropenia (recurrent bacterial infections) + splenomegaly + positive RF/ANA in a middle-aged adult should prompt haematology assessment (persistent LGL expansion, often with T-cell receptor clonality testing).

Assessment — Blood Film, History & Investigations

Blood film — reactive vs suspicious
Helps distinguish reactive from suspicious morphology; diagnostic confirmation is by flow cytometry and haematology review — morphology alone is insufficient. Reactive: atypical lymphocytes (e.g. Downey cells — large, irregular nucleus, abundant basophilic cytoplasm; not specific for EBV). Suggests CLL: small, mature-appearing lymphocytes ± smear cells (supportive, not diagnostic). Blasts (large, high N:C ratio, nucleoli visible): urgent specialist review and immunophenotyping. Cleft or irregular nuclei may suggest mantle cell/follicular lymphoma — needs flow cytometry ± cytogenetic/molecular testing. Large granular lymphocytes with azurophilic granules: LGL leukaemia needs persistent LGL expansion and specialist assessment (often T-cell receptor clonality).
History
Confirm the absolute lymphocyte count (not percentage) and review previous FBCs and timing. Duration: with an acute illness (reactive likely) vs persistent after recovery (investigate further). Associated symptoms: pharyngitis + fever + fatigue (EBV); cough paroxysms + whoop (pertussis); weight loss + night sweats + lymphadenopathy (lymphoma/CLL Binet B/C). Drug history, smoking, recent infection or vaccination, splenectomy status. Vaccination status (pertussis). Previous lymphocytosis on old blood tests (CLL may have been incidental finding for years).
Examination
Lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, signs of infection, bruising or petechiae, pallor, B symptoms. Assess the remaining cell lines (Hb, neutrophils, platelets).
Investigations
Repeat FBC with differential (absolute lymphocyte count; other cell lines) · Blood film · CRP/ESR (according to clinical context) · LFTs + renal function (EBV hepatitis, lymphoma infiltration) · HIV 4th gen (where clinically indicated — seroconversion lymphocytosis) · EBV/CMV testing (only where the clinical syndrome supports it; local laboratory EBV serology pathway — interpret VCA IgM/IgG and EBNA, not IgM alone) · Pertussis PCR or serology (only where the cough syndrome supports it) · Flow cytometry (peripheral-blood immunophenotyping via the local laboratory reflex or haematology pathway for persistent unexplained lymphocytosis, suspicious morphology or suspected lymphoproliferative disorder — follow local laboratory criteria; not repeated routinely in established stable CLL/MBL unless advised by haematology) · CT staging scan (if lymphoma suspected — haematology-arranged)
Flow cytometry for lymphocyte immunophenotyping is the gold standard investigation for distinguishing reactive from clonal lymphocytosis — it measures the surface antigen profile of lymphocytes, identifying: clonal B-cell populations (CD5+/CD19+/CD23+ = CLL; CD5+/CD19+/cyclin D1+ = mantle cell lymphoma; CD10+ = follicular lymphoma), T-cell receptor clonality (T-LGL), and the ratio of kappa to lambda immunoglobulin light chains on B-cells (a skewed kappa:lambda ratio indicates a monoclonal population — clonal is not necessarily malignant, e.g. MBL). Many UK laboratories use reflex or clinician-requested lymphocyte immunophenotyping pathways for persistent absolute lymphocytosis — request through the local laboratory or haematology pathway according to local criteria. GPs should refer promptly when the blood film and clinical picture suggest clonal lymphocytosis, rather than waiting further — early haematology assessment allows proper characterisation and staging, which determines management (watchful waiting vs treatment initiation).

CLL Staging & MBL Monitoring

Binet staging (CLL)
Stage A: Hb >10 g/dL, platelets >100 × 10⁹/L, <3 lymph node areas enlarged. No treatment indicated. Median survival >10 years. Stage B: Hb >10, platelets >100, 3+ lymph node areas enlarged. No treatment if asymptomatic (active monitoring). Stage C: Hb <10 or platelets <100 (regardless of nodes). Advanced disease — treatment decisions are based on active-disease criteria and specialist assessment, not stage alone. Haematology-led.
Criteria for CLL treatment initiation
Haematology decides, based on active/progressive disease: progressive marrow failure (worsening anaemia/thrombocytopenia); progressive or symptomatic lymphadenopathy; progressive or symptomatic splenomegaly; rapid lymphocyte progression; autoimmune cytopenia (AIHA, ITP) poorly responsive to corticosteroids; disease-related B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss); symptomatic extranodal involvement. A high lymphocyte count alone is not an indication for treatment.
CLL treatment options (haematology-led)
Treatment is haematology-led. The choice of targeted therapy depends on disease biology, TP53/17p status, IGHV status, comorbidity, interactions, patient preference and current NICE technology-appraisal guidance and local commissioning — refer to the current local haematology protocol rather than a fixed first-line drug list.
MBL (Monoclonal B-Lymphocytosis) monitoring
MBL is a clonal B-cell population below 5 × 10⁹/L without lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, cytopenias or other evidence of a lymphoproliferative disorder. The flow-cytometry report should state the clonal B-cell count and the recommended follow-up plan. Follow the flow-cytometry and haematology plan: high-count MBL commonly requires periodic FBC and clinical review, with the frequency specified in the haematology letter (annual progression risk approximately 1–2% — not a basis for a rigid schedule independent of local advice). Re-refer for new lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, B symptoms, cytopenias or a rising clonal B-cell count.
The CLL watchful waiting approach requires specific patient communication skills — patients who are told they have 'leukaemia' but are then told nothing will be done about it understandably find this distressing and confusing. The key explanation: 'CLL is a type of blood cancer, but it behaves very differently from most cancers. In most people, it grows very slowly — so slowly that we can watch it for years, or even decades, without needing to treat it. The research shows clearly that treating it early (when it is not causing symptoms) does not help and can harm — it does not extend life or prevent the disease from progressing. We will monitor you closely with regular blood tests, and the moment it starts to cause symptoms or affect your other blood cells, we will act.' This reframing of 'watchful waiting' as active evidence-based management (not neglect) significantly improves patient acceptance. Many CLL patients live normal lives for 10-15 years without ever needing treatment.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day / same-shift haematology discussion
Blasts or suspected acute leukaemia (immediate discussion + hospital assessment) · Severe cytopenias with clinical deterioration · Rapidly progressive lymphocytosis with B symptoms · Severe infection or bleeding in a patient with suspected haematological malignancy · Suspected acute transformation or rapidly enlarging nodes with systemic illness
Suspected cancer: haematology
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly in adults · Lymphadenopathy with B symptoms · Blood film or FBC suggesting leukaemia · Local haemato-oncology criteria for concerning persistent lymphocytosis
Urgent haematology referral (local pathway)
Not NG12 criteria on a count alone: significant or progressive anaemia, thrombocytopenia or neutropenia · Rapidly rising lymphocyte count · Recurrent or severe infection · Unexplained bruising or bleeding · Progressive lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly
Haematology advice / routine referral
Persistent unexplained lymphocytosis after reactive causes have been excluded (local advice-and-guidance and reflex flow-cytometry pathway) · Abnormal flow cytometry · Confirmed MBL or CLL where a formal haematology plan is required · Unexplained cytopenias
GP management
Reactive lymphocytosis with clear cause (EBV, CMV, pertussis, other viral illness): repeat FBC after recovery, commonly 4–6 weeks (confirm resolution); review differential and film if it persists. Children: interpret against the age-specific reference range. Post-splenectomy mild stable lymphocytosis: no action needed. Known MBL: follow the monitoring plan in the flow-cytometry/haematology report.
The pertussis notification obligation for GPs is a legal requirement under UK public health legislation — pertussis is a notifiable disease and GPs who suspect or diagnose pertussis must notify the local Health Protection Team (HPT) by telephone as soon as the diagnosis is made (not waiting for laboratory confirmation). The UK still experiences significant pertussis outbreaks — annual confirmed case numbers vary widely, with a marked resurgence in 2024. The maternal pertussis vaccination programme (ADACEL (Tdap) since 1 July 2024, replacing Boostrix-IPV; offered from 16 weeks, ideally 20–32 weeks) has dramatically reduced infant pertussis deaths by providing passive immunity to newborns before their own vaccination at 8 weeks. GPs who identify pertussis in a pregnant woman or a close contact of a newborn should: notify the UKHSA Health Protection Team, arrange azithromycin post-exposure prophylaxis for close contacts, and notify obstetric team.

EBV Infectious Mononucleosis Management

Diagnosis
Usually clinical, with FBC/LFTs where indicated. If confirmation is required, use the local laboratory's EBV serology pathway. Heterophile (Monospot) testing may be unreliable early in illness and in younger children; EBV IgM alone does not always confirm acute infection — interpret with VCA IgM/IgG and EBNA where necessary.
Specific treatment
No antiviral treatment effective for acute EBV infection. Aciclovir: does not significantly reduce duration or severity of EBV mononucleosis in RCTs. NSAIDs/paracetamol for fever and pharyngeal pain. Saltwater gargles for throat comfort. Adequate hydration.
Corticosteroids — when indicated
Do not use corticosteroids routinely in uncomplicated infectious mononucleosis. Consider specialist-led corticosteroid treatment only for impending or established upper-airway obstruction (drooling, stridor, inability to swallow — urgent hospital assessment), severe immune thrombocytopenia, severe haemolytic anaemia or significant neurological complications.
Activity restrictions
Avoid contact sports and strenuous activity during the acute symptomatic phase (splenic rupture risk — see splenomegaly algorithm). Give individualised advice based on illness severity, splenic enlargement, occupation and sport; routine ultrasound to document splenic normalisation is not required in every patient — seek specialist advice for athletes or those with persistent splenomegaly. Driving: not restricted unless severely unwell. Return to work, education and exercise should be gradual and guided by symptoms and functional recovery.
Amoxicillin avoidance
Avoid amoxicillin/ampicillin — causes widespread maculopapular rash in a large proportion of EBV patients (older series quote 80-90%; more recent studies report lower rates) — a transient drug–virus interaction, not penicillin allergy. If tonsillitis requires antibiotic: use phenoxymethylpenicillin or clarithromycin. Do NOT code as penicillin allergy if rash occurred during EBV.
Complications to monitor
Rare but important: splenic rupture (0.1-0.5%), haematological (ITP, AIHA, pancytopenia), neurological (Guillain-Barre, meningitis, Bell's palsy), hepatitis (ALT elevation common — usually self-limiting), airway obstruction (tonsils — hospital if severe). Persistent or severe fatigue warrants reassessment for alternative diagnoses and post-infectious fatigue.
EBV infectious mononucleosis post-acute fatigue is one of the most important patient communication challenges in primary care — a minority of patients develop prolonged fatigue after the acute illness (post-infectious fatigue), and a subset meet the criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS); persistent or severe fatigue warrants reassessment for alternative diagnoses. GPs should: (1) at the initial consultation, warn patients that a period of prolonged fatigue is possible after EBV; (2) avoid reassurance that they will 'be back to normal in 2 weeks' — this creates unrealistic expectations that damage the patient-GP relationship when prolonged fatigue develops; (3) arrange FBC + LFTs at 6-8 weeks to confirm resolution of acute haematological changes; (4) screen with PHQ-9 (depression is a common co-morbidity in prolonged post-EBV fatigue); (5) refer to CFS/ME specialist services if fatigue persists beyond 3 months with significant functional impairment.

CLL — Shared Care (Written Haematology Plan)

GP role in CLL shared care
GP monitoring is appropriate only where the haematology team has formally transferred follow-up and provided a written monitoring and re-referral plan (diagnosis and risk category, monitoring frequency, blood-test parameters, clinical triggers for re-referral, who is responsible for vaccine advice and infection management, and whether the patient remains under haematology review). Key parameters to track: Hb (falling indicates CLL progression or AIHA), platelets (falling = marrow infiltration), lymphocyte count trend. Refer back to haematology per the written plan — e.g. falling Hb or platelets, rapidly rising lymphocyte count, B symptoms, new or progressive lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly.
Infections in CLL
CLL is associated with immunodeficiency (hypogammaglobulinaemia — low Ig production by malignant B-cells; T-cell function impaired). Check immunoglobulins in CLL with recurrent or severe infections. Consider immunoglobulin replacement only after haematology/immunology assessment (infection burden, documented antibody deficiency, vaccine responses, treatment history) — an IgG threshold alone is not sufficient. Ensure vaccination is reviewed against the current UK immunisation schedule (Green Book): inactivated influenza, COVID-19, recombinant zoster (Shingrix) and indicated pneumococcal vaccination are generally considered, but responses may be reduced and timing should be coordinated with treatment. Avoid live vaccines unless specifically advised by haematology.
Secondary autoimmune complications
AIHA (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia — CLL most common underlying cause of acquired AIHA): Hb falling + raised bilirubin + raised LDH + positive DAT (direct antiglobulin test) → haematology urgently. ITP (immune thrombocytopenia — CLL B-cells produce platelet antibodies): platelets falling + bruising → haematology. Both may occur without progression of CLL stage.
CLL and COVID-19
CLL patients are at high risk of severe COVID-19 (impaired humoral and cellular immunity). Ensure: up-to-date COVID boosters (may respond poorly to vaccination — discuss with haematology about antibody monitoring). Patients with CLL may qualify for early COVID-19 treatment, but eligibility, renal function, timing and interactions must be checked urgently through the current NHS antiviral pathway and the haematology/medicines-information team. Do not prescribe nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid) without checking interactions with CLL treatment (venetoclax, ibrutinib, acalabrutinib and other CYP3A4 substrates).
The CLL vaccination strategy requires specific planning — patients with CLL are immunosuppressed (particularly those who have received treatment) and should receive: annual influenza vaccine (inactivated IM), pneumococcal vaccination (per Green Book chapter 25), COVID-19 boosters, Shingrix (recombinant zoster), and hepatitis B vaccine if not immune. However, vaccine responses may be suboptimal because CLL B-cells cannot produce adequate antibody responses to vaccine antigens. Specialist teams may measure antibody responses to vaccines (anti-pneumococcal IgG, anti-HBs) in CLL patients to confirm adequate protection. Patients who fail to respond to standard doses may benefit from higher-dose or repeat vaccination schedules. Avoid live vaccines (e.g. MMR, yellow fever, varicella/ZVL) unless specifically advised by haematology, and review the schedule against the current Green Book.

Lifestyle

EBV Recovery, CLL Living Well & Patient Support

EBV recovery graded activity plan Return to work, education and exercise should be gradual and guided by symptoms and functional recovery. Avoid contact sports and strenuous activity during the acute symptomatic phase, then give individualised advice based on illness severity, splenic enlargement, occupation and sport. Routine ultrasound to document splenic normalisation is not required in every patient — seek specialist advice for athletes or those with persistent splenomegaly.
Nutrition in EBV recovery Adequate protein and iron intake during recovery (viral infections increase metabolic demands). Small frequent meals if throat pain limits eating. Avoid alcohol while acutely unwell or if liver enzymes are elevated.
CLL patient education Explain the watchful waiting principle: "CLL is often described as a slow-growing condition. We are watching it carefully — this is the evidence-based approach, not doing nothing." UK CLL Support Association (cllsupport.org.uk) provides excellent patient information, forums, and events. Lymphoma Action (lymphoma-action.org.uk) — broader lymphoma patient support.
Infection vigilance in CLL Contact GP promptly for any: temperature >38°C, rapidly worsening cough or breathlessness (pneumonia risk), spreading skin infection (cellulitis). Do not wait the standard 7 days advised for healthy patients. Medical alert card: "I have chronic lymphocytic leukaemia — please treat my infections promptly with antibiotics."
Sun protection in CLL CLL is associated with increased risk of skin cancer, particularly aggressive or recurrent keratinocyte cancers. Advise sun protection and prompt assessment of new, changing, bleeding or non-healing lesions. Skin surveillance should follow local dermatology/haematology arrangements. High SPF sunscreen daily. Avoid peak sun hours. Prompt attention to any non-healing skin lesion.
Psychological impact of CLL diagnosis Being told you have leukaemia but will not be treated is one of the most psychologically complex situations in medicine — patients experience significant anxiety, confusion, and sometimes feel that the health system has "given up" on them. PHQ-9 at 3 and 6 months post-diagnosis. NHS Talking Therapies if depression or health anxiety develops. Peer support via CLL Support Association significantly reduces anxiety.
Pertussis vaccination update Adults who had pertussis: natural immunity wanes after 5-10 years. There is no routine adult booster programme; contacts in UKHSA priority groups may be offered vaccination during public health management of a case (UKHSA pertussis guidance). Pregnant women: ADACEL (Tdap; Boostrix-IPV before July 2024) from 16 weeks, ideally 20–32 weeks (maternal vaccination programme) — provides passive immunity to neonate before their own vaccination.
Smoking and lymphocytosis Chronic smoking is associated with mild persistent lymphocytosis (particularly CD4+ T-cell elevation). If lymphocytosis is noted in a smoker: exclude malignant cause, but if reactive and stable, smoking cessation advice. Lymphocyte count often normalises within months of stopping smoking.
The CLL and skin cancer connection is clinically important and frequently underemphasised — CLL patients have an increased risk of skin cancer, particularly aggressive or recurrent keratinocyte cancers such as cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), driven by: T-cell immune surveillance impairment (failing to eliminate UV-damaged keratinocytes), B-cell immune abnormalities, and treatment-related immunosuppression. GPs monitoring CLL patients in watchful waiting should incorporate: skin surveillance per local dermatology/haematology arrangements (including sun-exposed areas), advice about daily SPF50+ sunscreen and sun protection clothing, prompt biopsy of any non-healing, crusting, or growing skin lesion. Dermatology referral (suspected cancer pathway referral if suspicious) for any concerning skin lesion in a CLL patient. The management of SCC in immunosuppressed patients is more aggressive than in immunocompetent patients because of the significantly higher risk of metastasis.

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Reactive lymphocytosis
Repeat FBC after recovery (commonly 4–6 weeks): confirm resolution. If not normalised: blood film, review previous counts, examine for nodes/spleen, HIV test where clinically indicated, and flow cytometry / haematology advice per the local pathway. Document: lymphocytosis noted, reactive cause (EBV/viral) identified, repeat FBC arranged, result reviewed.
MBL monitoring
Follow the flow-cytometry and haematology plan (frequency specified in the haematology letter). Re-refer for new lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, B symptoms, cytopenias or a rising clonal B-cell count.
CLL watchful waiting (GP-led only with a written haematology plan)
FBC at the frequency set in the haematology plan. Vaccination reviewed against the current UK schedule. Skin surveillance per local arrangements; sun-protection advice. Refer to haematology if plan triggers are met. PHQ-9 annually. Annual review: are immunoglobulins known? Is shingrix vaccination given?
After EBV illness
FBC + LFTs at 6-8 weeks (confirm resolution). Individualised return-to-sport advice; routine USS spleen is not required — seek specialist advice for athletes or persistent splenomegaly. Reassess persistent or severe fatigue (alternative diagnoses, post-infectious fatigue; PHQ-9 if low mood). Document: contact-sport advice given and acknowledged.
Refer urgently
Blasts on blood film / suspected acute leukaemia → immediate haematology discussion + hospital assessment · Severe cytopenias with clinical deterioration, severe infection or bleeding → same-day haematology discussion · EBV: sudden severe LUQ pain → 999 (splenic rupture)
Prompt / per local pathway
Persistent unexplained lymphocytosis on repeat FBC → film + flow cytometry / haematology advice per local pathway · Suspicious film (e.g. new smear cells or abnormal lymphocytes) without prior diagnosis → flow cytometry and haematology review · New B symptoms in known CLL (fever, night sweats, weight loss) → haematology within 1 week
Smear cells (Gumprecht shadows) represent fragile lymphocytes that rupture during preparation of the blood film. They may support CLL in the appropriate clinical and morphological context but are not diagnostic — they also occur in other lymphoproliferative and reactive conditions and never replace flow cytometry. A film reported as showing smear cells or suspicious lymphocytes without a prior diagnosis should prompt peripheral-blood flow cytometry through the local laboratory or haematology pathway; the report should state the clonal B-cell count (≥5 × 10⁹/L = CLL; below 5 × 10⁹/L without nodes, organomegaly or cytopenias = MBL) and a recommended follow-up plan. The finding of smear cells is a morphological cue to request haematology assessment — it is not a wait-and-watch result.
