1
Safety

Red Flags — Surgical, neurological and metabolic emergencies

Vomiting is the final common pathway of a long list of dangerous conditions. The safety task is to identify the small number of patients in whom the vomiting is a symptom of obstruction, raised intracranial pressure, or metabolic collapse.

Bilious or faeculent vomiting + distension + absolute constipation Bowel obstruction → same-day surgical admission, nil by mouth
Peritonism Rigid, guarded abdomen, rebound tenderness → perforation or ischaemia → 999
Vomiting worse on waking / lying flat + headache Possible raised intracranial pressure (with drowsiness, papilloedema, focal signs, seizure, posture-related headache) → urgent same-day neurological/acute assessment; imaging is decided by that pathway
Thunderclap headache + vomiting Subarachnoid haemorrhage → 999. Neck stiffness, photophobia, fever, non-blanching rash → suspected bacterial meningitis or meningococcal disease → 999; if meningococcal disease is strongly suspected, IM/IV benzylpenicillin 1.2 g (or ceftriaxone) unless it will delay transfer (NG240)
Ketosis, thirst, polyuria, Kussmaul breathing Suspected DKA with Kussmaul breathing, shock, altered consciousness or severe illness → 999; check capillary glucose and ketones in any vomiting person with diabetes
Haematemesis or coffee-ground vomit Upper GI bleed → same-day admission; fresh red blood, shock or postural drop, syncope, or known cirrhosis → 999
Chest pain, sweating or breathlessness with vomiting; unexplained vomiting in an older person or in diabetes Silent MI can present as vomiting alone → suspected ACS: 999 (do not delay transfer for an ECG); otherwise ECG now — ischaemic changes → 999
Hypotension, pigmentation, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia Suspected adrenal crisis → hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV + 999 — do not delay for cortisol; ask about long-term steroids abruptly stopped
Unable to keep any fluid down >24 h Dehydration with electrolyte disturbance → same-day admission for IV fluids; drowsy, confused or shocked → 999
Pregnancy Unable to tolerate oral fluids or antiemetics, dehydration, weight loss ≥5%, AKI or electrolyte disturbance, significant comorbidity or failed outpatient treatment → same-day obstetric assessment (ketonuria does not assess severity — RCOG 2024; use PUQE); vomiting after 20 weeks → consider pre-eclampsia, HELLP, AFLP → same-day obstetric assessment; BP ≥160/110 or neurological signs → 999
The two classic primary-care misses in this presentation are raised intracranial pressure and bowel obstruction, and both are missed for the same reason: the vomiting dominates the consultation and the accompanying feature is not sought. Posture-dependent vomiting — worse on waking, easing after being upright for an hour — is the cardinal history in raised ICP and is almost never volunteered spontaneously. In obstruction, the giveaway is the combination of vomiting with absolute constipation (no stool and no flatus); diarrhoea makes gastroenteritis more likely but does not exclude obstruction with overflow. Silent myocardial infarction deserves its place on the list because in older patients and those with diabetes, autonomic neuropathy can reduce a coronary event to nausea and vomiting alone — the threshold for an ECG in an older person with unexplained vomiting should be very low. Finally, any patient on long-term steroids who has been vomiting cannot absorb their replacement dose and is at risk of an adrenal crisis within hours.
2
Diagnose

History — Timing, content, and the company it keeps

Duration
Acute (<1 week) → infection, drugs, surgical, vestibular. Chronic or recurrent (>1 month) → gastroparesis, obstruction, metabolic, functional, cyclical vomiting, raised ICP.
Timing within the day
Early morning before eating → nonspecific (pregnancy, migraine, reflux, alcohol, drugs, uraemia); concerning for raised ICP only with new progressive or posture-related headache, visual or neurological change. 1–3 hours after eating → gastroparesis or gastric outlet obstruction. During or immediately after eating → functional or psychogenic.
Content
Bile = duodenal contents; may occur with obstruction distal to the ampulla — interpret with distension, bowel function and illness severity. Undigested food hours later = gastroparesis or outlet obstruction. Faeculent = concerning for distal obstruction or severe stasis. Blood / coffee grounds = upper GI bleed pathway.
Nausea vs effortless regurgitation
Effortless return of food with no nausea suggests achalasia or a pharyngeal pouch, not vomiting. Rumination is repeated effortless regurgitation and re-swallowing.
Abdominal pain
Site, character, relation to vomiting. Relate pain to meals, vomiting and bowels, but relief after vomiting is not diagnostic — consider obstruction, pancreatitis, biliary disease, ulcer and ischaemia on the full picture. Epigastric pain to the back → pancreatitis.
Bowels
Diarrhoea makes gastroenteritis possible but does not exclude obstruction with overflow, IBD, ischaemia, C. difficile, sepsis or toxicity. Absolute constipation with no flatus → obstruction. Recent antibiotics → C. difficile.
Headache and neurology
New or changed headache, worse on waking, coughing or bending; visual disturbance; focal weakness; seizure; personality change. Migraine history and typical features.
Vertigo and hearing
Room-spinning, positional triggers, tinnitus, hearing loss, gait unsteadiness → vestibular cause. New gait ataxia in an older vasculopath → posterior circulation stroke.
Drug history — line by line
Opioids, NSAIDs, antibiotics (macrolides, metronidazole), metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SSRIs, digoxin, levodopa, chemotherapy, iron, colchicine, theophylline. Recent dose increase? Cannabis (hyperemesis) and alcohol.
Systemic clues
Weight loss, thirst and polyuria, amenorrhoea, heat or cold intolerance, bone pain (hypercalcaemia), steroid use and withdrawal, recent head injury.
Psychosocial
Self-induced vomiting, dietary restriction, weight and body-image concern, laxative or diuretic misuse, dental erosion → eating disorder. Anxiety and anticipatory nausea.
Timing and content do more diagnostic work here than any test available in primary care. Vomiting undigested food eaten many hours earlier is close to pathognomonic of delayed gastric emptying — either gastroparesis (classically in long-standing diabetes) or mechanical outlet obstruction from a peptic stricture or tumour. Bilious vomiting indicates duodenal contents and suggests the pylorus is patent, but its significance depends on distension, bowel function and overall illness severity. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome is now common enough to belong on every list: cyclical severe vomiting in a regular cannabis user, characteristically relieved by hot showers, and frequently investigated for years before the connection is made. GLP-1 receptor agonists have become one of the most common drug causes of nausea in UK practice, and a recent dose escalation is usually the answer.

Diagnose

Classification — Seven cause groups

1 · GI and surgical
Gastroenteritis (commonest overall), bowel obstruction, appendicitis, pancreatitis, biliary colic and cholecystitis, peptic ulcer, gastric outlet obstruction, gastroparesis, gastro-oesophageal malignancy
2 · Neurological / raised ICP
Migraine (very common), raised intracranial pressure from tumour, haemorrhage, hydrocephalus or idiopathic intracranial hypertension; meningitis; SAH; concussion
3 · Vestibular
BPPV, vestibular neuronitis, labyrinthitis, Ménière's disease, vestibular migraine, motion sickness, posterior circulation stroke
4 · Metabolic and endocrine
DKA, uraemia, hypercalcaemia (malignancy, hyperparathyroidism), hyponatraemia, Addison's disease, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism
5 · Drugs and toxins
Opioids, GLP-1 agonists, chemotherapy, antibiotics, digoxin toxicity, NSAIDs, dopamine agonists, alcohol, cannabis hyperemesis, carbon monoxide
6 · Pregnancy
Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, hyperemesis gravidarum; after 20 weeks consider pre-eclampsia, HELLP, acute fatty liver of pregnancy
7 · Functional and psychiatric
Functional nausea and vomiting (Rome IV), cyclical vomiting syndrome, rumination, anxiety, bulimia nervosa, anticipatory nausea
Also consider
Cardiac (inferior MI), renal or ureteric colic, urinary and other sepsis, glaucoma (acute angle closure with vomiting: red painful eye, haloes, fixed mid-dilated pupil → same-day emergency ophthalmology), radiotherapy, post-operative
Grouping by mechanism is not academic tidiness — it directly determines which antiemetic will work, because each group acts through a different receptor pathway. Vestibular nausea is histamine and muscarinic driven, so cyclizine works and ondansetron largely does not. Chemical and metabolic nausea acts on the chemoreceptor trigger zone via dopamine and 5-HT3, so ondansetron or haloperidol are effective. Gastric stasis needs a prokinetic. Choosing an antiemetic by mechanism rather than by local habit is one of the clearest examples in general practice of pathophysiology changing a prescription. Acute angle-closure glaucoma is worth remembering as the ophthalmic cause that reaches the surgery as "vomiting and headache" — the red painful eye with a hazy cornea and fixed semi-dilated pupil is the clue, and it is sight-threatening within hours.
4
Diagnose

Examination — Hydration, abdomen, neurology

Hydration status
Pulse, BP lying and standing, capillary refill, mucous membranes, skin turgor, urine output and colour, weight change. A postural drop suggests significant depletion.
Observations and NEWS2
Temperature (infection), respiratory rate (Kussmaul breathing in DKA or acidosis), oxygen saturation, conscious level
Abdomen
Distension, visible peristalsis, tenderness and its site, guarding and rebound, masses, succussion splash (gastric outlet obstruction), hernial orifices, scars from previous surgery (adhesions)
Bowel sounds
Unreliable — never use them to confirm or exclude obstruction; rely on distension, peristalsis, tenderness, hernias, scars and bowel function
Rectal examination
Selective — suspected impaction, melaena, rectal mass or lower-GI symptoms
Neurological
Fundoscopy for papilloedema, visual fields, cranial nerves, limb power and coordination, gait, plantar reflexes. Neck stiffness and Kernig's sign if febrile
Vestibular assessment
Nystagmus and its direction, Dix-Hallpike for BPPV, head impulse test, Romberg and gait. HINTS only in acute vestibular syndrome (continuous vertigo, spontaneous nystagmus, vomiting, gait unsteadiness) by a trained clinician — if unavailable or abnormal, immediate stroke assessment (NG127)
Cardiovascular
Pulse rate and rhythm, heart sounds; low threshold for an ECG in anyone older or with diabetes; chest pain, sweating or breathlessness → 999 (see step 1)
Other
Pigmentation of buccal mucosa and palmar creases (Addison's), thyroid, lymph nodes, dental erosion and parotid swelling (self-induced vomiting), pupils and eye if headache
Fundoscopy is the examination most often skipped and most often decisive. Papilloedema in a patient with morning vomiting and headache converts a symptomatic prescription into urgent same-day neurological/acute assessment (which decides imaging), and it takes under a minute. A succussion splash — audible gastric fluid four hours or more after the last meal — is a genuinely useful bedside sign of gastric outlet obstruction that has largely fallen out of use. In acute vertigo with vomiting, the HINTS examination (head impulse, nystagmus, test of skew) outperforms early MRI for distinguishing vestibular neuronitis from posterior circulation stroke, and the single most important discriminator in practice is gait: a patient with vestibular neuronitis can walk unaided if unsteadily, whereas one who cannot stand without falling should be assumed to have a central cause. Dental erosion and parotid enlargement may be the only objective findings in concealed self-induced vomiting.

Diagnose

Investigations — Targeted, not reflexive

Possible pregnancy
Pregnancy test for anyone in whom pregnancy is biologically possible (whatever the contraception) when the diagnosis is uncertain and before potentially teratogenic investigation or treatment. Repeat a negative test if the LMP is uncertain.
Anyone with diabetes
Capillary blood glucose + ketones. Ketones ≥3.0 mmol/L, or lower ketones with hyperglycaemia, acidosis, dehydration or systemic illness → urgent same-day hospital; severe illness → 999. On an SGLT2 inhibitor, DKA can occur with near-normal glucose — check ketones whatever the glucose (MHRA). Reinforce diabetes sick-day rules.
Baseline bloods (persistent or unwell)
FBC U&Es (AKI, hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia) LFTs Glucose · if indicated: Calcium CRP TFTs Lipase (preferred, per lab) if pancreatitis suspected
Second line
Coeliac serology; 8–9 am cortisol only if stable and adrenal insufficiency suspected (never delay hydrocortisone in suspected crisis); digoxin level if toxicity possible; urine dip and culture; ECG with electrolyte disturbance, cardiac symptoms, significant dehydration or QT-prolonging drugs; urine tests only if urinary or renal disease suspected
Stool
Culture and C. difficile toxin if diarrhoea with recent antibiotics, hospitalisation, blood, or a persistent course; not routinely in simple self-limiting gastroenteritis
Imaging
Suspected raised ICP, haemorrhage, meningitis or stroke → urgent same-day neurological/acute assessment, which sets imaging — not a routine outpatient CT. Abdominal imaging via the acute take if obstruction suspected — do not arrange routine outpatient imaging for a suspected obstruction.
Endoscopy
NG12 consider non-urgent direct-access OGD in people 55 and over with nausea or vomiting and weight loss, reflux, dyspepsia or upper abdominal pain; also with raised platelet count. 60 and over with nausea or vomiting plus weight loss → consider urgent direct-access CT for pancreatic cancer (USS if CT unavailable). Dysphagia at any age → suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)). Aged 55 and over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia (with or without nausea) → suspected cancer pathway referral (NG12 recs 1.2.1/1.2.7, amended May 2025).
Gastric emptying
Gastric emptying study or scintigraphy for suspected gastroparesis — secondary care, and only after mechanical obstruction has been excluded endoscopically
Do not do
Do not order a routine abdominal X-ray in primary care for vomiting; do not start empirical PPI or antiemetic in place of a pregnancy test; do not request a gastric emptying study before an OGD
The pregnancy test is first because getting it wrong has two distinct consequences: a missed ectopic pregnancy, and the prescription of a teratogen to a woman who did not know she was pregnant. Calcium is included in the baseline panel because hypercalcaemia is a treatable and easily overlooked cause of nausea, constipation, thirst and confusion, and it is frequently the first sign of malignancy or hyperparathyroidism. Hypokalaemia in a persistent vomiter is both a consequence and a compounding cause — it worsens ileus and gastric stasis, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that has to be broken by replacement. On the NG12 wording: nausea and vomiting sits in the "consider non-urgent direct-access endoscopy" group in people aged 55 and over with additional features, whereas dysphagia at any age, or age 55 and over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia, is a suspected cancer pathway referral (the urgent route) — these two thresholds are commonly confused, and quoting the wrong one either delays a cancer diagnosis or floods the endoscopy service.
6
Refer

Referral Criteria — Emergency, suspected cancer, and routine pathways

999 now
Peritonism, perforation or ischaemia; shock or reduced consciousness; thunderclap headache; suspected bacterial meningitis or meningococcal disease; DKA with severe illness; suspected MI; suspected adrenal crisis; acute stroke; drowsiness or new confusion
Same-day surgical take
Suspected bowel obstruction (bilious or faeculent vomiting, distension, absolute constipation); suspected appendicitis, pancreatitis or cholecystitis; upper GI bleed (999 if fresh red blood, shock, syncope or known cirrhosis)
Same-day medical take
Unable to tolerate oral fluids >24 hours; significant dehydration or AKI; hyponatraemia or hypokalaemia; suspected hypercalcaemia; suspected digoxin toxicity
Urgent neurology / acute
Vomiting with new progressive headache, worse on waking or lying flat; papilloedema; focal neurology; new seizure; personality change → urgent same-day neurological assessment (NG127/NG12)
Suspected cancer: upper GI
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): dysphagia at any age → suspected cancer pathway referral for oesophageal cancer. Consider non-urgent direct-access OGD in people aged 55+ with nausea or vomiting plus weight loss, reflux, dyspepsia or upper abdominal pain — or with a raised platelet count. Aged 55 and over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia (with or without nausea) → suspected cancer pathway referral (NG12 recs 1.2.1/1.2.7, amended May 2025).
Suspected cancer: other sites
NG12: 60 and over with nausea or vomiting and weight loss → consider urgent CT for pancreatic cancer; otherwise the relevant site-specific pathway by age and symptoms. Hypercalcaemia with vomiting in an older patient → investigate for myeloma (FBC, ESR/plasma viscosity, calcium, protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains, sent together) and malignancy
Obstetrics, same-day
Severe NVP — unable to keep down fluids or antiemetics, dehydration, weight loss ≥5%, AKI/electrolyte disturbance, comorbidity or failed outpatient treatment; any vomiting after 20 weeks with hypertension, headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain or jaundice; BP ≥160/110 or neurological signs → 999
Gastroenterology (routine)
Persistent or recurrent nausea/vomiting despite appropriate assessment and treatment (earlier if progressive or red flags); suspected gastroparesis; suspected functional or cyclical vomiting syndrome; suspected achalasia
Eating disorder service
Self-induced vomiting, dietary restriction, dental erosion or electrolyte disturbance — refer urgently if there are physical risk markers (bradycardia, hypotension, hypokalaemia)
Primary care manage
Gastroenteritis, migraine, drug-induced (dose review), vestibular causes, mild NVP, functional nausea with a clear diagnosis and safety-net
Suspected bowel obstruction goes to the surgical take, not to an outpatient scan, because the decision needed is fluid resuscitation, nasogastric decompression and imaging within hours; arranging an outpatient CT for a patient with bilious vomiting and a distended abdomen is a well-recognised route to a strangulated bowel. The brain tumour pathway is worth naming explicitly: NG12 directs consideration of an urgent direct-access MRI (or CT if MRI is contraindicated) within two weeks for adults with progressive sub-acute loss of central neurological function, and morning vomiting with headache in an adult with no migraine history is exactly that picture. Hypercalcaemia earns a mention on the cancer list in its own right because in an older patient with vomiting, constipation, thirst and confusion, it is often the presenting biochemical abnormality of myeloma — and NG12 names FBC, calcium, plasma viscosity or ESR, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains (urine Bence Jones only if light chains are unavailable) as the myeloma tests, sent together.

Treat

Treatment — Antiemetic matched to mechanism

Vestibular / motion
Cyclizine 50 mg TDS 1st line
H1 antihistamine (sedation, anticholinergic effects). Alternative: prochlorperazine 5 mg TDS or 3 mg buccal BD. Ondansetron is ineffective for vestibular nausea. Avoid prolonged use — it delays vestibular compensation.
Gastric stasis / gastroparesis
Metoclopramide 10 mg TDS prokinetic
Max 30 mg/24 h, maximum 5 days (MHRA) — watch for acute dystonia, akathisia and parkinsonism (stop if they occur); extra caution in younger people; avoid in Parkinson's; not in obstruction, perforation or GI haemorrhage. Domperidone: lowest dose, ≤1 week, max 30 mg/day (≥12 y and ≥35 kg); contraindicated with QT prolongation, cardiac disease/heart failure, electrolyte disturbance, moderate–severe hepatic impairment, QT-prolonging drugs or potent CYP3A4 inhibitors. Chronic gastroparesis → gastroenterology.
Chemical, metabolic, drug-induced
Ondansetron 5-HT3
Effective for chemotherapy, opioid and post-operative nausea. Dose per indication and SPC; GM: restricted outside oncology/post-operative use. Constipation; QT risk — correct K⁺/Mg²⁺, ECG if higher risk. Haloperidol for specialist or palliative use. In pregnancy see below.
Raised intracranial pressure
Dexamethasone + cyclizine
Specialist-directed: dexamethasone reduces peritumoural oedema. This is a referral, not a prescription made alone — arrange urgent same-day neurological/acute assessment first.
Pregnancy
First line (RCOG 2024) 1st line
Doxylamine/pyridoxine, cyclizine, promethazine or prochlorperazine. Second line: metoclopramide or ondansetron — not contraindicated; discuss about 3 extra orofacial clefts per 10,000 births with first-trimester exposure (MHRA) against the risks of untreated vomiting. Combine classes if one fails. Thiamine if admitted or very poor intake. See the NVP pathway.
Migraine
Prochlorperazine or metoclopramide + triptan
An antiemetic with prokinetic action both treats the nausea and improves absorption of the analgesic. Consider a non-oral route (buccal prochlorperazine, rectal domperidone) when vomiting prevents absorption.
Step 1Treat the cause. Stop or reduce the offending drug; correct hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia; treat infection; relieve constipation. Most drug-induced nausea resolves on dose reduction alone.
Step 2Rehydrate. Small, frequent sips of ORS or another tolerated fluid (care in heart failure or advanced CKD). Escalate to admission for IV fluids if oral intake fails for 24 hours or there is AKI.
Step 3Single antiemetic matched to mechanism, at an adequate dose, regularly rather than as required, for a defined short course with a review date.
Step 4Non-oral route if oral is not retained — buccal prochlorperazine, rectal domperidone, or subcutaneous route in palliative care. An oral antiemetic that is vomited back has done nothing.
Step 5Reconsider the diagnosis if no response in 48–72 hours — check hydration, route and adherence. If the cause still fits, a second agent from a different class is reasonable (especially pregnancy or palliative care), reviewing QT, sedation, anticholinergic and extrapyramidal risk.
The MHRA restrictions on metoclopramide (2013) and domperidone (2014) changed prescribing substantially and are frequently overlooked: metoclopramide is limited to a maximum of five days because of extrapyramidal reactions and tardive dyskinesia, with particular caution in young people, and domperidone is limited to short courses at the lowest effective dose because of QT prolongation and sudden cardiac death. Giving a prokinetic in mechanical obstruction risks perforation and is an absolute contraindication. The route matters as much as the drug — an oral tablet given to a patient who is actively vomiting is a wasted prescription, and buccal or rectal alternatives are underused in general practice. The most important line in this step is the last one: failure of a well-chosen antiemetic should prompt a return to step 1 first, because a common reason for treatment failure is an unrecognised cause; if the cause still fits, a second agent from a different class is reasonable, avoiding unnecessary polypharmacy.

Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Hydration, diet and behavioural measures

Small frequent sips Small frequent amounts are better tolerated than large volumes. Oral rehydration salts rather than water alone where there is diarrhoea or heavy losses.
Eat to the symptom Small, low-fat, low-fibre meals; dry starchy foods; avoid large meals, which worsen gastric stasis. Cold food has less odour and is often better tolerated than hot.
Gastroparesis diet Small frequent meals, liquid or pureed calories, low fat and low fibre, sit upright for an hour after eating, avoid carbonated drinks and alcohol.
Sick-day rules Diabetes: follow diabetes sick-day rules (never stop insulin in type 1; check ketones). Adrenal insufficiency or significant HPA-axis suppression only: individual steroid sick-day plan; if oral steroids cannot be absorbed, hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV and emergency assessment (NG243). Not routine for a short prednisolone course. Medicines: while unable to keep fluids down, pause metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics and NSAIDs; restart when eating and drinking normally. Give in writing.
Ginger and acupressure Ginger for mild–moderate NVP after discussing preference; acupressure as an adjunct. Modest evidence — never delay antiemetics or rehydration.
Cannabis cessation The only effective treatment for cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome. Explain the link; recurrence on re-exposure supports the diagnosis; resolution may take days to weeks; offer substance-misuse support for recurrent presentations.
Infection control Norovirus: stay off work or school until 48 hours symptom-free, hand washing with soap and water (alcohol gel is ineffective), do not prepare food for others.
Dental protection After vomiting, rinse with water or a fluoride mouthwash but do not brush for an hour — brushing enamel softened by acid causes erosion. Important in NVP and in eating disorders.
Sick-day rules are the single highest-value piece of non-drug advice in this pathway because they prevent two specific emergencies. In type 1 diabetes, the instinct to omit insulin when not eating precipitates ketoacidosis; the correct advice is to continue insulin, test glucose and ketones more often, and maintain carbohydrate intake in liquid form. In steroid-dependent patients, vomiting means the oral replacement dose is not absorbed, and an adrenal crisis can develop within hours — these patients need an emergency injection kit and a clear plan. Advising against brushing teeth immediately after vomiting is a small but genuinely useful detail: gastric acid demineralises enamel, and mechanical brushing in the following hour accelerates irreversible erosion, which is why dental damage is often the most visible long-term consequence of recurrent vomiting.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

48–72 hours
Telephone or face-to-face review if treating symptomatically: is the patient hydrated, is the antiemetic working, has anything new appeared? Failure to improve triggers re-assessment of the diagnosis first.
1 week
Vomiting beyond 5–7 days, recurring, or not following the expected course → reassess the diagnosis. Reconsider obstruction, raised ICP, metabolic causes, pregnancy and malignancy. Arrange bloods if not already done.
2–4 weeks
Review weight, hydration and electrolytes; stop short-course antiemetics (metoclopramide max 5 days, domperidone max 1 week); chase investigation results actively
Persisting despite treatment
Persistent unexplained nausea → gastroenterology when initial assessment and treatment fail (earlier with weight loss, early satiety, recurrent dehydration or red flags); reassess for functional nausea, gastroparesis, cyclical vomiting, eating disorder; review the drug list again
999 now
Severe abdominal pain with a rigid abdomen; vomiting blood; sudden severe headache; drowsiness or confusion; chest pain; seizure; a rash that does not fade under a glass; sudden weakness of the face, arm or leg, or slurred speech
Same-day GP
Unable to keep any fluid down for 24 hours; passing very little urine; dizzy on standing; headache worse on waking or lying flat; new weakness that came on gradually, or visual change; diabetes with ketones
Written safety-net
Give specific instructions on rehydration, when to return, and — in diabetes and adrenal insufficiency — the sick-day plan. Document what was said.
Remote or OOH
Convert to face-to-face or emergency assessment if observations, postural BP, abdominal examination, fundoscopy, a pregnancy test, or glucose and ketones cannot be obtained; the patient has diabetes, takes steroids, is pregnant, is frail or lives alone; symptoms change during the call; or timely review cannot be guaranteed. Under time pressure, choose the safer destination.
Re-investigate if
Weight loss, dysphagia, anaemia or a new abdominal mass develops → NG12 pathway; neurological signs appear → urgent same-day neurological assessment; the pattern changes character
The 48-hour review is where most diagnostic saves happen in this pathway. Acute vomiting from gastroenteritis is nearly always improving by then, so persistence is a strong signal that the original working diagnosis was wrong — and the conditions that masquerade as gastroenteritis for 48 hours (obstruction, raised ICP, DKA, Addison's, hypercalcaemia) are precisely the dangerous ones. The instinct at that review is to add or change antiemetic; the discipline is to go back to the history and examination. Equally important is the active management of short-course antiemetics: metoclopramide and domperidone prescriptions issued in an acute episode have a tendency to become repeat items, which is how MHRA duration limits are quietly breached over months. Every antiemetic prescription should carry a review date from the moment it is issued.
