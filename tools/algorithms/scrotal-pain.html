Acute and Chronic Scrotal Pain in Primary Care Adults and young people with testicular or scrotal pain, swelling or recurrent/chronic symptoms · Based on BASHH 2020, NICE NG12, GIRFT, Greater Manchester antimicrobial guidance, EAU
Scope: adults and young people presenting to general practice with testicular or scrotal pain, swelling, lump or recurrent/chronic symptoms. This guide separates four presentations: (1) acute scrotal pain — exclude torsion and other emergencies first (Step 1); (2) acute infective/inflammatory scrotal pain (Steps 2–8); (3) scrotal swelling or mass — see Scrotal Lumps and Swelling; (4) persistent or recurrent pain lasting ≥3 months (Steps 9–10).
This guide does not replace the local emergency urology pathway, local antimicrobial guideline, sexual-health advice or paediatric surgical pathway.

Safety

Step 1 — Immediate Safety Screen: Torsion & Other Emergencies

Sudden-onset or severe unilateral scrotal pain is a surgical emergency until torsion has been excluded. Do not delay urgent transfer or urological assessment for ultrasound, blood tests or response to analgesia. Give appropriate analgesia while arranging immediate transfer.

Use the local emergency pathway: transfer immediately to an emergency department or surgical/urological service able to assess and operate. Call 999 for an ambulance if there is haemodynamic instability, severe systemic illness, inability to transfer safely or another emergency indication. Children and young people: refer immediately to a centre able to provide surgical exploration — do not transfer a child merely to obtain an ultrasound (GIRFT).

Torsion is time-critical: the chance of testicular salvage declines the longer the testis is twisted. The 6-hour figure conveys urgency, but surgery is not futile after 6 hours — salvage may still be possible later. No single history or examination finding reliably confirms or excludes torsion, and colour Doppler ultrasound has a user-dependent false-negative rate; BASHH advises that suspected clinical torsion needs urgent exploration rather than reassurance from imaging. Give analgesia promptly — it must not delay transfer. A painless lump without acute pain follows the suspected cancer route (NICE NG12) — see Scrotal Lumps and Swelling.

Take a focused history before examination. Use risk factors, not age alone, to estimate the likely cause and pathogen.

Onset & pattern
Exact time and mode of onset. Sudden → torsion / trauma. Gradual (hours–days) → epididymo-orchitis. Intermittent or recurrent episodes. Insidious → structural cause or chronic pain
Severity & systemic
Severity and functional impact. Nausea/vomiting (raises concern for torsion). Fever or systemic symptoms
Age
Relevant (torsion is commoner in young people; enteric infection in older men with urinary pathology) but must not determine the likely pathogen or treatment by itself — combine with sexual and urinary risk
Sexual history
New or multiple sexual partners, condom use, insertive anal intercourse. Previous STI or epididymo-orchitis. Take sensitively and confidentially
Urinary symptoms
Dysuria, frequency, retention or urethral discharge. Previous urinary infection, catheterisation or instrumentation
Previous episodes
Short-lived severe episodes that resolved → suggest intermittent torsion (urgent urology). Recurrent epididymo-orchitis
Surgery & trauma
Trauma, vasectomy, hernia repair or abdominal surgery. Scrotal swelling or lump → see Scrotal Lumps and Swelling
Referred sources
Loin, groin, hip, back or pelvic-floor symptoms
Fertility

Medication, comorbidity and immunosuppression
The history sets the urgency before examination. Age is relevant, but the 2024 European epididymo-orchitis guideline cautions against using age as a surrogate for STI risk — estimate the likely pathogen from sexual and urinary risk factors. Previous brief, self-resolving episodes may represent intermittent torsion. Missing sexually acquired infection risks complications and onward transmission. A thorough sexual history is a core RCGP competency and must be taken sensitively.

Step 3 — Differential Diagnosis by Presentation

Classify into the main diagnostic categories to guide investigation and management pathway.

Testicular Torsion
Sudden-onset severe pain ± nausea/vomiting. High or horizontal testis and absent cremasteric reflex increase concern but do not confirm it. Any age. Immediate emergency transfer — do not wait for imaging
Torsion of appendix testis
Localised upper-pole tenderness ± blue-dot sign. If confidently diagnosed: analgesia, support and safety-net. Urgent surgical review if uncertain or worsening
Epididymo-orchitis
Usually gradual onset, tender epididymis ± testis, ± fever, urinary or urethral symptoms. Likely pathogen from risk factors, not age alone: sexually acquired (Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, M. genitalium) vs enteric/uropathogen (e.g. E. coli). Test before antibiotics where this will not delay treatment
Hydrocele
Usually painless, transilluminates, surrounds testis. In adults assess clinically; USS if the testis cannot be adequately examined or the cause is uncertain. See Scrotal Lumps and Swelling
Varicocele
"Bag of worms" palpable standing, reduces supine, usually left-sided. May cause dragging ache. Supportive treatment; urology if symptomatic, right-sided, bilateral, large or associated with subfertility
Epididymal cyst
Smooth, separate from testis, transilluminates. USS if uncertain; reassure if confirmed benign and asymptomatic
Inguinal hernia
Lump extending to groin, reducible lying down, cough impulse. Elective referral if uncomplicated; emergency if irreducible, vomiting or abdominal pain
Testicular malignancy
Non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of the testis, or a solid intratesticular lesion → suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12). An acutely painful mass needs emergency assessment for torsion, trauma or infection first
Referred pain
Renal/ureteric colic, inguinal hernia, ilioinguinal/genitofemoral nerve, lumbar radiculopathy, hip. Scrotal examination often normal — examine abdomen, loins, back and hips
Prostatitis / CPPS
Perineal/testicular pain with LUTS or ejaculatory pain, often without infection — see Step 10
Chronic scrotal pain (orchialgia)
Persistent or recurrent pain ≥3 months after urgent pathology, malignancy and treatable infection have been considered — see Steps 9–10
Classification determines the management pathway. Torsion cannot be excluded by any single finding, so diagnostic uncertainty means emergency assessment. Epididymo-orchitis has two microbiological pathways (sexually acquired vs enteric) requiring different antibiotics — choose by risk factors, not age alone. A solid intratesticular lesion needs suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12). Right-sided varicocele should prompt USS to exclude a renal/retroperitoneal mass.

Step 4 — Targeted Examination

Examine standing and supine. Ensure privacy; offer a chaperone and document the offer.

Observations
Temp, HR, BP, RR, SpO₂ and sepsis assessment. Fever + tachycardia → severe epididymo-orchitis / Fournier's gangrene → escalate urgency
Abdomen & inguinal
Abdomen and renal angles (referred pain). Inguinal canals and hernial orifices, cough impulse → hernia. Lymphadenopathy → infection, malignancy
Scrotal skin
Erythema, oedema, necrosis, bullae or crepitus → Fournier's gangrene (emergency). Scrotal oedema/erythema → epididymo-orchitis. Skin changes → dermatitis, infection
Testicular position
Position and lie: high-riding or horizontal testis increases concern for torsion. A normal testicular position does not exclude torsion
Cremasteric reflex
Document as one finding only. An absent cremasteric reflex, high or horizontal testis, sudden pain, nausea/vomiting and marked testicular tenderness increase concern for torsion, but no single examination finding reliably excludes or confirms torsion. A preserved reflex does not exclude torsion
Testis, epididymis & cord
Testicular size, consistency, focal masses and tenderness. Distinguish the testis from an extratesticular swelling. Epididymal tenderness posteriorly → epididymitis. Palpate the spermatic cord. Do not use Prehn's sign to exclude torsion. It is unreliable.
Transillumination
Torch in dark room → glows red → hydrocele or epididymal cyst. Does not transilluminate → solid lesion or haematocele
Varicocele
Assess standing and supine: dilated veins standing that reduce lying → varicocele (left side predominant). Right-sided or non-reducing → imaging
Referred pain
Neurological, hip and lumbar assessment when referred pain is suspected
DRE
Only when clinically indicated, e.g. suspected prostatitis or significant LUTS
No single sign is diagnostic of torsion: an absent cremasteric reflex raises concern, but a present reflex or normal testicular position does not exclude it. Prehn's sign (historically taught as differentiating epididymitis from torsion) is unreliable and should not alter management. Transillumination is a key clinical skill — a glowing swelling almost always reflects a benign fluid collection. Fournier's gangrene is a life-threatening necrotising fasciitis of the perineum — even mild skin changes with systemic upset warrant immediate escalation.

Step 5 — Investigations

Do not arrange or await investigations — including ultrasound — when clinical suspicion of torsion is high. For suspected epididymo-orchitis, take samples before antibiotics where this will not delay treatment.

Urinalysis + MSU 1st line
Urine dipstick. MSU for culture and sensitivities where urinary/enteric infection is possible. Positive nitrites/leucocytes → uropathogen more likely. Negative doesn't exclude STI-related disease
NAAT swab 1st line STI
First-pass urine (or appropriate) NAAT for Chlamydia trachomatis + Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Consider urethral microscopy/swab if discharge is present and available through sexual health services
Scrotal USS Doppler Only if stable
Do not arrange or await USS when clinical suspicion of torsion is high. If torsion is possible but not clinically clear and the patient is stable, an urgent Doppler USS may assist, provided this does not delay surgical review. A negative ultrasound does not reliably exclude intermittent torsion or torsion in an equivocal case
Tumour markers Per local pathway
Do not delay urgent urology referral for tumour markers. If a testicular malignancy is suspected, AFP, β-hCG and LDH may be requested according to the local pathway, ideally before definitive treatment. Normal markers do not exclude testicular cancer. Not routine for every scrotal lump
FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures
If systemically unwell (usually in hospital). Not routine for mild presentations
STI screen
Offer HIV and syphilis testing where an STI is suspected or confirmed. Consider hepatitis B/C testing according to sexual and injecting-risk history
Lump or persistent/unexplained symptoms
Urgent direct-access scrotal USS (NICE NG12). Urgent urology/suspected cancer referral for non-painful testicular enlargement, change in shape/texture or a suspicious intratesticular lesion — see Scrotal Lumps and Swelling
Uropathogen confirmed
Particularly with recurrent infection or a urinary tract abnormality → arrange urological assessment to exclude obstruction or structural disease (BASHH)
Do not routinely include
PSA during acute prostatitis or active epididymo-orchitis. Testosterone, LH/FSH, fasting glucose or ESR for uncomplicated acute scrotal pain. CT/MRI as first-line. Semen analysis after a single episode — reserve for fertility concerns, bilateral disease or persistent concerns
Colour Doppler ultrasound has a user-dependent false-negative rate, so clinical suspicion overrides a "normal" scan (BASHH). NAAT is needed because culture alone misses Chlamydia. NICE NG12 recommends considering a suspected cancer pathway referral for a non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of the testis, and considering direct-access ultrasound for unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms. Tumour markers help staging after referral but must never delay it.
Refer

Step 6 — Referral Criteria and Pathways

Emergency transfer / 999
Suspected torsion (any age) — immediate transfer via the local emergency pathway to a service able to assess and operate; 999 ambulance if haemodynamically unstable, severely unwell or unable to transfer safely. Fournier's gangrene. Incarcerated inguinal hernia. Trauma with suspected rupture
Same-day hospital
Sepsis or haemodynamic compromise. Severe pain or vomiting preventing oral treatment. Suspected abscess, infarction or Fournier's gangrene. Immunocompromise with significant infection. Failure to improve after 48–72 h. Diagnostic uncertainty regarding torsion (including suspected appendage torsion)
Suspected cancer: urology
Non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of the testis. Solid intratesticular lesion on USS (NICE NG12). Unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms → urgent direct-access USS
Urology
Urgent: episodes suggesting intermittent torsion; confirmed uropathogen with recurrent infection or urinary tract abnormality. Routine: varicocele if symptomatic, right-sided, bilateral, large or with subfertility; large symptomatic hydrocele; epididymal cyst requiring treatment; chronic scrotal pain that is persistent, disabling, post-vasectomy, associated with varicocele or not responding to initial measures
Sexual health
Refer or discuss for: gonorrhoea risk or urethral discharge, positive NAAT, partner notification, contact tracing, test of cure where gonorrhoea is confirmed, suspected or confirmed Mycoplasma genitalium. Mixed STI/enteric risk → discuss with sexual health/microbiology
Physiotherapy / pain service
Pelvic-floor physiotherapy for pelvic-floor overactivity, trigger points or CPPS features. Pain service for neuropathic pain, significant disability, sleep disturbance, psychological comorbidity or failed conservative treatment
Primary care manage
Uncomplicated epididymo-orchitis responding to treatment. Confidently diagnosed appendage torsion. Confirmed benign, asymptomatic hydrocele or epididymal cyst. Asymptomatic small varicocele in men not seeking fertility
Torsion is time-critical — use the local emergency pathway; for children and young people the GIRFT pathway supports immediate referral to a centre able to provide surgical exploration. Suspected cancer referral criteria follow NICE NG12. Sexual-health services provide partner notification, contact tracing and test of cure, preventing reinfection and onward transmission. Recurrent uropathogen epididymo-orchitis may indicate obstruction or structural urinary tract disease (BASHH).

Step 7 — Acute Treatment by Presentation

Treat epididymo-orchitis by likely pathogen using risk factors, not age alone. Follow current BASHH, local sexual-health and Greater Manchester antimicrobial guidance.

Likely sexually acquired
Ceftriaxone + Doxycycline Sexual health
Ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose + doxycycline 100 mg BD PO for 14 days (BASHH 2020 regimen, 10–14 days; 14 days standardised here). Use the local sexual-health pathway and gonorrhoea guidance. Avoid fluoroquinolones (gonorrhoea resistance). No sexual contact until treatment completed, symptoms improved and partners assessed/treated per sexual-health advice
Likely enteric / uropathogen
Per GM guideline MSU first
Consider with positive urine leucocytes/nitrites, older age with low STI risk, recent urinary instrumentation or catheterisation, urinary tract abnormality or obstruction, recent urological procedure, or no STI features — not age alone. Send MSU before treatment where possible; follow the Greater Manchester antimicrobial guideline and susceptibilities (GM lists ofloxacin 200 mg BD for 14 days for suspected epididymitis in men over 35 with low STI risk). MHRA (January 2024): use a systemic fluoroquinolone only when other commonly recommended antibiotics are inappropriate, taking account of microbiology, contraindications, resistance, previous treatment and local advice. Tell patients to stop and seek advice if tendon pain/swelling, neuropathic symptoms, marked muscle or joint symptoms, severe mood change, confusion or other serious adverse effects

Step 8 — Supportive Care & Prevention

Analgesia Give promptly; paracetamol if appropriate; NSAID only after checking renal, GI, cardiovascular and anticoagulant contraindications. Do not exceed the maximum daily dose
Scrotal support / elevation Supportive underwear or jockstrap. May ease pain in epididymo-orchitis, varicocele and chronic scrotal pain
Sexual health No sexual contact until treatment completed, symptoms improved and partners assessed/treated. Condom use. Sexual-health services for partner notification and STI prevention advice
Testicular awareness Know what is normal. Report any new lump, swelling or change in shape or texture promptly
Rest & activity modification Rest and avoid heavy lifting during the acute phase. Resume activity gradually as pain settles
Local cooling May help acute inflammatory pain. Wrap cold packs — avoid direct ice contact with skin
Fertility Semen analysis and fertility advice only for fertility concerns, bilateral disease or persistent concerns — not routinely after a single episode. Sperm banking discussion before cancer treatment
Smoking cessation Smoking impairs sperm quality and testicular blood flow. Offer referral to NHS Stop Smoking service
Alcohol reduction Excess alcohol impairs spermatogenesis. AUDIT-C score; brief intervention if score ≥5
Supportive measures relieve symptoms while antibiotics act. Testicular self-examination is not recommended as a population screening tool, but awareness of what is normal helps men present promptly with a new lump. Semen analysis is not routine after a single episode of epididymo-orchitis; reserve it for fertility concerns, bilateral disease or persistent concerns. Men diagnosed with testicular cancer should be offered sperm banking before treatment. Partner notification and treatment reduce reinfection and onward transmission.

Step 9 — Chronic Scrotal Pain (≥3 Months): Define & Exclude

Persistent or recurrent scrotal/testicular pain for at least 3 months that affects function, sleep, sexual activity or quality of life, after urgent pathology, malignancy and treatable infection have been considered.

Exclude first
Torsion, including intermittent torsion. Testicular malignancy. Epididymo-orchitis or abscess. Hernia. Varicocele. Hydrocele or epididymal cyst. Post-operative/inguinal nerve pain. Renal colic. Lumbar radiculopathy. Hip or pelvic-floor pathology. Chronic pelvic pain syndrome
Terminology
The EAU uses primary scrotal pain syndrome or primary testicular pain syndrome when there is no proven infection or other local pathology. "Chronic orchialgia" remains understandable clinically but is a descriptive term, not a formally specific disease
History
Vasectomy, hernia repair (ilioinguinal nerve), previous epididymo-orchitis, trauma. LUTS or pain on ejaculation (prostatitis/CPPS). Back, hip or groin pain. Prolonged sitting or cycling. Treatments already tried
Impact
Document pain severity (e.g. 0–10 scale) and functional impact on sleep, work, sexual function and mood. Review sexual, urinary, bowel, musculoskeletal and psychosocial factors, including cancer worry
Examination
As Step 4, plus inguinal tenderness after hernia repair, groin/inner-thigh sensation, lumbar spine and hip, pelvic-floor and trigger-point tenderness. DRE only if prostatitis or significant LUTS
Ultrasound
Request urgent direct-access scrotal ultrasound for unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms, a new lump, uncertain examination or where the testis cannot be adequately assessed. Do not routinely repeat ultrasound when a recent high-quality scan is normal and there has been no change in examination or symptoms
Normal ultrasound
Excludes many structural lesions but does not exclude chronic pain, neuropathic pain, pelvic-floor dysfunction or intermittent torsion
Other tests
Urinalysis, MSU and NAAT if infection is possible. Imaging for referred pain (e.g. renal tract, lumbar spine) only when clinically suspected. Suspected chronic bacterial prostatitis → obtain appropriate cultures and seek urological/microbiological advice
Chronic scrotal pain should only be labelled after structural, malignant, infective and referred causes have been considered — but it should not be presented as a formally specific disease (EAU chronic pelvic pain guidance). Ultrasound is not mandatory in every man and will often be normal; it excludes structural pathology, while repeated normal scans can reinforce reassurance-seeking without changing management.
Treat

Step 10 — Chronic Scrotal Pain: Multimodal Management

The central message is multimodal management, not repeated antibiotic or analgesic prescribing.

Explain
Explain the diagnosis and that cancer has been excluded, without reinforcing repeated reassurance-seeking. Set realistic, function-based goals
Self-management
Scrotal support and activity modification where helpful. Simple analgesia by shared decision-making — regular use is not necessarily better than as-required; never exceed the maximum daily dose
Physical activity & psychology
NICE NG193 prioritises physical activity and psychological therapies (e.g. CBT, ACT) for chronic primary pain
Physiotherapy
Pelvic-floor physiotherapy if pelvic-floor overactivity, trigger-point tenderness or CPPS features. Physiotherapy for post-hernia-repair or musculoskeletal pain
Urology
If pain is persistent, disabling, post-vasectomy, associated with varicocele or not responding to initial measures. Nerve blocks or surgical options are specialist decisions
Pain service
Neuropathic pain, significant disability, sleep disturbance, psychological comorbidity or failed conservative treatment
Neuropathic phenotypeOnly if convincing: cautious off-label trial using general neuropathic-pain guidance (NICE CG173) — amitriptyline 10 mg at night, titrated cautiously by response and tolerability; or gabapentin, pregabalin or duloxetine depending on comorbidity, interactions and local formulary
ReviewReview benefit, function and adverse effects within an agreed timeframe. Avoid routine combination of neuropathic-pain medicines in primary care. Taper rather than stop abruptly when clinically appropriate
CautionsGabapentin/pregabalin: renal dosing, misuse/dependence assessment, controlled-drug prescribing requirements. Amitriptyline: avoid or use great caution in older adults, cardiac conduction disease, glaucoma, urinary retention and substantial anticholinergic burden
Primary painDo not initiate NSAIDs, opioids, paracetamol or gabapentinoids solely for chronic primary pain (NICE NG193)
AntibioticsDo not prescribe prolonged antibiotics (including fluoroquinolones) for CPPS without evidence of bacterial infection
TamsulosinNot routine for testicular pain. Consider only with significant LUTS and a prostate-pain/CPPS phenotype; review benefit
NICE CG173 supports offering a choice of amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin for neuropathic pain in general — this is not high-quality evidence specifically for chronic orchialgia. For chronic primary pain, NICE NG193 prioritises physical activity and psychological therapies and advises against initiating NSAIDs, opioids, paracetamol or gabapentinoids solely for chronic primary pain. The EAU chronic pelvic pain guidance supports a multimodal, phenotype-directed approach.

On a fluoroquinolone: stop and seek advice if tendon pain/swelling, neuropathic symptoms, marked muscle or joint symptoms, severe mood change or confusion
Fertility follow-up
Semen analysis only for fertility concerns, bilateral disease or persistent concerns — not routinely after a single episode. Refer fertility services if abnormal
Suspected cancer safety net
Any new non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of the testis → urgent USS / suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12), regardless of a previous normal scan
Chronic scrotal pain
Structured review of function, sleep, sexual function, mood, medicine benefit and adverse effects at agreed intervals. Refer urology/pain service if not improving. Avoid indefinite repeat prescriptions without review
Early review detects non-response, abscess formation or a missed alternative diagnosis. Persistent swelling must trigger USS because testicular tumours can present sub-acutely and may initially appear as "epididymo-orchitis". Patients with chronic scrotal pain can still develop torsion or other acute pathology — new acute severe pain needs fresh emergency assessment. Clear written safety-netting on when to seek emergency help is a patient safety imperative and RCGP examination competency.
