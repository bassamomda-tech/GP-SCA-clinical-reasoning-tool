Safety

Red Flags — Sepsis, Malignancy & Vasculitis

Suspected infection + abnormal physiology (fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnoea, altered consciousness) Suspected sepsis — assess immediately with physiological observations and NEWS2, while considering alternative diagnoses (NICE NG253). High risk: NEWS2 ≥7, or NEWS2 5–6 with a single parameter scoring 3 or additional clinical concern → emergency care, pre-alert and immediate transfer. Moderate risk: NEWS2 5–6 → urgent senior clinical assessment; hospital referral if the diagnosis is uncertain or safe community management cannot be established. NEWS2 does not override clinical judgement. Do not use CRP alone to diagnose sepsis — serious infection can occur with a modest or initially normal CRP. Fluids in hospital are individualised (smaller crystalloid boluses with reassessment; caution in heart failure, renal impairment, frailty) per local emergency protocols.
Age >50 + new localised headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication, visual symptoms, constitutional symptoms or compatible PMR symptoms Giant cell arteritis (GCA) — a clinical diagnosis; markers may be modestly raised, discordant or rarely normal. Ischaemic optic neuropathy risk — irreversible blindness. → Do not wait for CRP/ESR: start prednisolone 40–60 mg daily per the local fast-track protocol and refer same day. Visual loss, amaurosis fugax, diplopia or other ischaemic symptoms → immediate same-day emergency ophthalmology/acute medical assessment with glucocorticoid started immediately (specialist-directed high dose, may include IV methylprednisolone). Temporal artery ultrasound and/or biopsy urgently per the local GCA pathway — treatment is not delayed.
New back pain + focal spinal tenderness, persistent or nocturnal pain, fever, recent bacteraemia, immunosuppression or IV drug use (with raised markers) Spinal epidural abscess or discitis/vertebral osteomyelitis — urgency set by the whole picture, not CRP alone. → Emergency admission and urgent MRI if neurological deficit, cauda equina symptoms, cord compression, sepsis/haemodynamic instability or rapidly progressive symptoms. Without neurological deficit → urgent hospital/specialist assessment and MRI via the local spinal infection pathway — no routine outpatient MRI delay where suspicion is substantial.
Raised ESR/CRP + weight loss, night sweats, lymphadenopathy, bone pain or unexplained fracture Possible haematological malignancy (lymphoma, myeloma) or solid tumour. An isolated raised ESR is not enough to diagnose or refer. Myeloma (NICE NG12): aged 60+ with persistent bone (especially back) pain or unexplained fracture → FBC, calcium, ESR/plasma viscosity, SPEP + serum free light chains (urine Bence-Jones if unavailable); refer if results suggest myeloma. Lymphoma: unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly → consider a suspected cancer pathway, taking into account fever, night sweats, breathlessness, pruritus and weight loss. Same-day: suspected cord compression, AKI with possible myeloma, significant hypercalcaemia, severe anaemia or other major cytopenia, blood film suggesting acute leukaemia.
Raised CRP + pleuritic chest pain + tachycardia + recent immobility or surgery Pulmonary embolism or pleuritis/empyema. Wells score + CTPA if PE suspected. CXR + USS chest if pleural fluid. Do not attribute elevated CRP to musculoskeletal pleurisy without excluding PE.
Raised CRP/ESR + rapidly progressive renal impairment, active urinary sediment, pulmonary haemorrhage, mononeuritis multiplex, purpura or ENT disease Suspected ANCA-associated vasculitis (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, microscopic polyangiitis). ANCA supports but does not establish the diagnosis (positive in other autoimmune disease, infection, IBD, drugs and occasionally healthy people; ANCA-negative vasculitis occurs). → Urgent/same-day nephrology/rheumatology for organ-threatening features. Induction treatment is specialist-led (BSR 2025) — do not prescribe pulse methylprednisolone, cyclophosphamide or rituximab from primary care.
CRP (C-reactive protein) is a pentameric protein synthesised by the liver in response to IL-6 signalling from inflamed tissues — it rises within 6 hours of an inflammatory stimulus, peaks at 48 hours, and has a short half-life of approximately 19 hours, making it an excellent acute-phase marker that closely reflects current inflammation. ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) rises more slowly (peaks at 3-5 days) and falls more slowly — it reflects fibrinogen and immunoglobulin levels more than CRP and is influenced by RBC characteristics (anaemia falsely elevates ESR; polycythaemia falsely lowers it). The practical clinical distinction: CRP is more useful for monitoring acute responses (infection, acute inflammatory flares); ESR is more useful for monitoring chronic conditions (RA, myeloma, temporal arteritis long-term). Both are non-specific — they indicate that inflammation is present but do not identify its cause. CRP can be markedly raised in bacterial infection, severe viral infection, inflammatory disease, malignancy, tissue injury, pancreatitis, postoperative states and major trauma. NICE acute respiratory infection evidence (NG237) shows CRP thresholds cannot replace clinical assessment: CRP >100 mg/L only moderately increases the probability of pneumonia, and CRP <20 mg/L does not exclude bacterial infection. CRP supports assessment of inflammation but does not diagnose sepsis, bacterial infection or treatment failure — interpret it with examination, observations, NEWS2, source assessment, lactate, blood tests and trajectory.

Interpreting CRP and ESR — Classification

CRP interpretation
CRP <5 mg/L — normal. Not significant in isolation. CRP 5-20 mg/L — mild elevation: minor infection, early inflammatory arthritis, smoking, obesity, NAFLD, vigorous exercise. CRP 20-50 mg/L — moderate: significant inflammation; viral or early bacterial infection; active autoimmune disease flare; consider secondary cause workup. CRP 50-100 mg/L — marked: infection, inflammatory flare, malignancy or tissue injury — assess carefully. CRP >100 mg/L — prompts careful clinical assessment, not automatic antibiotics or a sepsis diagnosis: bacterial or severe viral infection, GCA, vasculitis, pancreatitis, malignancy, postoperative state or major tissue damage. These bands are supportive only — no CRP value diagnoses a condition.
ESR interpretation
ESR reference range — use the laboratory reference range, interpreted for age and sex (the age/2 and (age+10)/2 formulae are historical approximations only). ESR is raised by anaemia, renal disease, pregnancy and paraproteinaemia; plasma viscosity may be used locally. Mildly raised ESR is very common with ageing. Very high ESR — a prompt to investigate (myeloma when clinically plausible, GCA, autoimmune disease, chronic infection such as TB or endocarditis), but neither diagnostic nor specific; ESR bands are not reliable disease categories.
CRP vs ESR discordance
High CRP + normal ESR: an acute process (CRP responds faster than ESR) — not specific for bacterial infection. High ESR + normal/mild CRP: anaemia, renal disease, age, pregnancy, paraproteinaemia/myeloma (immunoglobulins raise ESR without triggering CRP), autoimmune disease such as SLE, or technical/biological variation. Polycythaemia lowers ESR. Both very high: significant chronic inflammation (TB, endocarditis, lymphoma, vasculitis, polymyalgia rheumatica).
The CRP-ESR discordance pattern is clinically useful — multiple myeloma classically produces a very elevated ESR with a disproportionately mild CRP elevation. The pathophysiology: the monoclonal immunoglobulin (paraprotein) produced by myeloma plasma cells reduces the negative surface charge (zeta potential) that normally keeps red blood cells apart, causing them to rouleaux (stack like coins), which increases the sedimentation rate dramatically. However, paraprotein does not stimulate IL-6 in the same way as a bacterial infection, so CRP remains relatively low. This pattern — very high ESR with mild CRP — is a prompt to consider myeloma when clinically plausible and request SPEP (serum protein electrophoresis) + serum free light chains + FBC per NICE NG12; it also occurs with anaemia, renal disease, age and pregnancy. Conversely, GCA produces a very high CRP (often above 50-100 mg/L) AND a very high ESR — both markers are markedly elevated, reflecting the extensive granulomatous inflammation of the arterial wall. The dual elevation of both markers supports the diagnosis, but GCA can occur with modestly raised, discordant or rarely normal markers — the diagnosis is clinical.

Differential Diagnosis by Clinical Pattern

Acute raised CRP + fever — infectious workup
Source identification: respiratory (CXR, sputum culture), urinary (MSU), skin (cellulitis — measure border, photograph), CNS (LP if meningism), cardiac (echo if valvular + bacteraemia risk — Osler nodes, Janeway lesions), bone/joint (X-ray + joint aspiration). Bloods: blood cultures x 2 + FBC (neutrophilia) + U&Es + LFTs + lactate (severity). Empirical antibiotic decision based on clinical assessment, likely source and NEWS2 — not on CRP alone. Blood cultures before antibiotics if possible.
Chronically elevated CRP/ESR — targeted autoimmune tests (guided by clinical features, not indiscriminate screening)
Joints: RF + anti-CCP/ACPA (RA); psoriatic arthritis is usually seronegative. Connective tissue: ANA (SLE, Sjogren, MCTD) + dsDNA + ENA. Vasculitis: ANCA (cANCA = PR3 = GPA; pANCA = MPO = MPA, EGPA). GCA/PMR: ESR + CRP, temporal artery tenderness, shoulder/hip girdle stiffness. IBD: faecal calprotectin per the IBD diagnostic pathway (also raised by infection, NSAIDs, colorectal neoplasia) ± colonoscopy. Sarcoidosis: ACE + CXR.
Persistent unexplained ESR + CRP in older patient — targeted investigation
SPEP + serum FLC (myeloma) · FBC + film (haematological malignancy) · LDH + urate · Renal function, LFTs, calcium, albumin, urinalysis + ACR/PCR · CXR (lung, lymphoma) · PSA, quantitative FIT and other imaging (including CT) according to symptoms and the relevant NICE NG12 pathway or specialist advice. SPEP + FLC when myeloma is clinically plausible.
Persistently mildly elevated CRP (5-20) — metabolic causes
Obesity (adipose tissue produces IL-6 + TNF — baseline CRP elevated proportional to BMI). T2DM + insulin resistance. NAFLD (hepatic inflammation). Smoking. Hypertension. Periodontal disease. Depression (bidirectional inflammatory association). These are not benign — chronic low-grade inflammation predicts CVD, T2DM, and dementia risk.
Persistently mildly elevated CRP (between 5-20 mg/L) in the absence of acute illness is one of the most frequently encountered and least acted-upon findings in primary care — it is almost always attributable to lifestyle and metabolic factors (obesity, smoking, physical inactivity, insulin resistance) rather than undiagnosed occult disease. The clinical significance: chronic low-grade inflammation is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, T2DM, Alzheimer's dementia, and depression. However, the finding of a mildly elevated CRP in an asymptomatic obese middle-aged patient should prompt: lifestyle modification counselling (weight loss lowers CRP), smoking cessation, and treatment of metabolic syndrome components — not a cascade of investigations for occult malignancy. The investigation threshold should be guided by: age, symptoms, rate of change (a CRP that has risen from 8 to 22 over 6 months warrants more investigation than a stable CRP of 12 over 3 years), and the clinical context.

Giant Cell Arteritis & Polymyalgia Rheumatica

Suspecting GCA — a clinical diagnosis
Suspect GCA in anyone aged over 50 with a new localised headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication, visual symptoms (amaurosis fugax, diplopia, sudden visual loss — ischaemic optic neuropathy, irreversible blindness), constitutional symptoms, abnormal temporal artery examination, compatible PMR symptoms or large-vessel symptoms such as limb claudication. Do not wait for CRP or ESR results before starting treatment when clinical suspicion is significant — markers can be modest, discordant or rarely normal. The 1990 ACR criteria are historical classification criteria (sensitivity quoted for ≥3 of 5) and must not be used as a standalone diagnostic test; clinical assessment and urgent specialist investigation are required.
GCA management timeline — time is sight
DAY 1: No visual or other ischaemic symptoms → start prednisolone, commonly 40–60 mg daily per the local fast-track protocol, and refer same day to the GCA fast-track pathway — do not wait for biopsy or imaging. Visual loss, amaurosis fugax, diplopia or other ischaemic symptoms → immediate same-day emergency ophthalmology/acute medical assessment (not routine outpatient ophthalmology), glucocorticoid started immediately; specialist-directed high-dose treatment may include IV methylprednisolone. Arrange temporal artery ultrasound and/or biopsy urgently per the local pathway — imaging is most useful before or within about 72 hours of starting glucocorticoids (EULAR 2023); the specialist decides whether biopsy is still needed. Gastric protection, calcium/vitamin D and bone protection: individualised after fracture-risk and GI-risk assessment.
PMR (Polymyalgia Rheumatica)
Consider PMR in a person aged over 50 with new bilateral shoulder and/or hip girdle pain and prolonged morning stiffness, supported by raised inflammatory markers (no mandatory ESR/CRP threshold; occasionally normal). Exclude GCA, infection, malignancy, inflammatory arthritis (RF + anti-CCP), endocrine disease (e.g. hypothyroidism), myopathy and drug-related causes. A prompt response to an appropriate prednisolone dose supports but does not prove the diagnosis. Commonly used BSR/BHPR-based taper (individualised): 15 mg daily for 3 weeks → 12.5 mg daily for 3 weeks → 10 mg daily for 4–6 weeks → then reduce by 1 mg every 4–8 weeks, adjusted to symptoms and inflammatory markers. Treatment beyond 2 years → review diagnosis, adherence, relapse pattern and steroid toxicity, and consider specialist referral.
Overlap: GCA + PMR
15-20% of PMR patients have concurrent GCA. Any PMR patient with new headache, visual symptoms, or jaw claudication = treat as suspected GCA (prednisolone 40–60 mg per local fast-track protocol; immediate same-day assessment if visual symptoms). All newly diagnosed PMR patients: always ask about and screen for cranial GCA symptoms at each review.
The jaw claudication symptom in GCA is the most specific feature of the condition — it occurs in approximately 40-50% of GCA patients and results from ischaemia of the masseter and temporalis muscles during chewing (supplied by branches of the external carotid artery that are involved in GCA). When present, jaw claudication has a positive likelihood ratio of approximately 4.2 for GCA — making it the single most discriminating symptom. Patients describe it as jaw tiredness or aching that comes on after chewing for a few bites and resolves with rest (analogous to intermittent claudication in the legs). This is distinct from jaw pain at rest or dental pain. The immediate risk of GCA that must be prevented is anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (AION) — caused by vasculitis obliterating the posterior ciliary arteries supplying the optic nerve head. Vision loss from AION is sudden, painless, and permanent. Prednisolone must be started immediately when GCA is clinically suspected — starting steroids while urgent ultrasound and/or biopsy are arranged carries minimal harm, whereas withholding steroids while awaiting tests can result in permanent blindness. Imaging is most useful before or soon after treatment begins.

Local (Bolton / Greater Manchester)
Use the current GM sepsis pathway, GM GCA fast-track pathway, local PMR/GCA steroid protocol, GM metastatic spinal cord compression pathway (NICE NG234), local rheumatology and nephrology Advice & Guidance routes, and local laboratory reference ranges and access to plasma viscosity/PCT.
The early prednisolone prescription for GCA-suspected patients before ophthalmology or rheumatology review is medico-legally important and clinically correct — a GP who delays starting prednisolone in a patient with jaw claudication, temporal tenderness, and ESR of 85 mm/hr 'waiting for a rheumatology appointment' and who then develops ischaemic optic neuropathy with permanent blindness has caused avoidable harm. The correct action: start prednisolone (commonly 40–60 mg daily per the local fast-track protocol) at the consultation where GCA is first suspected, refer urgently, and document the clinical reasoning. The diagnosis of GCA is clinical — biopsy confirms it but a negative biopsy does not exclude it (approximately 20-30% of biopsy specimens are false-negative due to skip lesions). Temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign — dark inflammatory halo around the arterial lumen) has equivalent sensitivity to biopsy in experienced hands and is increasingly used as a non-invasive diagnostic test in specialist centres.

Treat

Treating the Underlying Cause — Specific Pathways

Bacterial infection + elevated CRP
Identify source + treat with appropriate antibiotic. If a patient with a treated infection is not clinically improving, reassess the diagnosis, source control, antimicrobial choice, complications and alternative causes. CRP may support trend assessment but does not determine antibiotic duration in isolation — kinetics vary with source, drainage, renal function, immunosuppression and the inflammatory condition; a falling CRP does not prove source control and persistent elevation does not necessarily mean antibiotic failure. In pneumonia, NICE NG250 suggests considering CRP or PCT after 3–4 days only when there is clinical concern about treatment failure. In hospital sepsis care, some antimicrobial stewardship protocols use a falling CRP or procalcitonin to support de-escalation — a specialist decision, not a primary-care rule.
GCA treatment protocol
Prednisolone 40–60 mg OD per local fast-track protocol (visual or ischaemic symptoms: specialist-directed high-dose treatment, which may include IV methylprednisolone) until symptoms and ESR/CRP have settled. Reduction schedule (BSR 2020, rheumatology-guided): reduce by 10 mg every 2 weeks to 20 mg, then by 2.5 mg every 2–4 weeks to 10 mg, then by 1 mg every 1–2 months provided there is no relapse. Relapse (return of compatible symptoms with clinical evidence — not raised markers alone): increase back to the last effective dose. Total treatment duration: typically 18 months to 2 years. Tocilizumab (IL-6 inhibitor, Actemra): recommended by NICE TA518 (2018) for relapsing or refractory GCA only (stopped after 1 year of uninterrupted treatment at most) — reduces relapse rate significantly, allows steroid taper; specialist-initiated and commissioned. Not a primary-care treatment for acute suspected GCA and not monotherapy for an acute relapse. Tocilizumab suppresses CRP and fever and may mask serious infection — infection assessment must be clinical; specialist-led monitoring including liver enzymes (MHRA: caution initiating with significant transaminase elevation).
PMR treatment protocol
Prednisolone 15 mg OD: a prompt response supports but does not prove the diagnosis. 15 mg for 3 weeks, 12.5 mg for 3 weeks, 10 mg for 4–6 weeks, then reduce by 1 mg every 4–8 weeks, individualised (BSR/BHPR-based). Monitor symptoms with ESR/CRP. If markers rise, assess symptoms and examination before changing treatment — do not escalate steroids solely because ESR/CRP is raised; consider infection, malignancy, RA and other causes. Interleukin-6 inhibitor (sarilumab) for refractory PMR — specialist.
Chronic inflammatory arthritis + elevated markers
RA: DMARDs (e.g. methotrexate + folic acid), hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide and biologic/JAK escalation are rheumatology-initiated, with GP prescribing only under local shared-care arrangements. Remission is assessed with a validated composite measure (DAS28, CDAI or SDAI) plus joint examination and patient-reported outcomes — CRP/ESR are supportive components, not standalone targets. SLE: hydroxychloroquine (specialist-initiated cornerstone). IBD: mesalazine (UC), azathioprine/biologics (Crohn) — gastroenterology-led.
The tocilizumab (IL-6 inhibitor) approval for GCA (NICE TA518, 2018) represents a major advance in GCA management — the GiACTA trial showed that tocilizumab plus a tapered prednisolone course achieved 56% sustained remission at 52 weeks compared to 14% with prednisolone alone, while also significantly reducing the cumulative steroid dose (and therefore steroid-related side effects). NICE TA518 (2018) recommends it only for adults with relapsing or refractory GCA who have not had tocilizumab before, stopping after 1 year of uninterrupted treatment at most. Tocilizumab is given as 162 mg SC weekly or every 2 weeks as an add-on to prednisolone. The mechanism: GCA involves IL-6-driven Th17 cell activation and granulomatous arteritis; tocilizumab blocks the IL-6 receptor, interrupting this pathway. GPs should know that tocilizumab suppresses CRP and fever and can mask serious infection, so infection must be assessed clinically. GPs managing patients with GCA should be aware that tocilizumab is available via rheumatology services for selected relapsing or refractory disease and that patients who relapse on prednisolone, or who develop significant steroid side effects (diabetes, osteoporosis, cataracts, infections), should be referred for tocilizumab consideration.

Monitoring Inflammation in Chronic Disease

CRP/ESR monitoring targets by condition
RA: remission defined by a validated composite measure (DAS28, CDAI or SDAI) with joint examination and patient-reported outcomes — CRP/ESR are components, not standalone targets. SLE: CRP is often not raised in flares, but may rise with infection, serositis, synovitis, major systemic inflammation or macrophage activation syndrome — do not use CRP to distinguish infection from lupus activity without clinical assessment. Complement C3/C4 + anti-dsDNA antibodies are the primary SLE activity markers. PMR/GCA: monitor symptoms with CRP/ESR during treatment; if markers rise, assess symptoms and examination before changing treatment — do not escalate glucocorticoids solely because ESR/CRP is raised, particularly if clinically well. IBD: CRP can be normal in active IBD; faecal calprotectin is used per the IBD diagnostic/monitoring pathway (also affected by infection, NSAIDs and colorectal neoplasia).
Interpreting ESR/CRP in treated patients
Steroids: significantly suppress CRP and ESR even in the presence of ongoing inflammation — do not use CRP/ESR alone to assess disease activity in patients on corticosteroids. Tocilizumab: profoundly suppresses CRP (blocks IL-6 → blocks CRP synthesis in liver) — CRP is unreliable as infection marker in patients on tocilizumab and fever may also be suppressed — infection assessment must be clinical, with specialist-led monitoring. NSAIDs: modestly reduce CRP (anti-inflammatory effect). DMARDs: reduce CRP proportional to disease control.
Using procalcitonin (PCT) alongside CRP
Procalcitonin is not a routine primary-care test for deciding whether to prescribe antibiotics. Where available in hospital or specialist pathways, it may support — but must not replace — clinical assessment and microbiological/source investigations (NICE NG253 allows it to be considered in selected moderate- or high-risk adults). Interpretation depends on infection source, timing, severity, renal impairment, surgery or trauma, malignancy, immunosuppression, neonatal status and the local assay. A low PCT does not exclude early or localised bacterial infection; a raised PCT does not prove it. Do not withhold antibiotics solely because PCT is low when there is a high clinical likelihood of serious infection.
Procalcitonin (PCT) is a precursor of calcitonin produced by multiple cell types in response to bacterial toxins and infection; it tends to rise less in viral infection, but it is neither perfectly sensitive nor specific — it can be raised after surgery or trauma, in renal impairment and in malignancy, and can be low early in, or with localised, bacterial infection. Trial evidence of reduced antibiotic use applies only to the specific populations and validated hospital protocols studied. NICE HTG386 (formerly DG18, 2015) did not recommend routine procalcitonin testing to guide antibiotics in suspected sepsis, so availability and local use vary; inappropriate antibiotic prescribing remains the main driver of antimicrobial resistance. Antibiotic decisions in primary care rest on clinical assessment, not a PCT or CRP threshold.

Lifestyle

Reducing Chronic Inflammation Through Lifestyle

Weight loss and CRP Obesity is associated with chronically raised CRP (adipose tissue secretes IL-6 and TNF-alpha), and weight loss lowers it. This metabolic anti-inflammatory effect of weight loss is independent of its effects on blood pressure, lipids, and glucose. GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide) have direct anti-inflammatory effects in addition to weight loss.
Smoking cessation and inflammation Smoking raises CRP through oxidative stress and endothelial inflammation. Smoking also accelerates RA progression and increases the risk of developing RA in genetically susceptible individuals (anti-CCP positive smokers have very high RA risk). NHS Stop Smoking Service.
Mediterranean diet and inflammation The Mediterranean diet is associated with lower inflammatory markers — proposed mechanisms include: high polyphenol intake (olive oil, vegetables, berries — inhibit NF-kB inflammatory pathway), high omega-3 intake (EPA/DHA from oily fish reduce eicosanoid synthesis), high fibre (reduces LPS translocation from gut bacteria), and low processed meat consumption (reduces AGE and TMAO). It is reasonable general cardiovascular health advice.
Exercise and inflammation Regular moderate aerobic exercise (150 min/week) reduces chronic low-grade inflammation. Mechanism: skeletal muscle produces anti-inflammatory myokines (IL-6 from muscle — paradoxically anti-inflammatory in exercise context vs pro-inflammatory in fat tissue, IL-15, irisin) that counter the systemic pro-inflammatory state. High-intensity exercise transiently raises CRP (delayed-onset muscle damage) — advise patients not to measure CRP within 48h of intense exercise.
Sleep and inflammation Short sleep duration is associated with raised inflammatory markers. Sleep disorders (OSA, insomnia) are associated with chronically elevated CRP. Sleep hygiene advice: consistent sleep/wake times, dark cool bedroom, no screens 1 hour before bed, avoid alcohol and caffeine after 2pm. Assess and treat OSA on its own merits. PHQ-9 screen (depression profoundly disrupts sleep + raises CRP bidirectionally).
Alcohol and inflammation Advise in line with UK lower-risk drinking guidance (no more than 14 units/week, spread over 3 or more days) and assess alcohol use when clinically relevant. Heavy alcohol use raises CRP through: hepatic inflammation, increased gut permeability (LPS translocation), and direct immune activation. GGT elevation + raised CRP in a heavy drinker without other explanation: alcohol-related hepatitis. AUDIT-C at every review for patients with unexplained elevated inflammatory markers.
Dental hygiene and CRP Periodontal disease is a significant source of chronic systemic inflammation — oral bacteria and their products enter the bloodstream through inflamed periodontal tissue, and is associated with raised CRP. GPs should ask about dental health and recommend 6-monthly dental check-ups, twice-daily brushing, and daily flossing for all patients with chronically elevated CRP without other explanation.
Managing steroid side effects in GCA/PMR Prolonged systemic corticosteroids need an individualised monitoring plan (SPS) based on dose and duration: baseline blood pressure, weight/BMI and glucose/HbA1c; fracture-risk assessment with consideration of bone mineral density and bone protection (NOGG 2024; BSR 2020 GCA guideline); review of cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, psychiatric, diabetes and glaucoma/cataract risk; vaccination and infection-risk assessment. DEXA, gastric protection and eye-review intervals are individualised, not universal. Pneumocystis prophylaxis is specialist- and risk-dependent (dose, duration, lymphopenia, overall regimen).
Periodontal disease as a source of systemic inflammation and cardiovascular risk is one of the most underemphasised connections in primary care — the evidence base is now substantial: severe periodontal disease is associated with higher cardiovascular risk and raised CRP. The mechanism: Porphyromonas gingivalis and other oral pathogens translocate into the bloodstream through inflamed periodontal tissue, triggering systemic IL-6 and CRP production, promoting endothelial dysfunction, and contributing to atherosclerotic plaque formation. In patients with chronically mildly elevated CRP (5-20 mg/L) and no other identified cause, asking about dental health and recommending periodontal assessment by a dentist is a clinically appropriate and evidence-based intervention. Dental health, sleep, exercise, smoking cessation and weight management are reasonable general advice, but they must not distract from investigating persistent unexplained inflammation.

Safety

Follow-Up & Monitoring

Unexplained elevated CRP/ESR — follow-up
If clinically well with mild unexplained elevation: repeat after a defined interval (e.g. 4-6 weeks) with clinical reassessment — do not repeatedly recheck CRP/ESR without reassessing the patient. If persistent: FBC + film, renal and liver tests, calcium, urinalysis, SPEP/FLC when myeloma plausible, targeted autoimmune tests and imaging according to symptoms. Document clinical reasoning and plan in medical record.
GCA on prednisolone
Symptoms with ESR + CRP until in remission. Individualised steroid monitoring plan (SPS): BP, weight, glucose/HbA1c, fracture-risk assessment ± DEXA, eye and GI risk review — intervals set by dose, duration and risk, not universal.
PMR tapering schedule monitoring
ESR/CRP monthly during taper. Flare if: compatible symptoms return (with clinical evidence) → increase prednisolone to last effective dose; do not escalate on raised CRP/ESR alone. Avoid tapering too quickly. Treatment beyond 2 years → review diagnosis, adherence, relapse pattern, steroid toxicity and refer to a specialist.
CRP in sepsis recovery
Not clinically improving on treatment → reassess the diagnosis, source control, antimicrobial choice, complications and alternative causes. CRP trend may support assessment but does not define response or antibiotic duration on its own.
Same-day / 999
NEWS2 ≥7, or NEWS2 5–6 with a single parameter scoring 3 or clinical concern, with suspected infection → 999 · New visual symptoms with suspected GCA → immediate same-day emergency ophthalmology/acute assessment + glucocorticoid now · Organ-threatening vasculitis (e.g. rapidly progressive renal impairment) → nephrology/rheumatology same day · New focal neurology, cord compression or cauda equina symptoms → emergency assessment and urgent MRI
Within 48 hours
Treated infection not clinically improving → reassess diagnosis, source control (consider imaging for undrained abscess), antimicrobial choice and complications · Suspected discitis/vertebral osteomyelitis without neurological deficit → urgent hospital/specialist assessment and MRI per the local spinal infection pathway
CRP kinetics during antibiotic treatment vary considerably with source, treatment, drainage, renal function, immunosuppression and the inflammatory condition, so there is no universal percentage-fall rule. Lack of clinical improvement is what requires reassessment — common causes of failure to respond are: (1) inadequate antibiotic spectrum (wrong antibiotic, resistance, or insufficient dose); (2) an undrained focus of infection (abscess — pleural, abdominal, peri-nephric, dental) that antibiotics cannot penetrate effectively; and (3) incorrect diagnosis (the elevated CRP is from a non-infectious cause such as vasculitis, malignancy, or PE rather than bacterial infection). A patient who is not improving clinically after 3–4 days of antibiotics (NICE NG250 suggests considering CRP or PCT only when there is clinical concern about treatment failure) should prompt: CT imaging to exclude an undrained collection, repeat blood cultures, review of antimicrobial sensitivities (if cultures available), and reconsideration of the diagnosis.
