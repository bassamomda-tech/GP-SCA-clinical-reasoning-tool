1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Intrahepatic Cholestasis of Pregnancy (ICP) & other serious causes first

Never dismiss itching in pregnancy as trivial. Obstetric cholestasis (ICP) carries a significant risk of stillbirth and requires urgent investigation.

Obstetric cholestasis (ICP) pattern Consider ICP in any pregnant patient with new unexplained pruritus, particularly without a primary rash and with palmar/plantar or nocturnal predominance (usually third trimester, occasionally second). Exclude other causes clinically → Same-day obstetric assessment + urgent bloods (LFTs, bile acids)
Jaundice + itching Scleral icterus, dark urine, pale stools + pruritus → Same-day obstetric/medical assessment. 999 if haemodynamic instability, confusion, severe vomiting/dehydration, severe abdominal pain, hypoglycaemia, severe pre-eclampsia/HELLP features or features of acute liver failure. Acute fatty liver of pregnancy? Severe ICP? Viral hepatitis?
Reduced fetal movements Reduced or changed fetal movements → immediate contact with the maternity assessment/triage service per local fetal-movement guidance. Use 999 only for maternal collapse, severe illness or inability to transfer safely. ICP is associated with sudden intrauterine death.
Pemphigoid gestationis Bullous / blistering rash, urticarial plaques, periumbilical involvement → Urgent dermatology + obstetrics (skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence — not anti-BP180 alone). Risk of premature delivery + neonatal pemphigoid.
PUPPP with systemic features Polymorphic eruption of pregnancy (PUPPP) with fever, facial involvement, mucosal involvement → exclude secondary infection, pemphigoid → Same-day assessment
Severe generalised urticaria Anaphylaxis risk. Lip/tongue swelling, throat tightness, breathlessness → 999. IM adrenaline first per Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis algorithm; antihistamines must not delay it.
Scabies in pregnancy Intense generalised pruritus + burrows + household contacts affected → Treat suspected scabies promptly with permethrin 5% cream per product instructions and local pathway (preferred in pregnancy, including the first trimester when indicated). Avoid lindane. Treat household/sexual contacts simultaneously. Specialist advice if severe, crusted, treatment-resistant or diagnostically uncertain.
Pre-eclampsia / HELLP BP ≥140/90 → assess through the pre-eclampsia pathway; persistent hypertension with headache, visual disturbance or epigastric pain → Same-day maternity assessment (severe hypertension is generally ≥160/110). 999 for seizure, severe neurological symptoms, collapse, severe breathlessness or other instability. Itching is not a typical feature of HELLP.
Varicella exposure or rash Non-immune patient with significant chickenpox exposure → Same-day advice on post-exposure prophylaxis. New varicella rash → Same-day specialist/obstetric advice; isolate from other pregnant patients. Breathlessness or chest symptoms → 999 (varicella pneumonia).
Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) affects 0.5–1.5% of UK pregnancies and is associated with a stillbirth risk of about 3% when peak bile acids are ≥100 µmol/L — roughly ten times the background risk (Ovadia et al., Lancet 2019). Stillbirth risk is unpredictable and can occur at any gestational age once ICP is established. RCOG Green-top Guideline 43 (Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, 2022; replaced the 2011 obstetric cholestasis guideline) mandates urgent investigation of any suspected ICP presentation. The diagnosis must be actively excluded in EVERY pregnant woman presenting with pruritus, regardless of gestational age — it is the most time-critical diagnosis in this clinical scenario.

Diagnose

Characterise the Itch — History taking

Distribution
Consider ICP in any new unexplained pruritus, particularly without a primary rash and with palmar/plantar or nocturnal predominance — arrange bile acids and LFTs promptly. Abdominal striae = PEP/PUPPP. Periumbilical = Pemphigoid gestationis. Localised vulval = candida. Web spaces, wrists = scabies.
Rash present?
ICP: NO primary skin rash (excoriations from scratching only). Rash present = dermatosis of pregnancy (PUPPP, PG, atopic eruption, prurigo) or infection (candida, scabies, chickenpox).
Gestational age
ICP: typically >28 weeks (third trimester) — earlier in twins/liver disease. PUPPP: usually late third trimester, primigravida, multiple pregnancy. PG: any trimester, often second.
Previous pregnancies
ICP recurs in 70–90% of subsequent pregnancies. PG recurs. Ask whether oestrogen-containing contraception has previously caused cholestasis (relevant to UKMEC-based contraception counselling after delivery).
Nocturnal worsening
Classic for ICP — diurnal variation with worst itch at night. Also scabies (hallmark). Less typical for dermatoses which worsen with sweating/daytime activity.
Medications
Drug-induced cholestasis mimics ICP: co-amoxiclav, erythromycin, nitrofurantoin in pregnancy. Stop potential causative drug and retest LFTs 2 weeks later.
Family / personal history
Previous ICP in patient or family (genetic component — ABCB4 gene mutations in familial cases). Personal history of gallstones, chronic liver disease, thyroid disease.
Contact history
Household contacts with itch → scabies. Chickenpox contact → confirm whether exposure is significant and check immunity (same-day advice if non-immune). New chickenpox rash → same-day specialist/obstetric advice.
The absence of primary skin rash with generalised pruritus (especially palms/soles, nocturnal) in the third trimester is the hallmark of ICP — this combination should trigger immediate investigation regardless of liver function test results at presentation. LFTs can be normal early in ICP (bile acids are the more sensitive initial marker). Varicella in pregnancy is a medical emergency — maternal pneumonia affects 5–10% of infected pregnant women and carries significant maternal mortality. Varicella exposure without immunity in pregnancy → post-exposure prophylaxis with oral aciclovir or valaciclovir from day 7 to day 14 after exposure (UKHSA PEP guidance, 2025); VZIG only if antivirals cannot be taken.

Diagnose

Classify the Cause — Differential diagnosis framework

Intrahepatic cholestasis (ICP)
Pruritus without rash, palmar/plantar, nocturnal. Raised peak (non-fasting) bile acids ≥19 µmol/L (RCOG GTG 43, 2022). May have raised LFTs (ALT/AST). Third trimester. Risk of stillbirth.
Polymorphic eruption of pregnancy (PEP/PUPPP)
Most common dermatosis (1 in 160). Itchy urticarial papules in striae distensae → spreads from abdomen. Spares periumbilical area. Usually primigravida, large twins. Third trimester. Benign — no fetal risk.
Pemphigoid gestationis (PG)
Rare autoimmune (anti-BP180). Urticarial plaques → tense blisters, periumbilical initially. Any trimester. Associated with preterm birth + SGA. Diagnosis requires dermatology assessment and skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence — not anti-BP180 alone.
Atopic eruption of pregnancy (AEP)
Encompasses atopic eczema, prurigo of pregnancy, pruritic folliculitis. Most common in women with atopy. Excoriated papules, dry skin. Occurs first/second trimester (earlier than ICP). Normal bile acids. Benign fetal outcome.
Vulvovaginal candidiasis
Localised vulval pruritus + thick white discharge. Common in pregnancy due to altered pH/glycogen/immune tolerance. 7-day topical azole course (e.g. clotrimazole); avoid oral fluconazole during pregnancy unless specifically advised by a specialist.
Physiological / dry skin
Mild abdominal itch due to skin stretching, dry skin. Diffuse, non-specific, no rash, responds to moisturiser. Diagnosis of exclusion after ICP excluded.
Scabies
Intense pruritus, web spaces, wrists, finger clefts, household transmission. Burrows visible. No fetal risk but maternal distress + secondary infection risk. Treat with permethrin 5% (preferred in pregnancy) and treat contacts simultaneously.
Varicella
Rash + itch + systemic illness. Chickenpox vesicles. Potentially life-threatening in pregnancy. → Same-day specialist/obstetric advice; oral aciclovir generally considered if within 24 hours of rash onset, particularly after 20 weeks; IV aciclovir for severe disease (see Step 7).
PEP/PUPPP (Polymorphic Eruption of Pregnancy) is the most common dermatosis of pregnancy and has an excellent prognosis — reassurance is the most important management once ICP is excluded. Its periumbilical sparing differentiates it from Pemphigoid gestationis, which starts periumbilically. Pemphigoid gestationis is associated with thyroid autoimmunity — check TFTs if thyroid disease is clinically suspected. The key clinical priority is distinguishing the benign dermatoses from ICP, which requires laboratory investigations in all cases of pruritus without obvious localised cause in pregnancy.

Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Systematic skin and obstetric assessment

Full skin inspection
Assess entire body surface systematically. Document rash characteristics: primary lesion type (macule/papule/vesicle/bulla/urticaria), secondary changes (excoriation/lichenification), distribution, periumbilical involvement or sparing.
Jaundice / icterus
Check sclerae for yellow discolouration. Jaundice in pregnancy is always serious — ICP, acute fatty liver, viral hepatitis, HELLP. → Same-day urgent review.
Obstetric examination
Fundal height (consistent with gestation?), fetal lie and presentation, fetal heart rate (auscultate with Doppler), SFH (concern for IUGR in ICP). Document fetal movements from maternal report.
Blood pressure
Mandatory. BP ≥140/90 → assess through the pre-eclampsia pathway; severe hypertension is generally ≥160/110. Hypertension + epigastric/RUQ pain → consider HELLP (itching is not a typical HELLP feature).
Urinalysis
Proteinuria (pre-eclampsia), haematuria (UTI causing pruritus/discomfort), bilirubin (suggests cholestasis). Test urine where there is hypertension or pre-eclampsia concern, proteinuria concern, urinary symptoms, systemic illness or another clinical indication — not routinely at every consultation.
Vulval examination
If vulval itch predominates — speculum for discharge (candida), erythema/satellite lesions. A full speculum examination is not essential if the history and examination are clearly consistent with candidiasis.
Lymph nodes
Generalised lymphadenopathy + rash → viral exanthem. Axillary adenopathy + eczema = staphylococcal superinfection. Generalised + weight loss → haematological malignancy (rare).
Documentation of fetal movements at every GP contact with a pregnant woman presenting with pruritus is critical — stillbirth in ICP can occur without warning, and a normal CTG does not eliminate that risk. Assessment of blood pressure is mandatory because pre-eclampsia and HELLP syndrome (Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) may present with upper abdominal pain alongside hypertension — itching is not a typical HELLP feature. Full skin inspection is essential — any primary lesion excludes ICP and triggers a dermatological differential.

Diagnose

Investigations — Targeted tests; bile acids + LFTs in suspected ICP

Serum bile acids
Mandatory in all pregnant women with pruritus without clear localised cause. ICP diagnosed with itch plus peak random bile acids ≥19 µmol/L. Mild ICP 19–39. Moderate 40–99. Severe ≥100 µmol/L (highest stillbirth risk) (RCOG GTG 43, 2022). A non-fasting sample is used — do not delay testing for fasting. Severity bands apply to singleton pregnancy without other risk factors; twins, diabetes, pre-eclampsia, fetal growth concerns, early-onset disease and other risk factors alter management. If itch persists despite initially normal results, repeat bile acids and LFTs — frequency determined by the obstetric team and gestation.
Liver function tests
ALT/AST (raised in ICP in 60%), GGT (raised in ~30%), bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (physiologically raised in pregnancy — not reliable). Normal LFTs do NOT exclude ICP — bile acids are more sensitive early.
Clotting (PT)
Do not routinely request coagulation studies in uncomplicated ICP. Consider PT and specialist advice where there is jaundice, steatorrhoea, suspected hepatic synthetic dysfunction, bleeding or abnormal LFTs.
FBC
FBC and platelets if pre-eclampsia/HELLP or systemic disease is suspected. Renal function and urine protein assessment where hypertensive disease is suspected.
Hepatitis screen
Hepatitis B and C serology — if LFT abnormalities are atypical or disproportionate. Already offered as routine antenatal booking test but may not have been done yet. Check records before repeating.
TFTs
Not routine — only where thyroid disease is clinically suspected (e.g. pemphigoid gestationis, which is associated with autoimmune thyroid disease, or weight loss, palpitations, excessive sweating — hyperthyroidism of pregnancy (gestational thyrotoxicosis vs Graves').
Autoimmune screen
Not routine. Hepatitis/autoimmune/liver investigations if LFT abnormalities are atypical or disproportionate. Suspected pemphigoid gestationis (tense blisters, periumbilical) → dermatology for skin biopsy + direct immunofluorescence; do not diagnose on anti-BP180 alone.
CTG (cardiotocograph)
Not a routine GP test. Do not use a normal CTG as reassurance that eliminates the risk of stillbirth from ICP. Antenatal surveillance and intrapartum fetal monitoring are determined by the obstetric team according to bile-acid concentration, gestation and co-morbidities.
Serum bile acids are the most sensitive diagnostic test for ICP and must be performed urgently — same-day where possible. LFTs alone are insufficient: a prospective study showed normal LFTs in 21% of confirmed ICP cases at initial presentation. Fasting bile acids are more sensitive but should not delay testing. The severity classification (mild, moderate, severe) directly determines obstetric management — in singleton pregnancies without other risk factors, bile acids ≥100 µmol/L are managed as severe ICP and planned birth at 35–36 weeks may be considered, individualised by the obstetric team. A normal CTG does not eliminate stillbirth risk in ICP; fetal surveillance follows the local obstetric plan.

Refer

Referral Criteria — Urgency stratified

999
Maternal collapse or haemodynamic instability. Seizure/eclampsia. Severe breathlessness or suspected varicella pneumonia. Anaphylaxis. Abnormal fetal assessment with maternal instability. Jaundice with confusion, hypoglycaemia, severe abdominal pain or acute liver failure features.
Same-day Obstetrics DAU
Suspected ICP with unexplained pruritus and no primary rash — urgent bile acids + LFTs. Persistent itch with initially normal bile acids/LFTs. Jaundice. Reduced fetal movements (immediate maternity triage contact). Suspected pre-eclampsia/HELLP. Varicella exposure in a non-immune patient. Active varicella rash in pregnancy. Generalised urticaria / pemphigoid gestationis with blistering.
Urgent Dermatology
Suspected pemphigoid gestationis (for skin biopsy, DIF, anti-BP180 antibodies). Atypical or unresponsive skin eruption. Bullous / vesicular lesion of unclear diagnosis.
Routine Dermatology
Atopic eruption of pregnancy not responding to standard emollient + mild topical steroid. Persistent prurigo/folliculitis requiring expert skin management.
Hepatology
Raised LFTs not explained by ICP. Hepatitis serology positive. Known chronic liver disease. Severe ICP with markedly deranged LFTs.
GP manages
Only after ICP and other serious conditions have been appropriately excluded: dry skin / physiological itch, PEP/PUPPP, atopic eruption, uncomplicated vulvovaginal candidiasis, uncomplicated scabies. Monitor and symptomatic treatment.
RCOG GTG 43 mandates that all cases of suspected ICP are referred to an obstetric unit for assessment and management — this includes bile acid testing, fetal surveillance planning, and delivery timing decisions. GP management of ICP beyond initial recognition and referral is not appropriate. Pemphigoid gestationis requires skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence for definitive diagnosis — this is a specialised investigation not available in primary care. The urgency of same-day DAU (Day Assessment Unit) referral for suspected ICP reflects the unpredictable nature of stillbirth risk.

Treat

Treatment — Condition-specific & pregnancy-safe management

ICP — Managed by Obstetrics but GP may initiate symptomatic relief while awaiting assessment:

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA)
Obstetrician-led — may be considered by the obstetric team for maternal symptom relief, but its benefit for itch is modest and it has not been shown reliably to prevent stillbirth or other adverse perinatal outcomes (RCOG GTG 43, 2022; PITCHES trial). Dosing is weight-based and specialist-led — not a GP prescription.
Vitamin K
Not routine in uncomplicated ICP or solely because bile acids are high. Specialist-led: considered where there is evidence of reduced fat absorption (e.g. steatorrhoea) and/or an abnormal prothrombin time (RCOG GTG 43).
Delivery timing
Singleton pregnancy without other risk factors (RCOG GTG 43, 2022): mild (19–39) — consider planned birth by 40 weeks or ongoing care according to national guidance; moderate (40–99) — consider planned birth at 38–39 weeks; severe (≥100, where stillbirth risk is clearly higher than background) — consider planned birth at 35–36 weeks. These are not automatic prescriptions: timing of birth is an obstetric decision based on peak bile acids, gestation, fetal and maternal factors, and shared decision-making.
Antihistamines (symptomatic)
Chlorphenamine 4mg TDS/QDS or loratadine 10mg OD — limited evidence for reducing itch in ICP. Useful for sedation at night. Sedating antihistamines may be used when clinically appropriate — consider maternal sedation, falls and potential neonatal sedation near delivery; check current prescribing guidance and the obstetric/neonatal context.

Dermatoses of Pregnancy (managed in GP):

PEP/PUPPP
Emollients + Topical Steroids
Emollient (Aveeno, Diprobase) + topical steroid of appropriate potency for site and severity (e.g. hydrocortisone 1% mild, clobetasone butyrate moderate), smallest effective quantity; review if extensive or prolonged use is needed. Oral chlorphenamine for sleep. Resolves within weeks of delivery.
Atopic eruption (AEP)
Emollients + low-moderate steroids
Emollients liberally ×4/day. Topical steroid for inflamed areas — appropriate potency for site and severity, smallest effective quantity, review if extensive or prolonged use; severe eczema may need specialist-directed potent topical treatment. Wet wraps for severe eczema. Antihistamine for sleep.
Vulval Candidiasis
Clotrimazole topical Safe
7-day topical azole course (e.g. clotrimazole pessary, dose per BNF/local formulary) ± clotrimazole 1% cream; insert pessaries manually without an applicator. Avoid oral fluconazole during pregnancy unless specifically advised by a specialist. Confirm alternative diagnoses if symptoms recur or fail to respond.
Scabies
Permethrin 5% cream
Apply to the whole body according to the product instructions, leave 8–12 hours, wash off. Repeat at 1 week. Treat all household/sexual contacts simultaneously; environmental advice per local guidance. Preferred treatment in pregnancy, including the first trimester when clinically indicated. Avoid lindane. Seek specialist advice if severe, crusted, treatment-resistant or diagnostically uncertain.

Varicella in pregnancy:

Active chickenpox (new rash)
Same-day specialist/obstetric advice. Oral aciclovir generally considered if presenting within 24 hours of rash onset, particularly after 20 weeks (RCOG GTG 13). IV aciclovir for severe maternal disease, pneumonia, encephalitis, haemorrhage or significant systemic illness. Isolate from other pregnant patients in clinical settings; assess respiratory symptoms promptly; notify/seek UKHSA advice where required; urgent fetal medicine input if infection occurs in the first 20 weeks.
Exposure only (no rash)
Confirm whether the exposure is significant. Check varicella IgG if immunity is uncertain. If not immune: oral aciclovir 800 mg four times a day (or valaciclovir 1 g three times a day) from day 7 to day 14 after exposure, at any stage of pregnancy (UKHSA PEP guidance, 2025); VZIG only if antivirals cannot be taken. Discuss same day with obstetrics.
Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is widely used in ICP, but RCOG GTG 43 (2022) advises against routinely offering it to reduce adverse perinatal outcomes. It does not eliminate stillbirth risk — hence delivery timing decisions remain paramount. A large multicentre RCT (PITCHES, Lancet 2019) found UDCA did not reduce adverse perinatal outcomes and gave only a small reduction in maternal itch, prompting ongoing review of the evidence. Oral fluconazole in pregnancy: a Danish study suggested increased miscarriage and cardiac defect risk with doses ≥150mg — topical clotrimazole remains the recommended treatment for vulvovaginal candidiasis in all trimesters of pregnancy.

Lifestyle

Supportive Measures & Patient Guidance

Emollient use — all causes Apply emollient (Doublebase gel, Aveeno, Diprobase) liberally at least 4× daily. Apply immediately after bathing. Cool emollient from fridge provides additional symptomatic relief for ICP and dermatoses.
Cool baths and clothing Lukewarm (not hot) baths reduce pruritus. Pat dry gently. Loose-fitting, breathable cotton clothing. Avoid wool and synthetic fabrics. Cooling can reduce itch intensity by vasoconstriction.
Avoid itch triggers Heat, sweating, spicy food, alcohol (already contraindicated), and stress worsen pruritus. Identify and minimise individual triggers. Temperature control in bedroom — important for ICP nocturnal itch.
Nail care Keep nails short and clean — reduces skin damage from scratching. Scratch mitts at night (as used for eczema). Scratching worsens inflammation and introduces secondary bacterial infection risk.
Fetal movement awareness Advise the patient to remain aware of their baby's usual movement pattern and to contact maternity triage immediately if movements are reduced or changed. Do not wait for the next antenatal appointment.
Psychological support ICP is distressing — uncertainty about fetal wellbeing, sleep deprivation from nocturnal itch, and fear cause significant anxiety. Validate distress. Provide written ICP information (ICP Support charity: icpsupport.org). GP can monitor mental wellbeing between obstetric appointments.
Dietary advice Low-fat diet may reduce bile acid secretion in ICP — some evidence, low quality. No specific dietary intervention proven effective. Avoid prolonged fasting (worsens bile acid concentration) — regular small meals preferred.
Antihistamine for sleep Chlorphenamine 4mg at night provides sedation that helps with nocturnal itch (not specifically anti-pruritic for ICP). Near delivery, weigh maternal sedation, falls and potential neonatal sedation; check current prescribing guidance. Loratadine (non-sedating) alternative for daytime — limited ICP efficacy.
Awareness of the baby's usual movement pattern, with immediate contact with maternity triage if movements are reduced or changed, is one of the most important safety messages for women with ICP; formal twice-daily counting is not a universal UK recommendation and may increase anxiety. ICP Support (icpsupport.org) is a patient-led charity that provides evidence-based information — referral to this resource empowers patients with self-monitoring and reduces unnecessary GP visits while ensuring appropriate contact. Sleep disruption from nocturnal itch in ICP significantly affects quality of life and maternal mental health — addressing this directly improves adherence to monitoring and reduces anxiety-related complications.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

ICP — obstetric-led
Obstetric team lead for ICP. GP role: monitor wellbeing, mental health, symptom changes. Repeat bile acids and LFTs if itch persists despite initially normal results; in confirmed ICP, repeat frequency is determined by the obstetric team and gestation.
Dermatoses — GP-led
Review at 2–4 weeks. Assess response to topical treatment. If worsening or spreading: dermatology referral. Reassure — most dermatoses of pregnancy resolve within weeks postpartum.
Post-delivery ICP
Arrange postpartum review at approximately 4–6 weeks (at least 4 weeks after birth) to confirm that pruritus has resolved and bile acids/LFTs have normalised (RCOG GTG 43). If abnormalities or symptoms persist, investigate alternative or underlying liver disease and consider hepatology referral. Future pregnancies: high recurrence risk — baseline LFTs and bile acids at booking. Discuss contraception using UKMEC.
Future pregnancy counselling
ICP recurrence 70–90% in subsequent pregnancies. Baseline LFTs and bile acids at booking in the next pregnancy, with repeat testing guided by symptoms and clinical context. After ICP has resolved, discuss contraception using UKMEC guidance: previous ICP alone does not automatically contraindicate combined hormonal contraception. Avoid or reconsider oestrogen-containing contraception if it previously caused cholestasis or if liver abnormalities persist (use a progestogen-only or non-hormonal method).
999 safety-net
Maternal collapse or haemodynamic instability. Seizure. Jaundice with confusion, severe abdominal pain or severe vomiting (HELLP/acute fatty liver). Anaphylaxis. Varicella + breathlessness (pneumonia). For reduced or absent fetal movements, contact maternity triage immediately — 999 only if maternal collapse, severe illness or unable to transfer safely.
Same-day safety-net
Reduced or changed fetal movements (contact maternity triage immediately). Worsening or persistent itch despite treatment or normal initial bloods. New jaundice or dark urine. Headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain or swelling (pre-eclampsia). Chickenpox exposure if non-immune, or new chickenpox rash. Rash rapidly spreading. Fever + worsening rash (secondary infection, varicella). Any new or worsening ICP symptoms.
Documentation
Document ALL ICP cases in summary and flag for future pregnancies / contraception consultations. Alert midwife/obstetric team to ICP history at future booking visits.
Post-delivery follow-up for ICP is essential — RCOG GTG 43 recommends confirming resolution of itch and normalisation of bile acids/LFTs at least 4 weeks after birth; symptoms often improve within days, but biochemical normalisation may take longer. Persistent elevation suggests underlying liver disease (primary biliary cholangitis, hepatitis C, Gilbert's syndrome) requiring specialist investigation. The high recurrence rate (70–90%) means future pregnancy planning must include this history — RCOG GTG 43 recommends baseline LFTs and bile acids at booking in a subsequent pregnancy, with repeat testing guided by symptoms. Documentation in patient records is a GP responsibility — a future GP seeing the patient for contraception or pre-conception advice needs to know about ICP history to counsel appropriately about contraception (UKMEC — previous ICP alone does not contraindicate combined hormonal contraception) and future monitoring.
