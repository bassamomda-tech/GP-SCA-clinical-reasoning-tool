1
Safety

Red Flags — Skin Failure, Infection and Drug Reactions First

Look at the whole skin, the mouth, eyes and genitals, and take observations before naming the rash. Painful skin, mucosal erosions, a recent new drug or a systemically unwell patient make it an emergency. Non-blistering maculopapular rashes: see the maculopapular rash algorithm; itch without rash: widespread itch.

Mucosal erosions + painful dusky skin, blisters or skin peeling Usually 4–28 days after a new drug (allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, sulfonamides including co-trimoxazole, NSAIDs, nevirapine) → SJS/TEN → 999 or same-day emergency admission (dermatology or burns unit); stop the culprit drug now
Non-blanching rash in an unwell patient Fever, purpura, rapid deterioration → suspected meningococcal disease → 999; give IM or IV benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone if it will not delay transfer (NICE NG240)
Pain out of proportion, dusky or necrotic skin, crepitus, rapid spread Necrotising soft tissue infection → 999
Erythroderma Red, inflamed skin over most of the body surface (90% or more), shivering, fluid loss → Same-day dermatology or admission; 999 if haemodynamically unstable
Eczema herpeticum Painful, rapidly spreading clusters of punched-out erosions or vesicles on eczema, often febrile → Same-day: start oral aciclovir today and arrange same-day dermatology advice; eye involvement → same-day ophthalmology
Fever, facial swelling, widespread rash, lymphadenopathy 2–8 weeks after a new drug DRESS (anticonvulsants, allopurinol, sulfonamides, minocycline) → Same-day admission or dermatology; FBC (eosinophilia), LFT, U&E; stop the drug
Flaccid blisters, raw erosions and painful mouth ulcers Possible pemphigus vulgaris → Same-day dermatology discussion; admit if extensive or unable to eat and drink
Chickenpox or zoster in a pregnant or immunocompromised adult, or disseminated zoster → Same-day specialist advice (antivirals ± immunoglobulin); pneumonitis (breathlessness, cough) → 999
Blistering with sepsis or skin loss Staphylococcal scalded skin (adults with renal failure or immunosuppression), extensive bullous impetigo with systemic upset → Same-day; 999 if NG253 high-risk features
Urticaria with airway, breathing or circulation compromise Anaphylaxis → 999 and IM adrenaline 500 micrograms (adult) — see anaphylaxis

SJS/TEN carries a substantial mortality that rises with the area of skin detachment and with delay in stopping the culprit drug; early withdrawal of the drug and transfer to a unit used to managing skin failure are the two interventions that matter (BAD 2016). Skin pain out of proportion to the appearance, mucosal involvement at two or more sites and a positive Nikolsky sign are the clues.

Erythroderma, from any cause, is "acute skin failure" with risks of hypothermia, fluid and protein loss, high-output cardiac failure and sepsis. Eczema herpeticum can be fatal and causes herpetic keratitis; antiviral treatment should not wait for swab results.

2
Diagnose

History — Timing, Drugs and Systemic Features

Drug history (last 8 weeks)
Every new or restarted drug with start date, including over-the-counter, herbal and recent antibiotics — SJS/TEN 4–28 days, DRESS 2–8 weeks, fixed drug eruption within hours to days of re-exposure, gliptins (DPP-4 inhibitors) and bullous pemphigoid, vancomycin (linear IgA disease)
Onset & evolution
Hours (urticaria, contact dermatitis, burns, insect bites) · days (drug eruption, viral exanthem, impetigo) · weeks to months with itch first (bullous pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis)
Symptoms
Itch (urticaria, pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis, scabies) vs pain or burning (SJS/TEN, pemphigus, herpes, urticarial vasculitis, necrotising infection)
Mucosa
Mouth ulcers, sore eyes, genital erosions, painful swallowing — SJS/TEN, pemphigus, erythema multiforme major, mucous membrane pemphigoid
Systemic
Fever, malaise, sore throat (guttate psoriasis after streptococcal throat), arthralgia, weight loss, diarrhoea or bloating (coeliac disease with dermatitis herpetiformis)
Contacts & exposures
Chickenpox or shingles contacts, recent herpes simplex (erythema multiforme), new sexual partner (secondary syphilis, mpox), travel, insect bites, plants or chemicals (contact or phytophotodermatitis), sun exposure (porphyria, drug photosensitivity)
Background
Atopic eczema (eczema herpeticum), psoriasis (erythrodermic or pustular flare after steroid withdrawal), diabetes, immunosuppression, pregnancy (pemphigoid gestationis, varicella), HIV

The drug history decides more emergencies here than any test. Record every drug started in the previous 8 weeks with dates, because the latency between starting a drug and the reaction points to the culprit and the type of reaction.

3
Diagnose

Examination — Describe the Lesion, Then Look Everywhere

Observations
Temperature, pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, NEWS2; hydration
Blister type
Tense blisters on red or urticated skin → bullous pemphigoid. Flaccid blisters and erosions → pemphigus, bullous impetigo, SJS/TEN. Grouped vesicles → herpes simplex, zoster, dermatitis herpetiformis. Crops at different stages → chickenpox
Distribution
Dermatomal (zoster) · flexures, trunk and limbs (pemphigoid) · elbows, knees, buttocks, scalp (dermatitis herpetiformis) · palms and soles (secondary syphilis, erythema multiforme, pompholyx, hand foot and mouth) · sun-exposed (photosensitivity, porphyria) · on eczema (eczema herpeticum)
Nikolsky sign
Skin shears off with gentle lateral pressure → SJS/TEN, pemphigus, scalded skin syndrome — treat as an emergency
Mucosa & eyes
Mouth, conjunctivae, genitals — erosions or crusting
Target lesions
Typical three-ring targets on hands and limbs → erythema multiforme; atypical flat dusky targets on the trunk → SJS/TEN
Wheals
Transient (under 24 h) itchy wheals → urticaria; lesions lasting over 24 h, burning, leaving bruising → urticarial vasculitis
Surface area
Estimate % body surface involved (the patient's palm with fingers is about 1%) — record it
Other
Lymph nodes, liver and spleen (DRESS, syphilis), throat, nails and scalp (psoriasis), burrows (scabies)

The level of the split in the skin explains the clinical appearance: subepidermal blisters (pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis) are tense and stay intact; intraepidermal blisters (pemphigus, impetigo) are fragile and quickly become erosions. Mucosal involvement and a positive Nikolsky sign push the differential towards the dangerous conditions.

4
Diagnose

Investigations — Mostly to Support the Specialist

Unwell or drug reaction suspected
FBC with eosinophils, U&E, LFT, CRP; usually in hospital — do not delay admission for bloods
Infection
Viral PCR swab of a blister base (HSV, VZV); bacterial swab if crusted or pustular; do not delay aciclovir for results
Immunobullous
Skin biopsy for histology and direct immunofluorescence is done by dermatology; BP180/BP230 and desmoglein antibodies supportive only
Dermatitis herpetiformis
tTG IgA with total IgA while eating gluten (NICE NG20)
Sexual health
Syphilis serology and HIV test if rash on palms and soles, atypical pityriasis rosea, or sexual risk; mpox PCR via sexual health or UKHSA pathway
Urticarial vasculitis
ESR/CRP, urine dipstick, U&E, complement (C3/C4) and dermatology biopsy
Guttate psoriasis
Throat swab or ASO titre if recent sore throat (supportive only)
Photosensitive blisters
LFT, ferritin, hepatitis C serology and urine or plasma porphyrins (porphyria cutanea tarda)

Most blistering diagnoses are confirmed by a dermatology biopsy, so the GP's tests are aimed at safety (organ involvement in DRESS, eosinophilia, renal function), at treatable infections (HSV and VZV PCR) and at the associations that change management (coeliac disease, syphilis, HIV).

5
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Severe drug reactions
SJS/TEN · DRESS · AGEP
New drug, fever, painful skin, mucosal erosions; DRESS with facial oedema and eosinophilia; AGEP with sheets of tiny pustules
Older adult, itchy, tense blisters
Bullous pemphigoid
Often over 70; urticated plaques then tense blisters; mucosa spared or mild; check for gliptins — see bullous pemphigoid
Erosions + mouth ulcers
Pemphigus vulgaris
Middle age, painful oral erosions often first, flaccid blisters, Nikolsky positive
Very itchy grouped vesicles
Dermatitis herpetiformis
Elbows, knees, buttocks, scalp; excoriated; gluten-sensitive — coeliac serology
Viral
Chickenpox · disseminated zoster · HSV · mpox
Crops of vesicles, fever, contacts; dermatomal plus scattered lesions = disseminated; umbilicated pustules with genital or anal lesions → mpox
Wheals
Urticaria vs urticarial vasculitis
Transient itchy wheals vs fixed, burning lesions over 24 h with bruising — see urticaria
Papulosquamous
Guttate psoriasis · pityriasis rosea
Drop-like scaly papules after a sore throat; herald patch then "Christmas tree" oval patches on the trunk
Must-not-miss mimic
Secondary syphilis
Non-itchy rash including palms and soles, mucous patches, lymphadenopathy, malaise — serology
Local and other
Bullous impetigo · contact dermatitis · bites
Also erythema multiforme (targets, HSV), fixed drug eruption, pompholyx, porphyria cutanea tarda, pemphigoid gestationis in pregnancy

In older patients the commonest blistering disease is bullous pemphigoid, which often starts with weeks of itch and urticated plaques before blisters appear and is often labelled eczema or scabies. Pemphigus is rarer but more dangerous and usually involves the mouth. Secondary syphilis is "the great imitator" and should be considered in any unexplained widespread rash, particularly with palm and sole involvement.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria

999
Suspected SJS/TEN with skin detachment or systemic upset, meningococcal disease, necrotising infection, anaphylaxis, varicella pneumonitis, haemodynamically unstable erythroderma
Same-day
Suspected SJS/TEN without instability (emergency dermatology admission), erythroderma, eczema herpeticum (with aciclovir started), DRESS, suspected pemphigus, extensive or rapidly progressive bullous pemphigoid or unable to cope at home, chickenpox or zoster in pregnancy or immunosuppression, disseminated zoster, erythema multiforme with mucosal involvement, pemphigoid gestationis
Urgent dermatology
Localised bullous pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis with positive serology or high suspicion, suspected urticarial vasculitis, unexplained blistering
Sexual health
Suspected secondary syphilis or mpox — within a week, sooner if unwell; partner notification through the clinic (BASHH)
Routine dermatology
Guttate psoriasis that is extensive or not responding; pityriasis rosea lasting over 3 months or atypical; chronic urticaria not controlled on up to four times the licensed antihistamine dose

Severe cutaneous adverse reactions should also be reported to the MHRA through the Yellow Card scheme, and the culprit drug recorded prominently as an allergy in the record. Dermatology departments generally offer same-day telephone or photo advice for suspected immunobullous disease, which is often the quickest route.

7
Treat

Management at This Consultation

Any suspected drug reaction
Stop the culprit drug Today
Stop all non-essential new drugs started in the last 8 weeks; record as a suspected allergy; Yellow Card. Do not rechallenge
Eczema herpeticum
Oral aciclovir
Usual adult dose: 400 mg five times a day for 5–10 days, or as advised by dermatology (SPC/local guidance); same-day dermatology advice; stop topical steroid on affected areas until advised
Chickenpox in an adult
Aciclovir if within 24 h of rash
Usual adult dose: 800 mg five times a day for 7 days (SPC); paracetamol, not NSAIDs; avoid contact with pregnant women, neonates and the immunosuppressed until all lesions crust
Bullous pemphigoid (localised, awaiting dermatology)
Very potent topical steroid
Clobetasol propionate 0.05% to lesions on dermatology advice, plus emollient as soap substitute; stop a gliptin after discussion; leave blister roofs intact, drain large tense blisters with a sterile needle
Urticaria
Non-sedating antihistamine
For example cetirizine usual adult dose 10 mg once daily (SPC); in persistent urticaria the dose may be increased up to four times (off-label, BAD/EAACI); avoid NSAIDs and codeine if they trigger it
Bullous or widespread impetigo
Oral flucloxacillin (NICE NG153)
Usual adult dose: 500 mg four times a day for 5 days; penicillin allergy: clarithromycin 250–500 mg twice a day for 5 days
Skin careEmollients liberally and as a soap substitute for eczematous and blistering conditions; non-adherent dressings for erosions; analgesia
Eczema / dermatitisPotent topical steroid for a short course on inflamed (non-infected) eczema or contact dermatitis, once herpes and impetigo are excluded; avoid the face and flexures
Guttate psoriasisEmollient ± vitamin D analogue or a short course of a moderate-to-potent steroid; treat streptococcal throat if present per NICE NG84
Pityriasis roseaReassure — self-limiting over 6–12 weeks; emollient and a mild to moderate steroid for itch; check syphilis serology if atypical or at risk
Systemic steroidsDo not start oral steroids for an undiagnosed blistering rash, or for suspected SJS/TEN or pemphigus, without specialist advice — they can mask infection and the diagnosis

Next steps: see Bullous pemphigoid (and pemphigus) · Urticaria · Atopic eczema · Psoriasis · Pityriasis rosea · Shingles · Impetigo · Coeliac disease · Anaphylaxis

Stopping the culprit drug early is the single intervention that most improves outcome in severe drug reactions. Topical clobetasol is effective and safer than oral prednisolone for localised bullous pemphigoid in frail older people (BAD 2012), but the diagnosis should be confirmed by dermatology. Oral antivirals for chickenpox in adults are most useful within 24 hours of the rash; eczema herpeticum is treated on clinical suspicion.

8
Lifestyle

Self-Care and Preventing Spread

Blister care Keep blisters intact where possible, cover erosions with non-stick dressings, watch for infection.
Emollients Use liberally and instead of soap; warn about fire risk with paraffin-containing products on dressings and clothing.
Infection control Chickenpox: stay off work or school until all lesions have crusted; avoid pregnant women, newborns and the immunosuppressed. Impetigo: until lesions crust or 48 h after starting antibiotics.
Drug allergy Know the name of the culprit drug; tell pharmacists; consider a medical alert card or bracelet after SJS/TEN or DRESS.
Triggers Urticaria: avoid NSAIDs, alcohol and heat if they worsen it. Dermatitis herpetiformis: keep eating gluten until tested, then a strict gluten-free diet.
Sexual health Syphilis or mpox suspected: avoid sex until assessed and treated; partner notification through the clinic.

UKHSA advises exclusion from school or work for chickenpox until all vesicles have crusted, and for impetigo until lesions are crusted or 48 hours after starting antibiotics. People recovering from SJS/TEN or DRESS must never be re-exposed to the culprit or closely related drugs.

9
Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Safety-net 999
Skin peeling or blistering spreading quickly, sore red eyes with mouth or genital sores, a rash that does not fade under a glass with feeling very unwell, difficulty breathing or swelling of the lips or tongue
Safety-net same-day
New fever, painful skin, blisters spreading, eye pain or redness, unable to eat or drink, facial swelling, rash around the eye with shingles, breathlessness or cough with chickenpox
48–72 hours
Review any undiagnosed widespread rash, a stopped drug reaction, or eczema herpeticum after starting aciclovir (if not already under dermatology)
1–2 weeks
Bullous pemphigoid on topical steroid: response, skin thinning, and whether dermatology has seen the patient; urticaria response to antihistamine
Results
Act on coeliac serology, syphilis and HIV tests, eosinophil count and LFT; record confirmed drug allergies
Remote consultations
A photo helps triage but does not replace examination of the mouth, eyes and whole skin — see the patient face to face the same day if blistering is widespread

SJS/TEN and DRESS can evolve over days, so a patient who seems mildly unwell with an early drug rash needs a short review interval and explicit warning signs. Photographs alone miss mucosal involvement and the Nikolsky sign.
