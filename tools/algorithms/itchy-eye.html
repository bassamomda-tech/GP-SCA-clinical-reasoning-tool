Safety

Red Flags — Sight-Threatening Conditions Not to Miss

Itchy eye is almost always benign — but itching accompanied by pain, photophobia, visual change, or a unilateral red eye demands urgent ophthalmology exclusion of sight-threatening pathology.

Itchy + painful red eye + photophobia + reduced visual acuity Anterior uveitis (iritis) — ciliary flush, photophobia (especially consensual), blurred vision, small or irregular pupil; do not diagnose from symptoms alone. Seen in HLA-B27 conditions (ankylosing spondylitis, Crohn's, reactive arthritis, psoriasis), sarcoidosis, Behçet's. Itching is not the dominant feature but can be present. Same-day ophthalmology; do not prescribe steroid eye drops from primary care. Misdiagnosed as conjunctivitis leads to posterior synechiae and sight loss.
Itchy + painful eye + contact lens wearer + corneal haze or opacity Acanthamoeba keratitis or Pseudomonas corneal ulcer — contact lens-related infection. Sight-threatening — risk of corneal perforation. Contact lens must be removed immediately. Same-day emergency ophthalmology — a painful red eye in a contact-lens wearer is an emergency until keratitis is excluded; do not treat as allergy or conjunctivitis, and do not patch the eye. Do NOT prescribe topical steroids to a contact lens wearer with a red eye — steroids in infectious keratitis cause catastrophic corneal melting.
Itchy + unilateral sudden onset + dendritic ulcer appearance or history of HSV/HZV Herpes simplex keratitis (dendritic corneal ulcer — stained with fluorescein, branching pattern) — topical steroids are absolutely contraindicated (cause amoeboid ulceration and perforation). Same-day ophthalmology. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (shingles involving V1 of CN V): vesicular rash on forehead + periorbital + Hutchinson's sign (tip or side of nose — increases the likelihood of eye involvement, but its absence does not exclude it). Urgent ophthalmology for eye pain, photophobia, visual symptoms, red eye or corneal involvement.
Severe itching + giant papillae under upper lid + marked photophobia in a child or young adult Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) — sight-threatening seasonal allergic eye disease of childhood, commoner in boys and atopic individuals in warm climates. Giant cobblestone papillae on upper tarsal conjunctiva on eversion, limbal Horner–Trantas dots, thick mucus. Risk of corneal shield ulcers and scarring. Urgent ophthalmology — same-day if photophobia, reduced vision or corneal staining, otherwise same-week. Needs specialist topical anti-inflammatory/immunomodulatory treatment, not just antihistamines.
Itchy eye + acute onset severe pain + haloes around lights + cloudy cornea + nausea Acute angle-closure glaucoma — intraocular pressure crisis. Not primarily "itchy" but can have ocular irritation component. Nausea + vomiting + headache + visual disturbance. Do not palpate the globe to diagnose a "rock-hard eye" — unreliable; diagnosis needs urgent specialist IOP measurement. → 999 / emergency ophthalmology — do not delay transfer; IV acetazolamide is given in secondary care, not from primary care. Risk of rapid permanent vision loss. Most commonly triggered by mydriatics (dilating drops).
Itchy eye + purulent discharge in a neonate (<28 days) Ophthalmia neonatorum — Neisseria gonorrhoeae (profuse purulent, onset day 2–5) or Chlamydia trachomatis (moderate discharge, onset day 5–14). Both sight-threatening (corneal ulceration, perforation, blindness). Any purulent eye discharge in a neonate → urgent paediatric/ophthalmology assessment with urgent swabs capable of detecting gonococcus and chlamydia; systemic treatment while awaiting results when gonococcal infection is possible, according to specialist/local paediatric microbiology protocol (NICE neonatal infection guidance). Ophthalmia neonatorum has not been a notifiable disease in England since the Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010; refer to paediatric and sexual-health services for maternal and partner testing, treatment and partner management.
Severe orbital or facial infection, or anaphylaxis Orbital/facial infection with systemic illness, neurological symptoms or rapidly progressive swelling, or anaphylaxis with airway or systemic compromise → 999.
The topical steroid contraindication in contact lens-related keratitis and herpes simplex keratitis is one of the most important prescribing safety principles in primary care ophthalmology. Topical corticosteroids suppress the immune response that is normally keeping infectious keratitis in check — in HSV keratitis (dendritic ulcer), steroids cause the characteristic branching ulcer to spread into a large geographic amoeboid ulcer with rapid corneal thinning and risk of perforation. In bacterial and Acanthamoeba keratitis (both common in contact lens wearers), steroids allow unchecked microbial proliferation and corneal melting. The clinical rule is: never prescribe topical steroids for a red eye without a slit-lamp examination confirming there is no corneal involvement. Topical ocular steroids should be ophthalmology-led; in general practice the safest rule is: never prescribe topical steroids for any red eye in a contact lens wearer, and never prescribe topical steroids for any unilateral red eye with photophobia or corneal cloudiness without ophthalmology review. Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) is a sight-threatening condition that is distinct from common allergic conjunctivitis and is frequently under-diagnosed and under-treated in primary care — it affects primarily boys under 14, is associated with atopy (asthma, eczema, hayfever), is more prevalent in South Asian and Middle Eastern children in the UK, and can cause permanent corneal scarring (shield ulcers) from the mechanical trauma of the giant papillae rubbing on the cornea during blinking. The examination finding of giant cobblestone papillae under the upper eyelid (requiring upper lid eversion — a skill GPs should have) is diagnostic. These children need ophthalmology referral and specialist topical immunomodulatory treatment, not just OTC antihistamine eye drops. Hutchinson's sign in herpes zoster ophthalmicus (vesicular rash on the tip of the nose) is a clinical sign that strongly predicts eye involvement — the nasociliary branch of V1 supplies both the tip of the nose and the eye. A rash on the tip of the nose increases the likelihood of ocular complications (keratitis, uveitis, scleritis), but absence of Hutchinson's sign does not exclude ocular involvement. Urgent ophthalmology is required for eye pain, photophobia, visual symptoms, red eye or corneal involvement, and local HZO pathways should be followed when Hutchinson's sign is present.

Classification — The Main Causes of Itchy Eyes

Seasonal allergic conjunctivitis (SAC)
Most common cause of itchy eyes. Bilateral, seasonal (spring/summer — grass pollen; autumn — mould; winter — house dust mite dominant indoors). Itching = cardinal symptom. Watery discharge. Conjunctival redness. Chemosis (conjunctival oedema — "jelly eye"). Lid swelling. Associated rhinitis (allergic rhinoconjunctivitis). History of atopy (asthma, eczema, food allergy). Symptoms reproduced by specific allergen exposure. Responds to antihistamines and mast cell stabilisers.
Perennial allergic conjunctivitis (PAC)
Year-round symptoms — house dust mite (most common perennial allergen), pet dander (cat, dog), feathers, mould. Less intense than SAC but persistent. Itching + watery discharge + conjunctival redness. Associated perennial rhinitis. Management as per SAC; practical, proportionate trigger avoidance can help — measures such as mattress covers or HEPA filters may be considered selectively, but evidence and benefit vary.
Blepharitis
Lid margin inflammation — not primarily allergic but causes intense eyelid itching (especially on the lid margin), grittiness, crusting on lids in the morning, red lid margins, meibomian gland dysfunction (foamy or thickened tear film). Anterior blepharitis: staphylococcal (dandruff-like scales) or seborrhoeic (greasy scales). Posterior blepharitis: meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) — most common cause of chronic dry eye + itch. Chronic, relapsing — long-term lid hygiene; investigations not routinely required. Severe, resistant disease or suspected corneal involvement → optometry/ophthalmology.
Dry eye disease
Paradoxically causes itch (dryness = irritation perceived as itch), burning, grittiness, foreign body sensation, transient blurring that clears with blinking. Worsened by screen use, air conditioning, wind, low humidity. Risk factors: older age, female sex, contact lens wear, antihistamines, antidepressants, anticholinergics, isotretinoin, antihypertensives, post-LASIK. Itching alone is more suggestive of allergy, but dry eye and allergy commonly coexist. Chronic and fluctuating; severe disease can damage the ocular surface. Schirmer testing, tear break-up time and ocular-surface staining are optometry/ophthalmology investigations, not routine GP tests.
Contact dermatoconjunctivitis
Allergic reaction to topical eye drop preservatives (benzalkonium chloride — BAK — most common), cosmetics (mascara, eye shadow, liner), contact lens solution, nickel (eyelash curler). Periorbital eczema + conjunctival injection + intense itch. Treat by stopping unnecessary topical products and removing the offending agent. If persistent or recurrent, refer for specialist assessment and possible patch testing. Switch to preservative-free eye drops. Important in patients whose allergic eye disease is not responding to standard treatment.
Giant papillary conjunctivitis (GPC)
Contact lens-induced papillary reaction on the upper tarsal conjunctiva (similar to but distinct from VKC). Itching worst on lens removal, mucus discharge, lens intolerance, blurred vision. Cause: mechanical trauma from lens edge + protein deposits on lens. Also with ocular prostheses or exposed sutures. Management: remove lenses and avoid lens wear until symptoms and signs resolve; optometry/ophthalmology review for lens fit, material and replacement schedule; do not re-use lenses or cases that may be contaminated; topical mast cell stabiliser.
Atopic keratoconjunctivitis (AKC)
Usually with significant atopic dermatitis — chronic bilateral ocular irritation, eyelid eczema, photophobia, corneal disease, recurrent ocular-surface inflammation. Refer for ophthalmology if persistent, severe or associated with corneal involvement.
The distinction between itching (allergic/blepharitis/dry eye) and pain (infective/inflammatory) is the most important first clinical discriminator in eye presentations — itching is predominantly a feature of Type I hypersensitivity reactions (allergic conjunctivitis) and ocular surface disorders (dry eye, blepharitis), whereas pain, photophobia, and visual change are features of more serious pathology. However, this distinction is not absolute — patients with severe allergic conjunctivitis (especially VKC) can experience significant discomfort amounting to pain from the friction of giant papillae on the cornea, and patients with iritis can have some ocular discomfort described as "itching." The key principle is that any itchy eye with visual change, severe photophobia, or unilateral presentation should be examined carefully before attributing to simple allergy. Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) is one of the most important and most under-diagnosed causes of itchy eyes in adults — it causes posterior blepharitis through obstruction of the meibomian glands (which produce the oily outer layer of the tear film that prevents evaporation). When these glands are obstructed by abnormally viscous meibum (often keratinised), the tear film becomes lipid-deficient, evaporates rapidly, and the resulting dryness causes itch, burning, and grittiness. MGD is common in older adults. The treatment (warm compresses + lid massage to express blocked meibomian glands + lid cleaning) is largely primary care-managed. GPs should be aware that most patients who present with "itchy dry eyes" unresponsive to antihistamine drops have MGD and need lid hygiene rather than more antihistamine. Contact dermatoconjunctivitis from benzalkonium chloride (BAK) preservative is an important and very common cause of itchy eyes that paradoxically worsens with the eye drop treatment — BAK is the preservative in most multidose topical eye drops (including most antihistamine drops and artificial tears), and prolonged use can cause a toxic/allergic reaction, producing chronic itching, redness, and irritation that exactly mimics the condition the drops were prescribed to treat. If a patient's allergic eye symptoms worsen with eye drop treatment, switching to preservative-free formulations (single-dose units) will often produce dramatic improvement.

Targeted History & Examination

Key history
Bilateral vs unilateral (bilateral = usually allergy/dry eye; unilateral = always exclude infective/inflammatory) · Seasonal pattern (SAC) vs year-round (PAC, dry eye, blepharitis) · Discharge character: watery/mucoid (allergy/dry eye), purulent (bacterial), stringy/ropy (VKC, dry eye) · Atopy history (asthma, eczema, food allergy, hayfever) · Contact lens use (GPC, keratitis risk) · Pet or dust exposure · Topical medications (BAK sensitivity) · Cosmetic use (mascara, liner) · Screen use/dry environment (dry eye) · Recent viral URTI (viral conjunctivitis) · Itch vs pain, burning or grittiness · Photophobia, blurred vision, haloes or diplopia · Overnight lens wear and lens-care solution · Recent eye surgery, trauma or foreign body · HSV or zoster history · New cosmetics, creams or eye drops · Drug-related dry eye (antihistamines, antidepressants, anticholinergics, isotretinoin) · Occupational exposures · Rosacea or seborrhoeic dermatitis · Autoimmune disease, IBD or spondyloarthritis · Children: rubbing, school impact, possible VKC
Useful rule
Itch-predominant, bilateral, watery discharge → allergy more likely · burning/gritty with fluctuating vision → dry eye or blepharitis · pain, photophobia, reduced vision or unilateral marked redness → do not assume allergy
Examination (with direct illumination or slit lamp if available)
Visual acuity in each eye separately (any VA reduction → urgent ophthalmology) · Conjunctival injection pattern: diffuse (allergy/viral) vs ciliary flush (limbal redness = uveitis) · Discharge: watery vs purulent vs ropy · Lid margin: crusting/scaling = blepharitis; swelling = allergy/cellulitis · Chemosis (conjunctival oedema = severe allergy) · Cornea: clear (allergy) vs hazy/opacity (keratitis, glaucoma) · Papillae vs follicles (giant papillae under upper lid = VKC/GPC; follicles = viral/chlamydial) · Pupil: equal round reactive (allergy) vs irregular/miotic (uveitis) · Corneal clarity and fluorescein staining · Ocular movements · Contact-lens-related corneal opacity or infiltrate. If no slit lamp and there is diagnostic uncertainty, reduced VA, corneal staining, significant photophobia or contact-lens use → refer rather than relying on direct illumination alone. Do not palpate the globe to assess pressure.
Upper lid eversion
Essential for diagnosing VKC and GPC — must be performed in any child with severe chronic itchy eyes or any contact lens wearer with itch + lens intolerance. Technique: ask patient to look down, hold upper lashes, press a cotton bud on the upper lid crease, fold lid over the bud. Giant papillae (>1 mm, cobblestone appearance) = VKC (children) or GPC (contact lens wearers). Follicles = viral/chlamydial. Smooth surface = allergic conjunctivitis.
Allergen identification history
Tree pollen: late Feb–April (birch, alder). Grass pollen: May–July (peak UK allergic conjunctivitis season). Weed pollen: August–September. Mould: late summer/autumn (damp environments). HDM: year-round (worse in winter — more time indoors, less ventilation). Pet: cat dander most allergenic (Fel d 1 protein — lingers in environments for months after pet removed). Work exposure: flour dust, animal dander (occupational allergy).
The papillae versus follicles distinction on the conjunctival surface is one of the most clinically useful examination findings in red/itchy eye assessment. Papillae are vascular mounds with a central capillary core, appearing as polygonal red dots — they indicate a cellular/allergic reaction and are seen in allergic conjunctivitis (small papillae <1 mm on inferior tarsal conjunctiva), VKC (giant papillae >1 mm on upper tarsal conjunctiva), and GPC. Follicles are avascular lymphoid aggregates appearing as translucent round bumps with vessels going around rather than through them — they indicate a lymphoid immune response and are seen in viral conjunctivitis (adenovirus — the most common cause of follicular conjunctivitis), chlamydial conjunctivitis (which must be excluded in any sexually active adult with chronic follicular conjunctivitis), and molluscum contagiosum affecting the eyelid margin. A GP who can reliably distinguish papillae from follicles using direct illumination and lid eversion can make the correct diagnosis in the majority of itchy/red eye cases. Upper lid eversion is a clinical skill that should be in every GP's repertoire — it takes approximately 30 seconds to perform and provides the crucial examination of the upper tarsal conjunctiva that reveals VKC, GPC, and papillary/follicular patterns. The most common reason VKC is missed in general practice is that the upper lid is not everted, so the diagnostic giant papillae are never seen. The bilateral vs unilateral rule is highly sensitive — bilateral itchy eyes almost always have a benign systemic cause (allergy, dry eye), whereas unilateral itchy eyes require examination to exclude infective, inflammatory (uveitis), or structural causes. A unilateral allergic conjunctivitis does occur (unilateral allergen exposure — working with animals on one side, unilateral contact lens reaction) but is less common and should trigger a more thorough examination.

Investigations

Allergy testing
Specific IgE blood test (RAST/ImmunoCAP) — for common aeroallergens: grass pollen (Phleum pratense g6), house dust mite (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus d1), cat dander (Fel d1), dog dander (Can f1), tree pollen (birch, alder). Request only when it will change management — particularly before allergen immunotherapy (AIT), or for suspected occupational or contact allergy or diagnostic uncertainty — not as a routine response to uncomplicated itchy eyes. Skin prick testing (specialist allergy clinic — more sensitive, broad panel). Not routinely needed for typical seasonal allergic conjunctivitis with clear history.
Conjunctival swab
Not routinely required for allergic conjunctivitis. Indicated: purulent discharge (bacterial conjunctivitis — culture + sensitivity), neonatal conjunctivitis (urgent culture for Neisseria gonorrhoeae + Chlamydia PCR), contact lens wearer with acute red + painful eye (corneal scraping by ophthalmology), chronic unilateral follicular conjunctivitis in sexually active adult (Chlamydia trachomatis PCR — send as eye swab to virology).
Total IgE + eosinophil count
Not routinely required — request only if the result will alter allergy referral or immunotherapy planning. Not specific for allergic conjunctivitis.
When NOT to investigate
Typical bilateral seasonal itchy watery eyes + positive atopy history + clear allergen trigger in an otherwise well patient → no investigations required. Clinical diagnosis is sufficient. Prescribe empirically. Do not routinely request specific IgE, total IgE, eosinophil count, conjunctival swabs, Schirmer testing, tear osmolarity or imaging. Consider targeted investigation or referral when: the diagnosis is uncertain; symptoms are unilateral, persistent or refractory; immunotherapy is being considered; occupational or contact allergy is suspected; there is purulent discharge; chlamydial conjunctivitis is suspected; a contact-lens wearer has a painful red eye; or there is corneal staining, visual change or photophobia.
Schirmer's test (dry eye)
Specialist/optometry tests, not routine primary-care investigations: Schirmer testing, tear break-up time, ocular-surface staining and tear osmolarity may be used by optometry/ophthalmology when the diagnosis is uncertain. Not routinely available in GP but available in some optometry practices with medical-grade equipment.
The chlamydial conjunctivitis swab in sexually active adults with chronic follicular conjunctivitis is a frequently missed investigation — Chlamydia trachomatis (serovars D–K) causes a chronic, unilateral or bilateral, follicular conjunctivitis in sexually active adults, typically presenting as a red itchy eye with follicles on the inferior tarsal conjunctiva and preauricular lymph node enlargement (pathognomonic). It is caused by autoinoculation from genital chlamydial infection. The diagnosis is missed because: (1) it does not produce the acute purulent discharge of bacterial conjunctivitis; (2) it does not respond to topical antibiotics (chlamydia requires systemic treatment); (3) GPs do not routinely take a sexual history in the context of an eye presentation. Any adult with chronic unilateral follicular conjunctivitis not responding to topical treatment warrants a sexual history and chlamydial PCR swab from the eye and genital sites. Treatment follows the BASHH chlamydia guideline via sexual health — doxycycline 100 mg BD first-line, with the course length for ocular infection set by the GUM team. Partner notification is required. The specific IgE blood test for allergy immunotherapy (AIT) planning is important to understand — AIT (subcutaneous or sublingual desensitisation) is the only treatment that modifies the underlying allergic disease rather than simply suppressing symptoms. NICE TA1045 (updated August 2026) recommends house dust mite SLIT (12 SQ-HDM, Acarizax) for moderate to severe house dust mite allergic rhinitis (age 5–65) persisting despite symptom-relieving medicine, and subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) is effective for pollens. AIT requires confirmed specific IgE sensitisation before initiation — blood or skin prick testing is mandatory. GPs who identify patients with severe, refractory allergic conjunctivitis (particularly HDM-driven perennial disease) should refer to allergy/immunology for AIT consideration, as this can produce lasting symptom-free periods years after the course is completed.

Referral Pathways

999
Acute angle-closure glaucoma with severe pain, nausea/vomiting, headache, visual disturbance or a cloudy cornea · Severe orbital or facial infection with systemic illness, neurological symptoms or rapidly progressive swelling · Anaphylaxis with airway or systemic compromise. Do not give IV acetazolamide from primary care or delay transfer.
Same-day ophthalmology
Any itchy/red eye with: reduced or newly changed VA · moderate or severe pain · photophobia (especially consensual) · ciliary flush or suspected anterior uveitis · corneal opacity, haze, ulcer or fluorescein staining · painful red eye in a contact-lens wearer · dendritic staining / suspected HSV keratitis · suspected HZO with ocular symptoms or V1 rash · new diplopia or restricted ocular movements · suspected VKC or AKC with photophobia or corneal involvement · ophthalmia neonatorum (redness + purulent discharge)
Urgent ophthalmology (within 1 week)
Suspected VKC without photophobia, reduced vision or corneal staining (child with giant papillae on lid eversion, severe itching) · Persistent or severe atopic keratoconjunctivitis · Suspected GPC in contact lens wearer not responding to conservative management · Chronic unilateral conjunctivitis with follicles not responding to 4 weeks treatment (chlamydial exclusion + slit lamp) · Recurrent severe allergic conjunctivitis that may need ophthalmology-led topical corticosteroid
Routine ophthalmology / optometrist
Dry eye persisting despite an adequate lubricant + lid hygiene trial, significant visual fluctuation or diagnostic uncertainty (for slit lamp, TBUT, Schirmer's, meibomian gland expression) · Blepharitis severe, resistant or still troublesome after several weeks of consistent lid hygiene · Consideration of punctal plugs for severe aqueous-deficient dry eye
Allergy clinic / immunology
Allergic rhinoconjunctivitis severely impairing quality of life and not controlled by antihistamines + nasal steroid · Occupational allergic conjunctivitis (skin prick testing required) · Consideration of allergen immunotherapy (AIT) — confirmed specific IgE sensitisation required · Anaphylaxis risk with allergen exposure (add-on to eye referral)
GUM / sexual health
Chlamydial conjunctivitis confirmed or suspected (follicular conjunctivitis in sexually active adult) → conjunctival PCR swab + GUM referral for genital screen, treatment, and partner notification. Ophthalmia neonatorum: refer to paediatric and sexual-health services for maternal and partner testing, treatment and partner management.
Community optometrist (minor eye conditions service)
NHS Minor Eye Conditions Service (MECS) available in most areas — GPs can refer patients with red eye or itchy eye to optometrists with enhanced training for same-day or next-day assessment. More accessible than A&E or GP for many patients. The optometrist can differentiate, prescribe (where locally commissioned), and triage to ophthalmology if needed. Most suitable for mild-moderate itchy red eye without red flag features.
The NHS Minor Eye Conditions Service (MECS) is available in most ICB areas in England — it allows community optometrists with enhanced training to assess and manage minor eye conditions (including conjunctivitis, dry eye, blepharitis, and minor trauma) on the same day or next day, either as a direct patient self-referral or via GP referral. For straightforward itchy eye presentations, MECS provides a faster, more appropriate pathway than A&E ophthalmology (where simple allergy conjunctivitis should not be presenting) or a GP appointment where a slit lamp is unavailable. GPs should know whether their area has a MECS service and direct appropriate patients there. The VKC referral urgency (same-day if photophobia or corneal involvement, otherwise within 1 week — not routine) reflects the sight-threatening potential of this condition — corneal shield ulcers from giant papillae-induced mechanical trauma can develop rapidly during peak seasonal exposure and require urgent specialist treatment. A child with severe seasonal itchy eyes who has been treated with OTC antihistamine drops for months without significant improvement, particularly a South Asian or Middle Eastern boy, should have their upper lids everted at GP examination. Finding giant papillae mandates urgent ophthalmology referral, not an escalation to prescription antihistamine drops. The allergy immunotherapy referral pathway for severe rhinoconjunctivitis is one of the most under-used referrals in primary care — sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) with grass pollen or house dust mite allergen extracts can produce sustained benefit in seasonal allergic conjunctivitis and rhinitis, including reduced medication requirements that persist years after the treatment course ends. It is specialist-initiated, for adults with severe symptoms inadequately controlled by pharmacotherapy. The barrier is typically the multi-year commitment required (3–5 years of daily sublingual drops or monthly injections) and the availability of allergy clinic appointments — GPs who identify a patient with severe refractory allergic rhinoconjunctivitis should proactively refer to allergy/immunology rather than escalating pharmacotherapy indefinitely.

Treatment Ladder — Allergic Conjunctivitis

Step 1
Mild seasonalTopical antihistamine or mast-cell stabiliser from the local formulary (e.g. GMMMG Eye formulary): sodium cromoglicate, ketotifen, azelastine or olopatadine (dual action). Follow the individual product information for age restrictions, dosing, duration and preservative content — formulations and licences differ. Olopatadine and azelastine eye drops are prescription-only in the UK (check the BNF for ketotifen); sodium cromoglicate drops can be bought over the counter. Start before the allergy season (pre-seasonal dosing). Avoid contact lenses during active conjunctivitis or while using drops unless specifically advised; wraparound glasses outdoors during pollen exposure; preservative-free lubricants if coexisting dry eye. Cold compresses (cold water flannel to closed lids — 5 minutes) — immediate symptomatic relief, no side effects. Oral antihistamine (loratadine 10 mg OD or cetirizine 10 mg OD) if rhinitis co-exists — often less effective than topical treatment for isolated ocular symptoms and may worsen dry eye. Manage associated allergic rhinitis (intranasal corticosteroid and/or non-sedating antihistamine).
Step 2
Moderate / persistentMast cell stabiliser: sodium cromoglicate or lodoxamide (prescription), dosed per product information. Must be started 2–4 weeks before allergen season and continued throughout — does not provide immediate relief (prophylactic only). Combined antihistamine + mast cell stabiliser (olopatadine, azelastine) is more effective than either alone. Oral non-sedating antihistamine (loratadine 10 mg OD, cetirizine 10 mg OD, fexofenadine 120 mg OD) for combined rhinitis. Preservative-free topical antihistamine if BAK sensitivity (single-dose unit olopatadine).
Step 3
Severe / not controlledRefer to ophthalmology. Topical ocular corticosteroids are ophthalmology-led only — for a defined indication after corneal and infectious pathology (including herpes) and raised IOP have been excluded, with IOP monitoring. Risks: ocular hypertension and glaucoma, cataract, HSV/zoster reactivation, bacterial, fungal or acanthamoeba infection, corneal ulceration and perforation. GP should NOT initiate topical steroid for an undiagnosed red eye. Do not prescribe oral prednisolone routinely while awaiting ophthalmology. Topical NSAIDs (e.g. ketorolac) are not a standard primary-care step — they may irritate the ocular surface and need specialist selection. VKC/AKC: specialist topical anti-inflammatory/immunomodulatory therapy.
Olopatadine is one first-line topical option (choose per local formulary) for allergic conjunctivitis; it has a dual mechanism — it is both an H1 receptor antagonist AND a mast cell stabiliser, preventing both the immediate-phase reaction (histamine-mediated itch within minutes of allergen exposure) and the late-phase reaction (cytokine-mediated inflammation 4–8 hours later). This dual mechanism means it provides both immediate symptomatic relief and sustained prophylaxis with BD dosing, making it more effective and more convenient than antihistamines alone (which only block immediate-phase) or mast cell stabilisers alone (which only prevent late-phase when used prophylactically). The prescribing principle of pre-seasonal dosing for mast cell stabilisers (sodium cromoglicate, nedocromil) is important — these drugs do not provide immediate relief. They work by stabilising mast cell membranes, preventing degranulation on allergen contact. This prophylactic mechanism requires consistent use for 2–4 weeks before allergen exposure to achieve maximum effect. Patients who start taking sodium cromoglicate drops at the height of the grass pollen season in June will not achieve the same benefit as those who start in April. GPs prescribing or recommending mast cell stabilisers must explicitly advise patients to start before the season begins. Cold compresses are one of the most effective immediate interventions for allergic conjunctivitis itch and are entirely evidence-based — cold temperature reduces mast cell degranulation activity, constricts blood vessels (reducing conjunctival chemosis and hyperaemia), and provides a counter-irritation effect that overrides itch signalling. A cold flannel to closed eyes for 5 minutes can provide more rapid itch relief than any eye drop, costs nothing, and can be repeated multiple times daily. This should be taught to every patient with allergic conjunctivitis as first-line immediate management alongside pharmacological treatment.

Blepharitis & Dry Eye Management

Lid hygiene routine (blepharitis)
The cornerstone of blepharitis treatment — use regularly during flares. (1) Warm compress: warm (not hot) wet flannel or microwavable lid warmer held to closed lids for around 5–10 minutes — softens blocked meibum, opens meibomian glands. (2) Lid massage: using a clean finger, press the lid margin and massage from outer to inner corner — expresses liquefied meibum. (3) Lid scrubs: proprietary lid-cleaning product (e.g. Blephasol, Blephaclean — pharmacy OTC) or an appropriately diluted preparation — removes debris, demodex, bacterial biofilm from lid margins. Use regularly during flares, then maintenance once daily or as tolerated — long-term, individualised. Avoid eye make-up during active inflammation.
Topical antibiotics (blepharitis)
Topical antibiotic ointment (e.g. chloramphenicol 1% to lid margins at night) may be considered for selected anterior blepharitis with clinical evidence of bacterial involvement after adequate lid hygiene. Do not add topical steroid–antibiotic combinations routinely in primary care. Not effective for posterior (meibomian) blepharitis alone — lid hygiene is the primary treatment. Fusidic acid 1% gel (Fucithalmic) — alternative for marginal blepharitis. Azithromycin 1% drops — off-label, specialist option for meibomian gland dysfunction.
Systemic antibiotics (blepharitis — severe/rosacea-associated)
Oral doxycycline (or another systemic antibiotic) — reserve for significant meibomian-gland dysfunction or rosacea-associated disease, usually after optometry/ophthalmology or dermatology advice; particularly in acne rosacea-associated blepharitis (rosacea blepharitis: lid telangiectasia, facial flushing, papules — doxycycline treats both ocular and facial rosacea). Anti-inflammatory mechanism (inhibits matrix metalloproteinases — reduces lid inflammation independently of antibiotic effect). Avoid in pregnancy/breastfeeding and under 12 years. Oxytetracycline 250–500 mg BD as alternative.
Dry eye lubricants
Carbomer gel (Viscotears) QDS — viscous, longer contact time, good for moderate-severe dry eye. Hyaluronate drops 0.1–0.2% (Hylo-Tear, Hycosan) BD–QDS — mucoadhesive, stabilises tear film, excellent tolerability. Carmellose sodium 0.5–1% (Celluvisc) QDS — effective, inexpensive. Prefer preservative-free preparations when frequent dosing is required, there is ocular-surface disease, preservative sensitivity, or multiple topical preparations are used. For severe dry eye: high-viscosity gel (Viscotears BD) or ointment (Lacri-Lube) at night (may temporarily blur vision). Omega-3 supplementation is not routine (evidence mixed). Refer to optometry/ophthalmology if symptoms persist despite an adequate trial, or with suspected keratitis, significant visual fluctuation or diagnostic uncertainty. Topical ciclosporin is specialist treatment for severe keratitis not improved with tear substitutes (NICE TA369).
The lid hygiene routine (warm compress → massage → lid scrub) sequence must be performed in this precise order because each step prepares for the next — the warm compress first softens the solidified meibum inside the meibomian glands (at 37°C it becomes more viscous and blocks the gland openings; warming to approximately 45°C liquefies it), the massage then expresses this liquid meibum from the glands onto the lid margin, and the scrub then removes the expressed meibum and debris before it reaccumulates. Performing the scrub without the warm compress first is much less effective because the glands are not primed for expression. The Eyebag (a microwavable wheat-filled bag designed specifically for warm lid compression) maintains temperature better than a wet flannel and can be a convenient option for patients. The doxycycline mechanism for blepharitis is primarily anti-inflammatory rather than antibiotic at the doses used — doxycycline at sub-antimicrobial doses (40 mg OD — Periostat dosing) inhibits matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that are activated in the meibomian glands and conjunctiva in MGD and rosacea, causing local tissue inflammation and destruction of the lipid bilayer of the tear film. This MMP inhibition is independent of its antibiotic activity and explains why it works in posterior blepharitis where bacterial infection is not the primary mechanism. The 6–12 week course is typically needed before improvement is sustained, and courses may need to be repeated annually in chronic posterior blepharitis/rosacea. Preservative-free lubricants are significantly superior to preserved lubricants for chronic dry eye management — BAK preservative (present in most multidose lubricant eye drops) causes direct toxicity to corneal epithelial cells, goblet cells (which produce the mucin layer of the tear film), and meibomian gland cells with repeated use. In patients using lubricants more than 4 times daily, the BAK exposure becomes clinically significant and worsens the dry eye it is intended to treat. The NHS and RCOphth guidance is to use preservative-free lubricants when treatment frequency exceeds 4 times daily.

Lifestyle

Allergen Avoidance, Environmental Modification & Eye Care

Pollen avoidance (SAC) Check the pollen count (Met Office app — 5-day pollen forecast). Consider staying indoors on high pollen count days. Keep windows and doors closed during peak season. Shower and change clothes after being outside. Dry laundry inside (pollen adheres to wet fabric). Wrap-around sunglasses outdoors may reduce pollen-to-conjunctiva contact.
House dust mite reduction (PAC) HDM allergen (Der p 1) is the most common perennial allergen. Practical, proportionate measures may be considered selectively (evidence and benefit vary): wash bedding regularly at 60°C, mattress and pillow encasements, reduce dust-collecting soft furnishings in the bedroom, good ventilation.
Pet allergy management Cat allergen (Fel d 1) is sticky and persistent. Keep pets out of the bedroom, wash hands after pet contact and avoid touching the face. Other measures (air purifiers, pet bathing, specialist cat foods) may be considered selectively, but evidence and practical benefit vary.
Screen use and dry eye Digital eye strain and screen-induced dry eye (reduced blink rate during screen use). Apply the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds (reduces ciliary muscle fatigue and restores blink rate). Conscious blinking exercises during screen use. Screen position: slightly below eye level (less upper lid retraction, reduces corneal exposure). Reduce screen brightness. Increase font size to reduce focusing effort.
Cosmetics and contact lens hygiene Mascara and eye shadow: use hypoallergenic, fragrance-free products. Replace mascara every 3 months (bacterial contamination). Remove make-up completely before sleep — sleeping in mascara causes direct mechanical trauma to conjunctiva and corneal epithelium. Contact lens hygiene: daily disposable lenses are the most hygienic option (no protein build-up, no solution sensitivity). Never sleep in contact lenses. Replace lens case every month. Use any approved lens-care system exactly as advised by the optometrist or manufacturer — hydrogen-peroxide systems must be fully neutralised to avoid chemical injury. Avoid lens wear during active conjunctivitis or while using drops unless advised; do not re-use lenses or cases that may be contaminated.
Avoid eye rubbing Avoid eye rubbing, which can worsen symptoms and contribute to ocular-surface trauma — mechanical pressure on the cornea causes mast cell degranulation (releasing more histamine — creates a rubbing cycle), direct corneal trauma (contributing to keratoconus in severe allergic disease), introduction of allergens and bacteria from fingers. Advise patients explicitly: rubbing provides momentary relief but worsens symptoms. Instead: cold compress, instil antihistamine drop, blink firmly. Tell children specifically: "rubbing makes the itch worse tomorrow." In VKC: keratoconus risk from chronic rubbing is a serious long-term consequence.
Diet and omega-3 Omega-3 supplementation is not required for standard allergic eye care, and evidence for dry eye is mixed — it is not a routine treatment. A balanced diet including oily fish is reasonable general advice.
Workplace ergonomics and humidification Air conditioning, central heating, and air travel severely worsen dry eye by reducing ambient humidity Consider a humidifier if the environment is very dry. VDU positioning: screen below eye level, appropriate character size to reduce squinting. Wind, fans, and air vents directed at the face worsen tear evaporation — redirect desk fan away from face. Protective eyewear (wraparound glasses, swim goggles for wind/sport) reduces evaporative dry eye and allergen exposure simultaneously.
The keratoconus-eye rubbing link is one of the most important patient safety messages in allergic eye disease management — keratoconus (progressive corneal thinning and ectasia causing irregular astigmatism and visual distortion) is strongly associated with chronic vigorous eye rubbing, particularly in patients with VKC and atopic eye disease. The mechanical stress of rubbing causes corneal cell apoptosis, activation of corneal proteases (collagenases that break down the stromal collagen lamellae), and localised thinning that produces the characteristic conical corneal protrusion. Keratoconus is uncommon in the general population but has a higher prevalence in patients with atopic eye disease and chronic eye rubbing. It typically becomes clinically significant in the teens and 20s and can require corneal cross-linking (to halt progression) or corneal transplantation in advanced cases. Advising patients (especially children and adolescents with VKC) that eye rubbing must be avoided is a sight-preserving intervention. Pet-allergen interventions such as specialist cat foods have limited evidence and are optional, not essential treatment — give practical, proportionate trigger-avoidance advice.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Mild-moderate allergic conjunctivitis
No routine follow-up needed if responding to treatment. Review if symptoms are not controlled after a reasonable trial of appropriate topical treatment (usually 2–4 weeks). Review frequency based on severity, treatment response and risk factors — no routine annual review needed. Document: treatment given, patient education on pre-seasonal prophylaxis, avoidance measures discussed.
Blepharitis — 6–8 weeks
Review after several weeks of consistent lid hygiene if symptoms remain troublesome. Lid hygiene is long-term — improvement expected but not cure. Adjust routine if not compliant (simplified lid wipe protocol instead of 3-step if adherence is poor). If not responding → consider topical antibiotic ointment for anterior blepharitis with bacterial involvement; doxycycline for significant MGD/rosacea, usually after optometry/ophthalmology or dermatology advice. Review for rosacea features (facial flushing, telangiectasia, rhinophyma).
Dry eye — 3 months
Is lubricant frequency adequate? Preservative-free? Warm compress + lid massage routine adherence? Screen habits modified? If not responding to lubricants + lid hygiene after an adequate trial → optometrist or ophthalmology referral for Schirmer's, TBUT, punctal plug consideration, or topical cyclosporine (Ikervis 0.1% — licensed for severe keratitis not improved with tear substitutes, ophthalmology-initiated; NICE TA369). Dry eye is chronic and fluctuating; severe disease can damage the ocular surface — review based on severity and response.
Contact lens management review
Any contact lens wearer with GPC or recurrent lens intolerance: arrange optometry assessment of lens fit, material and replacement schedule; do not restart lenses until symptoms have resolved and the lens plan has been reviewed. If still symptomatic → ophthalmology. Annual review of lens prescription and health. Remind: never sleep in lenses, replace case monthly, attend annual eye examination with optometrist even if no symptoms.
Medication review — topical steroid monitoring
If topical steroids initiated by ophthalmology: GP must not renew prescription without ophthalmology review (steroid-induced glaucoma risk — raised IOP with prolonged topical steroid use; posterior subcapsular cataract with long-term use). Review IOP at 4–6 weeks of steroid use. Patients on long-term topical steroids for VKC require 3-monthly IOP checks by optometrist. Document: who initiated, for what indication, duration planned.
Same-day ophthalmology safety-net
Any new pain, photophobia, or VA change in a patient under treatment for itchy eyes → same-day ophthalmology. Contact lens wearer with new painful red eye during follow-up → remove lens immediately and same-day assessment. New corneal cloudiness or opacity on inspection. Unilateral severe red eye. Dendritic lesion or V1 vesicular rash (HZO). Severe symptoms in a child with suspected VKC. Neonatal purulent eye discharge. Nausea/haloes suggesting acute glaucoma → 999. Any episode of acutely raised IOP (haloes + nausea) in a patient on topical steroids.
Urgent review same week
Allergic conjunctivitis not responding to a reasonable trial (usually 2–4 weeks) of adequate topical treatment (reconsider diagnosis — VKC? Contact dermatoconjunctivitis? Chlamydial? Dry eye?) · Child with severe seasonal itchy eyes — evert upper lid before each treatment escalation · Blepharitis with corneal involvement (photophobia) → ophthalmology rather than GP escalation
The topical steroid monitoring imperative is one of the most important safety issues in primary care ophthalmology — a substantial proportion of people have a steroid responder phenotype that causes a significant rise in intraocular pressure (IOP) in response to topical ophthalmic corticosteroids. In steroid responders, IOP typically rises within 4–6 weeks of starting topical steroids and returns to normal after discontinuation. However, if sustained elevations are undetected and the steroid is continued, glaucomatous optic nerve damage can occur (steroid-induced open-angle glaucoma). GPs who receive a prescription for topical loteprednol, prednisolone acetate, or betamethasone eye drops from an ophthalmologist should: (1) ensure IOP was measured at the ophthalmology visit before prescription; (2) schedule an IOP check at 4–6 weeks with the community optometrist (most optometrists can measure IOP with non-contact tonometry); (3) not renew topical steroid prescriptions for eye conditions without ophthalmology review. The "treatment failure = reconsider diagnosis" principle for allergic conjunctivitis is practically important — many patients who do not respond to antihistamine drops and oral antihistamines have been treated for the wrong diagnosis. The three most commonly missed alternative diagnoses are: (1) dry eye masquerading as allergy (itch without watery discharge, worse with screen use, worse in the afternoon, BAK sensitivity from previous drops); (2) contact dermatoconjunctivitis from cosmetics or drop preservatives (worsens with continued use of the allergen-containing product); (3) chlamydial conjunctivitis (unilateral, follicular, sexually active, often accompanied by discharge that is not clearly purulent). Reviewing these three diagnoses at the "4 weeks not responding" review point, and examining the upper lid (eversion), testing VA, and taking a sexual history if appropriate, will identify the missed diagnosis in most cases.
