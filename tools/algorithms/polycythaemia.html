Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Hyperviscosity Crisis and Acute Thrombosis

Screen for hyperviscosity syndrome, acute arterial or venous thrombosis, and severe erythrocytosis requiring urgent assessment.

Severe erythrocytosis Hct ≥0.60 (men) or ≥0.56 (women) → urgent clinical assessment; same-day specialist discussion if headache, visual disturbance, neurological symptoms, chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, bleeding or thrombosis
Acute thrombosis Stroke, MI, PE, DVT, Budd-Chiari with polycythaemia → 999 / emergency pathway. Management depends on the clinical diagnosis, haemodynamic state, bleeding risk and specialist advice
Headache + visual symptoms Severe headache, visual disturbance, dizziness or confusion with erythrocytosis → same-day specialist discussion (possible hyperviscosity); focal neurology → 999
Chest pain + high Hct Chest pain or breathlessness with erythrocytosis → assess for ACS or PE; emergency pathway if significant symptoms or instability
Splenomegaly With erythrocytosis → urgent haematology discussion (suspected PV or another myeloproliferative neoplasm); same-day if symptomatic or with marked leucocytosis/thrombocytosis
Pruritus after bathing Aquagenic pruritus, erythromelalgia, headache, visual symptoms, splenomegaly and thrombosis increase suspicion of PV but are not diagnostic → urgent haematology referral if erythrocytosis persists
Gout flare Acute monoarthritis with erythrocytosis — a supportive feature, not diagnostic of PV. Treat gout per standard gout guidance
Plethoric facies Ruddy complexion, red palms, conjunctival injection — supportive features, not diagnostic → investigate (EPO and JAK2 per local pathway) and refer if persistent unexplained erythrocytosis

Severe erythrocytosis (Hct ≥0.60 in men or ≥0.56 in women) increases blood viscosity and thrombotic risk and needs urgent clinical assessment. In BSH diagnostic guidance this level allows true erythrocytosis to be assumed — it is not an automatic emergency-venesection threshold. Urgency depends on symptoms and clinical context; venesection decisions are specialist-led.

Hyperviscosity symptoms: headache, visual disturbance, dizziness, confusion; retinal vein engorgement may be visible on fundoscopy. Symptomatic patients need same-day specialist discussion; acute neurological or cardiac events go via emergency pathways.

Polycythaemia vera (PV) is a clonal myeloproliferative neoplasm; JAK2 V617F is present in approximately 95% of cases. Arterial and venous thrombosis are major complications, including unusual-site thrombosis such as Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis), presenting with ascites + hepatomegaly + abdominal pain.

Aquagenic pruritus (itching after bathing) is a useful clue to PV but is not pathognomonic — it can occur in other haematological and dermatological conditions. Gout, plethoric facies and conjunctival injection are likewise supportive, not diagnostic.

2
Diagnose

Confirm Polycythaemia — Repeat Hct and Exclude Pseudopolycythaemia

Confirm true polycythaemia (raised red cell mass) and exclude relative polycythaemia (reduced plasma volume). A single raised Hct may be dehydration or lab error.

Repeat FBC
Review the result in clinical context and repeat a venous FBC to confirm persistence, particularly where dehydration, acute illness or a laboratory error is possible (repeat interval per local laboratory/haematology pathway). Persistent erythrocytosis should be investigated rather than repeatedly deferred. Review Hb, Hct, RBC count, WCC, platelets, MCV and blood film.
True vs relative polycythaemia
True (absolute) polycythaemia: Raised red cell mass (measured by red cell labeling). Relative (pseudopolycythaemia): Normal red cell mass, reduced plasma volume (dehydration, diuretics, obesity, smoking).
Haematocrit thresholds
Investigate persistent venous Hct >0.52 (men) or >0.48 (women) (BSH) — an investigation threshold, not a diagnosis of PV. Haematocrit performs better than haemoglobin for identifying raised red-cell mass; if Hb is used, apply clearly labelled local laboratory/WHO thresholds (approximately >185 g/L men, >165 g/L women for PV assessment). Hct is higher at altitude.
Clinical context
Dehydration (diarrhea, vomiting, heat exposure)? Diuretics? Acute illness? Smoking or carbon monoxide exposure (carboxyhaemoglobin; pulse oximetry may be misleading)? Obesity (Gaisböck syndrome = relative polycythaemia)? High altitude residence?
Pseudopolycythaemia (Gaisböck)
Middle-aged, obese, hypertensive males. Hct 0.52-0.56. Normal red cell mass, reduced plasma volume. No splenomegaly. No thrombocytosis/leucocytosis. JAK2 negative. Treat cardiovascular risk factors, not venesection.
Persistent polycythaemia
Venous Hct above threshold on repeat testing (interval per local pathway). Hct ≥0.60 (men) or ≥0.56 (women) may allow true erythrocytosis to be assumed (BSH) and needs urgent clinical assessment.

Relative polycythaemia (pseudopolycythaemia) is common. Caused by reduced plasma volume (dehydration, diuretics, obesity). Hct typically 0.52-0.56 (mild elevation). No splenomegaly, no thrombocytosis, no symptoms. JAK2 negative. Does NOT require venesection; treat hypertension and cardiovascular risk factors.

Smoking causes pseudopolycythaemia via two mechanisms: (1) carboxyhaemoglobin (CO bound to Hb) falsely elevates measured Hb; (2) chronic hypoxia stimulates erythropoietin. Smoking cessation may improve Hct. Pulse oximetry can be misleading in smokers; consider carboxyhaemoglobin measurement (BSH).

Persistent haematocrit >0.52 (men) or >0.48 (women) warrants investigation (BSH) — it indicates erythrocytosis, not PV. A single elevated Hct can reflect dehydration, acute illness or laboratory error, so repeat to confirm persistence; the interval is set locally.

Red cell mass measurement is not routinely required in primary care and is rarely available; haematology may still consider it in selected cases of diagnostic uncertainty, such as suspected PV with borderline results.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Primary vs Secondary Polycythaemia

Classify polycythaemia as primary (PV, JAK2+ myeloproliferative neoplasm) or secondary (erythropoietin-driven). This determines investigation pathway and treatment.

Polycythaemia vera (PV)
Primary clonal disorder. JAK2 V617F positive in approximately 95% (exon 12 mutations in a small proportion of the rest). Raised Hct; may have thrombocytosis, leucocytosis and splenomegaly. Low/normal EPO. Aquagenic pruritus. Diagnosis requires specialist review.
Secondary polycythaemia
EPO-driven. High EPO. Normal platelets/WCC. No splenomegaly. JAK2 negative. Causes: Chronic hypoxia (COPD, OSA, altitude), renal disease (cysts, transplant, cancer), EPO-secreting tumors (HCC, RCC, cerebellar hemangioblastoma). Drugs: testosterone, anabolic steroids, erythropoietin. Consider family history or lifelong erythrocytosis.
Chronic hypoxia
COPD, cyanotic heart disease, OSA, high altitude (>2500 m). Hypoxia stimulates EPO; normal daytime SaO₂ does not exclude nocturnal hypoxia (OSA). No thrombocytosis. Treat the underlying cause; specialist venesection considered for hyperviscosity symptoms or Hct >0.56, generally targeting 0.50–0.52 (BSH).
Renal causes
Polycystic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, post-renal transplant, renal cell carcinoma. Inappropriate EPO secretion. Consider renal ultrasound or other abdominal imaging when EPO is raised, renal disease is suspected, there is haematuria, abdominal/renal abnormality, renal transplantation, or no other cause is identified.
EPO-secreting tumors
Hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, cerebellar hemangioblastoma, uterine fibroids, phaeochromocytoma. Paraneoplastic EPO secretion — fibroids, phaeochromocytoma and haemangioblastoma are uncommon causes and should not prompt indiscriminate imaging. Further imaging (e.g. CT) is specialist-directed, based on symptoms, examination, EPO and initial investigations.
Gaisböck syndrome
Relative polycythaemia (not true polycythaemia). Obese, hypertensive, middle-aged men. Hct 0.52-0.56. Normal red cell mass, reduced plasma volume. JAK2 negative. No venesection; treat cardiovascular risk.

Polycythaemia vera (PV) is a JAK2-driven myeloproliferative neoplasm with uncontrolled red cell, and often platelet and white cell, production. JAK2 V617F is present in approximately 95%. Thrombosis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality, and a minority progress to myelofibrosis or acute leukaemia over many years.

Secondary polycythaemia is reactive, driven by erythropoietin (EPO). Chronic hypoxia (COPD, OSA, altitude) causes physiological EPO elevation. Renal disease and tumors cause inappropriate EPO secretion. Treating the underlying cause is the mainstay of management.

EPO levels differentiate PV from secondary. PV has low/normal EPO (autonomous red cell production, no EPO needed). Secondary polycythaemia has high EPO (driving red cell production). Interpret JAK2 alongside EPO and the clinical picture; if JAK2 is negative with low EPO or high suspicion, refer to haematology, who decide on further testing (exon 12, bone marrow).

Thrombocytosis + leucocytosis alongside polycythaemia strongly suggests PV (trilineage proliferation). Secondary polycythaemia affects only red cells; platelets and WCC are normal. Splenomegaly favours PV over secondary erythrocytosis.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Splenomegaly, Plethora, and Hypoxia Signs

Examine for signs of PV (splenomegaly, plethora, thrombosis) and secondary causes (cyanosis, clubbing, hypertension).

General inspection
Plethora: Ruddy, flushed face, red palms, conjunctival injection → supportive of PV, not diagnostic. Cyanosis: Blue lips, fingers → chronic hypoxia (COPD, cyanotic heart disease). Obesity: BMI >30 → Gaisböck syndrome (relative polycythaemia).
Vital signs
BP elevated → PV (hyperviscosity increases peripheral resistance), Gaisböck syndrome (associated hypertension), renal artery stenosis. Low SaO₂ → chronic hypoxia (COPD, altitude); normal daytime SaO₂ does not exclude nocturnal hypoxia, and oximetry may be misleading in smokers (carboxyhaemoglobin).
Spleen
Percuss and palpate left upper quadrant. Splenomegaly → favours PV or another myeloproliferative neoplasm (urgent haematology). Massive splenomegaly → consider post-PV myelofibrosis. Absent splenomegaly → secondary polycythaemia more likely.
Cardiovascular
Examine for DVT (calf swelling), peripheral arterial disease (absent pulses, bruits). Check for signs of prior stroke (focal weakness, dysphasia). Elevated JVP → cor pulmonale (COPD, OSA causing secondary polycythaemia).
Respiratory
Auscultate chest. Crackles, wheeze, reduced air entry → COPD (chronic hypoxia). Measure SaO₂. Assess for COPD according to symptoms and smoking history and arrange spirometry where clinically indicated; consider overnight oximetry or sleep assessment if OSA is suspected. Clubbing → cyanotic heart disease, lung fibrosis (hypoxia-driven polycythaemia).
Abdomen
Hepatomegaly → hepatocellular carcinoma (EPO-secreting tumor), Budd-Chiari syndrome (PV-related hepatic vein thrombosis). Palpable kidneys → polycystic kidney disease (secondary polycythaemia). Ascites + hepatomegaly → Budd-Chiari (PV complication).
Skin
Aquagenic pruritus (ask about itching after hot bath/shower), scratch marks, erythromelalgia (burning red extremities) and gout are supportive of PV but not diagnostic.

Splenomegaly is a useful sign favouring PV over secondary erythrocytosis. Spleen size correlates with disease burden, and massive or enlarging splenomegaly may suggest transformation to post-PV myelofibrosis.

Plethora (ruddy complexion) is due to raised Hct. Red palms, injected conjunctivae, facial flushing. More pronounced in PV than secondary polycythaemia (higher Hct in PV typically). Plethora reduces post-venesection as Hct falls to target (<0.45).

Low SaO₂ on room air indicates chronic hypoxia, a common driver of secondary erythrocytosis (COPD, altitude, cyanotic heart disease). Normal daytime saturation does not exclude nocturnal hypoxia — consider overnight oximetry or sleep assessment if OSA is suspected. In smokers, carboxyhaemoglobin can make oximetry misleading; BSH recommends considering carboxyhaemoglobin measurement.

Aquagenic pruritus (itching after hot bath/shower) increases suspicion of PV but is not pathognomonic. Management of troublesome pruritus is guided by haematology.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — JAK2 Mutation, EPO Level, and Secondary Cause Workup

Investigate to differentiate PV from secondary polycythaemia. A positive JAK2 V617F is highly suggestive of a myeloproliferative neoplasm but does not by itself confirm PV; interpret with EPO and the clinical picture.

Baseline bloods
FBC + Hct (confirm Hct >0.52 M / >0.48 F) Platelets, WCC (thrombocytosis + leucocytosis suggests PV) Blood film (leucocytosis, basophilia in PV) U&E, LFT, calcium Ferritin Urinalysis (if renal disease suspected)
JAK2 mutation
JAK2 V617F — Request with serum EPO according to the local haematology/laboratory pathway (check whether JAK2 needs haematology approval locally) in persistent unexplained erythrocytosis. Present in approximately 95% of PV. A positive result is highly suggestive of a clonal myeloproliferative neoplasm — refer urgently to haematology; do not describe it as confirming PV without specialist review. If negative with low EPO or high suspicion → haematology (exon 12 testing is specialist-directed).
Erythropoietin (EPO)
Serum EPO — Alongside JAK2 where available. Low/normal EPO: PV (autonomous production). High EPO: Secondary polycythaemia (investigate cause). Low EPO with JAK2-negative erythrocytosis → haematology referral.
Hypoxia screen
SaO₂ (resting and exertion; may mislead in smokers), Carboxyhaemoglobin (if smoking/CO exposure relevant), ABG (if hypoxic), Spirometry (where COPD is suspected clinically), Overnight oximetry (OSA if snoring, witnessed apnoeas). Echocardiogram if cyanotic heart disease suspected.
Renal imaging
Ultrasound kidneys — Consider when EPO is raised, renal disease is suspected, there is haematuria, abdominal/renal abnormality, renal transplantation, or no other cause is identified. Detects polycystic kidney disease, renal masses (RCC), hydronephrosis. Renal artery Doppler if renal bruit (renal artery stenosis).
Tumor screen
Not a routine primary-care step: further imaging (e.g. CT) is specialist-directed, based on symptoms, examination, EPO and initial investigations. No routine alpha-fetoprotein — investigate suspected liver disease or malignancy clinically and radiologically.
Additional PV workup
Uric acid (elevated in PV, gout risk), LDH (cell turnover), Vitamin B12 (elevated in PV due to increased transcobalamin). Haematology performs bone marrow biopsy if diagnosis unclear.
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT perform bone marrow biopsy in primary care. Red-cell-mass measurement is not routinely required in primary care and is rarely available — discuss diagnostic uncertainty with haematology. Do NOT venesect suspected secondary erythrocytosis in primary care; symptomatic patients → same-day specialist discussion rather than waiting for JAK2 results. Do not routinely order renal ultrasound, CT or AFP for every patient.

JAK2 V617F is present in approximately 95% of PV and strongly supports a myeloproliferative neoplasm, but does not by itself establish PV. Diagnosis requires specialist correlation of Hb/Hct or red-cell mass, JAK2 status, EPO, exclusion of secondary causes and, where required, bone-marrow morphology. Exon 12 mutations account for a small proportion of JAK2 V617F-negative cases (specialist testing).

EPO level helps discriminate: PV usually has low/normal EPO because red cell production is autonomous, whereas secondary erythrocytosis often has normal or raised EPO. Interpret against the local laboratory reference range; a low EPO with JAK2-negative erythrocytosis needs haematology referral.

Overnight oximetry or sleep assessment helps diagnose obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) when suspected (snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence). Nocturnal desaturation stimulates EPO; treating OSA may improve the erythrocytosis.

Renal ultrasound is considered when EPO is raised, renal disease is suspected, there is haematuria, an abdominal/renal abnormality, renal transplantation, or no other cause is identified. It detects polycystic kidney disease, renal cell carcinoma (paraneoplastic EPO secretion), post-transplant erythrocytosis (native kidneys secrete EPO), and renal artery stenosis (ischaemia → EPO).

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to Involve Haematology or Respiratory

Refer based on symptoms, thrombosis, JAK2/EPO results, other blood count abnormalities and underlying cause — not on a rigid Hct cut-off alone. PV requires specialist management; secondary polycythaemia may be managed in primary care if mild.

999 Emergency
Acute stroke or focal neurological deficit; acute coronary syndrome; suspected PE with significant symptoms or instability; severe breathlessness or hypoxia; confusion, seizure or reduced consciousness; acute Budd–Chiari-type presentation (severe abdominal pain, ascites or hepatic decompensation). Management depends on the diagnosis, haemodynamic state, bleeding risk and specialist advice.
Same-day haematology
Marked or rapidly rising Hct with hyperviscosity symptoms; severe headache with visual or neurological symptoms; recent thrombosis with marked erythrocytosis; Hct ≥0.60 (men) or ≥0.56 (women), especially if symptomatic; suspected acute leukaemia, transformation or severe thrombocytosis; pregnancy with suspected or known PV. An isolated Hct of 0.55 in an otherwise well person does not automatically need same-day attendance.
Urgent haematology
Positive JAK2 mutation. Persistent unexplained erythrocytosis. Low EPO with JAK2-negative erythrocytosis. Erythrocytosis with thrombocytosis, leucocytosis or splenomegaly. Erythrocytosis with arterial, venous or unusual-site thrombosis. Symptomatic secondary erythrocytosis needing consideration of venesection. Unexplained erythrocytosis in a young person. Pregnancy planning with suspected/known PV. B symptoms (night sweats, weight loss).
Routine haematology
Advice and guidance via the local Bolton/Greater Manchester haematology pathway when investigation or interpretation is uncertain (e.g. borderline persistent Hct with normal EPO and negative JAK2, family history or lifelong erythrocytosis).
Primary care management
Relative polycythaemia (Gaisböck: obese, hypertensive, JAK2 negative, normal EPO). Asymptomatic secondary erythrocytosis with an identified cause: treat COPD/hypoxic lung disease, assess and treat OSA, stop smoking, review testosterone, anabolic steroids, EPO and other drugs — no routine venesection, and no aspirin solely for raised Hct. Known PV: follow the haematology plan and shared-care protocol.
Specialist referrals
Respiratory: hypoxic lung disease or suspected OSA — respiratory/sleep assessment; venesection considered for hyperviscosity symptoms or Hct >0.56 (BSH). Nephrology: Polycystic kidneys, post-transplant erythrocytosis, renal artery stenosis. Oncology: RCC, HCC suspected on imaging.

Severe erythrocytosis (Hct ≥0.60 in men or ≥0.56 in women) requires urgent clinical assessment; same-day specialist discussion is appropriate if accompanied by headache, visual disturbance, neurological symptoms, chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, bleeding or thrombosis. Urgency depends on symptoms, thrombosis, rate of rise, other count abnormalities, splenomegaly, pregnancy and underlying hypoxic disease.

JAK2 V617F positive is strongly suggestive of PV and warrants urgent haematology referral (BSH). Haematology confirms or refutes the diagnosis (WHO criteria, including bone marrow where required), risk-stratifies (age, thrombosis history), and initiates venesection + aspirin ± cytoreduction.

Relative polycythaemia (Gaisböck syndrome) does NOT require haematology referral or venesection. Treat cardiovascular risk factors (weight loss, BP control, smoking cessation). Venesection is not indicated.

Secondary erythrocytosis that is asymptomatic with an identified cause can usually be managed in primary care by treating the cause. For hypoxic pulmonary disease, BSH recommends respiratory assessment and consideration of specialist venesection when symptomatic from hyperviscosity or Hct >0.56, generally reducing Hct to 0.50–0.52 rather than the PV target of <0.45. Refer if the cause is unclear or symptoms develop.

7
Treat

Treatment — Treat the Cause; Haematology-Led PV Management

Treatment depends on cause. Confirmed PV: haematology-led venesection to Hct <0.45, low-dose aspirin unless contraindicated ± cytoreduction. Secondary erythrocytosis: treat the cause; do not venesect in primary care or prescribe aspirin solely for a raised Hct. Gaisböck syndrome: cardiovascular risk reduction, no venesection.

Polycythaemia vera
Venesection + Aspirin Haematology-led
Confirmed PV: venesection via the haematology/venesection service to maintain Hct <0.45 (volume and frequency individualised). Low-dose aspirin unless contraindicated (active bleeding, peptic ulcer, anticoagulation, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, allergy/intolerance; pregnancy per specialist advice). Cytoreduction for high-risk PV (age ≥65 and/or previous thrombosis) or selected lower-risk patients — a haematology decision. Do not start aspirin for suspected PV in primary care without specialist advice.
Secondary polycythaemia
Treat underlying cause First-line
Treat COPD/hypoxic lung disease per respiratory guidance, assess and treat OSA, stop smoking, review testosterone, anabolic steroids, EPO and other drugs, and manage renal or cardiac disease with the relevant specialty. No routine venesection of asymptomatic secondary erythrocytosis; specialist venesection considered for hyperviscosity symptoms or Hct >0.56 (hypoxic lung disease), generally targeting 0.50–0.52. No aspirin solely for raised Hct.
Relative polycythaemia (Gaisböck)
Cardiovascular risk reduction No venesection
Weight loss, BP control, smoking cessation and statin per NICE cardiovascular risk guidance. Review diuretics. Venesection not indicated.

PV treatment ladder (haematology-managed):

Step 1Venesection via the haematology/venesection service to maintain Hct <0.45; volume and frequency individualised. Low-dose aspirin in confirmed PV unless contraindicated.
Step 2Cytoreduction (hydroxycarbamide, interferon or other) for high-risk PV (age ≥65 and/or previous thrombosis), or lower-risk patients with uncontrolled Hct, poor tolerance of venesection, progressive leucocytosis, marked thrombocytosis, symptomatic splenomegaly or troublesome symptoms — not on age alone. Initiated and dose-adjusted by haematology; GP prescribing only under the current GMMMG shared-care protocol after formal transfer of care.
Step 3Ruxolitinib (specialist prescribing) for adults with PV resistant or intolerant to hydroxycarbamide, per NICE TA921 criteria. JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor. Reduces splenomegaly, controls blood counts, improves pruritus.
Step 4Interferon — specialist option, particularly relevant in younger patients and pregnancy planning. A history of depression needs careful specialist risk assessment. Flu-like symptoms common.
SupportiveGout or clinically significant hyperuricaemia: treat per standard gout guidance and specialist advice — not allopurinol solely because uric acid is raised. Pruritus: antihistamines or other options per haematology advice.
Venesection
Venesection is arranged and performed through the haematology/venesection service. The volume and frequency are individualised, particularly in older patients and those with cardiovascular disease or anaemia.
Monitoring on treatment
Follow-up frequency is haematology-led (FBC around every 3 months may be reasonable once stable). On hydroxycarbamide: monitoring per the current GMMMG shared-care protocol and specialist instructions. Annual review: thrombosis/bleeding events, splenomegaly progression, symptom control (pruritus, headache), cardiovascular risk factors.
Pregnancy in PV
Pregnancy planning or pregnancy in known or suspected PV requires early specialist haematology and obstetric review (high-risk, joint care). Hydroxycarbamide and other cytoreductive treatments require specialist pre-conception advice per the current haematology protocol. Antithrombotic treatment, aspirin, LMWH, interferon and venesection are individualised.

Venesection to Hct <0.45 is the cornerstone of confirmed PV treatment (BSH; CYTO-PV trial), for both men and women. Venesection removes iron, limiting erythropoiesis; iron deficiency may follow, but a low ferritin is not itself a treatment target and can cause symptoms — do not give iron replacement in PV without discussion with haematology.

Low-dose aspirin is recommended in confirmed PV unless contraindicated (BSH). Consider active bleeding, peptic ulcer disease, anticoagulant treatment, acquired von Willebrand syndrome (extreme thrombocytosis), aspirin allergy/intolerance and pregnancy (specialist advice). PPI co-prescription if GI risk.

Hydroxycarbamide (hydroxyurea) is a first-line cytoreductive agent for high-risk PV (age ≥65 and/or previous thrombosis). It inhibits DNA synthesis, reducing Hct, platelets and WCC; the main side effect is myelosuppression. It is initiated and stabilised by haematology, with GP prescribing only under the GMMMG shared-care protocol after formal transfer.

Secondary polycythaemia treatment targets the underlying cause. Treating OSA and hypoxic lung disease may improve Hct. Specialist venesection is considered for hyperviscosity symptoms or Hct >0.56 (hypoxic lung disease), generally targeting 0.50–0.52. Routine venesection in asymptomatic secondary polycythaemia does NOT improve outcomes and may worsen tissue hypoxia.

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Hydration, Cardiovascular Risk Reduction, and Thrombosis Prevention

Cardiovascular risk-factor control and smoking cessation matter in both PV and secondary erythrocytosis; lifestyle advice supports, but does not replace, specialist treatment.

Hydration Avoid dehydration and maintain normal fluid intake, taking account of cardiac and renal comorbidity (heart failure, CKD, cirrhosis, hyponatraemia risk, fluid restriction). Do not prescribe a fixed fluid volume for all patients.
Smoking cessation Smoking worsens hypoxia (COPD), raises carboxyhaemoglobin (false Hb elevation), and increases thrombosis risk. Offer varenicline or NRT. Quitting may improve Hct.
Blood pressure control Treat per NICE hypertension guidance. Hypertension adds to vascular risk. Review diuretics, which can worsen relative polycythaemia.
Weight loss Target BMI <25 kg/m² (especially Gaisböck syndrome). Weight loss may improve relative polycythaemia and OSA severity (reducing EPO drive). Mediterranean-style diet; weight-management options per NICE obesity guidance.
Alcohol moderation Limit ≤14 units/week. Alcohol causes dehydration → worsens hyperviscosity. Binge drinking increases thrombosis risk. Alcohol + aspirin increases GI bleeding risk. Red wine antioxidants do NOT offset thrombosis risk in PV.
Travel and immobility During prolonged travel, maintain mobility, avoid dehydration and follow general VTE-prevention advice. Consider pharmacological prophylaxis only after individual risk assessment and specialist/local guidance — not on Hct alone. Regular daily walking.
OSA treatment Assess suspected OSA via the local sleep pathway; CPAP per NICE OSA guidance. Treating OSA improves nocturnal oxygenation and may reduce EPO-driven erythrocytosis. Mandibular advancement device if CPAP-intolerant. Weight loss improves OSA severity.
Altitude awareness Avoid high altitude (>2500 m) if possible (worsens hypoxia, raises Hct). Seek travel-medicine advice before high-altitude trips; avoid dehydration and alcohol.

Avoiding dehydration limits further rises in blood viscosity. Advice should be individualised — fixed fluid volumes are inappropriate for people with heart failure, CKD, cirrhosis, hyponatraemia risk or fluid restriction.

Smoking cessation is critical in secondary polycythaemia. Smoking worsens COPD hypoxia (↑ EPO), raises carboxyhaemoglobin (falsely elevated Hb), and damages endothelium (↑ thrombosis). Quitting may improve Hct and reduces vascular risk.

Weight loss may improve Gaisböck syndrome (plasma volume expands) and OSA severity, lowering EPO drive. A Mediterranean-style diet supports cardiovascular risk reduction.

CPAP therapy for OSA normalizes nocturnal SaO₂, reducing EPO secretion. This may reduce EPO-driven erythrocytosis. Good CPAP adherence is essential. Side effects (nasal congestion, mask discomfort) improve with humidifier and mask re-fitting.

9
Safety

Follow-Up — Monitor Hct Target and Safety-Net for Thrombosis

Monitor Hct trajectory, watch for thrombotic events, and re-refer if Hct rising or new symptoms. Chronic polycythaemia requires long-term surveillance.

Weekly (venesection phase)
FBC per the venesection service protocol. Iron deficiency may develop with venesection — a low ferritin is not a treatment target and can cause symptoms; do not give iron in PV without haematology discussion. Monitor for symptoms (headache, dizziness improving as Hct falls).
3 months (maintenance)
Follow-up frequency is haematology-led (FBC around every 3 months may be reasonable once stable). Target Hct <0.45 in confirmed PV. If Hct rising, inform haematology. Monitor platelets, WCC (thrombocytosis/leucocytosis suggests inadequate control, may need cytoreduction).
On cytoreduction
If on hydroxycarbamide: monitoring, targets and treatment-hold thresholds follow the current GMMMG shared-care protocol and specialist instructions (baseline FBC, renal and liver function; regular FBC). Watch for cytopenia and seek haematology advice.
Annual haematology review
For PV patients: Review thrombosis/bleeding events, symptom control (pruritus, headache), splenomegaly progression, cardiovascular risk factors. Assess for transformation (myelofibrosis: tear-drop cells, rising LDH; acute leukaemia: blasts on film).
Safety-net 999
Sudden severe headache, focal weakness, speech changes (stroke). Chest pain, breathlessness (MI, PE). Severe abdominal pain + ascites (Budd-Chiari). Unilateral leg swelling (DVT). Confusion, seizures (hyperviscosity).
Safety-net same-day
Rising Hct with hyperviscosity symptoms despite venesection. New or rapidly enlarging spleen (possible transformation). Extreme thrombocytosis with bleeding, neurological symptoms or planned surgery → urgent haematology discussion (possible acquired von Willebrand syndrome). TIA symptoms.
Re-referral criteria
Hct rising despite regular venesection (needs cytoreduction). Thrombotic event on treatment (medication escalation). Intolerance to hydroxycarbamide (switch to ruxolitinib/interferon). Pregnancy planning (early specialist haematology and obstetric review).

Hct target <0.45 for both men and women with confirmed PV (BSH; CYTO-PV trial). This target does not apply to secondary erythrocytosis, where specialist venesection (if needed) in hypoxic lung disease generally targets 0.50–0.52.

Thrombotic events on treatment indicate inadequate control or high-risk disease. If stroke/MI/DVT occurs despite Hct <0.45 + aspirin, haematology escalates to hydroxycarbamide or ruxolitinib, adds anticoagulation, or intensifies venesection frequency. Primary care role is early recognition and urgent referral.

Transformation risk: a minority of PV evolves to post-PV myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukaemia over many years. Signs: progressive splenomegaly, tear-drop cells, rising LDH, anaemia, thrombocytopenia. Blasts on the film suggest leukaemic transformation — refer urgently.

Regular cardiovascular review is important: PV carries increased cardiovascular risk, so control BP, lipids, diabetes, smoking and weight. QRISK underestimates risk in PV (does not include polycythaemia as variable).
