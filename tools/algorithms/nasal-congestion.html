Step 8 · Self-management & modifiable factorsGet the basics right

Step 9 · Review & safety-netReassess & spot the sinister
Review at 4–6 weeks if not improving on optimised treatment — check adherence/technique before escalating or referring. Immediate same-day hospital assessment for sinusitis with eye swelling/proptosis, painful/restricted eye movements, visual change or severe headache/neurology (orbital/intracranial spread); suspected button battery → 999. 1
Safety

Red Flags — CSF Leak, Malignancy & Orbital Complications

Persistent unilateral clear watery rhinorrhoea, particularly after head injury, skull-base surgery or a procedure, or with a salty/metallic postnasal taste Suspected CSF leak (traumatic or spontaneous). → Urgent same-day discussion with ENT/neurosurgery or emergency department assessment according to the local pathway. Fever, severe headache, meningism, confusion, reduced consciousness or neurological symptoms → treat as an emergency (possible meningitis or intracranial complication). Do not perform nasal instrumentation or delay transfer for beta-2 transferrin testing (arranged by the hospital pathway). Do not advise patients to repeatedly test the fluid themselves.
Unilateral nasal discharge (purulent or blood-stained) + unilateral nasal obstruction + facial pain + weight loss in adult Sinonasal malignancy (squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma — associated with hardwood dust/nickel exposure). → Persistent unilateral nasal obstruction, unilateral blood-stained or offensive discharge, recurrent unilateral epistaxis, facial pain, facial swelling, anosmia, a visible mass or cervical lymphadenopathy requires urgent ENT assessment to exclude structural disease, including malignancy (local pathway; NICE NG12 is not a general sinonasal symptom pathway). Imaging is usually arranged by ENT or the local suspected malignancy pathway — do not order both CT and MRI from primary care.
Periorbital redness, swelling, proptosis, reduced eye movement + fever + severe headache + preceding sinusitis Possible orbital or intracranial complication of sinusitis (orbital cellulitis; cavernous sinus thrombosis is a hospital diagnosis). → Periorbital/orbital swelling, proptosis, painful or restricted eye movements, diplopia, reduced visual acuity, severe headache, neurological signs or systemic toxicity require immediate same-day hospital assessment (NICE NG79). Use local emergency, ENT, ophthalmology and antimicrobial protocols. Do not delay transfer to arrange imaging or prescribe a predetermined antibiotic regimen.
Nasal obstruction + epistaxis + pulsatile headache + cranial nerve palsies in young male Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (rare). → Urgent ENT discussion/referral according to the local pathway; imaging via ENT unless agreed locally. Do not biopsy or manipulate a suspected vascular nasopharyngeal mass (risk of severe haemorrhage).
Child with unilateral purulent malodorous discharge from one nostril Foreign body in nose — assess. → Same-day assessment if the foreign body is not safely removable, is not visible, or there is significant bleeding, respiratory distress or aspiration risk. Avoid primary care removal where there is a risk of pushing the object posteriorly. Suspected button battery → immediate emergency assessment (999); do not attempt removal.
Nasal congestion + saddle-nose deformity + auricular chondritis + inflamed sclerae Relapsing polychondritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) or cocaine-induced midline destructive disease. → Destructive nasal disease, septal perforation, saddle-nose deformity, persistent crusting or epistaxis, pulmonary symptoms, renal abnormalities, ocular inflammation or auricular chondritis → urgent ENT/rheumatology assessment. Initial tests may include FBC, U&E/eGFR, CRP/ESR, urinalysis and ANCA with PR3/MPO where systemic vasculitis is suspected. Results must not be used to exclude GPA or to diagnose it in isolation.
CSF rhinorrhoea is a diagnosis that must not be missed — a traumatic or spontaneous dural tear allows cerebrospinal fluid to drain through the cribriform plate into the nasal cavity. The distinguishing features: watery, clear, unilateral discharge that worsens on bending forward or performing a Valsalva manoeuvre; salty taste; often associated with anosmia (if cribriform plate involved). The halo test (placing a drop of the discharge on filter paper — CSF will form a clear halo around a central bloody spot) has limited sensitivity. The gold standard test: beta-2 transferrin assay (a CSF-specific protein absent from blood, tears, and nasal secretions) — sample collection and testing are arranged through the hospital pathway, not at the initial GP consultation. The risks of untreated CSF rhinorrhoea: ascending bacterial meningitis (pneumococcal meningitis is the most feared complication), and tension pneumocephalus (air entering the intracranial space). Positioning advice (head elevation, not suppressing sneezing) is not sufficient management and must not delay transfer; antibiotic decisions belong to the specialist team.

Causes of Rhinorrhoea & Nasal Congestion — Classification

Classification (simplified)
1 Allergic rhinitis · 2 Non-allergic/irritant rhinitis · 3 Infective rhinitis/rhinosinusitis · 4 Structural disease · 5 Medication-induced rhinitis · 6 Inflammatory/systemic disease · 7 CSF leak or neoplastic disease
Allergic rhinitis (most common)
Type I hypersensitivity (IgE-mediated). Seasonal (SAR): tree pollen (Feb-May), grass pollen (May-July), weed pollen (Aug-Oct). Perennial (PAR): house dust mite (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus), cat/dog dander, mould. Symptoms: sneezing (often paroxysmal in clusters), bilateral watery rhinorrhoea, nasal itch, nasal congestion, associated conjunctivitis, post-nasal drip. Associated: asthma (common — united airway disease), eczema, food allergy.
Non-allergic rhinitis (NAR)
Several subtypes: Vasomotor rhinitis (autonomic — triggered by temperature change, smoke, perfume, alcohol, exercise); Hormonal (pregnancy — oestrogen increases nasal vasodilation; hypothyroidism is an uncommon, unproven association); Medication-induced (rhinitis medicamentosa from overuse of topical decongestants beyond 5 consecutive days (MHRA Drug Safety Update, April 2026); ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers — assess causality individually, as ACE inhibitors more often cause cough; respiratory reactions to aspirin/NSAIDs are a possible feature of AERD); Occupational (chemical exposure — isocyanates, latex, flour, wood dust).
Infective rhinitis
Acute viral URTI (rhinovirus most common). Acute bacterial sinusitis (secondary to viral — Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae): features increasing likelihood are symptoms >10 days without improvement, severe localised unilateral facial or dental pain, fever, and marked deterioration after initial improvement — discoloured discharge alone does not indicate antibiotics. Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS): a separate condition — symptoms >12 weeks + objective evidence of inflammation (see Step 4). Fungal sinusitis: uncommon, a specialist diagnosis rather than a routine GP diagnosis.
Structural causes
Nasal polyps (CRS with nasal polyposis — eosinophilic): bilateral translucent grape-like masses, anosmia, possible aspirin/NSAID sensitivity (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, AERD = nasal polyps + asthma + aspirin/NSAID respiratory reactions). A unilateral polyp or mass needs ENT assessment. Deviated nasal septum (DNS — unilateral obstruction). Adenoid hypertrophy (children — mouth breathing, snoring). Choanal atresia (neonate — respiratory distress feeding).
Inflammatory / systemic
GPA, relapsing polychondritis, cocaine-induced midline destructive disease — see Step 1.
CSF leak / neoplastic
Suspected CSF leak, sinonasal malignancy, angiofibroma — see Step 1.
Rhinitis medicamentosa (RM) is one of the most common and most easily preventable causes of chronic nasal obstruction seen in primary care — it is caused by the rebound vasodilation (tachyphylaxis) that follows prolonged use of topical alpha-adrenergic decongestants (oxymetazoline, xylometazoline, naphazoline). These preparations work by vasoconstricting the nasal turbinate vessels within 2-5 minutes; the problem is that repeated use causes progressively shorter duration of action and paradoxically worsening nasal congestion as the drug effect wears off, driving the patient to use the spray more and more frequently. The pharmacological mechanism: down-regulation of alpha-adrenergic receptors + rebound vasodilation mediated by reduced nitric oxide synthesis. The GP management: explain rebound congestion, stop the topical decongestant, use regular intranasal corticosteroid and saline, consider a staged withdrawal if abrupt cessation is not tolerated, and review if symptoms persist or the diagnosis is uncertain. The clinical history that identifies RM: patient using nasal decongestant spray more than twice daily for more than 5 consecutive days, without relief lasting more than 2-3 hours, and having started the spray for a common cold that resolved weeks ago.

Assessment — History & Examination

History
Onset and duration (acute <4 weeks = infective/allergic; chronic >12 weeks = structural/non-allergic). Seasonality (tree/grass/weed pollen timing). Triggers (specific allergens, occupational exposure, temperature, smoke, perfume). Symptom pattern: sneezing + watery rhinorrhoea + nasal itch = allergic; purulent discharge, facial pain, fever and double-worsening suggest sinusitis. Unilateral vs bilateral. Anosmia (nasal polyps, GPA). Current use of nasal sprays (decongestant overuse). Asthma association. Drug history: sprays, drops, oral decongestants and combination cold remedies; ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers (assess causality individually); NSAID/aspirin reactions, including wheeze or nasal symptoms after ingestion (AERD). Also ask about: recurrent epistaxis; facial swelling or dental pain; visual symptoms and eye-movement pain; headache severity and neurological symptoms; anosmia or altered smell; asthma control and exacerbations; cocaine use (destructive or crusting disease); pregnancy; occupational exposure and whether symptoms improve away from work; previous nasal trauma or surgery.
Examination
Anterior rhinoscopy: bilateral pale, mobile lesions may be consistent with polyps (document carefully; a unilateral mass should not be confidently labelled a polyp — ENT assessment), crusting, bleeding, septal perforation, turbinate hypertrophy (pale/boggy = allergic; red = infective), deviated nasal septum, purulent discharge (posterior drip). Periorbital: dark circles (allergic shiners), facial puffiness. Throat: post-nasal drip (mucus trickling down posterior wall). Chest (wheeze — united airway). Skin (eczema). Cervical lymphadenopathy (infective or malignant). Observations where infection or systemic disease is possible. Oral/dental assessment where facial or maxillary pain is present. Visual acuity and eye movements if facial/periorbital symptoms. Blood pressure and pulse if considering sympathomimetic decongestants. Urinalysis if GPA is a realistic differential.
Investigations
Usually none for uncomplicated allergic or viral rhinitis. Skin prick testing (or specific IgE serology — RAST/ImmunoCAP) if: allergy uncertain, immunotherapy planned, occupational rhinitis suspected. Nasal endoscopy / CT sinuses (through ENT — objective confirmation of suspected CRS, polyps, structural disease or persistent unilateral symptoms; not ordered routinely in primary care, and not for acute viral URTI). Spirometry / FeNO (according to the asthma pathway). Suspected CSF leak (urgent specialist testing, including beta-2 transferrin, via the hospital pathway). FBC, CRP/ESR, U&E/eGFR, urinalysis, ANCA with PR3/MPO (only when systemic vasculitis is clinically suspected — cannot diagnose or exclude GPA in isolation).
The united airway disease concept is clinically important for GP management of allergic rhinitis — allergic rhinitis and asthma commonly coexist. The reason: the upper and lower respiratory tract are anatomically and immunologically continuous — the same type I hypersensitivity response (mast cell degranulation, IgE-mediated) occurs in both nasal mucosa and bronchial mucosa in response to the same allergens (house dust mite, pet dander, pollen). The clinical implication: inadequately treated rhinitis worsens asthma control (post-nasal drip triggers bronchospasm; mouth breathing in nasal obstruction bypasses upper airway humidification and filtering; nasal nitric oxide production — which is bronchodilatory — is impaired). Every asthmatic patient should have their rhinitis formally assessed and treated. Review asthma control alongside rhinitis treatment.

Chronic Rhinosinusitis & Nasal Polyps

CRS diagnostic criteria (EPOS 2020 — international)
Duration >12 weeks (without complete resolution). TWO or more symptoms required: nasal blockage, nasal discharge (anterior or posterior drip), facial pain/pressure, reduced/absent smell. PLUS: objective evidence on endoscopy (polyps, mucopurulent discharge, oedema in middle meatus) OR CT sinuses (mucosal changes). Two subtypes: CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP — eosinophilic) and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP). In primary care, record as suspected CRS unless objective evidence (nasal endoscopy or CT) is available. Do not order CT routinely just because symptoms exceed 12 weeks. Persistent unilateral symptoms are not typical uncomplicated CRS — use the urgent unilateral pathway. EPOS 2020 is a specialist reference, not a substitute for the local ENT referral pathway.
Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD)
Nasal polyps + asthma + aspirin/NSAID respiratory reactions. Mechanism: COX-1 inhibition by aspirin diverts arachidonic acid metabolism to lipoxygenase pathway → excess leukotriene production → bronchospasm + nasal congestion. Suspected AERD should prompt specialist respiratory/ENT assessment. Aspirin challenge only in a specialist setting — never in primary care. Avoid aspirin or non-selective NSAIDs if there is a convincing history of respiratory reactions, unless specialist advice indicates otherwise; do not advise blanket avoidance without assessing the history and cardiovascular indications. Intranasal corticosteroid. Montelukast may be considered for coexisting asthma or specialist-managed AERD, not routinely for rhinitis alone (10 mg is the adult dose; age-specific dosing in children). MHRA warning: neuropsychiatric reactions (nightmares, mood/behaviour change, depression, suicidal ideation) — stop and review if new symptoms occur.
Biological therapy for severe CRSwNP
Dupilumab (IL-4Rα blocker): specialist-initiated. NICE TA1134 recommends it as add-on to intranasal corticosteroids for severe CRSwNP in adults when disease is not adequately controlled by systemic corticosteroids or sinus surgery, the patient has had at least one sinus surgery, SNOT-22 is at least 50, and the commercial arrangement applies. SNOT-22 scoring is a specialist assessment, not needed for every patient in primary care. Also treats coexisting asthma and eczema (shared type 2 inflammatory pathway). Reduces disease burden and may reduce the need for further surgery in selected patients, but response is variable. Other biologics (e.g. mepolizumab, omalizumab): check current NICE guidance and local commissioning — not generally available alternatives. MHRA: ocular adverse reactions — refer for persistent ocular symptoms or conjunctivitis on dupilumab.
The type 2 inflammation pathway is the unifying mechanism behind the triad of eosinophilic CRS with nasal polyps, atopic asthma, and atopic eczema — all three conditions are driven by Th2 lymphocyte activation, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 signalling, which drives eosinophil recruitment and IgE production. This shared pathway explains why dupilumab (a biologic that blocks IL-4Rα, the shared receptor subunit for both IL-4 and IL-13 signalling) simultaneously improves nasal polyps, asthma, and eczema when all three coexist. NICE TA1134 (2026) recommends dupilumab for severe uncontrolled CRSwNP and for severe atopic eczema (TA534) and for severe asthma (TA751). In a patient with all three conditions, dupilumab can potentially be prescribed for one indication while also benefiting the other two — this requires specialist coordination between ENT, respiratory, and dermatology. GPs should identify patients with this triple burden (CRSwNP + asthma + eczema) and refer to the appropriate specialist for biologic consideration.

Referral Pathways

Emergency / immediate hospital
Suspected orbital/intracranial complication: proptosis, painful/restricted eye movements, diplopia or reduced vision · Severe systemic illness or sepsis · Meningism, confusion, severe headache or neurological signs with possible CSF leak · Significant facial swelling or airway compromise · Suspected nasal button battery → 999 · Suspected foreign body with respiratory distress or aspiration risk
ENT (urgent referral, local pathway)
Persistent unilateral nasal obstruction · Unilateral blood-stained or offensive discharge · Recurrent unilateral epistaxis · Unilateral nasal mass · Persistent facial pain/swelling · Unexplained anosmia with unilateral symptoms · Suspected CSF leak (urgent same-day discussion) · Suspected angiofibroma · Destructive nasal disease or septal perforation · Possible sinonasal malignancy · Foreign body not visible or not safely removable (same-day)
ENT (routine)
Suspected CRS with persistent symptoms despite regular intranasal steroid + saline irrigation (timing per local ENT pathway — refer earlier for substantial polyps, severe anosmia, recurrent systemic steroid use or major obstruction) · Nasal polyps — functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) assessment · Significant DNS causing obstruction
Allergy / immunology
Immunotherapy consideration (allergen-consistent history, objectively confirmed sensitisation, troublesome symptoms despite appropriate treatment) · Suspected occupational rhinitis → occupational health or specialist respiratory/allergy service (assess for occupational asthma) · Severe atopic rhinitis + asthma + eczema (biologic consideration)
GP management
Allergic rhinitis: intranasal corticosteroid + antihistamine. Viral URTI: symptomatic treatment only. Rhinitis medicamentosa: stop decongestant + start intranasal corticosteroid. Acute sinusitis with symptoms around 10 days or less: no antibiotic; around 10 days or more without improvement: consider no antibiotic or a back-up prescription; immediate antibiotic only if systemically very unwell, serious illness or high risk of complications; if an antibiotic is needed, phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg QDS x 5 days (NICE NG79).
The delayed antibiotic prescription for acute rhinosinusitis is an important antimicrobial stewardship strategy — NICE NG79 (2017) recommends that GPs offer a 'delayed' or 'back-pocket' prescription for acute bacterial rhinosinusitis to patients who are not severely unwell, with instructions to use the prescription only if symptoms do not improve within 7 days or worsen significantly at any time. The rationale: most acute rhinosinusitis episodes are viral and resolve without antibiotics, commonly within 2–3 weeks; immediate antibiotics provide little benefit and cause harms including diarrhoea, thrush and rash. The features that increase the probability of bacterial sinusitis requiring antibiotics: symptoms worsening after initial improvement (the 'double-sickening' pattern), symptoms persisting beyond 10 days without improvement, fever, and severe localised unilateral facial or dental pain — discoloured discharge alone is not an indication. Saline nasal irrigation can be offered as self-care if the patient prefers it.

Treat

Allergic Rhinitis — Step-Up Treatment Ladder

Step 1 (mild intermittent)Oral second-generation (non-sedating) antihistamine, e.g. cetirizine or loratadine, or intranasal antihistamine (azelastine) — use intranasal when a rapid local effect is desired. Chlorphenamine is not a routine option: sedating and anticholinergic — warn about driving, alcohol and impairment. Decongestants are not routine first-line treatment.
Step 2 (moderate-severe persistent)Regular intranasal corticosteroid (INCS), e.g. mometasone, fluticasone or beclometasone — product and dose per product information and local formulary. Demonstrate technique. Benefit is not immediate and requires regular use — explain to patient. Continue during the relevant pollen season, or longer for perennial disease. NICE TA1045: oral/intranasal antihistamines first-line for mild-to-moderate intermittent or mild persistent symptoms; INCS for moderate-to-severe persistent symptoms or inadequate response. Pregnancy: see Step 8.
Step 3 (not controlled on INCS)1) Check diagnosis, adherence and technique. 2) Ensure adequate duration. 3) Consider INCS plus intranasal antihistamine. 4) Use a licensed combination spray where appropriate (Greater Manchester formulary: generic fluticasone/azelastine, with local criteria for combination sprays). 5) Intranasal ipratropium if watery rhinorrhoea is the predominant persistent symptom. Adding an oral antihistamine to INCS is not automatically the preferred next step. Montelukast only for coexisting asthma or specialist-managed AERD, not rhinitis alone — MHRA neuropsychiatric warning (see Step 4).
Step 4 (severe / uncontrolled)Allergen immunotherapy (AIT) — specialist-led; consider referral only when the clinical history is consistent with a specific allergen, sensitisation is objectively confirmed, symptoms remain troublesome despite appropriate treatment, and the specialist service considers the patient suitable. Subcutaneous (SCIT) or sublingual (SLIT). NICE TA1045: 12 SQ-HDM SLIT for moderate-to-severe persistent house-dust-mite allergic rhinitis, aged 12–65, with compatible history, positive sensitisation and persistent symptoms despite symptom-relieving treatment (not recommended for HDM allergic asthma in adults). NICE TA1087: 12 SQ-Bet SLIT for selected moderate-to-severe birch-homologous tree-pollen allergic rhinitis/conjunctivitis with positive sensitisation. Products (e.g. Grazax, Staloral) are not interchangeable — licensing, age range, allergen, NHS availability and commissioning differ. Benefits depend on allergen, product, age and service. Specialist considerations: first dose under medical supervision, asthma assessment before initiation, oral adverse effects, rare systemic reactions, possible eosinophilic oesophagitis, adherence and response review, treatment interruption advice.
Allergen immunotherapy (AIT) is the only disease-modifying treatment for allergic rhinitis — all other treatments (antihistamines, intranasal steroids, decongestants) are purely symptomatic. AIT works by inducing immune tolerance to the specific allergen through repeated low-dose exposure: subcutaneous injections (SCIT — monthly after updosing) or sublingual tablets/drops (SLIT — daily self-administered at home). Benefits and eligibility depend on the allergen, product, age and local service — do not promise sustained remission or reduced asthma risk. NICE TA1045 recommends 12 SQ-HDM SLIT for selected people aged 12–65 with moderate-to-severe persistent HDM allergic rhinitis, and TA1087 covers 12 SQ-Bet SLIT for selected birch-homologous tree-pollen allergy; products are not interchangeable and initiation is specialist-led. GPs should be aware that sublingual immunotherapy is available and refer appropriate patients (allergen-consistent history, objectively confirmed sensitisation, troublesome symptoms despite appropriate treatment) for specialist AIT consideration.

Treat

Sinusitis, Polyps & Non-Allergic Rhinitis Management

Acute viral URTI / rhinosinusitis
No antibiotics for viral URTI or acute sinusitis with symptoms around 10 days or less (acute sinusitis symptoms commonly last about 2–3 weeks). Symptom relief: paracetamol 1g QDS + ibuprofen 400 mg TDS (if no contraindication). Intranasal saline irrigation (NeilMed sinus rinse, Sterimar — isotonic) if preferred. Symptoms around 10 days or more: consider high-dose nasal corticosteroid for 14 days in adults and children aged 12 and over (NICE NG79). Topical xylometazoline/oxymetazoline: maximum 5 consecutive days (MHRA Drug Safety Update, April 2026); follow product information; caution in cardiovascular disease, hypertension, glaucoma, hyperthyroidism and prostatic symptoms. Oral sympathomimetics not routinely recommended. Steam inhalation: limited evidence but safe.
Acute bacterial rhinosinusitis (antibiotic indications)
NICE NG79: symptoms around 10 days or less → no antibiotic. Around 10 days or more without improvement → no antibiotic or a back-up prescription. Immediate antibiotic only if systemically very unwell, serious illness or high risk of complications. Purulent/discoloured discharge alone does not indicate antibiotics. If an antibiotic is indicated (adults): phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg four times daily for 5 days. Penicillin allergy/intolerance: doxycycline (not in pregnancy) or clarithromycin 500 mg BD x 5 days. Co-amoxiclav (625 mg TDS x 5 days) first choice if systemically very unwell/high risk, or second choice after deterioration on first-choice treatment. Analgesia alongside. Suspected orbital/intracranial complications → immediate hospital assessment; antibiotic choice per local hospital protocol.
Suspected CRS / nasal polyps — medical management
Mometasone 50 micrograms/spray, 2 sprays each nostril OD (200 micrograms daily), increased to BD if needed (higher dose than standard AR — BNF). Saline irrigation (reduces mucosal oedema + debris). Short courses of systemic corticosteroid may be used by ENT or respiratory specialists for selected patients with severe nasal polyposis or acute inflammatory exacerbation — not routine primary-care treatment for uncomplicated nasal congestion; consider cumulative steroid exposure, diabetes, hypertension, infection, osteoporosis, mood disorder and other contraindications. Repeated systemic steroids → specialist management, not serial GP rescue prescriptions. Refer ENT (timing per local pathway, not an automatic 3-month rule) for persistent symptoms, significant polyposis, severe anosmia, recurrent systemic steroid use, major obstruction, unilateral disease, structural complications, suspected malignancy or suspected AERD.
Non-allergic rhinitis (vasomotor)
Intranasal ipratropium (dose per UK product and local formulary; listed in the Greater Manchester formulary for non-allergic rhinitis) when watery rhinorrhoea is the dominant symptom — it does not treat obstruction or inflammation. Cautions: nasal dryness and epistaxis, glaucoma risk if sprayed into the eyes, urinary retention/prostatic symptoms, anticholinergic burden in older adults. Intranasal corticosteroid if mucosal hypertrophy present. Avoidance of triggers (cold air, smoke, perfume). Capsaicin nasal spray (Sinus Buster) — substance P depletion — for refractory vasomotor rhinitis. Rhinitis medicamentosa: explain rebound congestion; stop the topical decongestant (staged withdrawal if abrupt cessation not tolerated); regular INCS and saline; review if symptoms persist or the diagnosis is uncertain.
The nasal saline irrigation evidence for rhinosinusitis and CRS is stronger than many GPs appreciate — a Cochrane review (Chong 2016) found that nasal saline irrigation significantly reduces nasal symptoms, improves quality of life, and reduces medication use in CRS and acute rhinosinusitis, with minimal side effects. The mechanism: physical removal of thick mucus, allergens, and inflammatory mediators from the nasal mucosa; improvement in mucociliary clearance; reduction of nasal mucosal oedema. The standard preparation: 240 ml (8 oz) isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl) delivered at positive pressure via a neti pot or squeeze bottle into one nostril, allowing it to drain from the other. Hypertonic saline (2-3% NaCl) provides additional benefit for CRS. Commercial preparations (Sterimar, NeilMed, etc.) are convenient but expensive — patients can make their own saline at home (1/4 tsp non-iodised salt + 1/4 tsp bicarbonate of soda in 240 ml previously boiled and cooled water). Offer nasal irrigation where tolerated and preferred.

Lifestyle

Allergen Avoidance, Air Quality & Lifestyle Modification

House dust mite allergen avoidance Avoidance advice should be individualised and proportionate. Consider mattress and pillow encasings and regular hot washing of bedding (NICE NG149), but explain that clinical benefit from individual avoidance measures is variable.
Pet allergen reduction Complete pet removal from home is the most effective strategy but rarely accepted by patients. Reducing exposure is reasonable (e.g. keep pet out of the bedroom, wash hands after handling), but evidence for individual measures is limited. Cat allergen can persist in homes long after pet removal.
Outdoor pollen avoidance Monitor daily pollen count (Met Office UK pollen forecast). Use pollen forecasts and reduce exposure when symptoms are triggered. Consider keeping windows closed on high pollen days. Shower and change clothes after spending time outside. Wrap-around sunglasses to protect eyes. Avoid freshly cut grass. Dry clothes indoors on high pollen days.
Air quality and rhinitis Indoor air pollution (gas cooking, wood burning stoves, VOCs from paint/cleaning products) can worsen rhinitis. Ventilate cooking areas well (NICE NG149). Avoid scented candles and plug-in air fresheners. Evidence that air purifiers or HEPA filters reduce clinically important rhinitis symptoms is limited. Outdoor air quality: check UK DAQI (Daily Air Quality Index) — high pollution days worsen rhinitis even without infection.
Intranasal steroid technique Critical for efficacy: tilt head forward (not back — drug drains to throat instead of mucosa). Insert nozzle just inside nostril, aiming toward the outer nasal wall (not the septum — avoids epistaxis). Breathe in gently through the nose while spraying. Avoid forceful sniffing immediately after. Rinse nozzle weekly. Switch to alternate nostril each spray to reduce septal bleeding. Demonstrate technique at first prescription.
INCS and children Use the lowest effective dose and follow the licensed age and dose for the specific product — age limits vary, so check the current SPC/BNFC. Ask about other corticosteroid exposure, including inhaled, topical and oral corticosteroids. Monitor growth as part of routine paediatric care, particularly where prolonged or multiple corticosteroid treatments are used.
Occupational rhinitis management Identification of occupational allergen is critical (flour, latex, isocyanates, animal dander, wood dust). Ask whether symptoms improve on days away from work or during holidays. Document the suspected exposure and timing. Refer to occupational health or specialist respiratory/allergy services. Consider concurrent occupational asthma and objective lung-function assessment. Advise on exposure reduction. RIDDOR reporting is an employer/legal responsibility depending on diagnosis and circumstances — not a standard GP action; do not guarantee eligibility for Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit.
Rhinitis in pregnancy Hormonal rhinitis (oestrogen-mediated nasal vasodilation) is common in pregnancy, but do not assume it when there are red flags or unilateral symptoms. Prioritise saline irrigation and correct use of an intranasal corticosteroid when needed; check current product information and local maternity/pharmacy advice. Use antihistamines only after considering sedation and pregnancy-specific advice (chlorphenamine may be used when clinically appropriate but is sedating). Avoid routine oral sympathomimetic decongestants. Do not use prolonged topical decongestants (maximum 5 consecutive days, MHRA). The same principles apply during breastfeeding, with product-specific advice where needed.
The intranasal corticosteroid technique education is one of the most impactful interventions a GP can make for allergic rhinitis management — many patients with allergic rhinitis use their nasal spray incorrectly (tilting the head back rather than forward, aiming toward the septum, sniffing forcefully after spraying). Incorrect technique delivers drug to the posterior pharynx (causing throat irritation and systemic absorption) rather than the nasal mucosa, dramatically reducing efficacy and potentially causing septal bleeding. A 1-minute demonstration of correct technique (head tilted forward, aim away from septum, gentle sniff, breathe out through mouth) significantly improves outcomes. The NHS has a 'how to use your nasal spray' educational video available on the NHS website — GPs should signpost to this at the first INCS prescription.

Safety

Follow-Up & Monitoring

Allergic rhinitis on INCS
Review at 4-6 weeks if treatment is newly started or symptoms are troublesome: diagnosis, symptom control, technique, adherence, epistaxis or mucosal irritation, asthma control, need for escalation or referral. Annual review of every patient on INCS is unnecessary; monitor growth in children as part of routine care. Escalate to combined INCS+antihistamine if single-agent insufficient. Refer allergy if considering immunotherapy.
Nasal polyps on INCS
Review symptom response and adherence. If inadequate: ENT referral per local pathway — do not routinely prescribe an oral prednisolone rescue course without specialist input. Post-FESS: continue INCS post-operatively (reduces recurrence). Follow-up interval is set by the ENT team according to disease severity.
Rhinosinusitis after antibiotics
CRP is not routinely required. Review if symptoms significantly worsen, fail to improve, complications are suspected, an alternative diagnosis is possible, or there is no clinical response to antibiotics — reassess diagnosis, adherence, complications and need for hospital assessment; no automatic ENT referral or routine CT after treatment failure. Refer according to complications, persistent unilateral disease, suspected polyps or failure of appropriate management. Document: antibiotic duration, clinical response, any concerns about orbital/CNS complications.
After ENT referral
Ensure referral result received within 8 weeks (routine) or 2 weeks (urgent local pathway). If not received: chase proactively. Document outcome in clinical record.
Same-day return
Periorbital swelling, proptosis, painful/restricted eye movements, diplopia or reduced vision → immediate same-day hospital assessment · Button battery in nose (child) → 999 · Unilateral clear rhinorrhoea with fever, severe headache, meningism, confusion or neurological symptoms (possible CSF leak) → emergency
Within 2 weeks
No improvement on antibiotics → clinical reassessment (diagnosis, adherence, complications, need for hospital assessment) — not automatic ENT referral · Child with persistent unilateral nasal discharge (foreign body not excluded) → assess; same-day if not visible or not safely removable
Reviewing a patient whose sinusitis fails to improve or worsens matters for two reasons: (1) the diagnosis, adherence and need for second-choice treatment (co-amoxiclav after deterioration on first choice, NICE NG79) should be reassessed; and (2) complications of bacterial sinusitis (orbital cellulitis, meningitis, brain abscess) require immediate identification. Safety-netting for bacterial sinusitis must be explicit: if symptoms worsen rather than improve after 48 hours of antibiotics, or if periorbital swelling, visual changes, severe headache, or neck stiffness develop, the patient should attend A&E immediately. The complications of bacterial sinusitis are rare but can be life-threatening, and the GP who has given antibiotics needs to ensure the patient understands the red flag symptoms requiring emergency care.
