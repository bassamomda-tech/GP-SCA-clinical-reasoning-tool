Hyperkalaemia in adults — triage and diagnostic approach

A result on a screen, usually in a well patient. Decide the urgency from the level and the ECG together, exclude a spurious sample, find the cause, then protect the drugs that are protecting the kidney and heart.

AdultsLab resultK⁺ above 5.5v1.0 · Sep 2026
Core rule. K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L is a medical emergency at any level of wellness, and any ECG change potentially attributable to hyperkalaemia is an emergency at any potassium — arrange transfer, do not repeat the sample first. One classification throughout: mild 5.5–5.9 · moderate 6.0–6.4 · severe ≥6.5; 5.1–5.4 is above the local reference range, not hyperkalaemia. 6.0–6.4: ECG and same-day clinical assessment, with admission according to the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state. Two questions follow every raised result: is it real (haemolysis, a fist-clenched or delayed sample) and is there AKI. A normal ECG does not exclude severe hyperkalaemia. Act first on a critical value and confirm in parallel — never instead. Hospital treatment appears on this sheet only so you know what is coming and what to hand over; it is not for primary-care administration.
0
On seeing the result: is this an emergency now? Arrange emergency admission — 999 or immediate discussion with the acute team
K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L, however well the patient seems · any ECG change at any potassium — peaked or tented T waves, flattened or absent P waves, broad QRS, bradycardia, sine-wave pattern · palpitations, syncope, chest pain or new arrhythmia · muscle weakness, flaccid paralysis or paraesthesiae · features of AKI — oliguria, new confusion, volume depletion, rapidly rising creatinine · suspected rhabdomyolysis, crush injury, tumour lysis or massive haemolysis · known dialysis patient with a missed session. Do the ECG if it does not delay transfer, stop the culprit drugs, and hand over the potassium, the eGFR and the drug list.
1
Is the result real, and how urgent? A critical value is acted on first and confirmed in parallel
Could it be spurious? Haemolysis, fist clenching or tourniquet, delay or cold in transit, thrombocytosis or leucocytosis, sampling above a drip — ask the lab. A clean repeat is reasonable only below 6.5 in a well patient with no AKI and a normal ECG. Point-of-care or blood-gas potassium is usable for rapid decisions, confirmed by laboratory testing when safe; gas analysers may not flag haemolysis, so a discrepancy needs clinical correlation, not automatic dismissal. Urgency: severe ≥6.5, or any ECG change, significant symptoms, AKI or oliguria, missed dialysis, rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis or a rapid rise → emergency transfer, whatever the number; moderate 6.0–6.4 → ECG and same-day assessment, admission on the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state; mild 5.5–5.9 → primary care if asymptomatic, well and no AKI. A normal ECG never excludes severe hyperkalaemia. Is the kidney failing? Compare creatinine and eGFR with previous values — oliguria, volume depletion, sepsis, obstruction, or recent NSAID or contrast exposure makes this AKI, which changes the destination regardless of the potassium. What is the patient on? ACE inhibitor or ARB, spironolactone or eplerenone, amiloride, sacubitril–valsartan, potassium supplements, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, ciclosporin or tacrolimus, beta-blockers, digoxin — and potassium-containing salt substitutes.

Assessment and investigation Above 6.0 this needs an ECG and a face-to-face assessment
ECG: peaked T waves → flattened or absent P waves and PR prolongation → broad QRS → sine wave and asystole; also bradycardia and new arrhythmia. A new broad QRS may equally be bundle branch block, pacing or sodium-channel blockade — compare with a previous ECG, and read peaked T waves in multiple leads and in context. Clinical: volume status and BP with postural drop, pulse and rhythm, palpable bladder, muscle power and reflexes, capillary glucose, Addisonian picture. Bloods — conditional, not a panel. All confirmed hyperkalaemia: U&E and eGFR on a clean unclenched sample delivered promptly, bicarbonate, glucose, FBC, medication review, ECG by severity. Only on trigger: muscle injury or tumour lysis → CK, urate, phosphate, calcium, LDH via acute services · low sodium with hypotension → emergency adrenal assessment; cortisol only if it does not delay treatment · persistent hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate → renal assessment. Do not delay transfer to repeat a critical value, use a binder as emergency treatment, recheck at 2 weeks at ≥6.0, stop a mortality-reducing drug on one mild rise, or order aldosterone and renin routinely.

5
Cause classifier: spurious, shifted, retained, or loaded Only once the urgency is settled
Spurious, then drugsPseudohyperkalaemia: haemolysis, fist clenching or tourniquet, delay or cold in transit, thrombocytosis or leucocytosis, sampling above a drip. A clean repeat is acceptable only below 6.5 with no AKI and a normal ECG. Reduced excretion — drugs, the commonest real cause in primary care: ACE inhibitor, ARB, sacubitril–valsartan, spironolactone or eplerenone, amiloride, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, ciclosporin or tacrolimus, potassium supplements — often two or three together, plus a salt substitute the patient does not count as a medicine.
Kidney, endocrine, shift, load or releaseKidney: AKI, CKD 4–5 or obstruction — the question is always what changed (a new drug, dehydration, sepsis, obstruction) rather than the CKD itself. Endocrine: Addison's or hypoaldosteronism — hyperkalaemia with hyponatraemia, postural hypotension, fatigue, weight loss, pigmentation; type 4 RTA in diabetic CKD gives hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate and a normal anion gap. Shift: metabolic acidosis, insulin deficiency or DKA, beta-blockers, digoxin toxicity, severe hyperglycaemia — a high potassium in a hyperglycaemic unwell patient is a DKA question first. Load or release: rhabdomyolysis, crush injury, burns, haemolysis, tumour lysis, massive transfusion; dietary excess or supplements where excretion is already impaired. Potassium-based salt substitutes in CKD are a frequent and invisible cause. Do not conclude by default: "probably haemolysis" without asking the lab or at ≥6.5 · "chronic and stable" without comparing the trend · "diet" with a normal eGFR and no drugs — look again for adrenal or tubular causes; never treat the number alone when hyponatraemia or a low bicarbonate sits beside it.
6
Refer or seek advice routinely from this consultation Not same-day — the cases that leave by letter or advice-and-guidance; §7 is what primary care does today
Persistent or recurrent hyperkalaemia despite removing reversible contributors → nephrology (NG203) · hyperkalaemia limiting a needed ACE inhibitor, ARB or MRA → renal or cardiology advice on dose and on a potassium binder. The NICE criteria are specific: CKD 3b–5 or heart failure · confirmed K⁺ ≥6.0 mmol/L · not on dialysis · RAAS inhibition not optimised because of hyperkalaemia (TA623, TA599) — a potassium above 5.5 is not itself a reason to start one; initiation and prescribing follow local renal/cardiology arrangements and the Greater Manchester formulary or shared-care protocol · declining eGFR or proteinuria → nephrology · stable suspected Addison's or hypoaldosteronism → endocrinology with a 9 am cortisol (a crisis is an emergency, not a test) · suspected type 4 RTA → nephrology — fludrocortisone is a specialist decision, not a primary-care option · heart failure needing an MRA → the heart-failure team, not unilateral withdrawal.
7
The only decisions that belong in this consultation What primary care manages today — mild hyperkalaemia in a well patient
Review every drug — including the invisible ones — before changing a prognostic oneStop potassium supplements and potassium-containing salt substitutes (LoSalt and similar) — patients do not report these as medicines. Stop or switch trimethoprim, NSAIDs including over-the-counter ibuprofen, and heparin where possible; review amiloride and any MRA dose. ACE inhibitors, ARBs and MRAs reduce mortality in heart failure and slow CKD progression, so think before stopping: on the emergency or same-day pathway, withhold contributors pending assessment; for a stable mild rise, remove the reversible contributors first, assess AKI, diet and volume status, then consider dose adjustment or specialist advice. Do not make eGFR-based stop decisions in isolation — follow CKD and heart-failure guidance, seek renal or cardiology advice where the drug matters, and in heart failure or albuminuric CKD plan reintroduction rather than indefinite discontinuation. Give a personalised sick-day plan with explicit restart advice, not a blanket instruction; NSAIDs are generally avoided altogether rather than paused.
Diet, the driver, and the recheck rule — write it downDiet — individualised, substitution not exclusion. Start with potassium-containing salt substitutes (avoid entirely), potassium supplements and the concentrated sources the history identifies; do not reflexively restrict milk, pulses, nuts and vegetables. Boiling and discarding the water is a practical technique whose effect varies, not a fixed percentage; numeric targets and advanced-CKD advice come from the renal dietitian. Never rely on diet alone at ≥6.0. Driver: correct dehydration and constipation, treat infection, relieve obstruction, optimise glycaemic control. Assess hydration individually — no standard fluid target in CKD, heart failure, oliguria or suspected obstruction. Recheck: U&E within 3–5 days for a mild rise, sooner if a drug was changed, on a clean unclenched sample delivered promptly. Record and tell the patient what triggers what: ≥6.5, any ECG change, symptoms, AKI or a rapid rise → emergency transfer; 6.0–6.4 → ECG and same-day assessment. After hospital treatment rebound is expected — those treatments shift potassium rather than remove it, so check the discharge plan names a recheck interval. Name who is checking the result.
What not to doDo not repeat a K⁺ ≥6.5 instead of transferring. Do not start a potassium binder outside the NICE criteria and local shared care. Calcium resonium is not acute treatment — UKKA removed it from the acute protocol in 2023 (slow, unpredictable, risk of intestinal injury), and it is never a community substitute for admission. Do not give sodium bicarbonate routinely for hyperkalaemia. Do not initiate fludrocortisone. Do not stop long-term steroids abruptly. Do not file a raised potassium as "borderline" without a plan and a named reviewer — and a critical result must never sit in an inbox or a routine repeat queue overnight.

Endpoint and documentation Finish with one of four conclusions
Why primary-care management is safe today: K⁺ 5.5–5.9 · asymptomatic and well · no AKI · ECG normal · a modifiable cause identified and acted on · sodium, bicarbonate and glucose reviewed · repeat booked within 3–5 days with a named reviewer and written escalation thresholds. Safety-net wording: contact us or 111 the same day for palpitations, feeling faint or blacking out, new muscle weakness or numbness, being unable to keep fluids down, or passing much less urine; come for the repeat even if you feel well — a high potassium causes no symptoms until it affects the heart. Remote and out-of-hours rule. 6.0 or above cannot be managed by telephone — it needs an ECG and face-to-face assessment the same day; convert to emergency assessment where an ECG cannot be obtained today or the patient cannot attend. Safety

Red Flags — Life-Threatening Hyperkalaemia & Pseudohyperkalaemia

Adult primary-care algorithm. IV treatment is hospital-only and appears in step 6 for reference — it is not a primary-care instruction.

One rule, everywhere: if K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L, there are ECG changes, significant symptoms, AKI or oliguria, or a rapidly rising potassium — initiate emergency transfer and do not wait for repeat confirmation. Obtain a repeat or point-of-care potassium in parallel where that does not delay treatment; a critical potassium is never converted into a routine repeat-blood task, and must not remain in an inbox or a routine repeat queue overnight. Categories used throughout: mild 5.5–5.9 · moderate 6.0–6.4 · severe ≥6.5 mmol/L; 5.1–5.4 is above the local reference range, not "mild hyperkalaemia". A normal ECG does not exclude severe hyperkalaemia.

K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L on any sample Severe hyperkalaemia — risk of ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest at any moment. Immediate emergency admission / ambulance transfer. Do not wait for repeat testing. Perform an ECG only if immediately available and it does not delay transfer. Do not attempt IV calcium in routine primary care unless an explicit local emergency protocol, equipment and trained, authorised staff are available — the decision to give calcium belongs to the receiving emergency or acute team.
ECG changes at any potassium level New broad QRS, bradyarrhythmia, sine-wave pattern, PR prolongation, loss of P waves, or peaked T waves (read in multiple leads and in clinical context) potentially attributable to hyperkalaemia — treat as an emergency. A broad QRS may also be bundle branch block, ventricular pacing or sodium-channel blockade, so read it against the clinical picture and any previous ECG. Emergency transfer; hospital treatment is set out in step 6 for reference.
Significant symptoms at any potassium level Muscle weakness, paralysis, palpitations, syncope — neuromuscular toxicity, with ascending flaccid weakness that can reach the respiratory muscles. Emergency transfer. Any symptom attributable to hyperkalaemia is a life-threatening emergency, whatever the number.
AKI, oliguria or anuria — and the other escalation triggers Renal failure driving the potassium, which cannot be excreted; the risk compounds rapidly. Also escalate for a missed dialysis session, rhabdomyolysis or tumour lysis, and for a rapidly rising potassium. Emergency transfer. Withhold contributing drugs pending acute assessment — ACE inhibitor, ARB, MRA, potassium-sparing diuretic, NSAID, trimethoprim, potassium supplements and potassium-containing salt substitutes — and record what was withheld in the handover.
Suspected digoxin toxicity Not every incidental potassium rise on long-term digoxin. If toxicity is suspected — particularly with arrhythmia, bradycardia, gastrointestinal, visual or neurological symptoms, or significant hyperkalaemia — arrange emergency assessment with an ECG, a digoxin concentration and urgent toxicology or acute medical advice. Chronic stable digoxin use with a mild incidental rise is assessed on the usual criteria above. Calcium is neither categorically contraindicated nor inevitably harmful here — the receiving team follows the relevant toxicology and resuscitation protocol.
Pseudohyperkalaemia — only where no red flag applies Haemolysed sample, delayed processing or a prolonged tourniquet; the commonest cause of apparent hyperkalaemia in primary care. Repeat with free-flow venepuncture (no tourniquet, or released after vein entry), minimal fist clenching, prompt processing under 30 minutes and no transit delay. Repeat first only when the patient is asymptomatic, the ECG is normal and none of the rows above applies — otherwise transfer, and confirm in parallel.
The cardiac membrane stabilisation principle of calcium gluconate is one of the most important emergency pharmacology facts in primary care — IV calcium gluconate does NOT lower the serum potassium. Its mechanism is to raise the threshold potential of the cardiac myocyte membrane, reducing the depolarisation risk from the elevated extracellular K⁺. It buys time while definitive K⁺-lowering measures are started, and the patient still has hyperkalaemia afterwards, so K⁺-lowering treatment remains mandatory. This is hospital pharmacology, given in a monitored environment by staff trained and authorised to administer IV calcium, at the dose and preparation set by the local UKKA-aligned protocol (step 6). In primary care the action is immediate transfer, not improvised IV treatment. Pseudohyperkalaemia is responsible for a substantial proportion of 'hyperkalaemia' results in primary care — estimated at 20–40% of K⁺ results above 5.5 mmol/L. The mechanism is potassium released from red blood cells during haemolysis in the sample tube, which can be caused by: prolonged tourniquet use (avoid or release after vein entry), repeated fist clenching (dramatically increases local K⁺), delayed sample processing (red cell K⁺ leaks into plasma over time), mechanical trauma during venepuncture, and extreme thrombocytosis or leucocytosis (release from platelets/WBCs — check FBC). The clinical implication is that a single asymptomatic K⁺ of 5.5–6.4 mmol/L in a patient without CKD, without precipitating drugs, and with a haemolysed or borderline sample can reasonably be repeated before acting. That logic applies only when no red flag is present — at 6.5 or above, or with ECG changes, significant symptoms, AKI or oliguria, or a rapidly rising potassium, transfer is arranged first and confirmation happens in parallel. A critical result must not remain in an inbox or a routine repeat queue overnight.

Classification — Causes of True Hyperkalaemia

Reduced renal excretion (most common overall cause)
CKD/AKI: reduced glomerular filtration → reduced K⁺ excretion. CKD stage 3b+ significant risk; stage 4–5 = major risk. Drugs impairing renal K⁺ excretion: ACE inhibitors (reduce angiotensin II → reduce aldosterone → reduce K⁺ excretion), ARBs (same mechanism), K⁺-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, eplerenone — aldosterone antagonists; amiloride, triamterene — direct ENaC blockade), NSAIDs (reduce prostaglandin-mediated renin release → reduce aldosterone), trimethoprim (blocks ENaC — same mechanism as amiloride at high doses — relevant in prophylactic dose for pneumocystis), pentamidine.
Mineralocorticoid deficiency
Addison's disease (primary adrenal insufficiency): aldosterone deficiency → K⁺ retention + Na⁺ loss + hypotension. Classic electrolytes: hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia. Pigmentation, postural hypotension, fatigue, weight loss. Short synacthen test. Hyporeninaemic hypoaldosteronism (type 4 RTA): diabetic nephropathy most common cause — low renin → low aldosterone without adrenal pathology. Heparin: suppresses aldosterone synthesis (rare, dose-dependent).
Transcellular shift (K⁺ moves from intracellular to extracellular)
Acidosis (metabolic or respiratory): H⁺ enters cells, K⁺ exits. Each 0.1 unit fall in pH → approximately 0.5–0.6 mmol/L rise in K⁺. Diabetic ketoacidosis: total body K⁺ depleted but serum K⁺ high (acidosis-driven shift) — must supplement K⁺ during insulin treatment as K⁺ will fall precipitously. Insulin deficiency: insulin drives K⁺ into cells — absence = K⁺ exits. Beta-blockers: block beta2-mediated K⁺ uptake into cells. Digoxin toxicity: inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Succinylcholine: depolarising muscle relaxant.
Excessive K⁺ intake (rare cause alone)
K⁺ supplements (oral or IV if excess), K⁺-containing salt substitutes (LoSalt, Miso, mineral-rich alternatives — significant K⁺ content), blood transfusions (stored blood has high K⁺ from cell lysis), rhabdomyolysis (massive cell K⁺ release), tumour lysis syndrome (chemotherapy-induced cell death).
Drug review — high-yield causes
ACEi/ARB + spironolactone/eplerenone combination (heart failure triple therapy — very high risk) · NSAIDs (impair renal K⁺ excretion + worsen CKD) · Trimethoprim (at high prophylactic doses) · Heparin (LMWH > UFH) · Ciclosporin/tacrolimus (renal vasoconstriction) · K⁺ supplements in CKD · Digoxin toxicity
The ACEi/ARB + spironolactone triple therapy combination for heart failure is the highest-risk prescribing scenario for hyperkalaemia in primary care — RALES trial (spironolactone + ACEi in CHF) showed 30% reduction in mortality but a 2% rate of serious hyperkalaemia. When ARBs are added to ACEi + spironolactone (quadruple RAAS blockade, sometimes seen in complex heart failure management), the hyperkalaemia risk is very high. Guidelines recommend monitoring K⁺ and eGFR 1–2 weeks after each dose change and at least every 4–6 months in stable patients. The trimethoprim hyperkalaemia mechanism is underappreciated — trimethoprim at the high doses used for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis (960 mg OD) structurally resembles amiloride and blocks the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the distal nephron with the same mechanism as K⁺-sparing diuretics, causing meaningful K⁺ retention. This is clinically significant in patients on trimethoprim prophylaxis who also have CKD, are taking ACEi/ARB, or have other K⁺-elevating risk factors. K⁺-containing salt substitutes (LoSalt and similar products contain approximately 350 mg of K⁺ per gram — compared to approximately 0 mg in regular NaCl) are frequently recommended by dietitians and cardiologists for hypertension management without specific warning to patients with CKD or on ACEi/ARB. These products can cause dangerous hyperkalaemia in at-risk patients. Asking about salt substitute use should be part of the dietary history in any patient with hyperkalaemia.

Investigations & ECG Interpretation

Repeat K⁺ — but never before transfer
Confirm before acting only when no red flag applies; at K⁺ ≥6.5, with ECG changes, significant symptoms, AKI or oliguria, or a rapidly rising potassium, transfer first and confirm in parallel. Free-flow venepuncture: no prolonged tourniquet, no fist clenching, prompt processing under 30 minutes, non-haemolysed sample. Request "urgent". Plasma K⁺ (lithium heparin) is preferred to serum (SST) — less pseudohyperkalaemia artefact. Point-of-care or blood-gas potassium may be used for rapid decision-making; confirm with a laboratory sample when clinically safe. Blood-gas analysers may not reliably flag haemolysis, so a discrepancy needs clinical correlation, not automatic dismissal of either value.
ECG
In confirmed K⁺ ≥5.5, and at ≥6.0 before any decision to manage in the community. Mild (5.5–5.9): tall, narrow, symmetrical peaked T waves — an early sign, best seen V2–V4, and interpreted in multiple leads and in clinical context rather than as a standalone diagnosis. Moderate (6.0–6.4): PR prolongation, P-wave flattening, broadening QRS. Severe (≥6.5): sine-wave pattern, loss of P waves, junctional rhythm, VF, asystole. New broad QRS, bradyarrhythmia, sine-wave pattern or another ECG abnormality potentially attributable to hyperkalaemia is an emergency — but a broad QRS may equally be bundle branch block, ventricular pacing or sodium-channel blockade, so compare with a previous ECG. At ≥6.5 the ECG must not delay transfer, and a normal ECG does not exclude severe hyperkalaemia.
Investigations — conditional, not a panel
All confirmed hyperkalaemia: U&E + eGFR bicarbonate glucose FBC (thrombocytosis or leucocytosis causing pseudohyperkalaemia), a medication review, and an ECG according to severity. Only if the trigger is present: muscle injury or tumour lysis → CK, urate, phosphate, calcium, LDH via acute services · hyponatraemia, hypotension or pigmentation → emergency adrenal assessment; cortisol only if it does not delay treatment · persistent unexplained hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate → renal assessment for type 4 RTA or hypoaldosteronism, where aldosterone and renin are specialist-directed. Do not order the whole list routinely — it generates inappropriate testing and distracts from transfer.
Risk stratification — one set of categories
Emergency transfer Severe: K⁺ ≥6.5, or ECG changes, significant symptoms, AKI or oliguria, missed dialysis, rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, or a rapidly rising potassium — at any level. Do not wait for a repeat · Same-day Moderate: K⁺ 6.0–6.4 — ECG and same-day clinical assessment; admission according to the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state · Primary care with review Mild: K⁺ 5.5–5.9, asymptomatic, normal ECG, confirmed on repeat — reversible contributors, AKI assessment, hydration and diet reviewed, with a named reviewer, a repeat date and a written escalation threshold · K⁺ 5.1–5.4 is above the local reference range rather than hyperkalaemia — recheck in context, review contributors in CKD or on RAAS therapy
The ECG changes of hyperkalaemia follow a predictable progression from mild to severe, and the QRS width is the single most important parameter for urgency — a widened QRS (>120 ms) from hyperkalaemia indicates that the ventricular conduction system is critically compromised, and VF or cardiac arrest can occur within minutes. This makes a new broad QRS more urgent than the absolute number: a K⁺ of 6.0 with a newly broad QRS is more immediately dangerous than a higher level with an unchanged narrow QRS — provided the broadening is genuinely new, since bundle branch block, pacing and sodium-channel blockade produce the same appearance. The peaked T waves are the earliest ECG sign and can appear in the mild range (5.5–5.9) — they are tall, narrow, and symmetrical, best seen in the precordial leads (V2–V4), and are often described as 'tented' (pointed rather than rounded apex). This contrasts with the tall broad asymmetric T waves of myocardial ischaemia. The sine wave pattern (QRS and T wave fusing into a continuous oscillating waveform) represents an immediately pre-arrest rhythm — any patient with a sine wave ECG should receive IV calcium gluconate immediately and 999 should be called if in primary care. The risk stratification table is important for primary care decision-making — the dividing lines between 'phone 999,' 'send to hospital same day,' and 'urgent next-day review' are based on K⁺ level, ECG status, symptoms, and renal function. A K⁺ of 6.2 with a haemolysed sample, a normal ECG and no symptoms in a patient with CKD 3b warrants same-day assessment with a repeat rather than an ambulance — but the clinician must be certain the ECG is genuinely normal and the patient genuinely well, and a normal ECG does not exclude severe hyperkalaemia. At 6.5 or above, or with any red flag, the repeat happens in parallel with transfer, never instead of it.

Drug Review & Dietary Assessment

Systematic drug review
List every current medication and check each against K⁺-elevating risk. High-risk combinations: ACEi + ARB (dual RAAS blockade — avoid in routine practice), ACEi/ARB + K⁺-sparing diuretic (spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride), ACEi/ARB + NSAIDs (very common combination — NSAIDs impair renal K⁺ excretion via prostaglandin suppression + reduce GFR → less K⁺ filtered). Consider dose reduction or cessation of offending drug — weigh benefit vs risk (e.g., spironolactone in resistant hypertension may be more beneficial than its K⁺ risk warrants withdrawal).
Dietary potassium assessment
Start with the high-yield sources, not a long exclusion list. Potassium-containing salt substitutes (LoSalt, Nu-Salt and similar) — ask at every hyperkalaemia consultation; they are the single most commonly missed cause and should be avoided in CKD or on RAAS therapy. Then potassium supplements, and any concentrated sources the history actually points to — tomato concentrate, dried fruit, large amounts of fruit juice or chocolate. Advice is individualised and framed around substitution rather than broad exclusion: healthy foods such as milk, pulses, nuts and vegetables should not be restricted reflexively. Advanced CKD needs renal dietetic input rather than a generic list.
Renal function review
Potassium rises progressively from CKD stage 3b. Do not make eGFR-based stop decisions in isolation — ACE inhibitors and ARBs are often appropriately continued at low eGFR, and the decision depends on the indication, albuminuria, the potassium, the change in creatinine, blood pressure and specialist context. Follow CKD and heart-failure guidance, and seek renal or cardiology advice where RAAS therapy is clinically important. AKI: any acute potassium rise with a rising creatinine is AKI until proved otherwise — look for dehydration, obstruction, nephrotoxins and infection.
Context assessment
Diabetes: insulin deficiency + renal insufficiency = highest-risk combination. DKA: K⁺ high despite total body depletion — monitor K⁺ hourly during insulin treatment (will fall sharply). Type 4 RTA (diabetic nephropathy): hyporeninaemic hypoaldosteronism — K⁺ chronically elevated despite only moderate CKD. Adrenal: Addison's = bilateral adrenal failure. Hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia = Addison's pattern — check morning cortisol.
The ACEi + ARB + spironolactone combination (sometimes called 'triple RAAS blockade') is explicitly discouraged in most UK guidelines because of the high risk of hyperkalaemia and AKI without additional cardiovascular benefit compared to optimal single-agent RAAS blockade. NICE guidance on CKD (NG203) specifically states that dual RAAS blockade (ACEi + ARB together) should not be used. The prescribing scenario that commonly creates triple RAAS blockade is: a GP prescribes ACEi for hypertension/heart failure, a cardiologist adds spironolactone for resistant hypertension or heart failure, and the patient also self-medicates with ibuprofen — neither prescriber sees the complete picture. Medication reconciliation at each consultation (reviewing the full drug list including OTC medications) is the single most important preventive action for iatrogenic hyperkalaemia. The salt substitute issue deserves special attention because it creates a false sense of safety: patients with CKD or heart failure who have been advised to reduce salt intake appropriately switch to salt substitutes — which contain potassium chloride instead of sodium chloride and can deliver several grams of potassium per day. A patient with CKD-4 on spironolactone who uses LoSalt liberally and eats a high-potassium diet can have K⁺ levels that are genuinely dangerous. This dietary history question takes 30 seconds to ask but is rarely included in hyperkalaemia assessments.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Emergency transfer
K⁺ ≥6.5 on any sample — do not wait for repeat testing · any ECG change potentially attributable to hyperkalaemia at any level (new broad QRS, bradyarrhythmia, sine wave, PR prolongation, loss of P waves, peaked T waves in context) · significant symptoms — weakness, paralysis, palpitations, syncope · AKI, oliguria or anuria · missed dialysis, rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis · a rapidly rising potassium · suspected digoxin toxicity (arrhythmia, bradycardia, GI, visual or neurological symptoms). ECG only if immediately available and it does not delay transfer. Withhold contributing drugs and record what was withheld
Same-day assessment
Moderate: K⁺ 6.0–6.4 — ECG and same-day clinical assessment; admission according to the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state · K⁺ 5.5–5.9 with a rising creatinine · hyperkalaemia with hypotension and hyponatraemia — treat a suspected adrenal crisis as an emergency in its own right, ahead of any cortisol test · unable to obtain an ECG or reliable follow-up with a confirmed K⁺ ≥5.5
Nephrology / cardiology
CKD with persistent hyperkalaemia despite removal of reversible contributors · where RAAS therapy is clinically important and hyperkalaemia is limiting it — heart failure, albuminuric CKD · potassium-binder consideration where the NICE criteria are met (CKD 3b–5 or heart failure, confirmed K⁺ ≥6.0, not on dialysis, RAAS inhibition not optimised because of hyperkalaemia) · persistent unexplained hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate — type 4 RTA or hypoaldosteronism · pre-dialysis assessment. Do not make eGFR-based stop decisions in isolation.
Endocrinology
Suspected adrenal crisis is an emergency, not a test request — hypotension with hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia goes for emergency assessment, and cortisol is taken only if it does not delay treatment. Routine endocrine referral is for stable suspected Addison's (pigmentation, postural symptoms) for a short synacthen test, and for specialist assessment of hypoaldosteronism — aldosterone and renin are specialist-directed, not routine primary-care tests.
Renal dietitian
CKD with dietary potassium genuinely contributing — advanced CKD needs renal dietetic input rather than a generic restriction, and any target is set by that service, not applied universally. Clarify salt substitute use. Advice is framed around substitution rather than broad exclusion, so healthy foods are not restricted unnecessarily.
Patiromer (Veltassa) and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (Lokelma) are two novel K⁺-binding agents recommended by NICE (TA623 for patiromer in CKD, TA599 for SZC) that represent a significant advance in chronic hyperkalaemia management in primary care. Unlike the older polystyrene sulphonate resins — which UKKA removed from the acute protocol in 2023 because their efficacy is slow and unpredictable and they carry important gastrointestinal risks including intestinal injury and necrosis — patiromer and SZC are tolerated oral preparations designed for long-term outpatient use. They work by binding K⁺ in the gastrointestinal tract and preventing its absorption, providing a sustained K⁺-lowering effect over days to weeks. Clinical significance: they allow patients who could not otherwise tolerate optimal RAAS blockade for heart failure or CKD to remain on prognostically important medication. But the NICE criteria are specific — selected adults with CKD 3b–5 or heart failure, confirmed K⁺ ≥6.0 mmol/L, not on dialysis, where hyperkalaemia is limiting optimised RAAS-inhibitor therapy (TA623, TA599). The eligibility criteria matter as much as the drugs. NICE recommends them in defined settings — selected adults with CKD stage 3b–5 or heart failure, confirmed K⁺ ≥6.0 mmol/L, not on dialysis, where hyperkalaemia is limiting optimised RAAS-inhibitor therapy — so a potassium above 5.5 is not itself a reason to start one. Initiation and prescribing responsibility follow local renal or cardiology arrangements and the current Greater Manchester formulary or shared-care protocol, rather than generic prescribing from the algorithm. Neither is emergency treatment: SZC's hospital correction phase is typically 10 g three times daily for up to 72 hours, and patiromer does not act quickly enough to be an emergency option at all.

Treat

Hospital acute treatment — reference only, not for primary-care administration

⚠ Hospital treatment only — not for routine primary-care administration. This module is here so you know what the receiving team is likely to do, what to hand over, and when to contact renal or critical care. Every dose and rate below follows the local UKKA-aligned protocol, given in a monitored environment by staff trained and authorised to administer it. In primary care the action is immediate transfer.

What to hand overThe potassium and its trend and timing, whether the sample was haemolysed, renal function and urine output, the ECG if one was done, the full drug list and which drugs were withheld, dialysis status and last session, comorbidity (heart failure, CKD, diabetes), and any suspected precipitant — AKI, rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, adrenal insufficiency, digoxin toxicity.
1. Calcium — cardioprotectionGiven only in an appropriately monitored clinical environment by staff trained and authorised to give IV calcium. The UKKA adult hospital algorithm uses 10% calcium gluconate 30 mL IV over 10 minutes, or 10% calcium chloride 10 mL IV, according to the local protocol and the access available — calcium chloride is more irritant and is generally reserved for large-bore or central access and for cardiac arrest. Hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest follows the resuscitation protocol, which is a separate pathway. Calcium does not lower the potassium — it raises the myocyte threshold potential and buys time, so K⁺-lowering treatment must follow. Do not attempt IV calcium in routine primary care unless an explicit local emergency protocol, equipment and trained staff are available.
2. Insulin–glucose — and the hypoglycaemia regimen10 units soluble insulin IV with 25 g glucose — equivalently 50 mL of 50%, 125 mL of 20% or 250 mL of 10% glucose. Check the glucose immediately before treatment. If the pre-treatment glucose is below 7.0 mmol/L, follow with 10% glucose at 50 mL/hour for 5 hours (UKKA). Monitor glucose at baseline and at 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300 and 360 minutes — six hours, because hypoglycaemia occurs after the bolus has been metabolised while the insulin is still acting. This is the most reliably effective acute K⁺-lowering treatment.
3. Salbutamol nebuliserSalbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised — a beta-2 agonist that drives K⁺ into cells, additive to insulin–glucose and given alongside it. Causes tachycardia; less reliable in patients already on a beta-blocker. It is an adjunct, not a substitute for insulin–glucose.
4. Sodium bicarbonate — not routineDo not use sodium bicarbonate routinely for hyperkalaemia. Consider it only for selected patients with significant metabolic acidosis, or in hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest, according to the local renal, critical-care or resuscitation protocol. No fixed volume applies — a standard 500 mL may be hazardous in heart failure, renal failure or fluid overload, and concentrated bicarbonate has a role in selected resuscitation protocols under specialist guidance.
5. Potassium binders — hospital adjunctDo not use calcium resonium as a substitute for emergency treatment or as routine acute therapy — UKKA 2023 removed it from the acute protocol; its efficacy is slow and unpredictable and it carries important gastrointestinal risks including intestinal injury and necrosis. Consider current potassium binders only in accordance with the hospital protocol and specialist decision-making. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate may be considered alongside standard emergency treatment per the local acute protocol — it does not replace calcium where indicated, or insulin–glucose. Patiromer is not an immediate emergency potassium-lowering treatment. Outpatient use is a separate question — see step 7.
6. Dialysis — escalate earlyContact renal or critical care early if severe hyperkalaemia is refractory or recurrent, or associated with anuria or oliguria, severe AKI or advanced renal failure, severe metabolic acidosis, fluid overload, ongoing potassium release (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis), failure of initial medical treatment, or hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest, or in a dialysis patient with significant hyperkalaemia — and whenever definitive potassium removal is likely to be needed. Do not wait for K⁺ above 7.0 mmol/L.
7. After treatment — reboundPotassium at approximately 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 hours after moderate or severe treatment; glucose monitored to 6 hours after insulin–glucose. Insulin, salbutamol and bicarbonate shift potassium into cells rather than removing it, so rebound is expected unless the cause is corrected or the potassium is removed — clear re-treatment and escalation thresholds must be documented before step-down.
The insulin-dextrose infusion is the cornerstone of acute hospital hyperkalaemia management and works via a beautifully simple physiological mechanism — insulin activates the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump on skeletal muscle cells, driving K⁺ from the extracellular space into the intracellular compartment. This is why hyperkalaemia is a key feature of diabetic ketoacidosis (absent insulin) despite total body K⁺ depletion (K⁺ cannot enter cells without insulin). The dextrose is given simultaneously to prevent the hypoglycaemia that the exogenous insulin would otherwise cause. The glucose monitoring protocol after insulin–glucose is mandatory because hypoglycaemia occurs after the glucose bolus has been metabolised while the insulin is still acting — which is why UKKA specifies monitoring to six hours and a follow-on 10% glucose infusion at 50 mL/hour for 5 hours when the pre-treatment glucose is below 7.0 mmol/L. The salbutamol additive effect is clinically important — the combination of insulin-dextrose + salbutamol is superior to either alone for acute K⁺ reduction, and both treatments can be administered simultaneously in the emergency department. The total K⁺ reduction from combined insulin + salbutamol is approximately 1.0–2.0 mmol/L — which is often sufficient to convert an immediately dangerous ECG pattern to a manageable one while dialysis is arranged if needed. GPs need these treatments in order to hand over well and to explain them — not to administer them. The insulin and glucose are being given to move potassium into cells, not to manage diabetes. Note too that insulin, salbutamol and bicarbonate all shift potassium rather than removing it, so the level rebounds unless the cause is corrected or dialysis removes it — the reason early renal or critical-care contact matters more than any particular number.

Treat

Primary Care Management — Chronic Hyperkalaemia

Above the reference range (K⁺ 5.1–5.4) on RAAS therapy
This is above the local reference range, not "mild hyperkalaemia". 1. Repeat to exclude pseudohyperkalaemia. 2. Remove reversible contributors first — NSAIDs, potassium supplements, potassium-containing salt substitutes, trimethoprim. 3. Assess for AKI, and assess volume status. 4. Individualised dietary advice (step 4). 5. Recheck in 1–4 weeks with a named reviewer. Only then consider dose adjustment or specialist advice — and in heart failure or albuminuric CKD, document a plan for reintroduction or optimisation rather than indefinite discontinuation.
Mild hyperkalaemia (K⁺ 5.5–5.9) confirmed
1. ECG — any change means emergency transfer. 2. Stop NSAIDs and potassium supplements and salt substitutes. 3. Assess for AKI and check renal function and volume status. 4. Review the potassium-sparing diuretic and the ACE inhibitor or ARB — reduce or withhold on the clinical picture, not on a formula, and in heart failure or albuminuric CKD plan the reintroduction rather than stopping indefinitely; seek renal or cardiology advice where the drug matters. 5. Individualised dietary review. 6. Recheck within days, with a named reviewer, a repeat date and a written escalation threshold. 7. Same-day assessment if an ECG or reliable follow-up cannot be arranged. Moderate (6.0–6.4): ECG and same-day clinical assessment — admission according to the ECG, the trend, renal function and clinical state.
Potassium binders — persistent outpatient hyperkalaemia
Not because a potassium is above 5.5. NICE recommends patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate in defined settings: CKD stage 3b–5 or heart failure · confirmed K⁺ ≥6.0 mmol/L · not on dialysis · RAAS inhibition not optimised because of hyperkalaemia (TA623, TA599). Initiation and prescribing responsibility follow local renal or cardiology arrangements — check the current Greater Manchester formulary and shared-care protocol rather than prescribing generically. Practical points: patiromer also binds magnesium, so monitor it; separate binders from other oral medicines by the interval the product specifies. Neither agent is emergency treatment — SZC's correction phase in hospital is typically 10 g three times daily for up to 72 hours, and patiromer is not an immediate potassium-lowering treatment at all.
Type 4 RTA / hypoaldosteronism
Persistent unexplained hyperkalaemia with a low bicarbonate warrants renal assessment for type 4 RTA or hypoaldosteronism. Fludrocortisone is not a primary-care treatment for hyperkalaemia — it can worsen hypertension, fluid retention, heart failure and oedema, and can cause hypokalaemia. Specialist endocrine or renal initiation only, with the contraindications considered and monitoring specified by the initiating team; primary care may continue a specialist-initiated prescription within that plan.
Patiromer (Veltassa) is a particularly useful drug for the very common clinical scenario of heart failure + CKD + spironolactone-induced hyperkalaemia — the RAAS inhibitor combination is the cornerstone of HFrEF management with proven mortality benefit (RALES showed 30% reduction in all-cause mortality with spironolactone + ACEi), but is frequently stopped or under-dosed in primary care because of hyperkalaemia. Patiromer allows the clinician to maintain optimal RAAS blockade while controlling the K⁺ rise. The AMBER trial (2019, Lancet, n=295) showed that in patients with CKD + heart failure who would otherwise require RAAS inhibitor discontinuation due to hyperkalaemia, patiromer allowed continuation of RAAS therapy in 66% vs 43% in the placebo group at 12 weeks. This is a meaningful clinical benefit — GPs managing complex heart failure patients with CKD should be aware of patiromer as an enabling strategy. Fludrocortisone is a specialist decision, not a primary-care option. Mechanistically it replaces the deficient aldosterone in type 4 RTA and restores distal nephron potassium secretion — the only K⁺-lowering drug that corrects the underlying defect rather than binding potassium in the gut or shifting it into cells. But its sodium-retaining effect raises blood pressure and causes fluid retention, and it can precipitate hypokalaemia and decompensate heart failure — precisely the comorbidities that accompany type 4 RTA in diabetic nephropathy. It therefore belongs to endocrinology or renal, with primary care continuing a specialist-initiated prescription within the specialist's monitoring plan.

Lifestyle

Dietary Modification, Patient Education & Monitoring Plan

Dietary potassium — individualised, substitution not exclusion Target the high-yield sources first: potassium-containing salt substitutes, potassium supplements, and the major dietary contributors the history actually identifies. Do not reflexively restrict milk, pulses, nuts and vegetables — unnecessary restriction of healthy foods has its own harms. Lower-potassium swaps (rice, pasta, bread, eggs, apples, pears, berries) are offered as substitutions. Boiling and discarding the water is a practical dietetic technique whose effect varies with the food, how finely it is cut, the water volume and the method — not a guaranteed percentage. Any numeric target belongs to the renal dietetic service, not to a universal rule for CKD 4–5.
Salt substitute elimination LoSalt, Nu-Salt, and similar products contain potassium chloride and are potentially dangerous in CKD or on RAAS therapy. Ask every patient with hyperkalaemia specifically: "Do you use any low-sodium salt or salt substitute?" Advise stopping immediately. Document in clinical records. Remind at each review — patients may restart without telling their GP.
Hydration — assess individually Dehydration reduces GFR and raises potassium, so avoiding it matters. Do not prescribe a standard fluid target in CKD, heart failure, oliguria or suspected obstruction — a generic 30 mL/kg/day instruction risks pulmonary oedema and hyponatraemia. Follow the renal or heart-failure fluid plan where one exists, and individualise otherwise. Avoid excess alcohol.
Sick-day plan — personalised, with restart advice Not a blanket instruction. A personalised plan naming which medicines to pause and when, with explicit restart advice and a clinician review if the illness persists — a blanket rule causes interruption of essential medicines, failure to restart, confusion about which drug is meant, and avoidable decompensation in heart failure. The usual trigger is significant vomiting or diarrhoea, or being unable to drink normally. NSAIDs are different: they should generally be avoided altogether in relevant CKD and heart-failure contexts rather than treated as a medicine to pause during illness. Give it in writing.
Medication compliance and education Explain clearly why drugs are being changed or stopped (language: "This medication has been building up potassium to a level that could affect your heart rhythm — we need to adjust it"). Give written list of current medications including changes made today. Advise not to restart stopped medications without GP review. Community pharmacy medicines use review (MUR) for complex polypharmacy patients.
Home BP and symptom monitoring Teach patients with CKD to recognise symptoms of hyperkalaemia: unexplained muscle weakness (especially legs), palpitations, feeling of fluttering in chest. Any of these symptoms → call GP same day. Home BP monitoring helps detect the hypotension of Addison's. Pulse check (irregular pulse = possible arrhythmia from electrolyte disturbance → same-day ECG).
Monitoring schedule CKD 3b on ACE inhibitor or ARB: K⁺ and eGFR every 3–6 months. CKD 4–5: every 1–3 months. After any RAAS dose change: K⁺ and eGFR at 1–2 weeks. Post-AKI: 2–4 weeks. On a potassium binder: as specified by the initiating service. After hospital treatment of moderate or severe hyperkalaemia the inpatient schedule is K⁺ at about 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 hours, with glucose to 6 hours after insulin–glucose — know it so the discharge plan can be checked. All results on a monitoring register so missed checks are flagged.
NSAID awareness NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, aspirin at anti-inflammatory doses) significantly worsen K⁺ in CKD or on RAAS therapy. OTC ibuprofen is the most dangerous — patients do not consider it a "real medication." Explicitly advise: "Do not take any ibuprofen or anti-inflammatory painkillers without checking with your GP first." Paracetamol is safe. Offer paracetamol for pain management as the default.
The sick day rules concept is one of the most important and underused patient safety interventions in CKD management — studies have shown that acute kidney injury (AKI) is responsible for approximately 10–15% of hospital admissions, and a significant proportion are caused by dehydration + continued nephrotoxic/RAAS drug use during illness in patients who were not told to temporarily stop these drugs. The 'STOP' mnemonic for sick day rules (Stop: Spironolactone, Trimethoprim, ACEi/ARBs, and NSAIDs during illness causing dehydration) is used in many NHS kidney patient education programmes. Written sick day rules cards (available from NHS Kidney Care/Kidney Research UK) should be given to every patient with CKD-3b or above on any combination of these drugs. The key message: 'These drugs are generally safe and important, but they can harm your kidneys and raise your potassium to a dangerous level if you become dehydrated from vomiting, diarrhoea, or not drinking enough during illness. Stop them temporarily if you can't keep fluids down for more than 24 hours, and restart them when you're better and eating and drinking normally.' The NSAID over-the-counter problem is a genuine patient safety crisis — ibuprofen is available at supermarkets and petrol stations without any prescription, and patients with CKD, heart failure, or on RAAS therapy frequently self-medicate with it for pain without realising it can cause AKI and hyperkalaemia. Verbal and written advice at each consultation, and recording the allergy/intolerance to NSAIDs in the patient's record (even if it is a relative contraindication rather than an absolute allergy) helps flag this when the patient or other prescribers attempt to prescribe it.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

After acute treatment / hospital discharge
The interval is individualised to the inpatient course, renal function and the ongoing cause — typically a potassium and eGFR within days of discharge, sooner where renal function is still moving or the cause is unresolved, and it should be named explicitly in the discharge plan rather than left as "routine". Insulin, salbutamol and bicarbonate shift potassium into cells rather than removing it, so rebound is expected unless the cause was corrected or potassium was removed. Check what was stopped in hospital and document a plan for reintroduction or optimisation — particularly ACE inhibitor, ARB or MRA in heart failure or albuminuric CKD — rather than leaving them indefinitely discontinued. Personalised sick-day plan in writing, dietetic referral if relevant, and the cause of any AKI identified.
Chronic CKD monitoring
CKD-3b + RAAS therapy: K⁺ + eGFR every 3–6 months minimum. CKD-4: every 1–3 months. Any K⁺ result >5.0 mmol/L in CKD should prompt drug and dietary review before the next routine check. Log on monitoring register. Recalls for overdue bloods.
RAAS therapy dose changes
Any change to ACEi/ARB/spironolactone dose → recheck K⁺ + eGFR at 1–2 weeks (NICE mandate). Do not routinely increase ACEi/ARB to maximum dose without intermediate K⁺ check in CKD-3b+. Document monitoring in records for medicolegal protection.
Potassium-binder monitoring
Per the initiating service and the local shared-care protocol: potassium after starting and on titration, then at the agreed interval. Patiromer also binds magnesium — monitor it. SZC contains sodium — monitor blood pressure and for fluid retention. Separate the binder from other oral medicines by the interval the product specifies. Confirm the shared-care arrangement is in place before taking over prescribing.
Return immediately
Any muscle weakness in legs or arms not explained by other cause → same-day ECG + K⁺ · Palpitations or irregular pulse → same-day ECG · Collapse or syncope → 999 · Any symptom of Addison's crisis (collapse + vomiting + hypotension) → 999 + hydrocortisone IM
Result handling
K⁺ ≥5.5 returning on routine bloods → same-day ECG and clinical assessment before any action · K⁺ ≥6.5, or any red flag, is an emergency transfer, not a repeat request · a new medicine that could raise potassium (NSAID, trimethoprim, another RAAS agent) → K⁺ at 1 week. A critical result must not remain in an inbox or a routine repeat queue overnight — name who is responsible for acting on it out of hours.
The 1–2 week K⁺ recheck after any ACEi/ARB dose change is a NICE-mandated monitoring requirement (CKD guideline NG203 and hypertension guideline NG136) that is frequently missed in general practice — the most common pattern of iatrogenic severe hyperkalaemia is: GP increases ACEi/ARB dose (appropriately, for blood pressure or CKD proteinuria), K⁺ rises to dangerous levels over 1–2 weeks, no repeat bloods are taken, patient develops ECG changes or cardiac arrest before the next routine blood test. The monitoring requirement is not bureaucratic box-ticking — it reflects the genuine physiological reality that RAAS agent dose increases can raise K⁺ by 0.5–1.0 mmol/L within 1–2 weeks of dose change, and this rise is unpredictable (patients with CKD-3b can have dramatic K⁺ rises to dangerous levels from a relatively small dose change). Implementing a simple practice recall system for 'K⁺ + eGFR at 1–2 weeks post-RAAS dose change' is a high-yield, low-effort patient safety intervention.
