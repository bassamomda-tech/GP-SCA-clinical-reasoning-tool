Safety

Red Flags — Haemochromatosis Organ Damage, Haematological Malignancy & Hyperferritinaemia Crisis

Ferritin >1000 µg/L + elevated ALT/AST + hepatomegaly + fatigue + bronze skin pigmentation or diabetes Hereditary haemochromatosis with organ involvement. → Urgent hepatology/iron-overload service. MRI liver iron quantification (liver biopsy not routinely required — reserved for selected diagnostic or staging situations); fibrosis assessment, usually transient elastography first. Specialist-led venesection before irreversible cirrhosis.
Ferritin >10,000 µg/L + fever, cytopenias, splenomegaly, liver dysfunction, coagulopathy or organ failure Urgently consider haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis / macrophage activation syndrome (HLH/MAS) — but ferritin is not diagnostic; also consider severe infection, liver injury, renal failure, malignancy and Still disease. → Same-day haematology/acute medicine discussion even if stable; 999/ED if haemodynamically unstable, hypoxic, confused, bleeding, in organ failure or clinically septic. Initial tests: FBC, film, ferritin trend, LFTs, U&E/creatinine, CRP, coagulation/fibrinogen, triglycerides, LDH, bilirubin, and clinical assessment for infection/malignancy. Specialist teams may add soluble IL-2 receptor, NK-cell studies and bone marrow examination. Treatment is specialist/critical-care led and differs between secondary HLH, malignancy-associated HLH and MAS.
Ferritin >500 µg/L + weight loss + night sweats + lymphadenopathy + hepatosplenomegaly Haematological malignancy (lymphoma, leukaemia, myeloma) or solid organ malignancy. → suspected cancer pathway referral by site (haematology for unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly). CT staging. Blood film + LDH + protein electrophoresis.
Ferritin elevated + unexplained arthritis (especially 2nd/3rd MCP joints) + skin bronzing + diabetes + hypogonadism in a man under 50 Classic haemochromatosis pentad — advanced iron overload. → Same-week hepatology. HFE gene testing. Ferritin + transferrin saturation + liver USS + FibroScan.
Ferritin >2000 µg/L + active inflammatory disease + multiple blood transfusions + thalassaemia or sickle cell disease history Secondary iron overload from transfusional haemosiderosis. → Specialist haematology management: serial ferritin trends with MRI liver iron concentration and cardiac T2* where indicated; chelation choice and dose are specialist decisions.
Recurrent high-spiking fever + inflammatory arthritis/arthralgia + evanescent salmon-pink rash + neutrophilic leucocytosis + liver dysfunction + markedly raised inflammatory markers/ferritin Consider Still disease (adult-onset Still disease, AOSD) after excluding infection, malignancy and HLH/MAS. No single diagnostic test: ferritin is supportive but non-specific, the rash is characteristic but not pathognomonic, and arthritis may be absent early. → Discuss urgently with rheumatology/acute medicine.
Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is one of the most rapidly fatal conditions associated with extreme hyperferritinaemia and must be recognised as an emergency — ferritin levels in HLH typically exceed 10,000 µg/L and can reach 100,000+ µg/L. HLH is caused by uncontrolled activation of macrophages and cytotoxic T-cells (a cytokine storm), leading to phagocytosis of blood cells in the bone marrow, spleen, and lymph nodes. Triggers: EBV (most common in adults), other viral infections, haematological malignancies (particularly T-cell lymphomas), autoimmune diseases (SLE, AOSD), and rarely genetic (familial HLH). The HScore identifies probable HLH: key components include temperature >38.5°C, splenomegaly, bicytopenia or pancytopenia, elevated ferritin (>500 µg/L), elevated triglycerides, low fibrinogen, haemophagocytosis on bone marrow, and known immunosuppression. Ferritin is only one component of the HLH-2004 criteria: diagnosis needs a compatible clinical context and multiple criteria, or specialist interpretation with an HScore/HLH pathway, and bone-marrow haemophagocytosis may be absent early (it should not delay specialist treatment). The BSH notes ferritin >10,000 µg/L may occur in HLH and Still disease but also in renal failure, hepatocellular injury, infection and malignancy. Any patient with unexplained fever + cytopenias + extreme hyperferritinaemia needs same-day haematology/acute medical discussion — untreated HLH has a mortality exceeding 90%.

Causes of Elevated Ferritin — Systematic Classification

Ferritin as an acute phase reactant (most common cause)
Ferritin is synthesised in response to inflammation and is elevated by any inflammatory stimulus — it is NOT a reliable marker of iron stores in the presence of inflammation. CRP should always be checked simultaneously. Common inflammatory causes: acute or chronic infection (most common globally), IBD (Crohn's, UC), RA and other inflammatory arthropathies, SLE, malignancy (especially haematological), liver disease (hepatitis, NAFLD/NASH, alcoholic liver disease). Ferritin in inflammation is often 200-1000 µg/L. Interpreting ferritin without CRP is clinically meaningless.
Iron overload states
Hereditary haemochromatosis (HH): autosomal recessive — HFE gene mutations (C282Y/H63D). C282Y homozygous = classic HH (more common in people of Northern European ancestry — use ancestry only to interpret pre-test probability, not to deny testing or referral; non-HFE haemochromatosis, ferroportin disease, transfusional iron overload and liver disease with secondary iron loading occur in all ethnic groups). Iron accumulates in liver, pancreas, heart, joints, pituitary. Transferrin saturation typically elevated (>50% in men, >40% in women — BSH) before ferritin rises significantly. Secondary iron overload: repeated blood transfusions (thalassaemia, sickle cell, MDS — each unit = 200-250 mg iron), ineffective erythropoiesis (thalassaemia intermedia — increased GI iron absorption), parenteral iron overtreatment.
Other causes
Alcohol excess (direct hepatotoxicity + increased intestinal iron absorption — ferritin may be the only abnormal LFT). NAFLD/metabolic syndrome (hyperferritinaemia + normal or mildly elevated transferrin saturation — ferroportin disease or dysmetabolic iron overload syndrome). Liver disease (any cause — ferritin released from damaged hepatocytes). Hyperthyroidism (increased ferritin synthesis). Haemolysis (ferritin released from lysed RBCs). Gaucher disease (glucocerebrosidase deficiency — extreme hyperferritinaemia). Adult-onset Still disease (ferritin often markedly raised — supportive, not diagnostic). Renal failure.
The ferroportin disease and dysmetabolic iron overload syndrome (DIOS) represent two important and frequently misdiagnosed causes of elevated ferritin in clinical practice. Ferroportin disease (haemochromatosis type 4): caused by mutations in the SLC40A1 gene encoding ferroportin (the iron exporter), presents with elevated ferritin but NORMAL or LOW transferrin saturation (distinguishing it from classical HFE haemochromatosis where transferrin saturation is elevated early). Iron deposits predominantly in macrophages (not parenchymal cells), and patients tolerate venesection poorly (develop anaemia quickly). DIOS: associated with metabolic syndrome (insulin resistance, obesity, dyslipidaemia, NAFLD) — elevated ferritin with normal transferrin saturation, mild hepatic iron loading on MRI, not a classical haemochromatosis gene mutation. Management: treat the metabolic syndrome (weight loss, exercise, alcohol cessation) rather than venesection — the BSH notes there is no evidence supporting venesection to reduce ferritin in NAFLD. The key practical lesson: elevated ferritin with elevated transferrin saturation points to primary iron overload (HFE haemochromatosis); elevated ferritin with NORMAL transferrin saturation is more likely inflammation, DIOS, ferroportin disease, or liver disease.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Symptoms of iron overload: fatigue (most common, non-specific), arthralgia (especially 2nd/3rd MCP joints — distinctive of haemochromatosis), abdominal pain (hepatomegaly), reduced libido/erectile dysfunction + amenorrhoea (hypogonadism from pituitary iron deposition), polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes from pancreatic iron deposition), palpitations/arrhythmia (cardiac iron), skin pigmentation (bronzing — iron + melanin in dermis). Alcohol use: AUDIT-C — alcohol is very commonly missed as a cause of isolated hyperferritinaemia. Medications: iron supplements and iron-containing multivitamins (over-supplementation), recent IV iron or blood transfusions. Liver disease history: hepatitis B/C, NAFLD, alcohol. Systemic inflammatory disease. Family history: haemochromatosis (first-degree relatives should be screened), haematological disease.
Examination
Hepatomegaly (haemochromatosis, liver disease, malignancy). Splenomegaly (haematological malignancy, portal hypertension, haemolysis, HLH). Skin: bronzing or slate-grey pigmentation (haemochromatosis — especially sun-exposed areas, scars, genitalia), jaundice (liver disease), salmon-pink evanescent rash (AOSD). Joints: MCP joint arthropathy (haemochromatosis — hook osteophytes 2nd/3rd MCPs on X-ray). Signs of chronic liver disease (spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, caput medusae). Lymphadenopathy (malignancy, HLH, AOSD).
Investigations — tiered approach
Ferritin + CRP + ESR (interpret together — is it inflammatory?) · Repeat ferritin (if clinically appropriate) · Transferrin saturation (serum iron / TIBC × 100) + serum iron + transferrin/TIBC (use local ranges; persistently >50% men, >40% women with raised ferritin → HFE testing or specialist advice; a fasting sample is not routinely essential — repeat a borderline or unexpected result) · U&E/eGFR (renal disease) · LFTs + GGT (liver disease, alcohol) · FBC (cytopenias, haemolysis; film if indicated) · HbA1c/glucose (diabetes, metabolic risk) · Hepatitis B/C (if LFTs abnormal or risk factors) · HFE gene testing (C282Y/H63D) (if transferrin saturation raised — GP can request per local lab pathway; do not diagnose from genotype alone) · Liver USS (if LFTs abnormal, hepatomegaly or suspected liver disease) · MRI liver iron quantification (preferred, specialist-requested; liver biopsy not routinely required for diagnosis). TFTs, protein electrophoresis and broad malignancy tests are not mandatory for a modest isolated ferritin rise — guide them by symptoms, examination and initial results.
The transferrin saturation is the most important single test for distinguishing primary iron overload (haemochromatosis) from reactive hyperferritinaemia — in hereditary haemochromatosis, the primary pathological mechanism is excessive intestinal iron absorption leading to progressive loading of parenchymal cells. Transferrin (the iron transport protein) becomes progressively saturated as iron overload develops, and transferrin saturation persistently above 50% in men or 40% in women (BSH; a fasting sample is not routinely essential, but repeat borderline results) is the most sensitive early marker of HFE haemochromatosis — it rises before ferritin becomes markedly elevated. The investigation protocol: elevated ferritin → check transferrin saturation simultaneously. If transferrin saturation is elevated AND ferritin elevated: HFE gene testing (C282Y and H63D) or specialist advice is indicated — ancestry informs pre-test probability but is not a reason to withhold testing or referral. If ferritin elevated with NORMAL transferrin saturation: HFE haemochromatosis is less likely — consider inflammatory, metabolic, alcoholic, or ferroportin causes. Do not send HFE gene testing without transferrin saturation — the clinical context is essential.

Haemochromatosis Diagnosis & Staging

HFE gene mutation interpretation
C282Y/C282Y homozygote (most common pathological genotype — approximately 80-90% of clinical HH): variable penetrance — approximately 28-38% of men, approximately 1-2% of women develop clinically significant iron overload. Genotype does not equal clinical disease: biochemical phenotype and organ assessment are required, and there is no indication for venesection when ferritin and transferrin saturation are normal. C282Y/H63D compound heterozygote: low clinical penetrance — do not attribute raised ferritin automatically to this genotype; confirm biochemical or MRI evidence of iron overload, consider other causes (alcohol, metabolic liver disease, inflammation) and discuss significant iron loading with hepatology/haematology. H63D/H63D homozygote: rarely causes clinical iron overload alone. C282Y heterozygote (carrier): generally not at significant risk of iron overload — no routine monitoring; repeat ferritin + transferrin saturation if persistently abnormal, symptoms develop or there is a strong family history.
Haemochromatosis staging (EASL/BSG)
Stage 1: gene mutation present, ferritin + transferrin saturation normal, no organ damage — lifestyle advice, no venesection. Stage 2: elevated ferritin + transferrin saturation, no organ damage — specialist-led venesection (induction). Stage 3: elevated ferritin + organ involvement (fibrosis, arthropathy, diabetes, hypogonadism) without cirrhosis — venesection beneficial for non-hepatic manifestations. Stage 4: cirrhosis — venesection reduces hepatic cancer risk; liver transplant if decompensated; 6-monthly liver ultrasound HCC surveillance (± AFP per local hepatology practice). Consider surveillance in advanced bridging fibrosis (F3) on hepatology advice.
Secondary iron overload assessment
Transfusional iron overload requires specialist haematology management. Assess iron burden using serial ferritin trends together with liver iron concentration by MRI and cardiac T2* MRI where indicated (BSH 2021). Cardiac T2* <20 ms indicates myocardial iron loading; T2* <10 ms is associated with increased risk of cardiac failure. Cardiac failure, arrhythmia or severe iron loading needs urgent specialist/cardiology management. Chelation (desferrioxamine, deferiprone, deferasirox — within licensed indications) and dose depend on the underlying anaemia, transfusion rate, renal/hepatic function, liver iron, cardiac T2*, previous toxicity and adherence.
The 6-monthly HCC (hepatocellular carcinoma) surveillance with AFP and liver ultrasound in haemochromatosis-related cirrhosis is recommended by NICE NG50 (Cirrhosis, 2016: ultrasound with or without AFP every 6 months) and by EASL (international) — HCC risk in haemochromatosis cirrhosis is approximately 200-fold higher than the general population, with a lifetime risk of approximately 10-30% in those with established cirrhosis. Critically, the HCC risk in haemochromatosis is not completely eliminated by venesection (iron removal) once cirrhosis has developed — the cirrhotic liver retains malignant potential even after iron is removed. This is in contrast to earlier haemochromatosis (stages 2-3) where venesection to normal ferritin prevents HCC development. GPs who receive haemochromatosis-cirrhosis patients in shared care must ensure: (1) 6-monthly liver ultrasound (the core test); (2) AFP if used by local hepatology practice; (3) specialist-directed MRI or CT if ultrasound is technically inadequate; (4) documentation of HCC surveillance compliance. Surveillance is not started solely because ferritin is high or because the patient is a C282Y homozygote. Failure to surveil is associated with late-stage HCC diagnosis and poor outcomes.

GP investigation + monitoring
Mild ferritin elevation (200-500 µg/L) + elevated CRP: treat underlying inflammation (infection, IBD flare) + recheck ferritin + CRP in 4-6 weeks after CRP normalised. Alcohol-related: AUDIT-C + reduction advice + repeat LFTs + ferritin in 3 months abstinence. NAFLD/metabolic: lifestyle + recheck in 3-6 months — no venesection simply to lower ferritin. C282Y heterozygote: no routine monitoring; repeat if ferritin/transferrin saturation persistently abnormal, symptoms develop or strong family history.
Ferritin below 1,000 µg/L
(1) Repeat ferritin if appropriate. (2) Transferrin saturation, FBC, CRP/ESR, LFTs, renal function, glucose/HbA1c. (3) Assess alcohol, BMI/metabolic risk, inflammatory symptoms, infection, liver disease, malignancy symptoms, transfusion/iron history and supplements. (4) If inflammation, infection, alcohol or metabolic disease is found, manage the cause and repeat after an appropriate interval. (5) If transferrin saturation is raised, arrange HFE testing or specialist advice. (6) If persistent unexplained elevation, especially approaching 1,000 µg/L, refer.
Ferritin ≥1,000 µg/L
Repeat/confirm if clinically appropriate. Refer to a specialist regardless of whether HFE haemochromatosis is confirmed. Check LFTs, transferrin saturation, FBC, renal function and liver imaging/fibrosis assessment per local pathway. Do not start venesection in primary care solely to lower ferritin.
Ferritin >10,000 µg/L
Assess urgently for organ failure, sepsis, HLH/MAS, Still disease, acute liver injury and malignancy. Same-day haematology/acute medicine discussion; 999 if unstable or critically ill. Do not diagnose HLH from ferritin alone.
The decision to test first-degree relatives of a C282Y homozygous haemochromatosis patient is a cost-effective and potentially life-saving GP intervention — adult first-degree relatives should be offered HFE genotyping, ferritin and transferrin saturation, with clinical assessment where indicated (siblings have a 25% chance of being C282Y homozygous). Children of a C282Y homozygote are obligate C282Y carriers; avoid routine testing of minors unless there is a clinical indication or specialist/genetic-counselling advice (BSH supports testing children over the age of consent). The rationale: haemochromatosis is one of the most treatable genetic conditions if identified before organ damage develops. A sibling identified as C282Y homozygous at stage 1-2 (elevated transferrin saturation, mildly elevated ferritin, no organ damage) can have venesection therapy initiated before any organ damage occurs — preventing diabetes, cirrhosis, arthropathy, and hypogonadism entirely. The NHS does not currently offer population screening for HFE mutations, but the EASL (European Association for the Study of the Liver; international) and the BSH haemochromatosis guideline (2018, endorsed by the BSG) recommend cascade genetic testing for all first-degree relatives of confirmed C282Y homozygous probands. GPs can request HFE gene testing directly in the UK via the NHS genetics laboratory.

Venesection Protocol & Chelation Therapy

Venesection (therapeutic phlebotomy) — haemochromatosis
Specialist-led, for confirmed HFE haemochromatosis with biochemical iron overload. Induction phase: usually 400–500 mL weekly or every 1–2 weeks depending on tolerance. Check Hb before each procedure; adjust frequency or defer if anaemia, frailty, hypotension or significant comorbidity develops. Ferritin approximately monthly during induction; transferrin saturation periodically (often every 1–3 months). Induction target: ferritin commonly approximately <50 µg/L (EASL); BSH describes approximately 20–30 µg/L with transferrin saturation <50% in fit patients. Duration depends on initial iron burden. Maintenance phase: individualised frequency, commonly every 1–4 months; ferritin approximately 50–100 µg/L (EASL; BSH has used <50 µg/L in fit patients; more relaxed targets may suit older or frail patients). Avoid persistent ferritin <20 µg/L (symptomatic iron deficiency). Safety and tolerability: generally well-tolerated; pre-hydrate with water before sessions; light meal 2h before.
Chelation therapy — secondary iron overload
Specialist haematology only — not GP prescribing. Options within licensed indications: deferasirox, desferrioxamine and deferiprone (NICE). Choice and dose depend on the underlying anaemia, transfusion rate, renal/hepatic function, liver iron, cardiac T2*, previous toxicity and adherence; no chelator is automatically superior for every patient with cardiac iron. GP awareness: drug-specific monitoring under the specialist plan (e.g. renal and liver function with deferasirox; FBC for agranulocytosis with deferiprone).
Alcohol reduction as primary treatment for alcohol-related hyperferritinaemia
Brief intervention at GP consultation + AUDIT-C scoring. Referral to the local community alcohol service or alcohol liaison team. Ferritin normalises within 3-6 months of abstinence in alcohol-related hyperferritinaemia. Liver biopsy not needed if ferritin falls to normal with alcohol cessation. GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) is a sensitive alcohol marker — falls rapidly with abstinence (half-life approximately 26 days) — use as objective response marker.
The venesection schedule for haemochromatosis is practically managed in NHS primary care or hospital phlebotomy departments, and GPs play a key role in monitoring and coordinating this — the monitoring protocol before each venesection session: Hb before every session (adjust or defer if anaemia, frailty, hypotension or significant comorbidity), ferritin approximately monthly during induction, transferrin saturation periodically, and clinical assessment (symptoms, joint swelling). Targets and intervals are set by the specialist service. The documentation and shared care: hepatology initiates and monitors overall progress; GPs often coordinate venesection appointments at local phlebotomy or blood donation units (NHS Blood and Transplant accepts some people with haemochromatosis as blood donors if they meet its donor criteria — the donated blood is used for transfusion). GPs should be aware that Haemochromatosis UK (haemochromatosis.org.uk) provides excellent patient support and information.

Treat

Inflammatory Hyperferritinaemia & NAFLD Management

Inflammatory hyperferritinaemia — management principles
The key principle: ferritin in acute/chronic inflammation is not a measure of iron stores — do not treat with phlebotomy. Treat the underlying inflammatory condition. Examples: Active RA with ferritin 400 µg/L + CRP 60 mg/L → DMARD escalation (methotrexate dose increase, add biologic). Active IBD flare + ferritin 600 µg/L → treat the flare (corticosteroids/biologics) + check if coexisting iron deficiency (sTfR elevated — if so, IV iron). Active bacterial infection + ferritin 800 µg/L → antibiotics + source control. Malignancy-related ferritin: treat the malignancy.
NAFLD/metabolic hyperferritinaemia (DIOS)
Dysmetabolic iron overload syndrome (DIOS): associated with metabolic syndrome (BMI >30, central obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, NAFLD). Ferritin elevated (200-1000 µg/L), transferrin saturation normal, mild hepatic iron on MRI, no HFE mutation. Treatment: standard weight-management, alcohol and metabolic-liver-disease guidance (NICE NG49) — sustained weight loss, aerobic exercise (150 min/week moderate intensity), alcohol reduction. Do not use venesection simply to lower ferritin in NAFLD, metabolic syndrome or dysmetabolic iron overload unless specialist assessment demonstrates another indication (BSH: no evidence supports it in NAFLD). Annual HbA1c + lipid profile.
Adult-onset Still disease (AOSD)
No single diagnostic test; ferritin is supportive but non-specific (often markedly raised) and can track activity. Consider Still disease with recurrent high-spiking fever, inflammatory arthritis/arthralgia, evanescent salmon-pink rash, neutrophilic leucocytosis, liver dysfunction and markedly raised inflammatory markers/ferritin, after excluding infection, malignancy and HLH/MAS (EULAR/PReS 2024). Classification: Yamaguchi criteria (fever ≥39°C for ≥2 weeks, arthritis ≥2 weeks, evanescent salmon-pink rash, leucocytosis ≥10 × 10⁹/L, sore throat, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly/splenomegaly, negative ANA/RF). Exclude: infection (blood cultures, viral serology including EBV, CMV, parvovirus) and malignancy before diagnosing. Treatment is specialist-led (rheumatology): NSAIDs, corticosteroids and IL-1 inhibition. Anakinra is not an immediate GP treatment — NICE TA685 recommends it in defined circumstances, including adult-onset Still disease inadequately responding to two or more conventional DMARDs.
The IL-1 inhibitor anakinra for adult-onset Still disease represents one of the most dramatic treatment responses in rheumatology — the extreme hyperferritinaemia of AOSD (reflecting macrophage activation) drops precipitously within days of starting anakinra (recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonist, 100 mg SC daily). AOSD is driven by IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18 — the IL-1 pathway is the most important driver of the AOSD cytokine storm. Anakinra is specialist-initiated within NICE TA685. Ferritin can be used as a treatment response biomarker — a falling ferritin with clinical improvement supports the diagnosis and treatment efficacy but does not prove it. For AOSD MAS (macrophage activation syndrome — the most life-threatening complication, equivalent to HLH), IV methylprednisolone + ciclosporin + anakinra is used.

Lifestyle

Dietary Iron, Alcohol & Patient Education

Dietary iron reduction in haemochromatosis Dietary measures have only a limited role and do not replace venesection (BSH). Advise: eat a balanced diet; do not take iron supplements unless specifically indicated; avoid iron-containing multivitamins; avoid high-dose vitamin C supplements; avoid excess alcohol, particularly if there is liver disease (alcohol enhances iron absorption and accelerates liver damage); do not undertake extreme iron restriction. Avoid raw oysters and other raw shellfish if there is significant iron overload or chronic liver disease because of the risk of severe Vibrio infection.
Vitamin C and iron absorption Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) enhances non-haem iron absorption. Avoid high-dose vitamin C supplementation and vitamin C-containing iron preparations unless specifically advised by the specialist. Normal dietary fruit and vegetables are not prohibited. Read multivitamin labels — choose iron-free products.
Family cascade testing — patient communication Explain to C282Y homozygous patients: their siblings have a 1-in-4 chance of being homozygous; if the other parent is not a carrier, all children inherit one C282Y allele but none will be C282Y homozygous; if the other parent is a C282Y carrier, each child has a 50% chance of C282Y homozygosity. Adult first-degree relatives: offer HFE genotyping, ferritin and transferrin saturation; avoid routine testing of minors unless clinically indicated or advised by a specialist/genetic counsellor. Haemochromatosis is treatable — early testing saves lives. Haemochromatosis UK (haemochromatosis.org.uk) provides family letter templates and support. NHS genetic testing: free via GP or hepatology referral letter.
Alcohol-related hyperferritinaemia — behaviour change Alcohol is the most common reversible cause of hyperferritinaemia in UK primary care. Use AUDIT-C (3 questions) at every ferritin review. Brief intervention (5 A's) at GP level. Referral to NHS Alcohol Services, community alcohol team, or mutual-aid (AA). Abstinence target vs reduction target: for alcohol-related liver disease, abstinence is the goal — even moderate ongoing drinking perpetuates liver injury. GGT provides an objective biochemical marker of alcohol reduction — use to motivate.
Metabolic syndrome and DIOS — lifestyle intervention DIOS ferritin elevation is driven by insulin resistance + hepatic steatosis. Use standard weight-management, alcohol and metabolic-liver-disease guidance (NICE NG49): sustained weight loss, aerobic exercise 150 min/week + resistance training 2x/week, and a healthy balanced diet. Individual effects on steatosis and ferritin vary. Avoid: fructose-rich beverages (high-fructose corn syrup drives hepatic lipogenesis + insulin resistance). Annual review: HbA1c, lipids, BMI, ferritin, LFTs.
Venesection — patient experience and adherence Venesection involves the same procedure as a blood donation — most patients tolerate it well. Practical advice: drink 500 mL water before each session + light meal 2h prior (prevents vasovagal reactions). Some fatigue for 24-48h post-venesection is normal. Expect 12-24 months of weekly or fortnightly sessions during induction — long-term commitment. Blood donation: many people with HH who meet NHS Blood and Transplant donor criteria can donate (financial and logistical benefit — therapeutic phlebotomy AND blood donation simultaneously).
Cardiac iron in transfusional overload — warning signs Cardiac iron deposition (from transfusional haemosiderosis in thalassaemia, sickle cell, MDS) is the leading cause of death in these patients. MRI cardiac T2* <20 ms indicates myocardial iron loading; <10 ms is associated with increased risk of cardiac failure. Symptoms of cardiac iron: fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, arrhythmias, palpitations, ankle oedema — cardiac failure or arrhythmia needs urgent specialist/cardiology management, not an outpatient GP chelation prescription. MRI monitoring intervals and chelator choice are set by the specialist haematology service (BSH 2021).
Pregnancy and haemochromatosis Haemochromatosis rarely causes organ damage in premenopausal women — menstruation provides natural iron loss. Most C282Y homozygous women remain asymptomatic until menopause. In pregnancy: avoid iron deficiency; most patients with mild to moderate iron overload can pause therapeutic venesection, individualised (EASL); avoid routine venesection unless specialist-directed for severe disease. Cirrhosis, cardiac iron loading or severe iron overload needs specialist obstetric/hepatology management. Reassess postpartum. Post-menopausal women: lose the protective effect of menstrual iron loss — ferritin often rises steeply at menopause, triggering the clinical presentation of HH.
The Vibrio vulnificus raw shellfish risk in haemochromatosis is a clinically critical patient safety warning — Vibrio vulnificus is a marine bacterium found in warm coastal waters and concentrated in filter-feeding shellfish, particularly oysters. In healthy individuals, Vibrio vulnificus ingestion causes mild gastroenteritis. In people with chronic liver disease/cirrhosis, iron overload, diabetes or immunocompromise, the bacterium uses excess free iron as a growth substrate, causing rapid septicaemia with mortality exceeding 50% in iron-overloaded patients. The clinical warning must be explicit at diagnosis: 'Avoid raw oysters and other raw shellfish if you have significant iron overload or chronic liver disease — they can cause a life-threatening Vibrio blood infection.' This warning should be provided in writing at diagnosis.

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Family Screening

Haemochromatosis monitoring protocol
Induction phase: Hb before every venesection; ferritin approximately monthly; transferrin saturation periodically (often every 1–3 months). LFTs every 3 months. HbA1c + lipids annually. Maintenance: individualised venesection, commonly every 1–4 months, with ferritin at intervals agreed by the specialist service (avoid persistent ferritin <20 µg/L). Liver fibrosis monitoring per fibrosis stage and local hepatology protocol — not universal FibroScan for every patient.
HCC surveillance (cirrhosis; consider in advanced fibrosis)
All patients with haemochromatosis and cirrhosis: liver USS every 6 months (NICE), ± AFP per local hepatology practice; specialist-directed MRI or CT if USS technically inadequate. Consider surveillance in advanced bridging fibrosis (F3), especially with additional risk factors, on hepatology advice. Do not start surveillance solely because ferritin is high or the patient is a C282Y homozygote. Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI for suspicious lesions. Refer immediately to hepatology if AFP rising or new nodule on USS.
Inflammatory hyperferritinaemia re-check
Recheck ferritin + CRP 4-6 weeks after CRP normalises (when inflammation resolves). If ferritin still elevated with normal CRP: further investigation (transferrin saturation, HFE gene testing, liver USS).
Relatives of C282Y homozygotes
Offer HFE genotyping + ferritin + transferrin saturation, with clinical assessment where indicated, to adult first-degree relatives (siblings have 25% chance of homozygosity). Children: avoid routine testing of minors unless clinically indicated or advised by a specialist/genetic counsellor. If the other parent is not a carrier, all children inherit one C282Y allele but none are C282Y homozygous; if the other parent is a C282Y carrier, each child has a 50% chance of being C282Y homozygous (or compound heterozygous if the other parent carries H63D). Inform via patient + GP letter.

The ferritin recheck after CRP normalisation is the most important practical step in the primary care evaluation of hyperferritinaemia — because ferritin is a reliable marker of iron stores ONLY in the absence of inflammation, any elevated ferritin result with a concurrent elevated CRP cannot be interpreted as reflecting true iron stores. The protocol: treat the underlying inflammatory cause (infection — antibiotics; IBD flare — steroids; RA — DMARD escalation), then recheck ferritin + CRP together 4-6 weeks after clinical recovery. If CRP has normalised and ferritin remains elevated: this now represents true hyperferritinaemia requiring further investigation (transferrin saturation, HFE gene testing, alcohol history, liver USS). GPs who review a ferritin result in isolation — without checking CRP, without knowledge of the inflammatory context — and either falsely reassure (missing haemochromatosis) or falsely alarm (diagnosing iron overload in an inflamed patient) are making a systematic analytical error.
