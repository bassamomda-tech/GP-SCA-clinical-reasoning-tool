1
Safety · Red Flags

Identify features requiring urgent action

The first question in every consultation is "how quickly did this come on?". Refer urgently to endocrinology (or gynaecology if the source looks ovarian) if any of the following are present — do not start a six-month hormonal trial first:

Rapid onset / progressionNew or worsening hair over weeks to months, rather than gradually since the teens, suggests an androgen-secreting tumour
Virilisation featuresClitoromegaly, voice deepening, temporal (male-pattern) balding, increased muscle bulk, breast atrophy
Very high testosteroneTotal testosterone >5 nmol/L warrants further investigation, confirmation by mass spectrometry and imaging (Society for Endocrinology, 2025)
Cushing's featuresProximal myopathy, wide purple striae, easy bruising, facial plethora, new hypertension or diabetes
Onset outside reproductive yearsNew hirsutism after the menopause (tumour or ovarian hyperthecosis) or before puberty (see the precocious puberty pathway)
Severe insulin resistanceMarked acanthosis nigricans with very high BMI or young-onset diabetes — high metabolic risk; screen for diabetes and discuss with endocrinology if extreme
2WW NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — ovarianAscites, or a pelvic or abdominal mass not obviously uterine fibroids → urgent suspected ovarian cancer referral. Aged 40 or over with persistent or frequent (particularly more than 12 times a month) bloating, early satiety or loss of appetite, pelvic or abdominal pain, or urinary urgency or frequency → CA125, with urgent direct-access ultrasound if CA125 is ≥35 IU/mL (40–49), ≥31 (50–59), ≥24 (60–69), ≥25 (70–79) or ≥31 (80 and over). Aged 39 or under: do not rely on CA125 alone — consider ultrasound for persistent symptoms
Distress or self-harmAppearance-related low mood, social withdrawal or thoughts of self-harm need assessing on the day, alongside the hair

Hirsutism has no NICE NG12 criterion of its own. The purple tile applies only when the examination or history finds an ovarian feature — which is one reason the abdominal and pelvic examination is part of this workup.

Androgen-secreting ovarian tumours (such as Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours) and adrenocortical tumours are rare — about 0.2% of women attending with androgen excess in a large US series (Azziz et al., JCEM 2004) — but they are the reason the pace of onset matters more than the amount of hair. The Society for Endocrinology guideline on the evaluation of androgen excess in women (Elhassan et al., Clinical Endocrinology 2025) treats a total testosterone above 5 nmol/L as the trigger for further investigation, including confirmation by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry and ovarian or adrenal imaging. The Endocrine Society (international, 2018) likewise advises urgent evaluation for rapid progression or virilisation. Cushing's syndrome is uncommon but causes serious morbidity if missed, and screening is reserved for women with the discriminating features (proximal weakness, wide purple striae, bruising, plethora) rather than central weight gain alone.

Diagnose · History

Focused history

Onset and rate
Gradual over years, starting around puberty → PCOS or idiopathic. Rapid over months, or starting in adult life without explanation → tumour or Cushing's until shown otherwise
Menstrual history
Cycles longer than 35 days, fewer than 8 periods a year, or amenorrhoea → ovulatory dysfunction (PCOS, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia). Regular cycles with normal androgens → idiopathic hirsutism
Family history and ethnicity
Familial hirsutism, PCOS, CAH or type 2 diabetes. Ethnicity changes what is "normal" hair density and the likelihood of non-classic CAH
Medications
Testosterone (including a partner's gel), anabolic steroids, danazol, androgenic progestogens, sodium valproate. Minoxidil, ciclosporin, phenytoin and diazoxide cause hypertrichosis rather than true hirsutism
Fertility wish
Pregnancy plans decide the whole treatment choice — anti-androgens are unsafe in pregnancy and subfertility needs a different route
Weight / BMI change
Weight gain worsens hyperandrogenism in PCOS through insulin resistance; rapid weight gain with other features suggests Cushing's
Skin symptoms
Acne, seborrhoea, scalp hair thinning — the other androgen-excess signs
Symptoms of Cushing's
Weight gain, low mood, proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair), easy bruising
Current hair removal
What she removes, how often and at what cost — threading, waxing and laser lower the visible score and hide the true burden
Impact
What it stops her doing, avoidance of photographs, swimming or intimacy, mood and anxiety

Score hair distribution using the modified Ferriman–Gallwey (mFG) score — nine body areas each scored 0–4 (maximum 36). A score of 8 or more is the conventional cut-off in White and Black women; the threshold is lower in East Asian women and higher (around 9–10) in Hispanic and Middle Eastern women (Endocrine Society, international, 2018). Distress that is out of proportion to the score still deserves assessment and treatment.

The mFG score is the standard visual scale (Ferriman and Gallwey 1961, later modified) and the Endocrine Society guideline (Martin et al., JCEM 2018) uses ethnicity-adjusted cut-offs because normal terminal hair density differs between populations. Recording a number at baseline is what makes the six-month review meaningful. The drug history is worth a separate question: exogenous androgens (including accidental transfer from a partner's testosterone gel) and anabolic steroids are under-disclosed and reversible, while minoxidil, ciclosporin and phenytoin produce generalised hypertrichosis that will not respond to anti-androgen treatment.

Diagnose · Examination

Clinical examination

Hirsutism or hypertrichosis?
Hirsutism = coarse, pigmented terminal hair in androgen-dependent sites (upper lip, chin, chest, linea alba, lower back, inner thighs). Hypertrichosis = fine or generalised hair in a non-androgenic distribution — look for drug, thyroid, nutritional (anorexia nervosa) or familial causes instead
BMI, waist and BP
Central adiposity and raised BP add to the cardiometabolic risk of PCOS and are part of the Cushing's picture
Hair distribution
mFG areas: upper lip, chin, chest, upper and lower abdomen, upper and lower back, upper arms, thighs
Signs of virilisation
Clitoromegaly, deep voice, temporal recession, increased muscle bulk, breast atrophy — any one → urgent referral
Skin
Acne, seborrhoea, acanthosis nigricans in the neck and axillae (insulin resistance), wide purple striae (Cushing's)
Thyroid
Goitre or clinical hypo- or hyperthyroidism — both disturb cycles and SHBG
Cushing's stigmata
The most discriminating are proximal myopathy, easy bruising, facial plethora and wide purple striae; moon face and central weight gain are common in PCOS too
Galactorrhoea
Suggests hyperprolactinaemia — check prolactin
Abdominal and pelvic examination
An adrenal or ovarian mass, or ascites → urgent referral (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) urgent ovarian pathway for ascites or a pelvic/abdominal mass)
Separating hirsutism from hypertrichosis is the step that stops months of ineffective anti-androgen treatment. Acanthosis nigricans is a visible marker of insulin resistance and moves the metabolic screen up the list. Clitoromegaly and voice change are the most specific signs of significant androgen excess and point towards a tumour or exogenous androgen rather than PCOS. For Cushing's, the features that best separate it from simple obesity or PCOS are those of catabolic excess — proximal myopathy, thin skin with bruising and wide purple striae — so these, rather than weight alone, are the trigger to test.
4
Diagnose · Investigations

First-line blood tests

Take an early-morning sample in the early follicular phase (days 3–5) if she is cycling, or at any time if oligomenorrhoeic or amenorrhoeic. Hormonal contraception suppresses androgens — where the result would change management, stop it for 6–8 weeks beforehand with alternative contraception (Society for Endocrinology, 2025).

Total testosterone
The key test. Normal or mildly raised → PCOS or idiopathic. >5 nmol/L → urgent referral for investigation of a tumour or CAH. Interpret against the local female reference range; a normal result does not exclude PCOS
SHBG
Often low in PCOS, obesity and insulin resistance, which raises free testosterone even when total testosterone is normal
Free androgen index
(Total testosterone ÷ SHBG) × 100 — use the laboratory's reference range; helpful when total testosterone is borderline
LH and FSH
A raised LH:FSH ratio supports PCOS but is neither required nor sufficient. High FSH with amenorrhoea points to ovarian insufficiency instead
Prolactin
Hyperprolactinaemia causes menstrual disturbance and can mimic PCOS
TSH
Thyroid disease disturbs cycles and SHBG
DHEAS
Where onset is rapid, there is virilisation, or an adrenal source is suspected. Markedly raised → urgent endocrinology. No single safe cut-off is recommended because assays vary (Society for Endocrinology, 2025)
17-OHP
Early morning, follicular phase, if non-classic CAH is suspected (onset around puberty, strong family history, higher-risk ethnicity, raised androgens). Raised → endocrinology for a short Synacthen test
HbA1c and lipids
Where PCOS is likely — the long-term cardiometabolic risk is the part with the biggest effect on health
Cortisol screen
Overnight dexamethasone suppression or late-night salivary cortisol — only if Cushing's features are present

Pelvic ultrasound: not needed to treat hirsutism. In adults it can complete the PCOS criteria — ≥20 follicles per ovary and/or ovarian volume ≥10 mL on a transvaginal scan (International PCOS guideline, 2023) — and it is needed urgently if a mass is suspected or androgens are markedly raised. Do not use ultrasound (or AMH) to diagnose PCOS within 8 years of menarche.

PCOS is diagnosed on two of three features — oligo- or anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology — originally set out in the Rotterdam consensus (ESHRE/ASRM, international, 2003) and carried forward in the International evidence-based PCOS guideline (2023), which now also allows AMH in adults and restricts ovarian imaging in adolescents. The Society for Endocrinology (2025) recommends a standard early-morning, early-follicular sample, a 6–8 week break from hormonal contraception where feasible, and mass-spectrometry confirmation of a high testosterone because immunoassays over-read at low female concentrations. Two traps: a normal total testosterone in a clearly hirsute woman is common (low SHBG raises the free fraction), and testing a woman who is already on the pill gives a falsely reassuring result.

Diagnose · Differential

Establish underlying diagnosis

By far the commonest
PCOS
Gradual onset around puberty, irregular cycles, normal or mildly raised testosterone, low SHBG. Two of three criteria. Screen HbA1c, lipids and BP.
Next most common
Idiopathic hirsutism
Regular ovulatory cycles and normal androgens. Thought to reflect increased skin sensitivity to androgen. Treated the same way as PCOS-related hirsutism.
Uncommon — separate by 17-OHP
Non-classic CAH
Looks like PCOS. Clues: family history, higher-risk ethnicity (for example Ashkenazi Jewish, Mediterranean), early onset. Raised 17-OHP → endocrinology.
Rare but important
Androgen-secreting tumour
Rapid onset, virilisation, testosterone >5 nmol/L or very high DHEAS. Urgent referral for imaging (pelvic ultrasound and adrenal CT per specialist).
After the menopause
Ovarian hyperthecosis vs tumour
New or worsening androgen excess after the menopause needs specialist assessment; hyperthecosis is slower but the two overlap.
Consider
Cushing's syndrome
Only if discriminating features: 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test or late-night salivary cortisol, then endocrinology.
Consider
Hyperprolactinaemia or thyroid disease
Menstrual disturbance plus galactorrhoea or thyroid symptoms. Easily treated — and correcting it often improves the cycle.
Drug-induced
Medication review
Androgens, anabolic steroids, danazol, valproate. Stop or switch where safe. Minoxidil, ciclosporin and phenytoin cause hypertrichosis instead.
In a series of 873 women with androgen excess, PCOS accounted for 82%, 21-hydroxylase non-classic CAH for 1.6% and androgen-secreting tumours for 0.2% (Azziz et al., JCEM 2004). That distribution is why the workup is a short targeted screen, not an exhaustive endocrine hunt: the job is to make the PCOS diagnosis positively, treat its metabolic risk, and pick out the few women whose pace, examination or testosterone does not fit. Non-classic CAH is the great mimic of PCOS, and a morning follicular 17-OHP separates them; the Endocrine Society CAH guideline (Speiser et al., JCEM 2018) uses a Synacthen (ACTH) stimulation test to confirm it. The Society for Endocrinology (2025) points out that severe androgen excess can also come from severe insulin-resistance syndromes, which is why marked acanthosis matters.

Refer

When and where to refer

Urgent Endocrinology
Testosterone >5 nmol/L, markedly raised DHEAS, virilisation, rapid onset, or new androgen excess after the menopause — suspected tumour
Urgent Endocrinology
Suspected Cushing's syndrome (positive screen, or strong clinical features)
2WW Gynaecology — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)
Ascites, or a pelvic or abdominal mass not obviously fibroids; or CA125 at or above the age-specific threshold with an ultrasound suggesting ovarian cancer
Routine Endocrinology
Raised 17-OHP (suspected non-classic CAH), diagnostic uncertainty, or failure of two appropriate treatments given for an adequate time
Routine Gynaecology / fertility
PCOS with subfertility, or ovulation induction needed
Consider Dermatology
Severe hirsutism unresponsive to hormonal treatment, or coexisting severe acne or hidradenitis; laser where locally commissioned
GP manage
Gradual-onset PCOS or idiopathic hirsutism with no virilisation and a testosterone below 5 nmol/L — managed in primary care using the hirsutism protocol

In the letter, give the pace of onset, the mFG score, the testosterone (and whether she was on hormonal contraception at the time), and any virilising sign — these are what the specialist triages on.

Most women with gradual hirsutism have PCOS or idiopathic hirsutism, and both are managed in primary care with a short screen and a structured six-month treatment plan. Urgent referral is kept for the features that predict a tumour or Cushing's — pace, virilisation and a testosterone above 5 nmol/L (Society for Endocrinology, 2025) — because delay in those cases risks progression of a malignant or hormonally active lesion. The purple 2WW route belongs to NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) ovarian criteria only; hirsutism alone is not a suspected-cancer referral. Subfertility in PCOS needs specialist ovulation induction, which is why fertility plans change the referral as well as the treatment.

Treat · Pharmacological

Pharmacological management

Allow 6 months before judging any drug — the hair cycle is slow. Doses, monitoring and the full ladder are on the hirsutism protocol; the reasoning is summarised here.

1st lineCombined hormonal contraceptive (CHC) — lowers ovarian androgen production and raises SHBG, and provides the contraception anti-androgens require. Co-cyprindiol (ethinylestradiol with cyproterone acetate 2 mg) is an option for moderate to severe hirsutism but carries a higher VTE risk than standard CHCs; check UKMEC first. MHRA (June 2020) meningioma restrictions apply to higher-dose cyproterone, not to co-cyprindiol
Add / 2nd lineAnti-androgen if the response is inadequate at 6 months — usually spironolactone (off-label; dose per protocol and BNF). Teratogenic: reliable contraception is essential. Check potassium, renal function and BP. Finasteride and flutamide are specialist-only.
PCOS ± insulin resistanceMetformin — modest effect on hair; more useful for cycle regularity, insulin resistance and fertility. Choose it when metabolic features dominate.
TopicalEflornithine 11.5% cream for facial hirsutism in women — applied twice daily at least 8 hours apart; improvement may be seen within 8 weeks, and the SPC advises stopping if there is no benefit after 4 months. Hair returns towards baseline within about 8 weeks of stopping.

All systemic anti-androgen treatments are teratogenic — reliable contraception is mandatory throughout, and pregnancy plans should be revisited at every review.

The Endocrine Society (international, 2018) suggests a combined oral contraceptive as first-line pharmacological treatment for most premenopausal women, adding an anti-androgen after six months if the response is not enough, and advises against anti-androgen monotherapy without reliable contraception. The MHRA Drug Safety Update of June 2020 restricted higher-dose cyproterone because meningioma risk rises with cumulative dose, and states that this advice does not apply to low-dose co-cyprindiol. Eflornithine slows growth rather than removing hair (Vaniqa 11.5% cream SPC), so it works best alongside a removal method. Metformin is not primarily an anti-hirsutism drug; its value is in the metabolic and reproductive features of PCOS.

Lifestyle · Cosmetic

Non-pharmacological and cosmetic measures

🏃 Weight loss (if BMI raised)Even modest weight loss in PCOS lowers insulin and androgen levels, raises SHBG, and can restore ovulation — it treats the hair, the cycle, fertility and metabolic risk together. Offer real support, not an instruction.
✂️ Cosmetic hair removalShaving, threading, waxing, plucking, depilatory creams and bleaching are legitimate treatment. Shaving does NOT make hair grow back thicker — correct this myth explicitly.
⚡ Laser / IPLWorks best on dark hair; long-pulsed Nd:YAG lasers are safer on darker skin types. Several sessions are needed. Usually private — be honest about NHS availability.
⚡ ElectrolysisThe established permanent method for small areas and for pale or grey hair that laser cannot target. Time-consuming and usually self-funded.
🧠 Psychological supportHirsutism often causes distress out of proportion to the score. Ask about mood, anxiety and avoidance; offer NHS Talking Therapies alongside treatment, not after it.
🚭 Medication reviewStop or switch androgenic drugs where clinically safe, and ask directly and without judgement about anabolic steroids or a partner's testosterone gel.
The International evidence-based PCOS guideline (2023) makes lifestyle intervention the foundation of PCOS care because weight loss improves insulin resistance and, through it, androgen levels and ovulation. Drug treatment only stops new terminal hairs forming; existing hair has to be removed by direct methods, so cosmetic measures are part of treatment rather than an inferior alternative (Endocrine Society, international, 2018). Laser choice depends on skin type because melanin in darker skin absorbs shorter-wavelength light and risks pigment change. Psychological impact is frequently the main reason she has come, and it is routinely under-asked.

Safety · Follow-up

Monitoring, safety-netting and follow-up

6-month review
Repeat the mFG score against baseline, ask about new growth and time spent on removal; add or escalate if inadequate. Benefit continues to build towards 9–12 months
CHC monitoring
Annual BP, BMI and UKMEC review; on co-cyprindiol, review the continuing need regularly because of the VTE risk
Spironolactone
Potassium, renal function and BP after starting (timing per protocol), then periodically; confirm contraception every time
PCOS long-term
Regular BMI, BP, HbA1c and lipids; ask about snoring and daytime sleepiness, and about mood. PCOS carries a raised risk of type 2 diabetes and gestational diabetes
Menstrual regularity
If she has fewer than one bleed every 3–4 months and is not on a CHC, induce a withdrawal bleed with a progestogen (or offer a CHC or the levonorgestrel IUS) to protect the endometrium; persistent abnormal bleeding needs assessment
Safety-net
Return promptly if hair growth speeds up, the voice deepens, the body or genital area changes, new bloating or pelvic pain appears, mood worsens, or pregnancy is possible on an anti-androgen
Fertility planning
When contraception is stopped for pregnancy, stop anti-androgens first and optimise weight; refer for ovulation induction if needed
The RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 33 (Long-term consequences of PCOS, 2014) advises inducing a withdrawal bleed at least every 3–4 months in women with oligo- or amenorrhoea to reduce the risk of endometrial hyperplasia, using a progestogen, a combined pill or the levonorgestrel IUS. The same guideline and the International evidence-based PCOS guideline (2023) highlight the higher risk of type 2 diabetes and gestational diabetes, and recommend periodic glycaemic assessment. A repeated mFG score gives an objective measure of response, and the safety-net covers the rare tumour that declares itself only by an accelerating course after an apparently typical start.
