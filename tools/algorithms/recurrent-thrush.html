1
Safety

Red flags — what hides behind "recurrent thrush"

Four or more episodes a year is a reason to stop and diagnose, not to reissue. Before another antifungal, screen for the conditions below.

Unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for vulval cancer in women (and trans men and non-binary people with female reproductive organs) with an unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding. A raised, ulcerated, warty or thickened area that persists despite antifungal treatment is unexplained → 2WW gynaecology
White, thickened or scarred vulval skin Lichen sclerosus (or lichen planus): itch, fissuring, loss of architecture, no discharge. Needs a potent topical steroid, not an antifungal, and long-term surveillance because of a small (under 5%) risk of vulval SCC (British Association of Dermatologists guideline, 2018).
Oral or oesophageal candida, weight loss, night sweats Painful swallowing with oral plaques suggests oesophageal candidiasis, an AIDS-defining illness → urgent HIV test and specialist referral. Unexplained systemic symptoms → HIV test, FBC and review for haematological disease.
Polyuria, thirst, weight loss Possible new diabetes — glycosuria favours candida. HbA1c for every woman with recurrent symptoms; capillary glucose and ketones today if symptomatic.
Failure to respond to fluconazole Consider a non-albicans species (C. glabrata is often azole-resistant) or a different diagnosis → culture with speciation and sensitivities before any further azole.
Fever, pelvic pain, abnormal bleeding Pelvic inflammatory disease or another gynaecological cause, not candida → same-day assessment.
Recurrent vulvovaginal symptoms are treated empirically for months or years in some women, and the conditions that are missed are the ones that matter: lichen sclerosus and lichen planus (which scar and carry a small cancer risk), vulval intraepithelial neoplasia and vulval cancer, undiagnosed diabetes, and HIV. NG12 frames the cancer criterion in terms of an unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding; in practice a vulval abnormality that has not settled with treatment is unexplained, and a further antifungal prescription is not a substitute for looking at the skin. The British Association of Dermatologists guideline (2018) quotes a lifetime SCC risk under 5% in genital lichen sclerosus, which is why the diagnosis needs making and follow-up is lifelong.

Diagnose

Is it really recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis (RVVC)?

Definition
BASHH UK guideline (2019): four or more symptomatic episodes in 12 months, at least two confirmed by microscopy or culture, and at least one of those a positive culture with moderate or heavy growth.
Typical candida
Vulval itch and soreness, superficial dyspareunia, external dysuria, thick white non-offensive discharge; erythema, fissuring, satellite lesions; vaginal pH normal (4.5 or below).
Species
C. albicans causes most episodes. Non-albicans species, especially C. glabrata, are more likely in women with type 2 diabetes, older and postmenopausal women (BASHH 2019), and respond poorly to azoles.
Predisposing factors
Diabetes and poor glycaemic control · SGLT2 inhibitors (genital fungal infection is a common adverse effect in the SPCs because they cause glycosuria) · recent broad-spectrum antibiotics · corticosteroids, immunosuppression, HIV · pregnancy · irritant products that damage the vulval skin barrier.
Severe or complicated episodes
Extensive vulval erythema, oedema, excoriation or fissuring, pregnancy, poorly controlled diabetes, immunosuppression or a non-albicans species make an episode complicated: longer or repeated treatment is usually needed, and the underlying driver deserves as much attention as the organism.
Pattern clues
Cyclical premenstrual flares; complete resolution between episodes (true recurrence) versus never fully clearing (think again); after antibiotics; after starting an SGLT2 inhibitor; self-diagnosis only, never confirmed.
The BASHH definition requires laboratory confirmation for a reason: many women with recurrent "thrush" have never had a positive culture, and self-diagnosis of candida is unreliable. Committing someone to six months of weekly fluconazole should therefore wait for culture proof and speciation. Normal vaginal pH is a useful discriminator: candida does not raise the pH, whereas bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis and atrophic vaginitis do. SGLT2 inhibitors deserve a specific question, because their glucose-lowering mechanism is glycosuria and genital fungal infection is one of their commonest adverse effects. For the SCA, a strong candidate states the definition, explains why confirmation matters before long-term treatment, and asks what the patient thinks is going on: many women have tried numerous over-the-counter products, and some fear a sexually transmitted cause or cancer. Naming those concerns and explaining the plan to test for them usually earns more trust than a further prescription.

Diagnose

Assessment and investigations

History
Number of episodes in the last year and how each was confirmed · itch versus burning versus pain · discharge character and odour · relation to menses, sex, antibiotics · response to each treatment and whether symptoms ever fully cleared · products used on the vulva · contraception, pregnancy possibility, menopause status · diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitor, steroids, immunosuppression · sexual history and STI risk.
Examination
Always examine, ideally while symptomatic. Vulval skin: erythema and satellite lesions (candida), whitening, thickening, loss of labial architecture, fissures (lichen sclerosus), erosions (lichen planus), ulcers, lumps or warty areas. Speculum: discharge character, vaginal walls, cervix.
Bedside tests
Vaginal pH 4.5 or below fits candida; above 4.5 suggests BV, trichomoniasis or atrophy. Cotton-swab touch test of the vestibule if pain or burning dominates.
Laboratory
High vaginal swab for microscopy and culture with speciation and sensitivities — ask for this explicitly on the form · HbA1c · STI tests if at risk · HIV test offered where there are risk factors or features of immunosuppression · pregnancy test before systemic azole.
Taking the swab
Take it from the lateral vaginal wall or posterior fornix, ideally while symptomatic and ideally at least a week after the last antifungal, which can otherwise give a false negative. State on the form: recurrent symptoms, request speciation and sensitivities.
Consider
FBC and ferritin if clinically suggested; vulval biopsy (via vulval clinic or dermatology) where a dermatosis or neoplasia is possible.
The investigation that changes management most is a swab taken while symptomatic and sent for culture with speciation: it confirms or refutes candida, identifies non-albicans species that will not respond to fluconazole, and guides specialist therapy. Many laboratories only speciate on request, so the request form matters. Examining the vulval skin itself, rather than only the discharge, is what detects lichen sclerosus, lichen planus and neoplasia. The HbA1c is cheap and occasionally transformative. A pregnancy test is needed because oral fluconazole should be avoided in pregnancy. Where the patient is between episodes and the examination is normal, arrange a return appointment to be examined and swabbed during the next flare, and ask her not to self-treat beforehand so that the swab can be interpreted.

Diagnose

Differential diagnosis — the feature that separates each

Bacterial vaginosis
Thin grey-white discharge with a fishy odour, often worse after sex or menses; little itch or inflammation; pH above 4.5. See the BV protocol.
Trichomoniasis
Frothy yellow-green offensive discharge, vulvitis, pH above 4.5; a sexually transmitted infection — test and refer to sexual health.
Lichen sclerosus
Intense itch, often nocturnal; white, crinkled or thickened skin in a figure-of-eight around the vulva and anus; purpura, fissures, loss of labia minora; little or no discharge; no lasting response to antifungals.
Erosive lichen planus
Painful glazed red erosions with a white lacy edge, vaginal involvement and adhesions, often with oral lesions.
Irritant or allergic contact dermatitis
Itch and burning linked to soaps, wipes, pads, spermicides, topical medicines (including repeated antifungal creams); erythema without satellite lesions; negative culture.
Vulvodynia
Burning or rawness rather than itch, provoked by touch, sex or tampons; normal-looking skin; repeatedly negative cultures; positive cotton-swab test at the vestibule. See vulval disorders.
Genital herpes
Recurrent painful blisters or ulcers in the same area, often with prodrome; swab for HSV PCR.
Atrophic vaginitis
Peri- or postmenopausal dryness, soreness and dyspareunia; pale thin mucosa; pH raised.
Desquamative inflammatory vaginitis
Purulent discharge, marked vaginal inflammation and dyspareunia, pH raised; many white cells and absent lactobacilli on microscopy; specialist diagnosis.
Eczema, psoriasis, intertrigo
Itchy red plaques in the skin folds and on the mons, with eczema or psoriasis elsewhere (scalp, nails, elbows); candida may colonise secondarily but is not the primary problem.
Vulval intraepithelial neoplasia / cancer
Persistent raised, pigmented, warty, ulcerated or thickened area, itch or bleeding → NG12 route (Step 1).
Each of these is commonly labelled as thrush, and none responds to antifungal treatment. Three bedside discriminators do most of the work: the pH (normal in candida, raised in BV, trichomoniasis, atrophy and DIV), the dominant symptom (itch favours candida and dermatoses; burning or pain with normal skin favours vulvodynia), and the appearance of the skin itself (white and scarred in lichen sclerosus, eroded in lichen planus). A swab that repeatedly grows nothing while symptoms persist is itself diagnostic information: it points away from candida and towards a dermatosis, vulvodynia or an irritant cause. Repeated use of topical antifungals can itself cause irritant dermatitis, so a woman who is worse the more cream she uses may have a contact problem rather than resistant candida.

Refer

Referral pathways

2WW · NICE NG12
Unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding → suspected cancer pathway referral to gynaecology (NICE NG12, updated April 2026). Includes a persisting abnormal area on a background of lichen sclerosus.
Urgent
Suspected oesophageal candidiasis → urgent HIV test and specialist referral. Fever with pelvic pain → same-day assessment.
Vulval clinic / gynaecology / dermatology
Suspected lichen sclerosus or lichen planus needing confirmation or not responding to treatment · vulvodynia · desquamative inflammatory vaginitis · RVVC not controlled on a suppressive regimen.
Sexual health (GUM)
Non-albicans or azole-resistant candida needing specialist agents · positive STI tests · complex or immunosuppressed patients.
Diabetes team
New diabetes or poor control · RVVC attributable to an SGLT2 inhibitor where the benefit of continuing needs weighing with the diabetes plan.
Primary care
Culture-confirmed C. albicans RVVC with no red flags: induction and maintenance regimen, trigger review, and a planned end-point.

Letters and the full referral table: recurrent thrush protocol.

Most culture-confirmed C. albicans RVVC is managed well in primary care. Referral is for the three groups primary care cannot resolve: a suspected cancer or scarring dermatosis (where NG12 and the vulval clinic apply and biopsy may be needed), a non-albicans or resistant organism (where the effective agents are specialist-initiated or unlicensed), and a diagnosis that is not candida at all (vulvodynia, DIV, lichen planus). The NG12 criterion is stated here with its exact wording because the commonest failure is repeated prescribing for a vulval abnormality that has never been examined.

Treat

Antifungal treatment — summary (detail in the protocol)

Acute episode
A single oral azole or an intravaginal imidazole, with a topical imidazole cream for vulval symptoms (BASHH 2019). Doses per BNF.
Confirmed C. albicans RVVC
BASHH 2019 induction: fluconazole 150 mg every 72 hours for 3 doses; then maintenance fluconazole 150 mg once weekly for 6 months. Explain that this suppresses rather than cures and that relapse after stopping is common.
Pregnancy
Topical imidazole (e.g. clotrimazole) for 7 days. Avoid oral fluconazole: the BNF advises avoiding it, following a regulatory review of miscarriage and malformation reports.
Contraception
Anyone who could become pregnant on weekly fluconazole needs effective contraception.
Before prescribing
Check the BNF for interactions: fluconazole inhibits the metabolism of several drugs (for example warfarin, some statins, sulfonylureas and QT-prolonging medicines). Hepatic disease and existing QT prolongation need care.
Non-albicans / azole-resistant
Do not keep prescribing fluconazole. Specialist options include nystatin pessaries and boric acid pessaries (unlicensed; intravaginal only). Ibrexafungerp is not licensed in the UK.

Regimens, monitoring and counselling: recurrent thrush protocol.

The induction-then-maintenance regimen is the BASHH 2019 recommendation, based on Sobel et al. (NEJM 2004), in which weekly fluconazole kept most women free of symptomatic episodes during treatment but relapse was common once it stopped. That is why the counselling matters as much as the prescription. Fluconazole should be avoided in pregnancy, and a woman on a six-month weekly course needs reliable contraception. The earlier version of this pathway described ibrexafungerp as MHRA-approved and gave a once-daily dose; it is not licensed in the UK, and specialist-only agents are now referred to the protocol without doses.

Treat

Specific scenarios that change the plan

On an SGLT2 inhibitor
Explain the link; treat the episode; discuss with the patient (and diabetes team if needed) whether the cardiorenal benefit justifies continuing with antifungal cover or whether an alternative is better. Do not stop it unilaterally where it is prescribed for heart failure or CKD.
Diabetes
Improve glycaemic control; recheck HbA1c; each episode is a prompt to review the diabetes plan.
Antibiotic-triggered
Avoid unnecessary antibiotics; some women benefit from an antifungal taken with each necessary course — a pragmatic, individual decision.
Hormonal pattern
Cyclical premenstrual flares or onset with a new combined hormonal method: discuss contraceptive alternatives if the pattern is clear.
Pregnancy
Topical treatment only; longer courses may be needed; check for gestational diabetes where risk factors exist.
Immunosuppressed
Steroids, biologics, chemotherapy or HIV: involve the specialist team; consider non-albicans species early.
Oral candida on an inhaled steroid
Check inhaler technique, use a spacer with a metered-dose inhaler and rinse and spit after each dose.
These scenarios are where the underlying driver, rather than the candida, needs addressing. The SGLT2 inhibitor question is a shared decision: the drug may be prescribed for heart failure or chronic kidney disease where its benefit is substantial, so managing the infection is usually preferable to stopping it. Glycaemic control and avoiding unnecessary antibiotics reduce the substrate and the disruption of vaginal flora that allow candida to recur. Numerical claims in the earlier version about inhaler rinsing and spacer effect could not be verified against a primary source and have been removed.

Lifestyle

Vulval skin care and trigger avoidance

Soap substitute Wash the vulva with water or a plain emollient; avoid soaps, shower gels, bubble bath, intimate washes and wipes.
No douching Vaginal douching disrupts normal flora and is not recommended.
Barrier and comfort A bland emollient can be used as a barrier for soreness; loose cotton underwear; change promptly out of wet or sweaty clothing.
Products Avoid perfumed pads and liners, spermicides and flavoured or sugary lubricants; stop repeated self-treatment with antifungal creams if they sting.
Probiotics Oral and vaginal lactobacillus products are widely sold; evidence of benefit in RVVC is limited and they are not a substitute for confirmed-diagnosis treatment.
Sex and dyspareunia Use a plain water-based lubricant; pause intercourse while the vulva is fissured or raw. Pain that persists between flares, with a normal-looking vulva, is a cue to reconsider vulvodynia (Step 4) rather than candida.
Clothing and exercise Breathable fabrics, avoid prolonged tight or synthetic clothing, and shower and change after swimming or exercise.
Partners A partner with symptoms (e.g. balanitis) should be assessed; routine treatment of an asymptomatic partner is not usually needed.
Irritant damage to the vulval skin both mimics candida and makes it more likely to recur, so skin care is part of the treatment rather than an extra. Avoiding soaps and using an emollient as a soap substitute is simple and safe. Probiotics and partner treatment are areas where the evidence is weak, and the pathway now presents them as clinical practice rather than as proven measures; the protocol page gives a stronger view on both, which has been flagged to the site owner.

Safety

Follow-up and safety-netting

Before the next prescription
If an episode has not been confirmed on culture, or the diagnosis has not been reviewed after four or more episodes a year, examine and swab before prescribing again.
During maintenance
Review tolerability, breakthrough episodes, pregnancy risk and interacting medicines; plan a documented stop date at 6 months.
After stopping
Relapse is common: agree what to do (repeat swab while symptomatic; restart if confirmed) rather than open-ended repeats.
Lichen sclerosus
Lifelong follow-up; any new lump, ulcer or thickened area → urgent review and NG12 route.
Documentation
Record the number of confirmed episodes, culture and species results, examination findings (including the vulval skin), HbA1c and the agreed stop date — the next clinician needs these to avoid starting the cycle again.
Seek help urgently
Painful swallowing, a vulval lump, sore or bleeding that does not heal, or fever with pelvic pain.
Book a review
No improvement 7–14 days after treatment, burning or pain without discharge, or skin that looks white or thickened.

Follow-up schedule and patient wording: recurrent thrush protocol.

Safety-netting in RVVC is mostly about preventing drift into years of unexamined empirical treatment. A clear rule helps: no further prescription without examination and culture once a woman meets the recurrence definition. A planned end-point for maintenance, with a written plan for relapse, keeps the regimen purposeful. The urgent return criteria cover the diagnoses that must not be missed: oesophageal candidiasis, vulval neoplasia and pelvic infection. In a consultation, a useful framing is to agree the plan in two halves: first confirm what it is (swab, blood test, examination), then treat properly with a defined course and a defined review, so the patient understands why today may not end with another tablet. Women who have been told repeatedly that they have thrush are often relieved rather than disappointed when someone looks for another explanation.
