Safety

Red Flags — Haematological Malignancy, Haemolytic Crisis & Aplastic Anaemia

Normocytic anaemia + thrombocytopenia + leucopenia (pancytopenia) + bone pain or constitutional symptoms Possible bone marrow failure — aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, myeloma, or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS); also consider infection, nutritional deficiency, drugs and hypersplenism. → Urgent review: repeat FBC with differential and blood film, reticulocytes, medication review. Same-day hospital/haematology assessment if blasts, febrile neutropenia, active bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, rapidly progressive cytopenias or significant clinical deterioration. Iron only if iron deficiency is documented — empirical treatment must not delay assessment for marrow disease.
Normocytic anaemia + rapidly falling Hb over days-weeks + jaundice + dark urine + splenomegaly Haemolytic anaemia — autoimmune (AIHA), haemoglobinopathy crisis (sickle cell, G6PD), TTP, HUS. → Suspected microangiopathic haemolysis (anaemia, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, neurological symptoms, renal impairment or fever — a complete pentad is not required): immediate hospital/haematology assessment; do not wait for ADAMTS13 results. Stable patient with suspected AIHA (positive DAT): urgent haematology discussion, not necessarily 999. Tests: FBC, reticulocytes, blood film, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, DAT, renal function, urine testing.
Severe or rapidly progressive anaemia + chest pain, syncope, haemodynamic instability, acute dyspnoea, confusion, active bleeding or suspected myocardial ischaemia → Emergency assessment. Do not delay transfer while attempting to establish a transfusion plan in primary care; transfusion decisions (a restrictive threshold around 70 g/L is common in stable adults) depend on symptoms, haemodynamic status, bleeding, rate of fall, cardiovascular disease, patient preference and local protocol.
Normocytic anaemia + elevated creatinine + proteinuria + hypertension Anaemia of chronic kidney disease (CKD) — EPO deficiency. → Urgency depends on Hb level and symptoms. Do not attribute anaemia to CKD from eGFR alone (NICE NG203): eGFR >60 — investigate other causes; 30–60 — investigate other causes using clinical judgement; <30 — CKD more likely to contribute but still consider other causes. Nephrology referral per CKD referral criteria (rate of decline, albuminuria, resistant hypertension, electrolyte problems, local pathway), not anaemia alone; renal ultrasound is not routine for presumed CKD anaemia.
Normocytic anaemia + weight loss + lymphadenopathy + night sweats + hepatosplenomegaly Lymphoma or chronic leukaemia. → haematology suspected cancer pathway referral (staging arranged by the pathway). Blood film for atypical lymphocytes or blast cells.
Anaemia in pregnancy (Hb <110 g/L first trimester; <105 g/L second/third trimester; <100 g/L postpartum) Risk of prematurity, low birthweight, postpartum haemorrhage. → Urgent obstetric/haematology advice for severe or symptomatic anaemia, rapid deterioration, poor response to oral iron, suspected haemolysis or marrow disease, late gestation with significant anaemia, or Hb <100 g/L approaching delivery. Ferritin + B12 + folate; haemoglobinopathy status (antenatal sickle cell/thalassaemia screen).
The blood film examination is the most important investigation in normocytic anaemia that GPs rarely request but haematologists rely on — the peripheral blood film provides information that the automated FBC cannot: the morphology of red cells (spherocytes in AIHA or hereditary spherocytosis; sickle cells; target cells in haemoglobin C disease; schistocytes/fragmented cells in TTP/HUS/MAHA — mechanical haemolysis); the white cell differential (blast cells in acute leukaemia, smear cells in CLL, atypical lymphocytes in viral infection); and platelet morphology (large platelets in ITP, platelet clumping). Any patient with unexplained normocytic anaemia — particularly with abnormal WBC or platelets — should have a blood film requested or the laboratory asked to perform a manual film review. This single investigation can identify TTP (schistocytes = haematological emergency), acute leukaemia (blasts), or CLL (smear cells) within minutes.

Classification of Normocytic Anaemia

Definition and MCV context
Normocytic anaemia: Hb below normal (men <130 g/L; non-pregnant women <120 g/L; pregnancy <110 g/L first trimester and <105 g/L second/third trimester; postpartum <100 g/L) with MCV 80-100 fL. MCV is a starting point, not a diagnosis — normocytic anaemia may reflect early iron deficiency, mixed iron/B12/folate deficiency, CKD, inflammation, blood loss, haemolysis, endocrine or liver disease, marrow disease, haemoglobinopathy, or alcohol/drug effects. Note: mixed deficiency states (iron + B12/folate combined deficiency) can produce a normal MCV masking both microcytic and macrocytic components — check ferritin + B12 + folate even with normal MCV. Also: early iron deficiency (before microcytosis develops) presents as normocytic anaemia with reduced ferritin.
Main causes — systematic framework
Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD) (a common cause): inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 → hepcidin) sequester iron in reticuloendothelial system — elevated ferritin + low serum iron + low TIBC. Occurs in: RA, SLE, IBD, malignancy, chronic infection (TB, HIV, osteomyelitis), CKD. CKD anaemia: EPO deficiency — more likely to contribute as eGFR falls (especially <30); with eGFR >60 investigate other causes (NICE NG203). Acute blood loss: haemorrhage (GI, trauma) — early phase normocytic (iron deficiency anaemia takes days-weeks to develop microcytosis). Hypothyroidism: usually normocytic (occasionally macrocytic). Haemolytic anaemia: elevated reticulocytes + unconjugated bilirubin + LDH. Aplastic anaemia/MDS: pancytopenia. Early pregnancy: physiological dilutional anaemia.
Distinguishing ACD from iron deficiency
Iron deficiency (microcytic or normocytic early): serum ferritin <30 µg/L in many otherwise well adults (<15 µg/L = absent stores); serum iron low (but affected by diurnal variation, recent iron intake and inflammation — do not over-emphasise); TIBC elevated (transferrin upregulated); transferrin saturation <20%. ACD alone: serum ferritin elevated (acute phase reactant); serum iron low; TIBC low/normal (transferrin downregulated); transferrin saturation variable. ACD + iron deficiency coexisting: most common in IBD, malignancy — ferritin may be normal (falsely reassuring) — a normal ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency with inflammation, infection, CKD, malignancy, liver disease or obesity; interpret with CRP and transferrin saturation. sTfR has limited availability and variable assays and is not generally required in primary care. Reticulocyte haemoglobin thresholds are validated in CKD (NICE NG203: CHr/Ret-He <29 pg or hypochromic red cells >6%) — do not transfer them to general adult anaemia without qualification.
Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD) and iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) frequently coexist — particularly in patients with inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, or malignancy. The challenge: ferritin (the standard iron stores marker) is an acute phase protein that rises with inflammation, meaning ferritin can be normal or elevated even when true iron stores are depleted in the context of inflammation. This leads to missed iron deficiency in inflammatory conditions. In practice, interpret ferritin with CRP, transferrin saturation and the clinical context rather than any single threshold. Reticulocyte haemoglobin content (CHr or Ret-He) or % hypochromic red cells are recommended in CKD where available (NICE NG203); sTfR and the sTfR/log ferritin ratio have limited availability and variable assays and are not routinely needed in primary care. Seek specialist advice if iron status remains uncertain.

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Previous FBCs and trend (MCV, MCH, RDW, WBC, platelets). Pregnancy status. Symptoms of anaemia: fatigue, dyspnoea on exertion, palpitations, pallor, headache, reduced exercise tolerance. Severity and speed of onset (acute blood loss = rapid onset; chronic ACD = insidious). Bleeding history: GI (melaena, haematochezia, haematemesis, rectal bleeding — see change in bowel habits algorithm), menorrhagia (often missed as cause of acute-on-chronic blood loss), haematuria. Chronic disease history: RA, SLE, IBD, CKD, malignancy, HIV, chronic infection (TB, osteomyelitis). Diet: vegan/vegetarian (B12 + iron), alcohol (folate, B12 malabsorption). Medications: NSAIDs (GI blood loss), chemotherapy (bone marrow suppression), immunosuppressants. Family history: haemoglobinopathy (sickle cell, thalassaemia). Ethnic background: haemoglobinopathy risk.
Examination
Conjunctival pallor (moderate/severe anaemia — unreliable sign). Jaundice (haemolysis, hepatic disease). Koilonychia (iron deficiency — late sign). Angular cheilitis, glossitis (iron, B12, folate deficiency). Lymphadenopathy (lymphoma, CLL, viral illness). Splenomegaly (haemolysis, haematological malignancy, portal hypertension). Hepatomegaly (malignancy, haematological, hepatic). Bone tenderness (sternum, long bones — myeloma, leukaemia, metastases). Signs of chronic disease (RA deformity, CKD — pallid + uraemic, SLE rash).
Investigations — initial set
Repeat FBC with differential + red-cell indices · Reticulocyte count (elevated = haemolysis or blood loss; low = underproduction) · Blood film (if unexplained, progressive, other cytopenias, abnormal indices or suspected haemolysis) · Ferritin (interpreted with CRP/inflammation; transferrin saturation where ferritin may mislead) · B12 + folate (where clinically indicated; total or active B12, MMA if indeterminate — NICE NG239) · CRP (ESR only if it will alter management) · U&Es + eGFR · LFTs + bilirubin · Urinalysis (haematuria, proteinuria) · TSH (if clinically indicated) · Pregnancy test (where relevant) · Coeliac serology (if iron deficiency, chronic diarrhoea or malabsorption)
If haemolysis suspected
LDH · Unconjugated bilirubin · Haptoglobin · DAT (Coombs test) · Blood film · Reticulocytes · urine testing for haemoglobin/urobilinogen where relevant
Guided by findings
Paraprotein studies and serum free light chains, HIV testing, haemoglobinopathy testing and imaging — according to history, examination and initial results, not automatically in all normocytic anaemia.
The reticulocyte count is the pivotal investigation that separates the two fundamental mechanisms of normocytic anaemia: reticulocytosis (elevated reticulocytes) indicates the bone marrow is responding appropriately to anaemia from haemolysis or blood loss — the marrow is working; reticulocytopenia (low or inappropriately normal reticulocytes) indicates impaired bone marrow production — hypoproliferative, aplastic, or infiltrative process. This single result directs the entire subsequent investigation pathway: elevated reticulocytes (>100 × 10⁹/L) → investigate for haemolysis (DAT, LDH, unconjugated bilirubin, haptoglobin, blood film for schistocytes/spherocytes) or acute haemorrhage; low or normal reticulocytes with anaemia → investigate for marrow failure, CKD, hypothyroid, ACD, or early iron/B12 deficiency. Requesting a reticulocyte count is a free addition to the FBC in most UK hospital laboratories and should be standard practice for any unexplained normocytic anaemia.

Haemolytic Anaemia, CKD Anaemia & Bone Marrow Assessment

Haemolytic anaemia — classification
Intravascular haemolysis (RBC destroyed within blood vessels): haemoglobinuria (dark urine), haemoglobinaemia, very low haptoglobin, elevated LDH + unconjugated bilirubin. Causes: TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura — ADAMTS13 deficiency), HUS, severe AIHA, transfusion reaction, G6PD deficiency with trigger, paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH). Extravascular haemolysis (RBC destroyed in spleen/liver): jaundice, splenomegaly, modest LDH elevation. Causes: AIHA (warm — IgG — positive DAT; cold — IgM — cold agglutinins), hereditary spherocytosis, haemoglobinopathies. AIHA: warm (most common, autoimmune — secondary to lymphoma, SLE, CLL, drugs including methyldopa, penicillin) or cold (secondary to Mycoplasma, EBV, lymphoma).
CKD anaemia
Mechanism: reduced EPO production from damaged peritubular cells + shortened RBC survival + iron deficiency (blood loss from dialysis, reduced GI absorption). Features: normocytic normochromic anaemia, low reticulocytes, normal/elevated ferritin (may coexist with functional iron deficiency). Investigate other causes first according to eGFR (NICE NG203). Iron status in CKD: % hypochromic red cells or Ret-He where available, otherwise ferritin with TSAT — not ferritin or TSAT alone. Oral vs IV iron depends on CKD stage, dialysis status, severity, previous response, tolerability and shared decision-making (people on haemodialysis generally need IV iron; non-dialysis CKD does not automatically). ESA or HIF-PHI therapy is initiated by the renal team with dosing per the renal shared-care plan — not in general practice. Aim for Hb approximately 100-120 g/L, not normalisation (avoid >130 g/L — cardiovascular risk).
Bone marrow assessment — when to refer
Haematology referral (bone marrow examination is a specialist decision): pancytopenia (Hb + WBC + platelets all low), blast cells or dysplastic cells on blood film, unexplained normocytic anaemia not responding to treatment after appropriate investigation, paraprotein on electrophoresis (myeloma screen), suspected MDS (elderly patient, macrocytosis, dysplastic neutrophils on film).
The TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura) pentad must be in every GP's emergency diagnostic vocabulary because it is rapidly fatal without treatment — the classic pentad: thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA — schistocytes on blood film), fever, renal failure, and neurological symptoms (confusion, stroke-like). In practice, the majority of patients present with only thrombocytopenia + MAHA without all five features. The mechanism: deficiency of ADAMTS13 (a protease that cleaves von Willebrand factor multimers) allows ultra-large vWF multimers to accumulate, causing platelet adhesion and microthrombi throughout the microvasculature. Diagnosis: blood film showing schistocytes (fragmented red cells) + thrombocytopenia in an unwell patient = haematology emergency — arrange immediate hospital assessment and do not wait for ADAMTS13 results. Treatment: plasma exchange (large volume — replaces ADAMTS13) + corticosteroids + rituximab. Without treatment, mortality is approximately 90%; with treatment, survival is approximately 80-90%.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day hospital / haematology (999 if unstable)
Suspected TTP/microangiopathic haemolysis · Severe symptomatic or rapidly falling Hb · Active bleeding or haemodynamic instability · Blasts or severe cytopenias · Febrile neutropenia · Severe sickle-cell complications · Suspected acute haemolytic crisis · Severe anaemia with chest pain, syncope, hypoxia or confusion
Haematology (urgent)
Unexplained normocytic anaemia not responding to treatment after 4-6 weeks · Suspected haematological malignancy (lymphadenopathy + constitutional symptoms) · Haemolytic anaemia confirmed (DAT positive or raised LDH + bili) — urgent discussion · Stable pancytopenia (urgent review) · Suspected MDS or aplastic anaemia
Nephrology
Suspected CKD anaemia that is persistent, symptomatic, severe, associated with advanced CKD, or requiring IV iron, ESA or HIF-PHI therapy (renal team) · CKD referral per NICE NG203 criteria — not anaemia alone
Gastroenterology
Iron-deficiency anaemia in men and post-menopausal women → bidirectional GI investigation (gastroscopy + colonoscopy, BSG 2021) · NICE NG12: offer quantitative FIT to adults with iron-deficiency anaemia and to people aged 60+ with anaemia even without iron deficiency — FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → colorectal suspected cancer pathway. A negative FIT does not exclude cancer; do not hold persistent or concerning symptoms in primary care solely because FIT is below threshold, and do not make rectal/abdominal masses wait for FIT — follow local colorectal pathways.
GP management
Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD): treat underlying disease (optimise RA/IBD treatment, treat infection). Iron deficiency (including with ACD): one oral iron tablet daily providing approximately 50–100 mg elemental iron (GMMMG first choice ferrous fumarate 322/305 mg ≈ 100 mg; alternative ferrous sulfate 200 mg ≈ 65 mg; alternate days if not tolerated — BSG 2021) + investigate the source. B12 deficiency: take samples before treatment; oral or IM according to cause, severity, neurological involvement, adherence and preference (NICE NG239) — IM hydroxocobalamin doses per BNF indication; lifelong IM for autoimmune gastritis, total gastrectomy or complete terminal ileal resection. Folate deficiency: folic acid 5 mg OD (commonly 4 months; follow local prescribing guidance and treat the cause) — check or treat possible B12 deficiency first; folate must not delay B12 treatment. Hypothyroid-related: levothyroxine.
The ferrous sulphate prescribing convention for iron deficiency anaemia — 200 mg tablet contains 65 mg elemental iron — is a source of confusion because oral iron preparations are labelled in total salt weight not elemental iron content. A practical starting dose is one preparation providing approximately 50–100 mg elemental iron once daily. BSG 2021 advises one tablet once daily (e.g. ferrous sulphate 200 mg = 65 mg elemental iron), or on alternate days if not tolerated — hepcidin rises after each dose and blocks further absorption for about 24 hours, so more frequent dosing adds side effects more than benefit. Tolerability differs between individuals and preparations; avoid modified-release preparations. The GMMMG formulary lists ferrous fumarate 322/305 mg (≈100 mg elemental iron) as first choice and ferrous sulfate 200 mg (≈65 mg) as an alternative. The most common reason for treatment failure with oral iron is GI side effects (nausea, constipation, abdominal cramps) leading to non-adherence — taking iron with food reduces side effects at the cost of approximately 40% reduction in absorption. IV iron is not a routine GP treatment: it is given through the local hospital/ambulatory pathway (resuscitation facilities, staff able to manage anaphylaxis, observation during and for at least 30 minutes after) when oral iron is not tolerated, not absorbed (e.g. coeliac, IBD), ineffective, or rapid correction is required (perioperative, late pregnancy under obstetric care). Ferric carboxymaltose carries a risk of hypophosphataemia (MHRA).

Treat

Anaemia Treatment by Cause

ACD — treat the underlying diseasePrimary treatment: optimise management of the inflammatory/chronic condition (RA → DMARD escalation; IBD → specialist review; CKD → optimise renal care; HIV → ART). Iron supplementation: only if coexisting iron deficiency confirmed (interpret ferritin with CRP and transferrin saturation — ferritin can be normal despite iron deficiency in inflammation). ACD alone: no role for iron, B12, folate, or EPO in primary care — refer if Hb significantly impacting QoL.
Iron deficiency (contributing to normocytic anaemia)One oral iron preparation providing approximately 50–100 mg elemental iron once daily (e.g. ferrous fumarate 322/305 mg — GMMMG first choice — or ferrous sulfate 200 mg); take fasting if tolerated, with food if needed for adherence; alternate-day dosing or a different preparation if GI adverse effects (BSG 2021); avoid modified-release preparations. Do not routinely delay iron while investigations are arranged. Assess response at approximately 2–4 weeks (a rise of at least 10 g/L within 2 weeks supports iron deficiency and treatment response). Continue for approximately 2–3 months after Hb normalises, longer if ongoing losses or malabsorption persist. IV iron: via the local hospital/ambulatory pathway (resuscitation facilities; observe for at least 30 minutes) when oral iron is not tolerated, not absorbed, ineffective, or rapid correction is required — check the local formulary and SPC. Ferric carboxymaltose: hypophosphataemia risk (MHRA — monitor phosphate with repeated high doses, long-term treatment or risk factors such as vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption, IBD, secondary hyperparathyroidism or osteoporosis). CRITICAL: identify and treat the CAUSE of iron deficiency (GI blood loss, menorrhagia, NSAIDs, urinary blood loss, malabsorption — consider coeliac disease) — do not just replace without finding source.
B12/folate deficiency (contributing to normocytic or macrocytic)Take B12 and folate samples before treatment where possible. If neurological symptoms or severe megaloblastic anaemia are present, start B12 promptly and do not wait for results. Choose oral or IM replacement according to cause, severity, neurological involvement, adherence and patient preference (NICE NG239). IM hydroxocobalamin (BNF): without neurological involvement 1 mg three times a week for 2 weeks, then every 2–3 months; with neurological involvement 1 mg on alternate days until no further improvement, then every 2 months. Lifelong IM for autoimmune gastritis (pernicious anaemia), total gastrectomy or complete terminal ileal resection. Dietary deficiency (e.g. vegan): oral cyanocobalamin 50–150 micrograms daily (BNF); if malabsorption is suspected but IM is not selected, at least 1 mg daily orally (NICE NG239). Check B12 before treating folate (treating folate deficiency without B12 in combined deficiency can precipitate subacute combined degeneration of the cord). Folate deficiency: folic acid 5 mg OD (commonly 4 months — follow local prescribing guidance and treat the cause); folate treatment must not delay B12 treatment where B12 deficiency is suspected. Periconceptional: 400 mcg OD (5 mg if high risk — previous NTD, diabetes, epilepsy on enzyme-inducing AEDs).
CKD anaemiaSuspected CKD anaemia should be investigated and managed with the renal team when persistent, symptomatic, severe, associated with advanced CKD, or requiring IV iron, ESA or HIF-PHI therapy. Iron status: % hypochromic red cells or Ret-He where available, otherwise ferritin with TSAT (NICE NG203). Oral or IV iron according to CKD stage, dialysis status, severity, previous response, tolerability and shared decision-making — IV iron is not automatically preferred outside haemodialysis, is not given during active infection and requires resuscitation facilities. ESA and roxadustat (HIF-PHI; NICE TA: symptomatic anaemia, CKD stage 3–5, no iron deficiency, not on dialysis at initiation — an alternative to ESA) are initiated and monitored by the renal service, with dosing per the renal shared-care plan. Aim for Hb approximately 100–120 g/L, not normalisation (avoid >130). Primary care monitors per the agreed shared-care plan and does not independently initiate ESA or roxadustat.
The subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (SACD) from B12 deficiency is a preventable neurological catastrophe — the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord are demyelinated by B12 deficiency, causing a progressive syndrome: posterior column signs (loss of vibration sense, proprioception → sensory ataxia — Romberg positive), lateral column signs (upper motor neurone — pyramidal weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia), and sometimes dementia or optic neuropathy. The critical principle: NEVER give folic acid alone to a patient with macrocytic anaemia without first confirming that B12 is normal — folate supplementation in B12-deficient patients can partially correct the haematological abnormalities (MCV, anaemia) while the neurological B12 deficiency continues to progress unchecked, potentially irreversibly. The rule: always check B12 before treating folate deficiency, or treat both simultaneously if B12 status is uncertain.

Treat

Haemolytic Anaemia & Sickle Cell — Hospital Pathways

AIHA (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia)
Suspected autoimmune haemolytic anaemia requires urgent haematology discussion. Treatment (corticosteroids, rituximab, splenectomy) is haematology-led — do not initiate corticosteroids or rituximab in primary care unless specifically directed by haematology. If transfusion is clinically required, do not delay it solely because compatibility testing is complex; liaise urgently with haematology and transfusion medicine. Cold AIHA: keep the patient warm (cold triggers haemolysis).
Sickle cell disease — acute problems
Patients with sickle-cell disease and severe/atypical pain, fever, hypoxia, chest symptoms, neurological symptoms, acute anaemia, splenic enlargement, priapism or persistent vomiting require same-day hospital assessment (acute chest syndrome, stroke, splenic sequestration, aplastic crisis and sepsis are emergencies). An acute painful episode is a medical emergency managed according to local hospital protocols with rapid analgesia and monitoring (NICE CG143). Follow the patient's individual pain plan and local sickle-cell pathway; do not delay transfer while attempting outpatient analgesic escalation. No generic opioid dose — consider the individual pain plan, opioid tolerance, renal function and age; NSAIDs may be contraindicated (AKI, CKD, peptic ulcer disease, anticoagulation, pregnancy). Assess hydration and avoid fluid overload; oxygen for hypoxia, not routinely. Triggers to avoid: cold, dehydration, hypoxia, infection, overexertion. Hydroxycarbamide is a specialist-initiated shared-care medicine (GMMMG — FBC, renal, liver and reticulocyte monitoring), not acute crisis treatment. Penicillin prophylaxis and vaccinations per specialist advice (duration varies with age and genotype). Exchange transfusion: haematology decision.
G6PD deficiency — trigger avoidance
G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) deficiency: X-linked, most common in African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern men. Haemolysis can be triggered by oxidative stress — infections, certain drugs and fava beans (favism); risk varies with dose, variant, infection and individual susceptibility — check drug safety against a current G6PD-specific resource. If acute haemolysis is suspected: stop the possible trigger, assess urgently with FBC, reticulocytes, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, renal function and urine testing, and discuss with haematology. Management includes removing the trigger, treating infection, monitoring renal function and potassium, and transfusion if clinically indicated.
The hydroxycarbamide (hydroxyurea) dose titration protocol for sickle cell disease is an underutilised treatment in primary care that dramatically reduces vaso-occlusive crises — the mechanism: hydroxycarbamide increases foetal haemoglobin (HbF) production by stimulating γ-globin gene expression, and HbF does not polymerise with HbS, thereby reducing sickling. Clinical trials (MSH, BABY HUG) showed fewer painful crises, acute chest syndrome episodes and transfusions. Hydroxycarbamide is initiated and titrated by haematology; under the Greater Manchester shared-care protocol the GP may continue prescribing with regular FBC, renal, liver and reticulocyte monitoring as specified. It is a preventive treatment, not acute crisis management.

Lifestyle

Fatigue Management, Nutrition & Chronic Disease Optimisation

Energy conservation in chronic anaemia Pacing activities: prioritise essential tasks in the morning (peak energy), defer non-essential to afternoon. Rest before activities that are known to cause fatigue. Sleep hygiene: consistent bedtime + wake time, avoid daytime naps longer than 20-30 min (perpetuates fatigue cycle). Occupational therapy: for severely fatigued patients — energy conservation strategies, home adaptation, assistive devices.
Nutritional support for iron deficiency Dietary iron: haem iron (red meat, poultry, fish) is absorbed 5-35%; non-haem iron (leafy greens, legumes, fortified cereals) absorbed 2-20%. Enhance absorption: take iron-rich foods with vitamin C (orange juice, bell peppers). Inhibit absorption (separate from iron supplements by 2h): tea, coffee, calcium (dairy), phytates (bran). Vegan/vegetarian: needs deliberate dietary planning to avoid iron and B12 deficiency.
B12-rich foods and supplementation for vegans B12 is found ONLY in animal products — meat, fish, eggs, dairy. Vegans must supplement: cyanocobalamin 50-100 mcg OD (NHS OTC — advise at registration), or weekly high-dose (1000 mcg). Fortified foods: nutritional yeast, fortified plant milks, fortified cereals. Rely on fortified foods or adequate B12 supplementation; test B12 if symptomatic, anaemic, at risk of malabsorption or taking relevant medication — no universal annual test (oral B12 replacement can be considered for dietary deficiency, NICE NG239).
Sickle cell — triggers and prevention Stay well hydrated and avoid dehydration (no fixed forced-fluid target). Warmth: avoid cold temperatures (peripheral vasoconstriction → sickling). Avoid hypoxia: no smoking; discuss long-haul flights and surgery/GA with haematology. Vaccinations and penicillin prophylaxis per specialist advice (duration varies with age and genotype). Know the individual pain plan and seek same-day hospital care for fever, chest or neurological symptoms, severe/atypical pain, priapism or persistent vomiting.
CKD anaemia and QoL Symptoms of CKD anaemia significantly overlap with general CKD symptoms (fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, breathlessness) — treating anaemia improves QoL substantially. Target Hb 100-120 g/L: improves energy, exercise tolerance, cognitive function. Above 130 g/L: increased cardiovascular events in CKD patients on ESA — avoid overtreating. Dietary modifications for CKD: low potassium, low phosphate, fluid restriction — dietitian involvement.
Alcohol and anaemia Alcohol causes anaemia through multiple mechanisms: direct bone marrow suppression (macrocytosis even without folate deficiency), folate deficiency (alcohol impairs folate absorption + increases excretion), GI blood loss (gastritis, oesophageal varices), liver disease (reduced EPO, reduced clotting factors), hypersplenism. AUDIT-C screening at every anaemia consultation in patients with relevant history. Any macrocytosis + anaemia without clear cause: ask specifically about alcohol use.
Pregnancy and anaemia Physiological haemodilution (plasma volume expands more than red cell mass in pregnancy) → Hb falls in first and second trimesters (normal Hb in pregnancy: >110 g/L first trimester, >105 g/L second/third trimester). Prophylactic folic acid 400 mcg OD from preconception to 12 weeks. Iron: only prescribe if iron deficiency confirmed (ferritin <30) — routine iron supplementation not recommended in UK (unlike WHO guidance for low-income countries). Seek urgent obstetric/haematology advice for severe or symptomatic anaemia, rapid deterioration, poor response to oral iron, suspected haemolysis or marrow disease, late gestation with significant anaemia, or Hb <100 g/L approaching delivery (postpartum anaemia: Hb <100 g/L).
Exercise capacity and anaemia monitoring Monitor Hb response at intervals guided by the cause and treatment (e.g. 2–4 weeks after starting oral iron; per the shared-care protocol for ESA or hydroxycarbamide). Routine 6-minute walk tests and exercise referral are not part of anaemia monitoring; support a graded return to activity once anaemia is corrected.
The iron supplementation patient counselling conversation is one of the most practically important in primary care — the rate of iron supplementation non-adherence exceeds 50% in most studies, primarily due to GI side effects (constipation, nausea, dark stools, cramping) that patients interpret as adverse reactions requiring stopping. The key counselling points: (1) dark/black stools are expected and normal — it is the iron; (2) take on an empty stomach if tolerated; (3) separate from tea, coffee and dairy where practical; (4) if GI effects are severe, take with food (absorption reduced by approximately 40% but better than not taking it); (5) try alternate-day dosing or a different preparation if not tolerated; (6) the anaemia will start to improve in 2-3 weeks but treatment continues for about 2–3 months after the Hb normalises to replete stores — do not stop when you feel better.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Iron deficiency treatment monitoring
Assess Hb response at approximately 2–4 weeks (a rise of at least 10 g/L within 2 weeks supports iron deficiency and treatment response). Continue iron for approximately 2–3 months after Hb normalises (longer if ongoing losses or malabsorption). If inadequate response: reassess diagnosis, compliance, coeliac serology, ongoing blood loss, malabsorption. Failure to respond to oral iron after 4 weeks + confirmed compliance = consider IV iron via the local hospital/ambulatory pathway or specialist review.
B12 monitoring
Follow up clinically, usually at around 3 months, earlier if symptoms are severe (NICE NG239). Clinical response, FBC and reticulocyte response (may occur at 7–10 days) are more useful than routine repeat serum B12, which may be misleading after treatment; there is no universal "MCV must normalise at 8 weeks" rule. Neurological symptoms: may take months to improve even with correct treatment.
Chronic disease and ACD monitoring
Monitoring is driven by the underlying disease, medication shared-care protocols and symptoms — not a universal annual FBC or routine ESR/CRP with Hb. Report new lymphadenopathy, weight loss, splenomegaly, or rapidly worsening anaemia immediately.
Same-day hospital (999 if unstable)
Severe symptomatic or rapidly falling Hb · Active bleeding / haemodynamic instability · Blasts or severe cytopenias · Febrile neutropenia · Suspected TTP/microangiopathic haemolysis · Suspected acute haemolytic crisis · Severe sickle-cell complications
Urgent haematology
Unexplained normocytic anaemia not responding to 4-6 weeks treatment · Constitutional symptoms + anaemia · Paraprotein on electrophoresis · Lymphadenopathy + anaemia · Stable pancytopenia · Suspected AIHA
Safety-net advice
Call 999 for chest pain, fainting or collapse, severe breathlessness, new confusion, haematemesis or heavy rectal bleeding · go to A&E now with fever if neutropenic or with sickle-cell disease · contact us or NHS 111 the same day for worsening breathlessness, palpitations, melaena or other rectal bleeding, jaundice or dark urine, new bruising or bleeding, or other new neurological symptoms · book a review if Hb fails to rise as expected.
The non-response to oral iron at 4 weeks in a patient with confirmed iron deficiency anaemia is a clinical prompt requiring systematic reassessment before escalating to IV iron — the causes of oral iron non-response: (1) non-compliance (most common — ask explicitly, check dispensing records); (2) ongoing blood loss exceeding replacement (unidentified GI bleeding, menorrhagia); (3) malabsorption (coeliac disease — anti-tTG IgA should be checked in all patients with iron deficiency before diagnosing the cause as dietary); (4) incorrect diagnosis (ACD misidentified as iron deficiency, B12 masquerading as normocytic anaemia); (5) drug interaction (proton pump inhibitors reduce iron absorption by approximately 50% — take iron 2 hours before PPI). A full reassessment protocol at 4 weeks of non-response before proceeding to IV iron prevents unnecessary infusions and identifies serious underlying pathology.
