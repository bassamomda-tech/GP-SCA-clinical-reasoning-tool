Red Flags — Severe Deficiency, Osteomalacia & Toxicity

Low 25-OH-D + proximal muscle weakness + bone pain + waddling gait + inability to rise from chair Suspected osteomalacia — treat as a specialist/urgent metabolic bone problem, not simply a GP loading regimen. Assess adjusted calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH, renal function, 25-OH-D, coeliac disease and other malabsorption, and medication causes; radiographs or specialist imaging if pseudofractures (Looser zones) are suspected. Risk of fragility fractures.
Child with bowed legs + wrist widening + craniotabes + delayed fontanelle closure Rickets (vitamin D deficiency in children). Suspected rickets, hypocalcaemic seizures, skeletal deformity or pathological fractures require urgent paediatric assessment. X-ray wrists: cupped/frayed metaphyses. Investigations (calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH, renal function, 25-OH-D ± urinary studies) and vitamin D/calcium treatment follow the paediatric metabolic-bone pathway; hypocalcaemic rickets may need calcium as well as vitamin D.
Low 25-OH-D + hypocalcaemia + tetany + Chvostek/Trousseau positive Symptomatic hypocalcaemia secondary to vitamin D deficiency. Manage as acute hypocalcaemia — urgent transfer if severe or symptomatic tetany (IV calcium gluconate); do not delay transfer while arranging vitamin D replacement. Check magnesium (required for PTH release — refractory hypocalcaemia if Mg low).
Hypercalcaemia during high-dose supplementation + nausea, vomiting, thirst, polyuria, weakness, confusion or renal impairment Suspect vitamin D toxicity — a clinical and biochemical diagnosis of hypercalcaemia, not a 25-OH-D threshold alone. Stop vitamin D and calcium supplements, check adjusted calcium, phosphate, renal function and ECG if severe, and seek urgent advice. Risk depends on cumulative dose, product errors, renal function, calcium intake and individual susceptibility — avoid prolonged high-dose prescribing without review.
Low 25-OH-D + normal PTH despite confirmed deficiency — no PTH response Possible hypoparathyroidism (PTH cannot rise despite low calcium). Or pseudohypoparathyroidism (PTH resistance — Albright hereditary osteodystrophy). Endocrinology referral.
Low vitamin D + hypercalcaemia (± unexplained weight loss, night sweats) Assess for primary hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, TB, lymphoma — macrophages convert 25-OH-D to 1,25-OH₂-D autonomously), excess calcium/vitamin D intake, renal disease and medication causes. Don't simply replace vitamin D — clarify calcium status first; replacement in granulomatous disease depends on calcium, 1,25-OH₂-D, disease activity and specialist input. Clinical assessment, calcium/PTH, chest imaging and specialist advice (ACE is not sensitive or specific enough to be a routine diagnostic test).
The granulomatous disease-vitamin D paradox is clinically important — in sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, and some lymphomas, activated macrophages within granulomata express 1-alpha-hydroxylase enzyme autonomously (independent of renal regulation by PTH and calcium), converting 25-OH-D to the active 1,25-OH-D. This autonomous production causes hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria even when serum 25-OH-D is low or normal. In these conditions, vitamin D supplementation can worsen hypercalcaemia, so replacement decisions need specialist input — increasing the substrate (25-OH-D) available for autonomous conversion by macrophages will worsen the hypercalcaemia. The practical implication: before prescribing vitamin D replacement for low 25-OH-D, check adjusted calcium. If calcium is elevated in the context of low 25-OH-D: check PTH, consider 1,25-OH₂-D, arrange chest imaging and seek specialist advice (respiratory/rheumatology/endocrinology) before supplementation; also consider primary hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, excess intake, renal disease and medication causes. ACE is not sensitive or specific enough to be a routine diagnostic test. The normal rule is: low 25-OH-D + high PTH + low-normal calcium = simple deficiency requiring supplementation. Low 25-OH-D + suppressed PTH + high calcium = PTH-independent hypercalcaemia (malignancy, granulomatous disease, excess intake) until proved otherwise.

Vitamin D Biology, Classification & Causes of Deficiency

Physiology
Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol): synthesised in skin by UV-B radiation on 7-dehydrocholesterol, or absorbed from diet (fatty fish, fortified foods). Converted in liver to 25-OH-D (storage form — measured in blood). Converted in kidney to 1,25-OH-D (calcitriol — active form) — regulated by PTH. Actions of 1,25-OH-D: increases intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption; reduces PTH secretion; maintains mineralisation of osteoid; immune modulation; muscle function.
25-OH-D classification (UK practice; SACN deficiency threshold <25 nmol/L)
Deficient: <25 nmol/L — symptomatic risk (osteomalacia, tetany). Possibly inadequate: 25-50 nmol/L — may be inadequate in some people; interpret in clinical context (ROS). Adequate: >50 nmol/L — adequate for most people (ROS). 50–75 nmol/L is not a universal treatment target; a higher target may be set by a specialist in malabsorption, osteomalacia or selected bone disease. Toxicity — diagnosed by hypercalcaemia during high-dose supplementation, not by a 25-OH-D threshold alone.
High-risk groups in UK
All: UK sunlight is insufficient for cutaneous synthesis October-April (latitude >52 degrees). Higher risk: dark skin (melanin reduces UV-B penetration — requires 3-6x longer sun exposure), housebound/care home residents, those who cover skin for religious/cultural reasons, breastfed infants (breast milk low in vitamin D), pregnant women, obese (vitamin D sequestered in adipose tissue), malabsorption (coeliac, IBD, post-bariatric), CKD (impaired 1-alpha-hydroxylation), liver disease (impaired 25-hydroxylation).
UK prevalence
Approximately 20% of UK adults have 25-OH-D <25 nmol/L at the end of winter. Dark-skinned populations: 30-40% severe deficiency. Housebound elderly: up to 60% severe deficiency. South Asian populations in UK: 50-70% have 25-OH-D <25 nmol/L year-round.
The UK vitamin D paradox: despite being an island with a relatively mild climate, the UK has extremely high rates of vitamin D deficiency — because at UK latitude (50-59 degrees North), UV-B radiation between October and April is insufficient to stimulate cutaneous vitamin D synthesis even on a sunny day. The sun's angle is too low for the UV-B wavelengths (280-315 nm) to penetrate the atmosphere sufficiently. This means that vitamin D synthesised during the summer months must be stored in adipose tissue and liver to last through the winter. People with dark skin, who have higher melanin concentrations reducing UV-B penetration, cannot synthesise adequate vitamin D even in UK summer sunlight — requiring approximately 3-6 times longer sun exposure than pale-skinned individuals to produce the same amount of vitamin D. This explains why South Asian, Black African, and Black Caribbean populations in the UK have dramatically higher rates of vitamin D deficiency than white populations living in the same geographic area. SACN (Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition) 2016 report established the current UK recommendation: all adults and children over 1 year should take 10 mcg (400 IU) vitamin D daily throughout the year, with higher doses for those at highest risk.

Diagnose

When to Test 25-OH-D vs Treat Empirically

When to test (measure 25-OH-D)
Test if: suspected osteomalacia or rickets · Bone pain or proximal myopathy with a possible metabolic bone cause · Malabsorption or bariatric surgery where deficiency is suspected · Persistent deficiency despite appropriate treatment · Complex osteoporosis/fragility-fracture assessment where the result will change management · Selected patients before IV zoledronate or denosumab, per the local osteoporosis protocol. Do not routinely test asymptomatic adults simply because they are dark-skinned, housebound, older, pregnant or taking an enzyme-inducing antiepileptic — they need empirical maintenance supplementation, not blood tests. Routine testing in pregnancy is not recommended solely because of a risk factor; give preventive supplementation by season and risk (NICE NG247).
When to treat empirically (no test needed)
DHSC advice based on SACN (2016): everyone over 1 year should consider 10 micrograms (400 IU) daily in autumn and winter. Housebound elderly, care home residents, dark skin: 10 micrograms (400 IU) OD year-round empirically without testing (DHSC/SACN 2016). Children under 5 and breastfed infants: Healthy Start vitamins (containing 10 micrograms / 400 IU vitamin D). Testing not required before prescribing standard supplementation doses.
Investigations when testing
25-OH-D (serum vitamin D — storage form, best clinical indicator) · Adjusted calcium (exclude hypercalcaemia before supplementation) · Phosphate (low in osteomalacia) · ALP (elevated in osteomalacia/rickets — bone remodelling) · PTH (elevated secondary hyperparathyroidism) · Magnesium (required for PTH release — check if hypocalcaemia not responding) · eGFR (CKD — impaired activation) · LFTs (liver disease — impaired 25-hydroxylation)
The bisphosphonate-vitamin D deficiency interaction is a critical prescribing safety point — oral bisphosphonates (alendronic acid, risedronate) bind to and inhibit osteoclast activity, reducing bone resorption. In a vitamin D-deficient patient, calcium absorption from the gut is already reduced (due to low 1,25-OH-D). When bisphosphonate further reduces bone resorption (the secondary source of calcium for maintaining serum calcium), severe hypocalcaemia can result. Cases of severe hypocalcaemia, tetany, and seizures have been reported after bisphosphonate initiation in vitamin D-deficient patients. The safe practice: denosumab — ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D and correct hypocalcaemia before administration; monitoring is particularly important in advanced CKD (eGFR <30) (Greater Manchester denosumab shared-care protocol). IV zoledronate — check renal function, calcium and vitamin D status before infusion. Oral bisphosphonates — vitamin D correction is important, but a loading regimen is not automatically required if deficiency is mild and treatment is clinically urgent; follow the osteoporosis pathway.

Interpreting the Results — Differential Diagnosis

Low 25-OH-D + raised PTH + low-normal Ca + raised ALP
Classic vitamin D deficiency causing secondary hyperparathyroidism and osteomalacia. Treatment: loading regimen (where rapid correction is clinically needed) + maintenance; suspected osteomalacia needs specialist assessment. PTH should fall as 25-OH-D is corrected, but may not normalise in a fixed timeframe in every patient.
Low 25-OH-D + suppressed PTH + raised Ca
PTH-independent hypercalcaemia: malignancy, granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, TB) or lymphoma — autonomous 1,25-OH₂-D production — excess calcium/vitamin D intake, renal disease or medication causes (a raised or inappropriately normal PTH suggests primary hyperparathyroidism). Hold supplementation until calcium status is clarified; replacement in granulomatous disease depends on calcium, 1,25-OH₂-D, disease activity and specialist input. Clinical assessment, chest imaging, lymphadenopathy check and specialist advice — ACE is not a routine diagnostic test.
Low 25-OH-D + normal PTH + normal Ca
Possible: low magnesium (blunts the PTH response), mild deficiency without secondary hyperparathyroidism yet, or laboratory error. Repeat with strict conditions. Supplement empirically if <50 nmol/L.
Persistently low 25-OH-D despite adequate supplementation
Malabsorption (coeliac, Crohn, post-bariatric surgery — dose per gastroenterology/bariatric protocol). Medication interference (rifampicin, antiepileptics, antiretrovirals — induce CYP450 enzymes that accelerate 25-OH-D catabolism). CKD (treat nutritional deficiency with colecalciferol/ergocalciferol; active analogues only under nephrology). Very obese (large volume of distribution in adipose tissue — may need higher doses). Check adherence.
Low 25-OH-D in CKD
Treat nutritional vitamin D deficiency with colecalciferol or ergocalciferol. Do not routinely use alfacalcidol or calcitriol in adults with CKD G3a–G5 not on dialysis; use them only under renal/nephrology guidance for persistent or severe CKD-related secondary hyperparathyroidism (generally CKD G4–G5), with calcium, phosphate, PTH and renal monitoring (NICE NG203).
Antiepileptic drug-vitamin D interaction is one of the most clinically significant and underappreciated drug-nutrient interactions — enzyme-inducing antiepileptics (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone, primidone) are potent inducers of CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 hepatic enzymes, which accelerate the catabolism of 25-OH-D to inactive metabolites, causing vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism even in patients with adequate sunlight exposure and dietary vitamin D. The clinical consequences: reduced bone mineral density, increased fracture risk, and osteomalacia in severe deficiency. In people taking long-term enzyme-inducing antiepileptic medicines, review vitamin D supplementation and bone-health risk: consider 400–1,000 IU daily or the local neurology/osteoporosis protocol, assess fracture risk, and arrange DXA according to age, duration of treatment, fracture risk and other clinical factors rather than automatically every 2–3 years. Any change of antiepileptic requires neurology input.

The practical vitamin D prescribing distinction between cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) and ergocalciferol (vitamin D2) matters — D3 (cholecalciferol) is the natural form synthesised in human skin and is approximately 1.7x more potent at raising serum 25-OH-D than the equivalent dose of D2 (ergocalciferol), which is the plant-derived form. UK NHS prescribing predominantly uses D3 (Invita D3, Fultium D3, Aviticol) for treatment and D3-containing supplements (Adcal-D3, Calcichew D3) for combined calcium and vitamin D supplementation in osteoporosis. The weekly high-dose preparations (Invita D3 25,000 IU weekly or 50,000 IU monthly) are particularly useful for loading regimens and for patients with malabsorption who cannot achieve adequate levels with daily supplements. GPs should prescribe by dose in IU or mcg (not by brand) to allow pharmacist substitution with equivalent products.

Loading Regimens and Maintenance Dosing

Severe deficiency 25-OH-D <25 nmol/L
Consider loading: ~300,000 IU cumulative
Not automatic for every level <25 nmol/L — consider symptoms, osteomalacia, calcium/phosphate/ALP/PTH, malabsorption, planned antiresorptive treatment, renal function, frailty and adherence; assess calcium and renal function first. When rapid correction is clinically needed: 50,000 IU weekly x 6 weeks; or 25,000 IU weekly x 12 weeks; or 4,000 IU daily x 10 weeks (ROS). Document the total cumulative dose, duration and subsequent maintenance to avoid accidental continuation of high-dose treatment (SPS). Then maintenance 800-2,000 IU OD. Check adjusted calcium about 1 month after completing loading. Assess dietary calcium intake; prescribe calcium only when dietary intake is inadequate, hypocalcaemia is present, or as part of an osteoporosis/osteomalacia treatment plan (routine high-dose calcium may cause constipation, hypercalcaemia or renal stones). Recheck selectively (symptomatic, malabsorptive, renal or treatment-resistant).
Moderate deficiency 25-50 nmol/L
Colecalciferol 1,000-2,000 IU OD
Or 20,000 IU fortnightly, with dietary advice. Treat more intensively only if symptomatic, osteomalacia is suspected, malabsorption is present or antiresorptive treatment is imminent. Routine recheck not required. Maintenance 800-1,000 IU OD thereafter. No calcium supplementation required unless dietary intake inadequate (<700 mg/day), hypocalcaemia, or an osteoporosis/osteomalacia treatment plan.
Maintenance (all)
Colecalciferol 400-1,000 IU OD
SACN: 400 IU (10 mcg) universal recommendation. Housebound/dark skin/institutionalised: 400 IU (10 mcg) OD year-round (DHSC/SACN). Pregnant/breastfeeding: 400 IU OD (NICE NG247; Healthy Start). Post-loading: 800-2,000 IU OD. Continue long term while the risk factor persists — not automatically lifelong after a temporary dietary or seasonal problem.
Malabsorption (coeliac, IBD, post-bariatric)
Specialist / bariatric team protocol
Dose and route (including any IM preparation) per the gastroenterology or bariatric nutritional protocol rather than a single generic dose. A level above 50 nmol/L is adequate for most; higher targets only if set by the specialist service. Recheck 25-OH-D at ~3 months as absorption is uncertain. Gastroenterology for underlying malabsorption management.
CKD
Colecalciferol or ergocalciferol
In CKD, treat nutritional vitamin D deficiency with colecalciferol or ergocalciferol. Use alfacalcidol or calcitriol only under renal/nephrology guidance for persistent or severe CKD-related secondary hyperparathyroidism, with calcium, phosphate, PTH and renal monitoring (NICE NG203). No GP-initiated fixed doses of active analogues.
The loading dose rationale: vitamin D is fat-soluble and has a half-life of approximately 4-6 weeks in blood, but the body stores are in adipose tissue and liver, with a much longer effective duration. A loading regimen rapidly repletes tissue stores and achieves therapeutic serum levels within 2-4 weeks, whereas daily low-dose supplementation alone in a severely deficient patient would take 3-6 months to normalise. The total cumulative loading dose (300,000 IU, approximately 7.5 mg) should be clearly documented with its duration and the planned maintenance dose — toxicity risk depends on cumulative dosing, product errors, renal function, calcium intake and individual susceptibility, so avoid prolonged high-dose prescribing without review (SPS). The practical prescribing choices for loading: (1) weekly 50,000 IU sachets (Invita D3 — dissolve in water) for 6 weeks = 300,000 IU total — good compliance; (2) daily 4,000 IU for 10 weeks = 280,000 IU total (ROS regimen) — suits patients who prefer daily routine; (3) intramuscular preparations only under a specialist malabsorption or bariatric protocol.

Special Populations — Children, Pregnancy & CKD

Children — rickets prevention and treatment
Infants from birth: Healthy Start vitamins (containing 10 micrograms / 400 IU vitamin D OD) if breastfed, or formula-fed receiving <500 ml/day formula (formula is fortified). Ages 1-4: Healthy Start vitamins continuing. Ages 5+: 400 IU OD recommended by SACN. Suspected rickets, hypocalcaemic seizures, skeletal deformity or pathological fractures require urgent paediatric assessment. Investigations (calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH, renal function, sometimes urinary studies) and vitamin D/calcium treatment should follow the paediatric metabolic-bone pathway; hypocalcaemic rickets may require calcium as well as vitamin D, supervised by paediatrics.
Pregnancy
400 IU (10 micrograms) OD in pregnancy and while breastfeeding — at least October to March, and all year for those with limited sun exposure or darker skin (NICE NG247, 2025; SACN Vitamin D and Health, 2016). Do not routinely check 25-OH-D in all pregnant patients, or because of a risk factor alone, unless clinically indicated. Severe deficiency in pregnancy: neonatal hypocalcaemia risk + rickets in infant. Treat confirmed deficiency in pregnancy with specialist/obstetric advice; avoid unmonitored high-dose loading. Healthy Start supplements contain 10 micrograms (400 IU) vitamin D + folic acid — available free for eligible pregnant women.
Bariatric surgery
After bariatric surgery, vitamin D deficiency is common because of reduced intake, altered digestion, obesity and, in bypass procedures, reduced absorption. Follow the specialist bariatric nutritional protocol rather than a single generic dose; a level above 50 nmol/L is generally adequate, with any higher target set by the bariatric service. Combined multivitamin (Forceval or Vitabiotics Wellwoman) plus specific vitamin D supplement. Calcium citrate preferred post-bariatric (does not require gastric acid for absorption).
Enzyme-inducing antiepileptics
Carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital: review vitamin D supplementation and bone-health risk with long-term use. Consider 400–1,000 IU daily or the local neurology/osteoporosis protocol. Assess fracture risk and arrange DXA according to age, duration of treatment, fracture risk and other clinical factors rather than automatically every 2–3 years. Any change of antiepileptic requires neurology input.
Healthy Start vitamins are a significant health equity intervention that GPs should actively promote — they are available free to: pregnant women who receive qualifying benefits, mothers with children under 4 who receive qualifying benefits, and pregnant women under 18 (regardless of benefits status). The vitamins contain 400 mcg folic acid + 70 mg vitamin C + 10 micrograms (400 IU) vitamin D — addressing three key nutritional deficiencies in at-risk populations simultaneously. Despite being free, uptake is poor — approximately 50-60% of eligible women do not receive them, most commonly because healthcare professionals do not mention them at antenatal appointments. GPs and midwives should mention Healthy Start vitamins at every first antenatal appointment with eligible women. The Healthy Start scheme also provides vouchers for fresh fruit, vegetables, pulses, and plain cow's milk for eligible families — GPs can signpost to Healthy Start (www.healthystart.nhs.uk) for application.

Lifestyle

Sun Exposure, Dietary Sources & Public Health

Sunlight and sun safety Vitamin D can be produced by sunlight during spring and summer, but deliberate unprotected exposure is not necessary and sunburn should always be avoided. Use normal sun-safety measures. Supplementation is the safer and more reliable approach for people at risk of deficiency.
Dietary sources of vitamin D Very few foods contain significant vitamin D naturally: oily fish (salmon 6-12 mcg/100g, mackerel 8 mcg/100g, sardines 5 mcg/100g), egg yolk (1 mcg each), beef liver (1.7 mcg/100g), fortified foods (most UK margarine, some breakfast cereals, some plant milks, some yoghurts — read labels). Diet alone cannot reliably prevent deficiency in UK. Supplementation is the primary intervention.
Supplementation adherence Daily supplement is easier for many than weekly/monthly. Combine with existing daily habit (brushing teeth, morning medication). Invita D3 (dissolves in water) or oil-based drops for children/elderly. Vitamin D supplements are cheap (Boots/Holland and Barrett D3 1000 IU OD approximately £3-5/year). NHS prescriptions available for high-dose preparations. App reminders for weekly dosing.
Vitamin D in care homes DHSC advice based on SACN (2016): care home residents and others at risk should take 10 micrograms (400 IU) daily year-round (housebound and limited sunlight exposure). Many care homes have vitamin D supplementation policies — GPs should check on admission. Quality improvement: care home vitamin D policy review + systematic supplementation for all residents.
Breastfeeding and infant supplementation Breast milk contains very little vitamin D (30-80 IU/L — far below the 400 IU/day infant requirement). ALL breastfed infants in the UK should receive vitamin D drops from birth. Formula-fed infants receiving >500 ml/day of formula do not need additional drops (formula is fortified at 400 IU/L). BabiesBasics Healthy Start drops or Abidec (400 IU per dose) — pharmacist advice.
Weight management and vitamin D Obesity (BMI >30): vitamin D is sequestered in adipose tissue, so standard doses may raise serum levels less. Use standard supplementation and recheck selectively if deficiency persists, rather than routinely using fixed higher loading doses. Weight loss improves vitamin D bioavailability.
Vitamin D and muscle function Vitamin D receptors (VDR) in skeletal muscle regulate protein synthesis and calcium handling within muscle fibres. Deficiency causes: proximal myopathy (difficulty climbing stairs, rising from a chair), increased falls risk (correcting deficiency improves myopathy; evidence for falls reduction is inconsistent), and reduced athletic performance. Muscle function is a sensitive indicator of vitamin D status — improvement within 4-6 weeks of supplementation often precedes biochemical normalisation.
Vitamin D and non-skeletal health Vitamin D supplementation is recommended to prevent or treat deficiency and support bone and muscle health. Evidence does not support prescribing vitamin D solely to prevent respiratory infections, autoimmune disease, COVID-19 or other non-skeletal conditions.
The vitamin D and falls prevention evidence is weaker than often assumed — a Cochrane review (Gillespie et al. 2012) found vitamin D did not reduce falls in community-dwelling older people overall, though it may in those with low vitamin D levels. The mechanism: vitamin D has direct effects on muscle function (VDR in skeletal muscle regulate contractile protein synthesis), which may improve muscle strength in deficiency, but this has not translated into a consistent reduction in falls. NICE NG249 (Falls, 2025, rec 1.3.4) finds the evidence insufficient to recommend vitamin D specifically for falls prevention, and advises following NHS advice on vitamin D for bone and muscle health. In elderly patients at high falls risk, follow DHSC advice on vitamin D for bone and muscle health (and correct deficiency if found), but do not prescribe it specifically as a falls intervention (NICE NG249).

Follow-Up & Monitoring

After loading regimen
Routine repeat 25-OH-D is not required after standard treatment in an otherwise well adult. Consider repeat testing after ~3 months if symptoms persist, malabsorption is present, osteomalacia was suspected, levels remain unexpectedly low, adherence or absorption is uncertain, or the patient is on specialist osteoporosis or renal treatment. A level >50 nmol/L is adequate for most — no universal 50–75 nmol/L target. Calcium: check adjusted calcium about 1 month after completing a loading regimen (NOS/NOGG, CKS — may unmask primary hyperparathyroidism); this is particularly important with osteomalacia, renal disease, hyperparathyroidism, granulomatous disease, malabsorption or symptoms. PTH may not normalise in a fixed timeframe. Continue maintenance while the risk factor persists — not automatically lifelong after a temporary dietary or seasonal problem.
Maintenance monitoring
No blanket annual 25-OH-D testing — base monitoring on the underlying condition (malabsorption, post-bariatric, renal or specialist osteoporosis treatment) and symptoms. Standard supplementation (400-800 IU) in otherwise healthy adults: no testing required — treat empirically. Do not routinely monitor PTH in uncomplicated nutritional deficiency.
Pre-antiresorptive check
Denosumab: ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D and correct hypocalcaemia before administration; monitoring is particularly important in advanced CKD (eGFR <30). IV zoledronate: check renal function, calcium and vitamin D status before infusion. Oral bisphosphonates: vitamin D correction is important, but loading is not automatically required if deficiency is mild and treatment is clinically urgent — follow the osteoporosis pathway.
CKD on active analogues (nephrology-led)
Alfacalcidol/calcitriol only under renal guidance — calcium, phosphate, PTH and renal function monitoring per the nephrology protocol (risk of hypercalcaemia with active vitamin D analogues).
Urgent review
Tetany or seizure in patient with low 25-OH-D → IV calcium gluconate + check Mg + admit · Signs of rickets in child → urgent paediatric referral · Hypercalcaemia during supplementation (especially with nausea, vomiting, thirst, polyuria, weakness, confusion or renal impairment) → stop vitamin D and calcium supplements, check adjusted calcium, phosphate, renal function (ECG if severe), seek urgent advice and identify the cause (primary hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, granulomatous disease, excess intake)
Within 2 weeks
25-OH-D <25 nmol/L with proximal myopathy or bone pain → osteomalacia workup (calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH, renal function) + specialist metabolic bone advice — not a loading regimen alone · Persistently low 25-OH-D despite adequate supplementation → malabsorption screen · PTH persistently raised despite adequate 25-OH-D → endocrinology (PTH may not normalise in a fixed timeframe)
The proximal myopathy-vitamin D connection is frequently missed in clinical practice because vitamin D deficiency-related myopathy mimics many common conditions (functional weakness, deconditioning, polymyalgia rheumatica, fibromyalgia). The characteristic features that point to vitamin D deficiency: difficulty rising from a chair without using arms, difficulty climbing stairs, waddling gait, and symmetrical proximal limb weakness with normal or only mildly elevated CK. Unlike inflammatory myopathy, there is no significant tenderness, fever, or systemic features. The diagnosis is confirmed by: 25-OH-D below 25 nmol/L + elevated ALP (high bone turnover) + low-normal phosphate + secondary hyperparathyroidism. Treatment response is dramatic — strength and pain typically improve within 4-6 weeks of high-dose supplementation, long before biochemical normalisation is complete. This rapid clinical improvement is a useful diagnostic confirmation.
