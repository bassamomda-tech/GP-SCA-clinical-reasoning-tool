Safety

Red Flags — Myeloma, Waldenström & Serious Immunodeficiency

Paraprotein or abnormal FLC + attributable CRAB features or SLiM biomarkers Suspected active myeloma requires urgent haematology assessment when there is a clonal plasma-cell disorder plus one or more myeloma-defining events (attributable CRAB features or SLiM biomarkers). The paraprotein concentration alone does not diagnose or exclude myeloma. CRAB: C calcium >0.25 mmol/L above the upper limit of normal or >2.75 mmol/L · R creatinine clearance <40 mL/min or serum creatinine >177 µmol/L · A haemoglobin <100 g/L or >20 g/L below the lower limit of normal · B one or more osteolytic lesions on CT or radiography — each must be attributable to the plasma-cell disorder, as anaemia, renal impairment and hypercalcaemia are common in older adults for other reasons (NICE NG35). → Urgent suspected-cancer haematology referral; emergency pathway if severe (see emergency tile).
Serum free light chains: markedly abnormal kappa/lambda ratio ± immunoparesis Light chain myeloma or AL amyloidosis — may be missed on standard SPEP. A myeloma-defining biomarker is an involved:uninvolved serum FLC ratio ≥100 (reciprocal approximately ≤0.01), provided the involved FLC is at least 100 mg/L; interpret with the clinical context and specialist assessment — an abnormal FLC ratio alone is not diagnostic of myeloma. Renal impairment, inflammation and infection can produce abnormal FLC results: use the local laboratory reference range and renal-adjusted interpretation where available; mild FLC abnormalities commonly occur without plasma-cell malignancy (BSH 2023). → haematology suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12: results suggesting myeloma).
IgM paraprotein + hyperviscosity symptoms (visual disturbance, headache, mucosal bleeding, confusion, dizziness, breathlessness or neurological symptoms) Suspected symptomatic hyperviscosity requires immediate discussion with haematology/acute oncology; severe symptoms → emergency admission. Plasma viscosity and other specialist investigations should be arranged urgently. Plasmapheresis is a specialist treatment and is not initiated solely on an IgM concentration threshold — the link between IgM level and hyperviscosity is variable, and hyperviscosity can occur at lower concentrations.
Paraprotein found + new bone pain, lytic lesion or pathological fracture Possible myeloma bone disease. → Urgent suspected-cancer haematology referral; emergency assessment for a pathological fracture with significant pain, deformity or instability, or a suspected unstable spine. Imaging is arranged through the haematology pathway: NICE recommends whole-body MRI first-line, or whole-body low-dose CT if MRI is unsuitable or declined; skeletal survey only when MRI and CT are unsuitable or declined; isotope bone scans should not be used to identify myeloma bone disease. Do not start bisphosphonate or denosumab in primary care — bone-modifying treatment is coordinated urgently with haematology, with dental and renal assessment.
Persistent low IgG (± low IgA/IgM) with recurrent or severe bacterial sinopulmonary infections, bronchiectasis or poor vaccine responses Possible primary antibody deficiency (e.g. CVID) or secondary antibody deficiency (post-BMT, CLL). → Refer to clinical immunology. CVID is not diagnosed solely from low immunoglobulins and recurrent infections — vaccine responses, exclusion of secondary causes (haematological malignancy, anti-CD20 therapy such as rituximab, protein loss, nephrotic syndrome, lymphoproliferative disease, immunosuppressive medication) and specialist assessment are required. Selective IgA deficiency is not CVID.
IgA deficiency + positive anti-tTG IgA (anti-tissue transglutaminase) result of zero Total IgA deficiency (1 in 700 population) — causes false-negative anti-tTG IgA in coeliac disease. → Always check total IgA alongside anti-tTG IgA. If IgA deficient: use anti-tTG IgG or anti-DGP IgG instead.
Emergency assessment / admission Use the local emergency/acute oncology pathway for: suspected spinal cord or cauda equina compression · severe symptomatic hypercalcaemia · acute kidney injury or rapidly deteriorating renal function with suspected myeloma · sepsis or neutropenic sepsis in a patient receiving myeloma treatment · symptomatic hyperviscosity · pathological fracture with significant pain, deformity or instability. Do not delay emergency transfer for imaging or specialist confirmation.
When to suspect a plasma-cell disorder Older adult with persistent or progressive back, rib or other bone pain; unexplained fracture; unexplained anaemia; renal impairment or AKI; hypercalcaemia; raised ESR/plasma viscosity; recurrent or unusual bacterial infections; neuropathy, nephrotic syndrome, cardiomyopathy, macroglossia or purpura; raised total protein or globulin; an incidental paraprotein. NICE NG12 recommends myeloma blood tests in people aged 60 and over with persistent bone pain, particularly back pain, or unexplained fracture.
The myeloma CRAB criteria (Hypercalcaemia, Renal impairment, Anaemia, Bone lesions) represent end-organ damage attributable to the plasma-cell disorder and, with the SLiM biomarkers, are the myeloma-defining events that trigger treatment — the mere presence of a paraprotein without CRAB criteria does not constitute myeloma (it may be MGUS or smouldering myeloma). The clinical significance: a patient with a paraprotein of 25 g/L but no anaemia, normal renal function, normal calcium, and no bone lesions does not have symptomatic myeloma and does not need immediate treatment (they have either MGUS or smouldering myeloma and need monitoring). A patient with a paraprotein of 15 g/L but Hb 8.5 g/dL, creatinine 180 μmol/L, calcium 2.8 mmol/L, and lytic bone lesions on imaging has symptomatic myeloma requiring urgent treatment regardless of the paraprotein level. Serum free light chain (FLC) measurement is a key test for plasma cell dyscrasias — it detects myelomas that do not secrete intact immunoglobulin (approximately 15-20% of myelomas produce only free light chains, which are not detected on standard SPEP). An involved:uninvolved ratio ≥100 with an involved FLC of at least 100 mg/L is a myeloma-defining biomarker; lesser abnormalities are common without malignancy, particularly in renal impairment, so an abnormal ratio alone is not diagnostic.

Types of Immunoglobulin Abnormality — Classification

Paraprotein (M-protein) — monoclonal immunoglobulin
Single homogeneous band (M-band) on SPEP representing monoclonal immunoglobulin. Quantified in g/L. Associated with: MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance) — most common (prevalence 3-4% in adults over 50); smouldering myeloma; multiple myeloma; Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (IgM paraprotein + lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma); POEMS syndrome; amyloid (AL) — light chain deposits in organs.
Polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia
Broad elevation across all immunoglobulin classes (no M-band) on SPEP — represents polyclonal B-cell activation. Causes: chronic infection (HIV, hepatitis B/C, TB), liver cirrhosis (hepatic IgA production), autoimmune disease (SLE, RA, Sjogren), sarcoidosis, lymphoma (diffuse stimulation). Elevated total protein without M-band = benign.
Immunodeficiency — low immunoglobulins
Hypogammaglobulinaemia: low IgG ± IgA ± IgM. Causes: primary (CVID, XLA, SCID, DiGeorge), secondary (CLL, lymphoma, post-rituximab, post-BMT, nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy). Risk: recurrent bacterial infections (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus — encapsulated organisms). Management: specialist (immunology) assessment; immunoglobulin replacement is specialist initiated where indicated.
IgA deficiency (selective)
Most common primary immunodeficiency (1 in 700). Usually asymptomatic. Associations: recurrent respiratory/GI infections (mild), coeliac disease (5x increased risk), autoimmune disease. Selective IgA deficiency is generally defined as IgA <0.07 g/L with normal IgG and IgM. Most patients with IgA deficiency do not experience anaphylaxis to blood components. Previous severe allergic/anaphylactic transfusion reaction → discuss future transfusion with transfusion medicine (washed red cells and platelets may be required for elective transfusion; IgA-deficient plasma if plasma is required). No previous reaction → standard components can generally be given with enhanced observation. Life-saving transfusion must not be delayed while IgA-deficient components are sourced (BSH 2023). Selective IgA deficiency, hypogammaglobulinaemia, anti-IgA antibodies and previous transfusion reaction are not interchangeable.
MGUS (Monoclonal Gammopathy of Undetermined Significance) is the most common plasma cell dyscrasia — it is present in approximately 3-4% of adults over 50 and increases to approximately 7% over 75. The vast majority of MGUS patients never develop myeloma — the overall risk of progression from MGUS to myeloma or a related disorder is approximately 1% per year; it varies with Mayo MGUS risk stratification (risk factors: paraprotein ≥15 g/L, non-IgG paraprotein, abnormal serum FLC ratio) — about 5% at 20 years with none of these factors (low-risk MGUS) rising to about 58% at 20 years with all three. The clinical implication: MGUS does not need treatment — it needs monitoring. GPs must understand that an incidentally discovered paraprotein on protein electrophoresis is most likely MGUS and is not a diagnosis of myeloma. The GP's role: arrange haematology referral for formal MGUS characterisation and risk stratification, then follow the haematology monitoring plan — BSH 2023: bloods at about 6 months, then annual follow-up for many patients, with longer intervals for selected low-risk patients; high-intermediate and high-risk MGUS usually remain under haematology. GP monitoring only where the haematology letter explicitly delegates it.

Assessment — History, SPEP Interpretation & Investigation

SPEP (serum protein electrophoresis) interpretation
Normal: polyclonal immunoglobulin "hump" in gamma region. M-band: sharp, narrow spike in gamma (occasionally beta) region = paraprotein = request immunofixation. Immunofixation identifies: the class (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE — free light chain only) and the light chain type (kappa or lambda). Quantify the paraprotein in g/L. Request serum free light chains (FLC kappa + lambda, FLC ratio) to complete myeloma screen. Request serum immunofixation if the SPEP is abnormal or clinical suspicion remains high. No single test should be used alone to exclude myeloma (NICE NG35) — do not reassure solely on a normal SPEP if suspicion persists; NICE NG12 includes serum FLC testing in the initial assessment. Local laboratory comments take precedence.
History
Bone pain (myeloma). Recurrent infections — type and site (sinopulmonary = antibody deficiency). Fatigue (anaemia from myeloma). Renal symptoms (myeloma — light chain nephropathy). Neurological symptoms (POEMS syndrome — polyneuropathy; Waldenström hyperviscosity — headache, visual blurring). Skin changes (POEMS — hyperpigmentation, oedema).
Investigations — all paraprotein findings
SPEP + immunofixation (characterise paraprotein) · Serum free light chains (FLC kappa + lambda + ratio) · Urine Bence Jones protein (BJP) (less sensitive than serum FLC — use when serum FLC is unavailable or haematology advises) · FBC (anaemia = myeloma feature) · Quantitative IgG, IgA, IgM · ESR or plasma viscosity (where clinically indicated) · Adjusted calcium (hypercalcaemia = CRAB) · Creatinine + eGFR (renal impairment = CRAB) · LDH + urate (malignancy) · Albumin (low in myeloma from inflammation + paraprotein displacement) · Beta-2 microglobulin (myeloma staging — if haematology requests) · Imaging via haematology (whole-body MRI first-line; whole-body low-dose CT if MRI unsuitable or declined; skeletal survey only if both unsuitable; no isotope bone scan)
Bence Jones protein (BJP) testing deserves special attention — the traditional 24-hour urine BJP test (heat precipitation method) is relatively insensitive and is being replaced by: urine immunofixation electrophoresis (uIFE — more sensitive and specific for free light chain detection in urine) and serum free light chain assay (extremely sensitive, detects free light chains in serum that are subsequently excreted in urine). The modern myeloma screen should include both serum immunofixation and serum FLC assay — this detects the large majority of plasma cell dyscrasias without requiring a 24-hour urine collection, although no single test excludes myeloma. The 24-hour urine BJP remains useful for quantifying the degree of light chain excretion (used to monitor treatment response in light-chain myeloma) but is no longer the primary diagnostic test. GPs requesting initial myeloma workup should request: SPEP + immunofixation + serum FLC + FBC + renal function + calcium — this comprehensive screen takes one blood sample and identifies most plasma cell dyscrasias; urine testing is reserved for when serum FLC is unavailable or haematology advises.

MGUS, Smouldering Myeloma & Myeloma — Key Distinctions

MGUS diagnostic criteria (IMWG)
All three required: Serum paraprotein <30 g/L (IgG or IgA) or IgM <30 g/L; AND Clonal bone marrow plasma cells <10%; AND No CRAB criteria (no end-organ damage). MGUS is not myeloma — it is a precursor state. Risk of progression: low risk (<5% 20-year risk): IgG, paraprotein <15 g/L, normal FLC ratio. High risk (about 58% at 20 years with all three factors): non-IgG, paraprotein ≥15 g/L, abnormal FLC ratio.
Smouldering myeloma
Serum paraprotein >30 g/L OR 10-60% clonal plasma cells in marrow. No CRAB criteria (no end-organ damage). Smouldering myeloma is a haematology-led diagnosis and follow-up condition — it should not be managed as ordinary MGUS in primary care. Treatment is not routine unless myeloma-defining events develop or the patient is entered into a specialist clinical trial. NICE NG35: monitor every 3 months for the first 5 years, after which frequency can be individualised (see also the BSH smouldering myeloma good-practice paper, 2024).
Myeloma — diagnosis
Clonal plasma cells in bone marrow >10% (or biopsy-proven plasmacytoma). PLUS one or more attributable CRAB features OR SLiM biomarkers: involved:uninvolved FLC ratio ≥100 with involved FLC ≥100 mg/L; ≥60% clonal plasma cells; >1 focal lesion on MRI. The paraprotein concentration alone does not diagnose or exclude myeloma. Treatment initiated immediately if any MDE or CRAB.
Waldenström macroglobulinaemia
IgM paraprotein + bone marrow infiltration with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL). MYD88 L265P mutation in >90%. Symptoms: hyperviscosity (IgM pentamer — very viscous) in large tumour burden, cytopenias, peripheral neuropathy. Treatment: ibrutinib (BTK inhibitor) or rituximab-based regimens (haematology). IgM MGUS more commonly progresses to lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/Waldenström macroglobulinaemia than to conventional myeloma.
The myeloma-defining events (MDEs) are an important update to myeloma diagnosis — prior to 2014, myeloma required CRAB criteria (end-organ damage) to initiate treatment. The 2014 IMWG update introduced three additional trigger points based on biomarkers that predict >80% risk of progression to end-organ damage within 2 years: (1) involved:uninvolved serum FLC ratio ≥100 with an involved FLC of at least 100 mg/L; (2) bone marrow clonal plasma cells >60%; (3) >1 focal bone lesion on MRI. These MDEs allow treatment to be initiated before actual end-organ damage develops, improving outcomes. The clinical implication: a patient with 'smouldering myeloma' who has a serum FLC ratio of 150:1 now meets criteria for treatment initiation as 'myeloma with MDE,' even without anaemia, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia, or bone lesions. This is a specialist decision — but GPs should be aware that 'no CRAB criteria' no longer automatically means no treatment for all smouldering myeloma patients.

Referral Pathways

Emergency
Local emergency/acute oncology pathway: suspected spinal cord or cauda equina compression · severe symptomatic hypercalcaemia · AKI or rapidly deteriorating renal function with suspected myeloma · sepsis or neutropenic sepsis during myeloma treatment · symptomatic hyperviscosity (IgM paraprotein + visual disturbance, headache, mucosal bleeding, confusion) → immediate haematology/acute oncology discussion, emergency admission if severe · pathological fracture with significant pain, deformity or instability
Suspected cancer · haematology
New paraprotein or abnormal FLC result with: unexplained anaemia · renal impairment · hypercalcaemia · persistent or progressive bone/back pain · pathological or unexplained fracture · lytic lesion · significant FLC abnormality · IgD or IgE paraprotein · IgG paraprotein generally >15 g/L · IgA or IgM paraprotein generally >10 g/L. These are practical referral thresholds from the Myeloma UK/BSH-supported primary-care pathway, not diagnostic criteria, and do not override clinical concern or local haematology advice
Haematology (routine)
New paraprotein without concerning symptoms or CRAB abnormalities → MGUS characterisation and risk staging, particularly where the isotype, level, FLC ratio or immunoglobulin suppression is not clearly low-risk · Paraprotein known and rising (trend data) → re-staging
Clinical immunology
Persistent low IgG, particularly with recurrent or severe bacterial sinopulmonary infections, bronchiectasis, poor vaccine responses or suspected primary antibody deficiency → immunology assessment (secondary causes considered) · IgA deficiency + coeliac disease + recurrent infections
GP-monitored MGUS (haematology-designated)
Only where the haematology letter explicitly delegates monitoring and specifies the diagnosis and risk category, the tests, the interval, the re-referral thresholds and the responsible haematology contact. Re-refer or discuss if: progressive rise in M-protein (particularly >25% and at least 5 g/L), new anaemia, renal deterioration, hypercalcaemia, new bone or back pain, fracture or lytic lesion, or neuropathy, nephrotic syndrome, cardiomyopathy, macroglossia, purpura or other possible MGCS features.
The AL amyloidosis diagnosis is one of the most frequently delayed and misdiagnosed conditions in medicine — AL amyloid results from the deposition of misfolded immunoglobulin light chain fibrils in organs (heart, kidneys, liver, peripheral nerves, autonomic nervous system, soft tissues). The average time from symptom onset to diagnosis is approximately 2-3 years. The clinical presentations that should prompt a myeloma/amyloid screen: (1) restrictive cardiomyopathy with heart failure without prior cardiac disease (amyloid heart — the most common cause of death in AL amyloid); (2) nephrotic syndrome (heavy proteinuria without haematuria — amyloid kidney); (3) peripheral sensorimotor neuropathy + autonomic dysfunction (amyloid nerves); (4) macroglossia (enlarged tongue — virtually pathognomonic of AL amyloid when present); (5) periorbital bruising (raccoon eyes) from amyloid deposits in facial skin vessels. In any of these presentations, a serum FLC assay + SPEP + immunofixation (amyloid screen) should be performed as part of the diagnostic workup.

MGUS Monitoring & Myeloma Treatment Overview

Low-risk MGUS monitoring (GP only if delegated by haematology)
After haematology has established MGUS and given a follow-up plan: repeat at about 6 months — FBC, creatinine/eGFR, calcium, paraprotein quantification/SPEP, and serum FLC if clinically appropriate or included in the haematology plan. Thereafter low-risk MGUS may be monitored annually or at a longer interval according to the specialist plan and life expectancy (BSH 2023). Use the same laboratory platform where possible — assay variation can be clinically relevant. Educate patient: MGUS is a benign finding in most cases; monitoring detects the minority who progress (approximately 1% per year). Symptoms to report immediately: bone pain, back pain, fatigue, thirst/polyuria, recurrent infections, unexplained swelling.
High-risk MGUS monitoring (haematology-led)
Non-IgG paraprotein or paraprotein >15 g/L or abnormal FLC ratio: 6-monthly paraprotein + FBC + creatinine + calcium + FLC. Imaging and frequency as set by haematology — high-intermediate and high-risk MGUS should usually remain under haematology-led follow-up. Consider bone marrow biopsy if any progression.
Myeloma first-line treatment (haematology-led)
Triplet induction: bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor) + lenalidomide + dexamethasone (VRd) or daratumumab + bortezomib + lenalidomide + dexamethasone (dara-VRd — increasingly preferred). Transplant-eligible (age <70, fit): autologous HSCT after induction. Transplant-ineligible (frail/elderly): continuous lenalidomide + dexamethasone. Maintenance: lenalidomide. Median PFS has doubled in the last decade — from approximately 3 years to 5-6 years. GP role: managing steroid side effects, infection prophylaxis, thromboembolic prophylaxis (lenalidomide + steroid = high VTE risk — rivaroxaban 10 mg OD).
Antibody deficiency — immunoglobulin replacement (specialist)
Immunoglobulin replacement (IVIg or SCIg) is specialist initiated, based on infection burden, organ damage, vaccine responses, IgG level, comorbidity and response to antimicrobial and vaccination strategies. Dose and trough targets are individualised — clinical infection control is more important than a universal numeric target. In myeloma-associated hypogammaglobulinaemia, NICE NG35 advises considering IVIg replacement with recurrent infections — not solely because the IgG is low. Home SCIg available for stable patients. GP role: annual review, infection surveillance, vaccination (no live vaccines), monitoring for complications (bronchiectasis, lymphoma in long-term CVID).
The lenalidomide VTE risk is a clinically important prescribing safety concern in myeloma management — lenalidomide (an immunomodulatory drug used in myeloma treatment) in combination with dexamethasone has a venous thromboembolism risk of approximately 15-20% without prophylaxis. Thromboprophylaxis is mandatory during lenalidomide + dexamethasone therapy: a LMWH or a DOAC for higher-risk patients, or aspirin for lower-risk patients, per the haematology protocol (dose per BNF / specialist). GPs who receive discharge medications for myeloma patients on lenalidomide should ensure: (1) that anticoagulation or antiplatelet prophylaxis is prescribed and the patient is taking it; (2) that the patient knows the symptoms of DVT and PE; (3) that the lenalidomide is on the clinical record with the associated anticoagulation. The contraception requirement for lenalidomide is also critical — lenalidomide is teratogenic (thalidomide analogue), and all men and women of reproductive age receiving it must use effective contraception. In the UK this is managed through the lenalidomide Pregnancy Prevention Programme (MHRA) via the haematology centre, but GPs should be aware of this requirement when reviewing medications.

Complications of Myeloma — Bone Disease & Renal Failure

Myeloma bone disease
Osteolytic lesions: RANKL overexpression by myeloma cells activates osteoclasts + inhibits osteoblasts → bone destruction without new bone formation (lytic "punched out" lesions on X-ray). Bone-modifying treatment for confirmed myeloma is haematology-led — the GP does not initiate it. NICE NG35: offer zoledronic acid; disodium pamidronate if zoledronic acid is contraindicated or not tolerated; sodium clodronate if these are unsuitable. Arrange dental assessment before treatment where possible, but do not delay clinically urgent treatment — dental care then as soon as possible. Zoledronic acid: measure renal function before each dose and ensure adequate hydration; severe renal impairment needs specialist risk–benefit assessment (MHRA). If the specialist selects denosumab: correct hypocalcaemia and vitamin D deficiency first, monitor calcium (especially in severe renal impairment), warn about hypocalcaemia symptoms, maintain dental prevention and assess osteonecrosis-of-the-jaw risk, and agree a plan for stopping or transitioning (rebound bone turnover and vertebral fractures after discontinuation). Calcium + vitamin D as advised by the specialist.
Vertebral fracture/spinal cord compression in myeloma
Vertebral fracture (pathological) → acute severe back pain, tenderness. Suspected MSCC (NICE NG234): neurological symptoms/signs — leg weakness, gait disturbance, sensory loss, radicular pain, saddle anaesthesia, bladder or bowel dysfunction — are an oncological emergency: contact the local MSCC coordinator/acute oncology service immediately; whole-spine MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 hours; immobilise without delay if symptoms/signs suggest spinal instability. Give dexamethasone 16 mg orally (or equivalent parenteral dose) as soon as possible when MSCC is suspected in the appropriate clinical context, continuing only under the specialist pathway; consider a proton-pump inhibitor and monitor glucose. Avoid steroids before tissue diagnosis when lymphoma is a significant alternative and there is no neurological emergency, unless the specialist team advises. Severe spinal pain without neurological signs (suspected spinal metastases) → seek MSCC advice within 24 hours and MRI according to local pathway urgency. Radiotherapy or surgical decompression are specialist decisions.
Myeloma renal failure (light chain nephropathy)
Cast nephropathy: light chains precipitate in renal tubules forming casts → direct tubular damage. Aim: rapidly reduce free light chain load with chemotherapy. Hydration (avoid dehydration). Avoid nephrotoxins: NSAIDs (worsens renal failure), iodinated contrast (higher risk of AKI), aminoglycosides. Monitor eGFR closely. AKI or rapidly deteriorating renal function with suspected myeloma → emergency assessment; renal input per haematology/renal advice.
Hypercalcaemia in myeloma
Severe or symptomatic hypercalcaemia requires same-day hospital assessment; urgent emergency management particularly with confusion, reduced consciousness, vomiting, dehydration, arrhythmia, acute kidney injury or calcium around/above 3.0 mmol/L — follow the local acute hypercalcaemia pathway. Interpret calcium with albumin, symptoms, renal function and rate of rise; a value below 3.0 mmol/L can still need urgent treatment if symptomatic or rapidly increasing. Hospital treatment (IV fluids, then specialist-chosen bone-modifying treatment) is not started in primary care.
Spinal cord compression in myeloma is the most devastating acute orthopaedic complication and requires rapid GP recognition — approximately 5-10% of myeloma patients develop spinal cord compression during the course of their disease. The pathology: lytic vertebral lesions weaken vertebral bodies → pathological fracture or direct plasmacytoma extension → compression of the spinal cord or cauda equina. The cardinal symptoms are: new or acutely worsening thoracic or lumbar back pain (particularly if band-like with radiation around the chest wall), leg weakness (bilateral), urinary retention or incontinence, and bowel dysfunction. The safe action: neurological symptoms or signs → immediate MSCC coordinator/acute oncology contact and whole-spine MRI within 24 hours; severe spinal pain without neurology → MSCC advice within 24 hours (NICE NG234). The treatment window is narrow — spinal cord injury that has been present for more than 12-24 hours before treatment begins often results in permanent neurological deficit. Dexamethasone 16 mg orally (or equivalent parenteral dose) as soon as possible when MSCC is suspected (NICE NG234) reduces cord oedema; continue only under the specialist pathway, and avoid it before tissue diagnosis where lymphoma is a significant alternative and there is no neurological emergency.

Lifestyle

MGUS, Myeloma & Immunodeficiency Patient Support

MGUS patient education Key messages: MGUS is not myeloma; the vast majority of people with MGUS never develop myeloma; monitoring follows the haematology plan (often at 6 months, then annually or less often if low risk); there is nothing you can do to prevent or accelerate progression. UK Multiple Myeloma UK (myeloma.org.uk) provides excellent MGUS patient information. Myeloma UK helpline: 0800 980 3332. Annual monitoring creates anxiety for some patients — acknowledge this and discuss the rationale.
Myeloma bone health Avoid: contact sports, heavy lifting, activities with high fracture risk. Low-impact exercise beneficial: swimming, walking, gentle cycling. Physiotherapy for balance and fall prevention. Dental hygiene critical (ONJ risk with bisphosphonate/denosumab — regular dental checks, no extractions without haematologist awareness). Calcium + vitamin D supplementation throughout treatment.
Myeloma infection prevention Vaccination: annual inactivated influenza; pneumococcal per the current UKHSA Green Book (chapter 25) and the patient's treatment/HSCT status; recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) for eligible immunosuppressed adults per Green Book chapter 28a (myeloma and Waldenström macroglobulinaemia are listed as causing severe immunosuppression); COVID-19 per current national advice. Avoid live vaccines during active myeloma treatment and seek specialist advice after transplantation or with significant immune deficiency. Responses may be reduced, but vaccination should not generally be withheld solely for that reason. Prompt antibiotic treatment for any infection (myeloma = severe immunosuppression). Daily cotrimoxazole or aciclovir prophylaxis: check with haematologist. Carry written explanation of immunosuppressed status.
CVID lifestyle and support CVID is lifelong — immunoglobulin replacement, where indicated, is not a cure but reduces infection burden. UK Primary Immunodeficiency Network (UKPIN). Immunodeficiency UK (immunodeficiencyuk.com). Regular dental care (oral bacteria are a major infection portal). Medical alert card: "I have primary immunodeficiency — please treat my infections promptly and do not give live vaccines."
IgA deficiency and blood products Selective IgA deficiency (IgA <0.07 g/L with normal IgG and IgM): most patients do not experience anaphylaxis to blood components. After a previous severe allergic/anaphylactic transfusion reaction, future transfusion is discussed with transfusion medicine (washed red cells and platelets for elective transfusion; IgA-deficient plasma if plasma is required). With no previous reaction, standard components can generally be given with enhanced observation. Life-saving transfusion must not be delayed while IgA-deficient components are sourced. Record as a clinically significant transfusion alert with the relevant history — not automatically as a drug allergy (BSH 2023).
Myeloma financial and practical support Myeloma UK: specialist information, patient support groups, financial advice. Macmillan Cancer Support: practical and financial advice (claiming PIP, disability benefits, blue badge). Citizens Advice: benefits entitlement. Travel insurance: declare myeloma — specialist cancer travel insurance (Free Spirit, Insurancewith). Fatigue management: occupational therapy referral.
Monitoring during myeloma remission GP role in shared care: prescription continuation (lenalidomide maintenance — with thromboprophylaxis, contraception monitoring), infection management (prompt antibiotic treatment), bone health (calcium/vitamin D, dental review), mental health (depression very common in myeloma — PHQ-9 annually), annual skin check (increased skin cancer risk), renal function (light chain monitoring).
Advance care planning in myeloma Myeloma is incurable (currently) but increasingly chronic — median survival has improved dramatically. Advance care planning conversations should happen early: DNAR status, preferred place of care, treatment escalation plan (resuscitation decisions), end-of-life preferences. GP and Macmillan nurse in parallel. ReSPECT process.
Myeloma UK is the leading UK charity for myeloma patients and GPs should routinely recommend it — the organisation provides exceptional patient education resources, maintains a myeloma specialist nurse helpline, runs patient support groups, advocates for treatment access, and funds research. Their website (myeloma.org.uk) has downloadable condition-specific guides for MGUS, smouldering myeloma, active myeloma, and relapsed myeloma — written by myeloma specialists and reviewed by patients. The specialist nurse helpline (0800 980 3332) provides telephone advice and is particularly valuable for patients who have questions outside of their haematology appointments. GPs who ensure newly diagnosed myeloma and MGUS patients receive this resource at the diagnosis consultation provide a significant quality-of-life benefit.

Safety

Follow-Up, MGUS Monitoring & Red Flags

MGUS monitoring (GP, only if delegated by haematology)
Per the haematology plan: about 6 months after diagnosis, then annually or at a longer interval for low-risk MGUS — paraprotein quantification/SPEP (document trend; same laboratory platform where possible), FBC, calcium, creatinine/eGFR, and serum FLC if clinically appropriate or in the plan (FLC is not always the best progression marker and is confounded by renal disease). Re-refer or discuss if: progressive M-protein rise (particularly >25% and at least 5 g/L), new anaemia, renal deterioration, hypercalcaemia, new bone or back pain, fracture or lytic lesion, or possible MGCS features (neuropathy, nephrotic syndrome, cardiomyopathy, macroglossia, purpura).
After myeloma treatment (shared care)
Monthly FBC + paraprotein during active treatment (haematology led). GP manages: infection treatment, VTE prophylaxis, steroid-related complications (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, osteoporosis), bisphosphonate/denosumab continuation, lenalidomide prescription continuation with contraception check. Annual dental review.
CVID IgG monitoring
Immunology-led: trough IgG against an individualised target (no universal numeric target), infection burden, and complication surveillance (bronchiectasis, lymphoproliferative disease) per the specialist plan.
IgA deficiency blood product alert
Record as a clinically significant transfusion alert with the relevant history (e.g. previous transfusion reaction, anti-IgA antibodies) — not automatically as a drug allergy. Ensure the alert is visible to any clinician ordering transfusion; life-saving transfusion is never delayed.
Same-day / urgent
Neurological symptoms or signs suggesting MSCC (leg weakness, gait disturbance, sensory loss, radicular pain, saddle anaesthesia, bladder or bowel dysfunction) → contact MSCC coordinator/acute oncology immediately; whole-spine MRI within 24 hours · severe spinal pain without neurology → MSCC advice within 24 hours · severe or symptomatic hypercalcaemia → same-day hospital assessment; emergency with confusion, reduced consciousness, vomiting, dehydration, arrhythmia, AKI or calcium around/above 3.0 mmol/L · symptomatic hyperviscosity, AKI, or sepsis/neutropenic sepsis on treatment → emergency pathway
Within 1 week
MGUS M-protein rising progressively (particularly >25% and at least 5 g/L) → discuss with or re-refer to haematology · New anaemia or renal impairment in known MGUS → haematology suspected cancer pathway referral (CRAB criteria developing) · Previous severe transfusion reaction with IgA deficiency → transfusion medicine/immunology advice before future elective transfusion
Do not
Diagnose myeloma from paraprotein concentration alone · use an isolated abnormal FLC ratio as diagnostic · delay emergency transfer for imaging or specialist confirmation · start bisphosphonate or denosumab treatment in primary care for suspected myeloma · assume every patient with IgA deficiency requires IgA-depleted blood · use a universal IgG trough target for CVID
The MGUS progression monitoring safety net requires specific patient education that is often not given at the time of the MGUS diagnosis — patients must know the symptoms of myeloma progression so they can self-report them between annual monitoring blood tests. The key symptoms: persistent bone pain (particularly back or ribs — lytic bone lesions), unexplained fatigue (anaemia from marrow infiltration), thirst and excessive urination (hypercalcaemia), recurrent infections, leg weakness or numbness (peripheral neuropathy from amyloid, or spinal cord compression). A myeloma patient who develops sudden severe back pain should attend the GP the same day — not wait for the next scheduled appointment. GPs should explicitly tell MGUS patients: 'If you develop significant new bone pain or any of these symptoms between your annual blood tests, please contact us immediately rather than waiting for your next appointment.'
