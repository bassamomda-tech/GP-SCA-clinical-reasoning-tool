Rectal bleeding in adults

For: adults (18 and over) with fresh PR bleeding, melaena, occult blood or haematochezia, in primary care. Not for: children. Known UC or Crohn's follows the specialist plan; upper GI symptoms with melaena are hospital-assessed.

NICE NG12HTG690BSG LGIB 2019BSG IDA 2021NG130
Core rule: never attribute rectal bleeding to haemorrhoids before examination and the NG12 FIT criteria. Visible piles do not exclude a proximal cancer, and a FIT <10 does not exclude cancer when symptoms persist.

0Stop — call 999 now — before PR, FIT or bloods

  • Shock, judged clinically (shock index HR/SBP >1 suggests it): pallor, cold peripheries, confusion, syncope → 999
  • Profuse fresh rectal bleeding with instability, or melaena with haemodynamic compromise → 999
  • Any GI bleeding after AAA repair: aortoenteric fistula until excluded (a herald bleed precedes exsanguination) → 999
  • While waiting: the patient does not transfer independently. Do not delay transfer for a PR, FIT or bloods. Do not stop anticoagulation without specialist advice.

2Screen — NG12 colorectal cancer (updated April 2026)

  • Offer FIT: unexplained rectal bleeding at ≥50; under 50 with bleeding plus unexplained abdominal pain or weight loss; change in bowel habit or iron-deficiency anaemia at any adult age; abdominal mass; anaemia at ≥60.
  • FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → Suspected cancer pathway
  • Rectal mass, unexplained anal mass or unexplained anal ulceration → consider a Suspected cancer pathway referral; do not wait for FIT.
  • Iron-deficiency anaemia + bleeding: a low FIT does not exclude; men and post-menopausal women generally need upper and lower GI assessment.
  • FIT <10 with persistent or recurrent bleeding → still needs colorectal assessment; judgement can override a negative FIT.
  • Acute severe UC (≥6 bloody stools a day + temperature >37.8°C, HR >90, Hb <105 g/L or ESR >30) → Same-day admission. Suspected new IBD, ischaemic colitis, recurrent significant diverticular bleeding → Urgent gastro

3Assess — stable patients only

☐ HR, BP lying and standing (systolic drop >20 mmHg = significant loss), temperature, RR, SpO₂, pallor  ☐ Abdomen: mass, liver, tenderness  ☐ Perianal inspection  ☐ PR with consent and chaperone; proctoscopy for internal piles; too painful → document and arrange specialist assessment  ☐ FBC, ferritin by indication; CRP and calprotectin only if IBD suspected; stool culture for acute bloody diarrhoea; rectal STI screen if possible

Do not repeat FIT to get a "normal", or use FIT to judge an acute bleed.

4Classify — only once block 2 is applied

  • Haemorrhoids — bright red, on the paper, painless; internal piles need a proctoscope → block 5.
  • Anal fissure — pain on defaecation, sentinel pile. Recurrent, lateral, multiple or non-healing → think Crohn's, cancer, infection, trauma.
  • IBD or infective colitis — bloody diarrhoea, mucus, fever, travel; IBD commonest under 40 → stool tests, block 2.
  • Diverticular or angiodysplasia — sudden, painless, large volume, older → hospital (block 1).
  • Colorectal cancer — blood mixed with stool, change in habit, weight loss, tenesmus, anaemia → block 2.
  • Uncertain — say so; apply the NG12 criteria and book review. Never default to "piles".

5Treat today — after cancer risk is assessed

  • Haemorrhoids grade I–II: fibre, fluids, no straining; laxative by stool consistency; one topical steroid/anaesthetic preparation, about 5–7 days at most. Avoid codeine.
  • Acute fissure: stool softening, fibre, fluids, analgesia, short topical anaesthetic. Persistent or chronic: GTN 0.4% (headache; not with PDE-5 inhibitors or hypotension) or diltiazem 2% (unlicensed, document consent) BD for about 8 weeks.
  • Anticoagulant or NSAID: review NSAIDs; do not stop anticoagulation without specialist advice.
  • Established UC: follow the specialist plan. Never treat undiagnosed bleeding as UC. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Refer routinely

  • Grade III–IV haemorrhoids, or selected grade II → banding or surgery.
  • Fissure not healed after 8 weeks of GTN or diltiazem → surgical (botulinum toxin, sphincterotomy). Complex fistula, rectal prolapse → colorectal.
  • Rectal STI → sexual health. Check NHS bowel screening enrolment (from 50).

7Never reassure by

  • Visible haemorrhoids, or bright red blood on the paper.
  • A FIT <10 when bleeding persists or anaemia develops.
  • A previous negative bowel-screening FIT in a symptomatic patient.
  • Young age.
  • Normal lying observations: check postural BP.
  • Stool colour alone: a brisk upper GI bleed can present as fresh blood.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 for heavy bleeding, collapse, feeling faint or dizzy with bleeding, vomiting blood, or severe tummy pain with bleeding.” Same-day “Black, tarry stools — go to A&E today. Bleeding that comes back heavily or has not settled after 48–72 hours, new weight loss or feeling unwell, or no cancer-pathway appointment arriving — contact us today.”

Follow-up: topical treatment reviewed at 1 week; FIT result actioned; fissure healing checked at 8 weeks. Persistent or recurrent bleeding → re-investigate, don't re-reassure.
