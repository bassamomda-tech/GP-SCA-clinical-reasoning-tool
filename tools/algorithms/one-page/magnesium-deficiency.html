Magnesium deficiency in adults

For: adults in primary care with serum magnesium <0.7 mmol/L (lab range), or low K⁺/Ca²⁺ that will not correct. Not for: children; hospital IV replacement (full algorithm, step 6); obstetric magnesium sulfate for pre-eclampsia (obstetric team); refeeding (same-day hospital, NICE CG32).

Core rule: potassium and calcium will not stay corrected while magnesium is low — check magnesium whenever K⁺ or Ca²⁺ is refractory and replace it first. IV magnesium is a hospital treatment only; review any long-term PPI.

0Stop — call 999 now — regardless of the exact magnesium value

  • Torsades, ventricular or haemodynamically significant arrhythmia, or significant QT prolongation → 999
  • Seizure, tetany or severe neuromuscular symptoms; muscle weakness with dysphagia or respiratory muscle involvement → 999
  • Syncope or collapse with suspected electrolyte-related arrhythmia → 999
  • Never in general practice: IV magnesium (hospital, with ECG, BP, renal and electrolyte monitoring) or IM magnesium. Torsades follows the resuscitation algorithm.

1Triage by level — confirm; repeat if unexpected

Mg mmol/LAction
<0.4, or <0.5 with symptomsImmediate Same-day hospital transfer, even if apparently asymptomatic at <0.4
0.4–0.5, no symptomsSame-day clinical review, ECG, K⁺/Ca²⁺; discuss with acute medicine — not automatically oral
~0.5–0.7, stable, eGFR ≥30Oral replacement in primary care + treat the cause (block 5)

2Screen — any one moves the patient out

  • Refractory low K⁺ or Ca²⁺ with low Mg → immediate Same-day hospital
  • AKI, eGFR <30, dialysis, transplant or oliguria (magnesium accumulates) → Same-day hospital; renal advice before any replacement.
  • Cannot take or absorb oral treatment → Same-day hospital
  • Suspected or high-risk refeeding → Same-day hospital/dietetic assessment (NICE CG32); not an isolated Mg plan.
  • On cisplatin, amphotericin or aminoglycosides → replacement directed by the prescribing specialist team; liaise.

3Assess — once blocks 0–2 are answered

☐ Cramps, tremor, weakness, paraesthesiae, palpitations; Chvostek/Trousseau  ☐ ECG if Mg substantially low, symptoms, low K⁺, QT-prolonging drugs or cardiac disease (long PR/QT, wide QRS)  ☐ U&E/eGFR before any replacement, K⁺, adjusted Ca²⁺, phosphate, albumin; glucose/HbA1c if relevant  ☐ Medicines, alcohol, diarrhoea, laxatives, nutrition; coeliac serology if malabsorption suspected

Not in primary care: IV/IM magnesium; urinary Mg or FEMg is a specialist-level test.

4Classify the cause — where is it being lost?

  • PPI (prolonged use; impaired gut absorption) → review indication and dose.
  • Renal loss — loop or thiazide diuretics, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, cisplatin, aminoglycosides, amphotericin, foscarnet; marked glycosuria; aldosteronism; Gitelman/Bartter.
  • GI loss — chronic diarrhoea, IBD, coeliac, laxatives, short bowel, post-bariatric → treat the cause.
  • Alcohol or poor intake → check K⁺, phosphate, Ca²⁺, glucose, nutrition; thiamine per protocol (Pabrinex has no Mg).
  • Shift — refeeding (block 2), hungry bone, acute pancreatitis. Uncertain or recurrent → block 6.

5Treat today — ~0.5–0.7, stable, eGFR ≥30

  • Oral Mg (GM formulary): magnesium aspartate 10 mmol sachets (first choice) or glycerophosphate 4 mmol chewable 1–2 TDS (not if GFR <30 or high phosphate). Start low, divided, with food; diarrhoea limits dose. Doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • PPI: lowest effective dose; step down or stop if the indication allows. Must continue (Barrett's, severe oesophagitis, high-risk gastroprotection) → oral Mg + periodic Mg.
  • Diuretic: lowest effective dose; do not switch loop to thiazide just for Mg. Amiloride only with specialist advice (hyperkalaemia).
  • Recheck Mg, K⁺, Ca²⁺, renal function 7–14 days; 1–4 weeks if substantially low or high dose; then 3–6-monthly if the cause persists.

6Refer routinely

  • Nephrology: unexplained, persistent, recurrent or familial; low K⁺ with metabolic alkalosis (Gitelman/Bartter); renal wasting not explained by drugs.
  • Gastroenterology: uninvestigated malabsorption, post-bariatric. Endocrinology: primary aldosteronism.
  • Low despite tolerated oral Mg and PPI cannot stop → gastroenterology, renal or acute medicine. Pregnancy → with the obstetric team.

7Never reassure by — risk depends on level, symptoms, ECG, K⁺/Ca²⁺ and renal function

  • Calling K⁺ or Ca²⁺ "refractory" before magnesium is checked and replaced.
  • Treating 0.4–0.5 as routine oral without a same-day discussion.
  • Starting oral magnesium before checking eGFR.
  • A "therapeutic" digoxin level when Mg is low — check digoxin and Mg together.
  • Adding a QT-prolonging drug before low Mg is corrected.
  • Assuming a long-term PPI still has an indication.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 if you have palpitations with dizziness, fainting or collapse, a fit, painful muscle spasms, or new weakness with difficulty swallowing or breathing.”

Same day: “Contact us today if you cannot take or keep down the magnesium. If it gives you diarrhoea, take a lower dose. Come for your blood test in 7–14 days.” Remote: Mg <0.5 or refractory low K⁺/Ca²⁺ needs same-day assessment, not a message.

✓Safe to manage in primary care only if — all five, documented

No block 0–2 feature; Mg ~0.5–0.7, stable eGFR ≥30; ECG done where indicated and normal K⁺, Ca²⁺, phosphate checked Cause acted on (PPI, diuretic, alcohol, gut); recheck booked Safety-net given
