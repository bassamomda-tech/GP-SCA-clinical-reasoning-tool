Blurry or reduced vision

For: adults with new or worsening blurred or reduced vision, including diplopia, face to face or remote. Not for: children, except an absent red reflex (block 2); eye injury beyond first aid goes to the eye emergency service.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG82NG81NG12RCOphthBSR GCA
Core rule: sudden visual loss is an emergency until proven otherwise. Cover each eye, check acuity with a pinhole, and never delay transfer for GP tests, treatment or a normal fundoscopy.

0Stop — call 999 now — most eye emergencies are same-day, not 999

  • Sudden visual loss with stroke features (facial weakness, slurred speech), reduced consciousness or focal neurology → 999 (thrombolysis window).
  • Painful red eye or angle closure with collapse, marked vomiting, confusion or systemic illness; orbital cellulitis with visual loss, proptosis or toxicity → 999
  • Pregnancy: visual symptoms with eclampsia or severe hypertension; chemical injury needing ongoing irrigation; anyone unable to transfer safely → 999
  • While waiting: chemical injury — irrigate now with water or saline, remove lenses if easy, continue during transfer until pH neutral; no neutralising chemicals. Do not delay transfer for GP tests.

1Triage by onset — monocular = eye; binocular = brain

OnsetAction
Sudden, persistentSame-day eye emergency / stroke pathway

Months–yearsPinhole improves → optometrist; else block 4

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Curtain, field loss, or flashes/floaters with reduced vision → Same-day. Flashes/floaters alone → Dilated exam ≤24 h
  • Painful red eye, reduced vision, haloes (angle closure) → Same-day eye casualty. No dilating drops; drugs and positioning as the eye team advises.
  • Over 50, headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication + visual symptoms (GCA) → steroid now + Same-day assessment.
  • Sudden painless monocular loss, including in diabetes → Same-day eye emergency / stroke pathway.
  • New diplopia with dilated pupil (3rd nerve), severe headache, neuro signs or proptosis; papilloedema → Same-day
  • Suspected wet AMD (new distortion, grey central patch) → Macula service ≤1 working day. Child, absent red reflex → consider Urgent ≤2 weeks (NG12)

3Assess — only once blocks 0–2 are answered

☐ Acuity each eye, with glasses and pinhole  ☐ Pupils, swinging torch (RAPD)  ☐ Confrontation fields  ☐ Eye movements  ☐ Red eye, proptosis  ☐ Fundoscopy (disc, macula)

GCA: FBC, ESR, CRP alongside treatment. Not in primary care: CT head for pinhole-correctable blur.

4Classify — only if the screen is negative

  • Refractive error or cataract — gradual, painless; glare, haloes → optometrist.
  • Dry eye — fluctuating, improves with blinking, gritty → lubricants. Not if painful, red and vision reduced.
  • AMD — central loss, distortion. Dry → optometry review; wet → macula service ≤1 working day.
  • Glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy — often silent; found by optometry or retinal screening. GPs do not re-grade images.
  • Optic neuritis — subacute, pain on eye movement, red desaturation, RAPD → urgent ophthalmology or neurology; no GP steroids.
  • Uncertain — say so; eye review with a date, and safety-net.

5Treat today — only these

  • Suspected GCA: prednisolone now per local pathway (BSR: 40–60 mg; visual loss commonly 60 mg); IV methylprednisolone is specialist-led.
  • Dry eye: hypromellose 0.3% 4–6 times daily ± carbomer gel at night; preservative-free if used often.
  • Vascular causes: BP, HbA1c, lipids, smoking, AF — never delaying eye referral. No antiplatelet or anticoagulant for unexplained visual disturbance alone.
  • Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Refer routinely

  • Gradual bilateral blur, mild dry eye, stable mild cataract → optometrist first.
  • Routine ophthalmology: cataract with functional impairment; suspicious disc, repeatable field defect or Goldmann IOP ≥24 mmHg (NG81); stable dry AMD.
  • Urgent ophthalmology: retinal vein occlusion, new proptosis, isolated stable diplopia. Neurology: bitemporal hemianopia, fatigable ptosis.
  • Hydroxychloroquine: hospital retinal monitoring yearly after 5 years (after 1 year if eGFR <60, tamoxifen or >5 mg/kg/day).

7Never reassure by

  • A normal GP fundoscopy or confrontation fields.
  • Normal ESR or CRP in suspected GCA.
  • Blurring that has fully recovered (possible TIA).
  • A diabetes label: sudden loss is not automatically a bleed.
  • Calling a painful red eye with reduced vision "dry eye".
  • A single non-contact tonometer reading.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 if you lose vision with a drooping face, weak arm, slurred speech, confusion, collapse or feeling very ill.”

Same-day “Go to the eye emergency service today for any sudden loss of vision, a curtain or shadow, new flashes and floaters, a painful red eye with cloudy vision, vision that dims then recovers, new double vision, or — if over 50 — headache with scalp tenderness or jaw pain on chewing.” Known AMD: new distortion or central blur → macula service ≤1 working day.
