Ankle pain in adults

For: adults with ankle pain, after an injury or without one, in primary care. Not for: children (use the limping child algorithm); ankle swelling without pain (use the ankle swelling algorithm).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG38NICE NG219NICE NG158NICE NG226MHRA fluoroquinolones
Core rule: apply the Ottawa ankle and foot rules after every injury, do the calf-squeeze (Simmonds–Thompson) test on every posterior heel pain or lost push-off, and treat a hot, swollen ankle as septic arthritis until excluded.

0Stop — call 999 now

  • Deformed ankle, open injury or neurovascular compromise (pallor, cold foot, absent pulse, paraesthesia) → 999 / ED for urgent reduction.
  • Possible PE: chest pain, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, syncope or unexplained tachycardia → Same-day hospital PE assessment, immediately; collapsed, severely breathless or haemodynamically unstable → 999

1Triage — Ottawa rules, age >5, reliable examination

FindingAction
Ottawa ankleMalleolar pain + bony tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus (lower 6 cm), or cannot take 4 weight-bearing steps both immediately and at assessment → X-ray via local pathway
Ottawa footMidfoot pain + tenderness at 5th metatarsal base or navicular, or the 4-step test → X-ray
Rule unreliableIntoxication, neuropathy, distracting injuries, altered mental state, suspected stress or pathological fracture, or concern remains → X-ray anyway

2Screen — serious causes

  • Achilles rupture: sudden "kick", absent plantar flexion on calf squeeze (a gap may be absent) → Same-day orthopaedics/ED; immobilise in plantar flexion, avoid dorsiflexion.
  • Hot, swollen ankle with severe pain or restricted movement → septic until excluded → Same-day hospital. Gout and sepsis can coexist.
  • New unilateral calf swelling, pain, warmth: two-level Wells (NG158): ≥2 → proximal leg-vein USS ≤4 h, or D-dimer + interim anticoagulation + USS ≤24 h; ≤1 → D-dimer, USS if positive.
  • Widened mortise (syndesmosis), osteochondral defect, peroneal tendon dislocation, or stress fracture at a high-risk site (navicular, talus, anterior tibial cortex, 5th metatarsal base) → Urgent orthopaedics.
  • Confirmed fracture: fracture clinic ≤72 h; VTE risk via local pathway.

3Assess

☐ Skin, deformity, pulses, sensation  ☐ Malleoli, 5th metatarsal base, navicular, proximal fibula (Maisonneuve), syndesmosis  ☐ Calf squeeze prone, compare sides  ☐ Anterior drawer, talar tilt vs other side  ☐ Gait, single-leg heel raise, "too many toes"  ☐ Suspected infection: FBC, CRP, blood cultures, aspiration  ☐ Urate during or after a flare; renal function before NSAID, colchicine or ULT

Not routine: imaging for OA or tendinopathy at presentation.

4Classify — only if blocks 0–2 are negative

  • Lateral ligament sprain — inversion, ATFL tenderness; grade I stretch, II partial tear, III complete rupture.
  • Achilles tendinopathy — heel pain worse mornings and after exercise, thickened tendon, calf squeeze normal. Ask about fluoroquinolones.
  • Gout — acute hot, red, swollen ankle or 1st MTP. Urate <360 μmol/L in a flare → repeat ≥2 weeks after it settles.
  • Tibialis posterior — medial pain, flat arch, "too many toes". Peroneal tendon — lateral pain after a sprain, snap or clunk.
  • Ankle OA — insidious, stiffness <30 min, previous fracture; diagnosed clinically.
  • Uncertain — not better at 4–6 weeks → re-examine, X-ray if not done, consider imaging (peroneal tendon, osteochondral).

5Treat today — doses per BNF and local formulary

  • Sprain I–II: protection, relative rest, ice, compression, elevation; early progressive weight-bearing; lace-up brace for significant sprains or sport. Short NSAID may be considered, not routinely.
  • Acute gout (sepsis excluded): offer NSAID, colchicine or short oral steroid by comorbidity, renal function, interactions. Keep established ULT.
  • Achilles tendinopathy: progressive tendon-loading programme over about 12 weeks. No steroid injection into or around the Achilles.
  • Ankle OA: exercise, weight management; consider a topical NSAID. Not routinely paracetamol or weak opioids; no strong opioids.

6Refer routinely and review

  • Physiotherapy: persistent, recurrent or unstable sprains; tendinopathy; tibialis posterior dysfunction; OA.
  • Orthopaedics/MSK: tendinopathy not better after about 12 weeks of loading; OA limiting function despite conservative care; progressive flat foot.
  • Rheumatology: inflammatory arthritis; gout refractory to ULT, tophi or stones.
  • Gout at 4 weeks: urate; offer ULT for multiple or troublesome flares, CKD 3–5, diuretics, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis (discuss after a first); target <360 μmol/L.

7Never reassure by

  • No palpable gap, or able to walk — Achilles rupture is still possible.
  • A negative Ottawa rule when the examination is unreliable.
  • Urate crystals or known gout in a hot joint — infection can coexist.
  • A normal urate during a flare, or a normal early X-ray in a suspected stress fracture.
  • "Another sprain" — recurrent giving way needs rehabilitation, not repeated rest.
  • A calf squeeze to rule out DVT.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 if you get chest pain, breathlessness, cough up blood, collapse or a racing heart, or if your foot goes pale, cold, numb or tingly.”

Same day: “Go to A&E today if you feel a sudden snap at the back of the ankle, cannot push off, or can no longer put weight on the foot. Contact us or NHS 111 straight away for a fever with a hot, swollen ankle, or new calf swelling or pain. Come back if not improving in 1–2 weeks.”
