Leg ulcers in adults

For: adults with an ulcer below the knee — GP, OOH and community nursing. Not for: children; ongoing compression, wound care and hosiery (full algorithm, steps 7–8). Local Greater Manchester tissue-viability, vascular and diabetic-foot pathways take precedence.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE CG168NG19NG12NG152SIGN 120
Core rule: never apply strong compression before arterial assessment (ABPI in both legs). A venous-looking leg does not exclude arterial disease, and a normal or high ABPI does not exclude it in diabetes or CKD. Assess arterial disease, diabetes, infection, atypical disease and malignancy first.

0Stop — call 999 now — before any wound-care decision

  • Necrotising fasciitis: pain out of proportion, rapidly progressive swelling or erythema, crepitus, bullae, dusky skin, systemic toxicity or shock → 999
  • Acute limb ischaemia (sudden pain, pallor, pulselessness, coldness, paraesthesia or paralysis); rapidly progressive tissue loss, or gangrene with spreading infection or sepsis → 999 / emergency admission
  • Sepsis with new confusion, altered consciousness or haemodynamic compromise; diabetic foot ulcer with sepsis, limb ischaemia, gangrene, deep abscess or necrotising infection; uncontrolled bleeding from varicose veins or ulcer → 999
  • While waiting: remove any compression; keep an ischaemic limb warm, dependent and protected — do not elevate it. Escalate before any wound-care decision.

1Triage by ABPI — both legs, trained practitioner

ABPIAction (practical, not absolute)
0.8–1.3Strong compression may be appropriate if venous on assessment (trained practitioner).
0.5–0.8Mixed disease likely → vascular/tissue-viability advice; mild compression only per local pathway.
<0.5Severe arterial disease → discuss with vascular Same-day. No strong compression.
>1.3Unreliable (incompressible) → toe pressure, waveform or vascular.

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Ischaemic rest pain, gangrene or tissue loss with a cold, pale or pulseless foot, rapidly worsening ulcer → Same-day vascular
  • Diabetic foot ulcer → foot-protection/MDT foot service within 1 working day; fever, ischaemia, gangrene, deep or bone infection, acute Charcot → Immediate hospital
  • Fever, rigors, tachycardia, raised NEWS2 → Same-day admission. Exposed bone → Same-day orthopaedic/foot team.
  • Rolled or everted edge, friable granulation, bleeding (SCC) → consider suspected cancer pathway or biopsy.
  • Violaceous undermined edge (pyoderma) → Urgent dermatology, no debridement. Purpura, livedo, multiple ulcers → urgent rheumatology.

3Assess — both legs, shoes and socks off

☐ Measure or trace; dated photo with a scale  ☐ Pulses (absent → Doppler; palpable do not exclude disease)  ☐ ABPI supine, rested  ☐ 10 g monofilament  ☐ Glucose/HbA1c, urinalysis, FBC, U&E, LFTs, lipids; CRP if infection  ☐ Drugs: nicorandil, hydroxycarbamide. Do not routinely swab (NG152) or request ANCA/ANA without compatible features.

4Classify — site, pain, pulses, ABPI

  • Venous: medial gaiter, shallow, oedema, eczema, staining; better on elevation; ABPI usually 0.8–1.3.
  • Arterial: lateral malleolus or toes, punched out; cold, pulseless; worse elevated, at night → vascular, no strong compression.
  • Mixed (ABPI often 0.5–0.8) → vascular/tissue viability before compression. Neuropathic: plantar, painless, callus, monofilament loss → foot service.
  • Pressure ulcer (immobile) → pressure-ulcer pathway; consider safeguarding if care was inadequate. Also nicorandil.
  • Uncertain — mixed disease is common; withhold strong compression.

5Treat today

  • Venous, ABPI 0.8–1.3: strong compression (about 40 mmHg) by a trained practitioner; simple low-adherent dressing.
  • Clinically infected (spreading erythema, warmth, pain, pus, fever) → oral flucloxacillin per NG152/local; review if worse or not improving in 2–3 days. Odour or exudate alone is not infection.
  • Varicose eczema: short moderate-potency steroid to the skin, not the ulcer bed; emollient; no topical antibiotics or lanolin.
  • Arterial disease: high-intensity statin, antiplatelet, BP, stop smoking. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Refer routinely

  • Vascular (CG168): every venous leg ulcer, active or healed. Tissue viability: compression, hosiery. Dermatology: biopsy, patch tests.
  • 4 weeks no clear progress → review diagnosis, compression, arterial status. 12 weeks not healed → reassess and escalate; biopsy if atypical.

7Never reassure by

  • A venous-looking leg or palpable pulses — arterial disease may still be present.
  • A normal or high ABPI in diabetes or CKD — above 1.3 is unreliable.
  • Pain or numbness under compression as "expected" — remove it, reassess same day.
  • Odour, exudate or a positive swab — colonisation is not infection.
  • A painless diabetic foot ulcer — it can deteriorate unnoticed.
  • "Granulation" at a chronic ulcer edge — it may be SCC.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 if the pain becomes far worse than the wound looks, the skin turns dusky, blisters or crackles, redness spreads fast or you feel very unwell, faint or confused; if your foot suddenly goes pale, cold, painful, numb or weak; or bleeding will not stop (press firmly and raise the leg while waiting).” Same day: “Remove the bandage and contact us today if pain increases under it or your toes go numb or cold; or if redness spreads, pus appears, the ulcer suddenly grows, black areas appear, you get a fever, or a new ulcer appears on a diabetic foot.”

Remote or OOH: convert to face to face if the ulcer cannot be seen and measured, diabetes, anticoagulation or immunosuppression, new pain under a bandage, or no timely nursing review.
