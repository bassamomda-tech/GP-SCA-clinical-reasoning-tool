Toe pain in adults

For: adults with toe or forefoot pain in primary care, with or without diabetes. Not for: children and young people (seek specialist advice; use the limping child algorithm); nail changes without pain (use the nail disorders protocol).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG19NICE NG219NICE NG12NICE NG253EULAR gout
Core rule: the first acute hot great toe is usually gout, but in a person with diabetes, or with systemic upset, exclude septic arthritis and the diabetic foot first. A toe can be ischaemic despite a palpable proximal pulse.

0Stop — call 999 now

  • Cold, pale, pulseless, painful or numb toe of acute onset (acute limb or digital ischaemia) → 999 vascular emergency. No compression; do not delay for routine investigations.
  • Hot swollen great toe + fever + rapidly spreading redness (septic arthritis, necrotising fasciitis) → 999 if signs of sepsis, septic shock or suspected necrotising fasciitis; otherwise Same-day hospital.
  • Diabetic foot with sepsis, limb ischaemia, gangrene or suspected deep soft-tissue or bone infection → Immediate hospital and inform the multidisciplinary foot service. Start antibiotics as soon as infection is suspected.

2Screen — serious causes

  • Deep ulcer, visible bone or positive probe-to-bone → Immediate hospital assessment.
  • Absent pulses or suspected ischaemia in a diabetic foot → Urgent vascular or diabetic foot assessment (toe pressures or Doppler).
  • Widening pigmented band in a toenail + Hutchinson's sign (subungual melanoma) → Suspected cancer pathway dermatology.
  • Bone erosion on X-ray + weight loss + known or suspected cancer (metastasis) → Suspected cancer pathway orthopaedic oncology.
  • High-risk foot with a painful ingrown toenail or pressure lesion → foot service ≤1 working day.

3Assess

☐ DP and PT pulses, capillary refill, temperature, colour, sensation, movement  ☐ 10 g monofilament + 128 Hz vibration  ☐ Skin: callus, ulcer, cellulitis; nails  ☐ Joints, alignment, dorsiflexion, Mulder's click  ☐ Urate (may be <360 µmol/L in a flare: repeat ≥2 weeks after); HbA1c, eGFR  ☐ Weight-bearing X-ray for deformity, arthritis, fracture

Not routine: imaging for typical Morton's, metatarsalgia, hallux valgus or uncomplicated gout. ABPI can be falsely reassuring in diabetes.

4Classify — only if blocks 0–2 are negative

  • Gout (podagra) — nocturnal onset, hot, red, very tender 1st MTP; clinical diagnosis.
  • Hallux valgus / rigidus — medial MTP prominence; or stiff toe, restricted dorsiflexion, pain on push-off.
  • Morton's neuroma — burning between 3rd/4th toes, "walking on a pebble"; Mulder's click supports, not diagnostic alone.
  • Metatarsalgia / sesamoiditis — pain under metatarsal heads or 1st MTP on weight-bearing.
  • Ingrown toenail / paronychia — nail fold pain, swelling, granulation.
  • Uncertain → X-ray; MRI for suspected osteomyelitis, Charcot, stress fracture or avascular necrosis.

5Treat today — doses per BNF and local formulary

  • Gout (septic arthritis considered): NSAID with PPI, colchicine or short oral steroid by renal, GI and CV risk. Avoid colchicine with clarithromycin or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, or seek advice.
  • Diabetic foot infection: antibiotics now, oral if stable, per NG19 and local guidance; ≥7 days, up to 6 weeks for osteomyelitis.
  • Ingrown toenail, low risk: salt soaks ± cotton wick (not in diabetes, neuropathy or PAD without podiatry advice); antibiotics only for clear infection.
  • Structural: wide toe-box, low-heel footwear; metatarsal pad placed proximal to the metatarsal heads.

6Refer routinely and review

  • Podiatry: ingrown toenail stage 3 or recurrent; onychogryphosis; painful callus in a high-risk foot.
  • Orthopaedics: hallux valgus with pain and functional limitation (no fixed conservative trial needed); severe hallux rigidus; hammer/claw toe with ulceration; persistent Morton's.
  • Gout: urate ≥2 weeks after the flare + renal function; offer ULT per NG219 with colchicine prophylaxis; target <360 µmol/L.
  • Diabetic foot review (NG19): low risk annual; moderate 3–6 months; high 1–2 months.

7Never reassure by

  • A palpable proximal pulse — the toe can still be ischaemic.
  • "It's just gout" in a diabetic or unwell patient — septic arthritis mimics podagra.
  • No pain in a hot swollen neuropathic foot — Charcot can be painless.
  • "Cellulitis" not improving on antibiotics in a neuropathic foot — think Charcot.
  • Normal markers, X-ray or probe-to-bone — osteomyelitis is not excluded.
  • A normal urate during a flare, or a normal ABPI in diabetes.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 if your toe or foot suddenly turns cold, pale or blue, numb or very painful, or you feel very unwell.”

Same day: “Contact us or NHS 111 the same day for a fever with a hot swollen joint, spreading redness, or any new wound, blister, swelling or colour change on a diabetic foot. Contact us if the pain worsens or is not settling.”
