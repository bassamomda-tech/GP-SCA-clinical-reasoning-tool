Hypernatraemia in adults

For: adults with serum sodium >145 mmol/L, in hours or OOH. Not for: infants and children (hypernatraemic dehydration → 999 / urgent paediatric admission; full algorithm, steps 1 and 7); hospital fluid and desmopressin treatment (full algorithm, step 6).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE CG174NG29CG185JBDS-IP HHS 2022Soc Endocrinology 2018
Core rule: hypernatraemia is usually a water deficit — ask why this person cannot get, or keep, water. Symptoms and tempo override the number. Unknown duration = chronic: sodium should fall no faster than about 0.5 mmol/L per hour and 10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours — too-rapid correction causes cerebral oedema.

0Stop — call 999 now — emergency transfer

  • Any sodium with confusion, reduced consciousness, seizures, shock or severe dehydration → 999
  • Na⁺ ≥160 mmol/L and drowsy, unable to drink, deteriorating or cannot be transferred safely → 999; otherwise urgent hospital assessment and monitored treatment → Same-day admission
  • Polyuria after head injury, neurosurgery, pituitary surgery, meningitis or encephalitis (acute central DI) → 999 / Same-day with endocrinology or acute medicine. Suspected HHS (hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality, reduced consciousness) → 999
  • Focal neurology is a separate emergency (stroke, haemorrhage, venous sinus thrombosis). Correction is a hospital task with sodium monitoring.

1Triage by sodium — mmol/L; ≥160 is block 0

Na⁺Action
150–159Same-day acute medical assessment in most; immediate if symptomatic, dehydrated, not drinking, polyuric, or DI/HHS suspected.
146–149, at riskSame-day if new, persistent, rising, unexplained or polyuric; or elderly, frail, symptomatic, confused, unable to drink or cause unclear.
146–149, wellMay start at home (block 5) only if clinically well, cognitively intact, cause clear and a named person will provide drinks; urgent repeat.

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Polyuria (>50 mL/kg/day, about >3 L/day) + thirst + dilute urine + rising sodium → possible DI → Same-day acute medicine; urgent endocrinology discussion.
  • On lithium and hypernatraemic, dehydrated, confused or not drinking → Same-day; 12-hour lithium level.
  • Dehydration with AKI (rising creatinine) → Same-day acute medical assessment.
  • Dependent adult, recurrent or with poor care → possible neglect → Same-day safeguarding concern to the local authority, alongside treatment (Care Act 2014); also salt poisoning.

3Assess — face to face; not by telephone

☐ GCS, orientation, focal neurology  ☐ Hydration, lying and standing BP, pulse, temperature, urine output, weight  ☐ Capillary glucose and ketones  ☐ Swallow; access to water — who gives the drinks?  ☐ U&E, glucose, HbA1c, calcium; paired serum and urine osmolality with urine sodium; 12-hour lithium level if on lithium  ☐ Drugs: lithium, diuretics, lactulose, effervescent medicines

Not in primary care: water-deprivation or copeptin testing; IV fluids.

4Classify — only once urgency is settled

  • Inadequate intake (commonest) — access, cognition, swallow, impaired thirst; concentrated urine (e.g. >600 mOsm/kg).
  • Extrarenal loss — fever, sweating, diarrhoea (incl. lactulose), vomiting, burns.
  • DI — dilute urine, high serum osmolality; central (head injury, pituitary) or nephrogenic (lithium, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, CKD).
  • HHS — sodium may look normal and rise as glucose falls → emergency admission (JBDS).
  • Sodium gain (rare) — saline, bicarbonate, feeds, enemas, effervescent medicines, deliberate salt.
  • Uncertain — say so; osmolalities, same-day advice.

5Treat today — mild, well, can drink

  • Fluids: plain water (not sports drinks or saline); ORS for diarrhoea. A named person gives measured drinks — if nobody can, same-day assessment.
  • Drugs: review diuretics, lactulose; stop effervescent medicines where possible. Lithium: no abrupt stop without urgent specialist advice unless toxic; no amiloride or thiazide without it. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.
  • Never fluid-restrict. Urgent repeat U&E at an interval set by the laboratory and context. On desmopressin: vomiting, nil by mouth or no water access is an emergency.

6Refer routinely

  • Pituitary or hypothalamic cause, recurrent or chronic DI → endocrinology.
  • NDI from CKD, or lithium DI with renal impairment → nephrology; lithium changes are specialist.
  • Care home: documented daily fluid target, monitored intake, thickened fluids for dysphagia.

7Never reassure by — symptoms, tempo and who gives the drinks matter more than the number

  • "Not thirsty" — thirst is impaired in older people and absent with altered consciousness.
  • "Encourage fluids" when nobody can bring, hold or help with a drink.
  • "Poor intake" when the urine is dilute — think DI or osmotic diuresis.
  • A normal-looking sodium with high glucose — HHS; sodium rises as glucose falls.
  • A fast fall because the number is high — cerebral oedema and seizures.
  • A sodium that settles in a dependent adult — the safeguarding question remains.

8Safety-net — say it to the patient and carer, then document it

999 “Call 999 for new confusion, drowsiness, a fit, collapse or being unable to drink.” Same day: “Contact us or NHS 111 today for vomiting, drinking less, passing much less urine, or passing large volumes of urine with thirst.”

Remote or OOH: hydration, cognition and access to drinks cannot be judged by phone — raised sodium with drowsiness or confusion, vomiting, reduced drinking, living alone or depending on carers → face to face the same day.

✓Safe to manage in primary care only if — all five, documented
