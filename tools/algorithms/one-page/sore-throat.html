Sore throat — acute and recurrent

For: adults and children with acute or recurrent sore throat in primary care. Not for: children's antibiotic doses (use BNFC) or hospital management of quinsy, deep-neck infection or Lemierre's syndrome.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG84NICE NG12UKHSA scarlet feverBASHH 2025ENT UK 2021
Core rule: most sore throats are viral — score FeverPAIN or Centor before any antibiotic. But drooling, a muffled voice or distress is epiglottitis until proven otherwise, even without stridor → 999, keep upright, do not examine the throat.

0Stop — call 999 now — threatened airway or invasive infection

  • Suspected epiglottitis/supraglottitis — drooling, muffled voice, severe odynophagia, tripod posture, distress; stridor may be absent → 999
  • Stridor, respiratory distress, rapidly progressive neck or floor-of-mouth swelling, severe sepsis; rash with toxicity, meningism, hypotension or non-blanching purpura → 999
  • While waiting: keep upright and calm; do NOT lie flat or examine the throat; stay with the patient; oxygen by loose mask if available. Never delay transfer.

1Triage by score — FeverPAIN or Centor (NG84)

ScoreAntibiotic decision
FeverPAIN 0–1 · Centor 0–2Do not offer an antibiotic; analgesia, fluids, safety-net.
FeverPAIN 2–3No antibiotic, or a back-up prescription.
FeverPAIN 4–5 · Centor 3–4Consider an immediate or back-up antibiotic.

FeverPAIN: Fever ≥38 °C in 24 h · Purulence · Attend ≤3 days · Inflamed tonsils · No cough/coryza. Back-up script: use if not improving in 3–5 days or worse rapidly.

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Quinsy — unilateral tonsillar swelling, uvula deviated away, trismus, “hot potato” voice → Same-day ENT.
  • Deep-neck infection, unable to swallow fluids, suspected diphtheria (isolate, notify UKHSA) → Same-day ENT / hospital.
  • Lemierre's — resolving pharyngitis + rigors, unilateral neck swelling or pleuritic pain → Same-day admission.
  • Carbimazole + sore throat, fever or mouth ulcers → stop it, Same-day FBC. Clozapine → immediate FBC via the clozapine team.
  • ≥45 with persistent hoarseness; persistent unexplained neck lump; oral ulcer >3 weeks → consider Suspected cancer (NG12). Persistent sore throat or one-sided tonsil → Urgent ENT (local).
  • After GAS: carditis, migratory arthritis or chorea; dark urine, oedema or high BP → Same-day assessment.

3Assess — only once blocks 0–2 are answered

☐ Temp, HR, hydration  ☐ Voice, drooling, trismus  ☐ Tonsils: exudate, asymmetry, uvula, membrane  ☐ Nodes: anterior vs posterior  ☐ Drugs: carbimazole, clozapine, methotrexate  ☐ Sexual history if relevant. No routine throat swab.

4Classify — only if the screen is negative

  • Viral pharyngitis (commonest) — coryza, cough, low score → no antibiotic; usually settles within about a week.
  • GAS tonsillitis — exudate, fever, tender anterior nodes, no cough; high score → consider antibiotic.
  • Scarlet fever — sandpaper rash, strawberry tongue → penicillin 10 days; notify UKHSA; off school until 24 h on antibiotics.
  • Glandular fever — young, fatigue, posterior nodes, splenomegaly → consider FBC + EBV serology.
  • Gonococcal pharyngitis (relevant sexual history) → urgent GUM referral or discussion. ANUG (gum ulcers, halitosis) → urgent dental referral.
  • Uncertain — say so; re-examine rather than repeat antibiotics.

5Treat today — doses per BNF and local formulary

  • All: paracetamol, or ibuprofen if suitable; fluids. No routine corticosteroids (NG84).
  • Antibiotic (adult): phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg QDS or 1 g BD for 5–10 days. Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin 250–500 mg BD for 5 days; pregnancy: erythromycin. No amoxicillin if EBV possible.
  • Glandular fever: supportive; avoid contact sport, heavy lifting and abdominal-trauma risk for at least 4 weeks from onset.
  • No better after 5 days of penicillin: re-examine (EBV? quinsy?); no NG84 second-line — microbiology advice.

6Refer routinely

  • Recurrent tonsillitis: ≥7 episodes in 1 year, ≥5/year for 2 years or ≥3/year for 3 years, documented and disabling → ENT. Record each episode.
  • Recurrent quinsy (one episode may suffice locally), OSA or significant tonsillar hypertrophy → ENT.
  • Confirmed EBV: improves over 2–4 weeks; no fixed review (sooner if worse, jaundice, airway or abdominal pain); nodes >6 weeks → lymphoma exclusion; fatigue >3 months → post-viral assessment.

7Never reassure by

  • No stridor — it may be absent in epiglottitis, especially in adults.
  • A fairly normal mouth in a patient with drooling, muffled voice or distress.
  • Calling it viral in a patient on carbimazole, clozapine or a myelosuppressant without an FBC.
  • The sore throat getting better — Lemierre's appears as pharyngitis resolves.
  • A negative Monospot in the first week of illness.
  • Tonsillar asymmetry “because of the infection” — if it persists after recovery, refer.

8Safety-net — say it, write it, document it

999 “Call 999 if your breathing becomes noisy or difficult, you drool or can't swallow your saliva, you get a stiff neck with a severe headache, a rash that doesn't fade under pressure, or fast-spreading neck swelling.” Same day: “Contact us today if you can't swallow fluids, your voice goes muffled, you can't open your mouth fully, you get neck swelling and pain, or you get rapidly worse — and at once for fever or sore throat on carbimazole or clozapine.”

Review: “It usually lasts about a week. Come back if it is not improving after a week, or for a sore throat or hoarse voice that won't go, one-sided symptoms or a neck lump.”

✓Safe to leave primary care only if — all five, documented

No airway, sepsis or deep-neck feature; epiglottitis not suspected FeverPAIN or Centor recorded; antibiotic decision matches it Carbimazole, clozapine and myelosuppressants checked Persistent or one-sided symptoms, neck lump and hoarseness considered Written 999 and same-day safety-net and back-up script advice given
