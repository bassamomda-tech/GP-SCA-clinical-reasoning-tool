Hypoglycaemia in adults

For: adults with diabetes on insulin or a sulfonylurea with a low glucose, and adults without diabetes with suspected hypoglycaemia, in hours or OOH. Not for: children (paediatric pathways). Ongoing glucose management: the type 1 and type 2 diabetes protocols.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
JBDS 2023NICE NG17NG28DVLAMHRA
Core rule: in diabetes, treat any glucose <4.0 mmol/L now — fast glucose if they can swallow, glucagon and 999 if they cannot — then find the cause. A sulfonylurea hypo can recur for 24–36 hours.

0Stop — call 999 now — treat first, never delay treatment to take samples

  • Unconscious, seizing or unable to swallow (level 3) → 999 Nothing by mouth. IM glucagon 1 mg (or nasal glucagon 3 mg) by a trained carer or clinician; recovery position.
  • Glucose still <4.0 mmol/L after 3 treatment cycles → glucagon if available and 999. Consciousness or focal signs not recovering once glucose is corrected → 999: think stroke, seizure, intracranial bleed, sepsis, intoxication.
  • Hypo with Addisonian features (pigmentation, postural hypotension, low sodium, high potassium) → 999; emergency hydrocortisone per the local adrenal-crisis protocol, plus glucose.
  • While waiting: IV glucose only by a skilled professional with IV access (100 mL 20% or 200 mL 10% over 15 min, per local protocol). Reassess at about 10 min; keep monitoring.

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Sulfonylurea hypo: severe, recurrent, needed glucagon/IV glucose, long-acting or MR, renal impairment, frail, uncertain overdose → Same-day hospital observation
  • Hypo with a focal neurological deficit (stroke mimic) → Same-day medical
  • Recurrent spontaneous hypo, no diabetes, no obvious cause → Urgent endocrinology (999 if unwell). If safe, in the episode: lab glucose, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, β-hydroxybutyrate, sulfonylurea screen.
  • No diabetes but access to insulin or a sulfonylurea → consider error or deliberate exposure (C-peptide suppressed = exogenous insulin); safeguarding if a child or vulnerable adult.
  • Care-home resident, recurrent hypos on insulin or sulfonylurea, no review >6 months → Same-day review

3Assess — once glucose is corrected

☐ GCS, focal signs, pulse, postural BP  ☐ Addison's signs, liver disease, injection sites (lipohypertrophy)  ☐ Timing: fasting, post-meal, nocturnal  ☐ Awareness: Gold score ≥4 = impaired  ☐ Lab glucose, HbA1c, eGFR, LFTs; cortisol ± ACTH if targeted

Not in primary care: 72-hour fast, mixed-meal test, imaging.

4Classify — find the cause

  • Insulin — dose, missed meal, exercise, alcohol. Timing points to the dose: late-morning hypo = breakfast rapid-acting; nocturnal = basal.
  • Sulfonylurea — risk rises with CKD, older age, frailty and poor intake; glibenclamide is the most prolonged.
  • Alcohol — 6–12 h after heavy drinking. Impaired awareness — repeated hypos, neuropathy, beta-blockers.
  • No diabetes — fasting (insulinoma, Addison's, alcohol); after meals (reactive, post-bariatric); sepsis or liver failure.
  • Uncertain — confirm with a lab glucose; consider anxiety, arrhythmia or TIA.

5Treat today — doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary

  • Can swallow: glucose tablets or 150–200 mL fruit juice; recheck at 10–15 min, repeat up to 3 times; then bread, two biscuits or the next meal. No chocolate. On acarbose: glucose only.
  • Review the drug: adjust the responsible insulin dose per the diabetes-team plan. Sulfonylurea: lowest effective dose or stop; avoid glibenclamide.
  • Type 1: never stop basal insulin. Not eating on a sulfonylurea → withhold or seek advice.
  • Prepare: always carry glucose; glucagon at home for type 1 and train carers; give and document DVLA advice.

6Refer routinely

  • Diabetes team / DSN: any level 3 episode, Gold score ≥4, recurrent hypos on insulin, HbA1c too low on insulin or a sulfonylurea.
  • CGM per NICE: all adults with type 1; type 2 on multiple daily insulin with recurrent or severe hypos, impaired awareness, or unable to finger-prick.
  • Post-bariatric hypos → Urgent endocrinology. Frail type 2 → relax HbA1c target individually.

7Never reassure by

  • A sulfonylurea hypo corrected by one sugary drink — it can recur for 24–36 h.
  • A capillary meter reading alone in someone without diabetes.
  • "Just a hypo" when consciousness or focal signs do not recover after correction.
  • No warning symptoms — age, beta-blockers and impaired awareness blunt them.
  • A low HbA1c on insulin or a sulfonylurea — that is over-treatment.
  • A glucagon response — less reliable after a sulfonylurea, fasting or alcohol.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “If they cannot swallow, are very drowsy, fitting or unconscious: nothing by mouth, give glucagon and call 999. Also call 999 if the glucose is still under 4 after three treatments.” Same day: “A hypo on gliclazide that needed help or keeps coming back needs a hospital check today.”

Driving: “Check your glucose before driving and every 2 hours. Never drive during or just after a hypo — wait until recovered and the DVLA waiting time has passed.” Diabetes team or DSN review after any severe or recurrent episode.
