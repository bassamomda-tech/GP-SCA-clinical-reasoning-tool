Vision loss

For: adults with sudden, transient or gradual loss of vision in primary care — recognise, give first aid, refer. Not for: children, except an absent red reflex (block 2). Local Bolton/Greater Manchester eye-emergency, TIA and stroke pathways take precedence.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG128NG82NG81BSR GCA 2020RCOphth
Core rule: acute vision loss is an emergency until proven otherwise. Refer via the local eye-emergency, TIA or stroke pathway, start steroid now if GCA is suspected, and never delay transfer for primary-care tests.

0Stop — call 999 now — most eye emergencies are same-day, not 999

  • Vision loss with facial droop, dysarthria, weakness, ataxia, reduced consciousness or severe headache; sudden binocular loss with neurological features → 999 stroke assessment.
  • Sudden persistent monocular loss (e.g. CRAO) → immediate eye-emergency/stroke pathway; 999 if neurological symptoms, acutely unwell or local transfer rules require.
  • Penetrating or severe eye trauma; chemical injury with systemic toxicity, severe bilateral exposure or unable to irrigate safely; instability or unsafe transfer → 999
  • While waiting: chemical injury — irrigate now (saline or tap water) before history or acuity, until pH neutral and during transfer; do not patch. No GP tests before transfer.

1Triage by onset — monocular = eye or optic nerve; bilateral = brain

OnsetAction

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Over 50 + headache, jaw claudication or tender temporal artery + visual symptoms (GCA) → prednisolone now + Same-day ophthalmology/rheumatology.
  • Curtain or shadow, new field defect, shower of floaters, flashes or vitreous haemorrhage → Same-day eye casualty.
  • Painful red eye + reduced vision, haloes or abnormal pupil; contact-lens keratitis; anterior uveitis → Same-day ophthalmology.
  • Orbital cellulitis (fever, proptosis), optic neuritis → Same-day. Papilloedema → Same-day neurology + imaging.
  • Suspected wet AMD (new distortion, central loss) → Macula service ≤1 working day, not 999.
  • Child, absent red reflex → consider Urgent ≤2 weeks (NG12); with new squint → refer immediately. Bitemporal hemianopia → Urgent MRI pituitary.

3Assess — only once blocks 0–2 are answered

☐ Acuity each eye, with correction (or CF/HM/PL)  ☐ Confrontation fields each eye  ☐ Swinging torch (RAPD)  ☐ Red desaturation  ☐ Fundoscopy  ☐ Temporal arteries if over 50  ☐ Cranial nerves III, IV, VI; BP

GCA: CRP, ESR or PV, FBC — never wait for results. Not in primary care: CT head for isolated monocular loss, GP MRI for optic neuritis.

4Classify — only if the screen is negative

  • CRAO — sudden painless monocular loss, RAPD, pale retina/cherry-red spot → eye-emergency/stroke pathway; exclude GCA.
  • Retinal vein occlusion — retinal haemorrhages → urgent ophthalmology (24–48 h); no anticoagulant or thrombophilia tests.
  • Optic neuritis — ache on eye movement, red desaturation, central scotoma → urgent ophthalmology/neurology; specialist MRI.
  • AMD — central loss, distortion. Wet → macula service ≤1 working day; dry → optometry.
  • Cataract, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy — gradual, painless → optometry or routine (block 6).
  • Uncertain — say so; eye review with a date, and safety-net.

5Treat today — only these

  • Suspected GCA: prednisolone now — usually 60 mg with visual symptoms, 40–60 mg without (BSR 2020); gastro- and bone protection by risk.
  • Suspected TIA: aspirin 300 mg stat only if TIA likely and no contraindication or significant bleeding risk.
  • Not in primary care: topical steroid without slit lamp; glaucoma drops unless ophthalmology directs.
  • Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Refer routinely

  • Cataract affecting function or quality of life — no fixed acuity threshold (NG77).
  • Glaucoma per NG81: disc damage, repeatable field defect or Goldmann IOP ≥24 mmHg.
  • Diabetes: DESP annual, or 2-yearly if no retinopathy on two screens; chase non-attenders.
  • Hydroxychloroquine: annual retinal monitoring after 5 years (after 1 year if >5 mg/kg/day, eGFR <60 or tamoxifen).

7Never reassure by

  • A normal ESR or CRP, or a non-tender temporal artery, in suspected GCA.
  • A normal GP fundoscopy or confrontation fields.
  • Vision that has fully recovered (amaurosis fugax is a TIA).
  • Normal acuity with new flashes and floaters.
  • No RAPD (bilateral disease may show none).
  • A cup-to-disc ratio or non-contact tonometer reading alone.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 if loss of vision comes with a drooping face, weak arm or leg, slurred speech, unsteadiness, confusion or a severe headache.”

Same-day “Go to the eye emergency service or A&E today for any sudden loss of vision, a curtain or shadow, new flashes or floaters, vision that goes dark then recovers, a painful red eye with blurred vision, or — if over 50 — headache with scalp tenderness or jaw pain on chewing.” Known AMD: new distortion → macula service ≤1 working day.

Driving: not until acuity and fields are assessed; tell DVLA if the standard is not met. Failsafe: confirm urgent referrals were received and attended.
