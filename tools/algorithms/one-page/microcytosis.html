Microcytosis (low MCV) in adults

For: adults with an MCV <80 fL, including the results inbox. Not for: children (paediatric guidance). Pregnancy goes to block 2 and the maternity pathway; anaemia with a normal or high MCV → anaemia algorithm.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG12BSG 2021BSH 2021BSH haemoglobinopathiesNG24
Core rule: ferritin is the pivot. Low ferritin = iron deficiency: find the source — in a man or post-menopausal woman that means GI investigation, never iron alone. Normal ferritin with a low MCV is usually not iron deficiency: think thalassaemia trait or chronic disease, and do not give iron.

0Stop — emergency now — on seeing the result or the patient

  • Collapse, haemodynamic instability or major active bleeding (haematemesis, melaena, heavy rectal bleeding) → 999
  • Severe anaemia with chest pain, breathlessness at rest, syncope or heart failure, or other active significant GI bleeding → Same-day hospital
  • Transfusion is a hospital decision on symptoms, haemodynamics and comorbidity, not Hb alone. Identify and control the bleeding source; iron is still needed afterwards.

2Screen — one row is enough

  • IDA in a man of any age or a post-menopausal woman (even without GI symptoms) → Urgent GI investigation, usually OGD + colonoscopy. Offer FIT; ≥10 µg Hb/g → Suspected cancer pathway.
  • New GI symptoms on iron → OGD or colonoscopy within 2 weeks
  • Very low Hb, marked microcytosis and/or splenomegaly (thalassaemia major/intermedia) → Urgent haematology
  • Other cytopenias or abnormal film; lead, alcohol or isoniazid (sideroblastic, MDS); iron overload after transfusions → haematology.
  • Pregnancy: Hb <110 g/L (1st trimester), <105 (later); severe, late, unresponsive or intolerant → obstetrics/haematology. Both partners carriers + pregnant → Urgent genetics + obstetrics

3Assess — before prescribing iron

☐ FBC: MCV, MCH, RDW, film  ☐ Ferritin; CRP/ESR if inflammation suspected; TSAT where ferritin may mislead; reticulocyte Hb if available  ☐ Renal function, coeliac serology (tTG-IgA + total IgA), urine dipstick  ☐ Family origin, periods, NSAIDs, diet

Not routine: serum iron alone, sTfR, zinc protoporphyrin, hepcidin, Mentzer index.

4Classify — only if the screen is negative

  • Iron deficiency — low ferritin. Blood loss (GI; periods if premenopausal), malabsorption (coeliac, H. pylori, IBD), demand, intake → iron + treat the cause.
  • Thalassaemia trait — persistent low MCV, normal ferritin, Hb normal or mildly low → HPLC: beta HbA2 usually ≥3.5% (iron deficiency lowers it); alpha often normal (DNA testing lab-led).
  • Chronic disease — normal/high ferritin, raised CRP; usually borderline MCV → treat the condition; check TSAT for co-existing deficiency.
  • Sideroblastic / MDS — iron-loaded pattern, other cytopenias → haematology.
  • Uncertain — unexplained persistent microcytosis, normal ferritin → haematology.

5Treat today — only these

  • Iron deficiency: ferrous sulphate 200 mg once daily (65 mg elemental); alternate days if not tolerated. Alongside GI investigation and FIT, never instead. Continue about 3 months after Hb normalises.
  • IV iron — consider if oral fails, is not tolerated or absorbed, or rapid repletion is needed → local pathway.
  • The cause: heavy periods (NG88); H. pylori test and treat; coeliac → gluten-free diet; stop NSAIDs if possible.
  • Thalassaemia trait: no iron unless deficiency is confirmed. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Refer routinely — and follow up

  • FBC at about 4 weeks (expect Hb up ~10 g/L by 2 weeks). No rise → check adherence, ongoing loss, diagnosis, malabsorption, inflammation, renal disease; then haematology.
  • IDA persisting or recurring after good-quality negative endoscopy → gastroenterology (small bowel, renal tract).
  • Carrier: document; partner testing (NHS screening programme) if pregnancy planned or ongoing; both carriers → specialist counselling.

7Never reassure by

  • A good Hb response to iron in a man or post-menopausal woman.
  • A FIT <10 µg Hb/g, or a FIT not returned, against strong clinical concern.
  • A normal ferritin with inflammation, infection, liver disease or cancer.
  • "Dietary" iron deficiency without investigation.
  • One end of the gut only: oesophagitis on OGD does not clear the colon.
  • A Mentzer index, to diagnose or exclude thalassaemia trait.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Call 999 or go to A&E if you vomit blood, pass a lot of blood or black tarry stools, feel faint or collapse, or have chest pain or severe breathlessness.” Same day: “Contact us today for bloody stools, difficulty swallowing, worse tummy pain, weight loss or worsening breathlessness. Iron turns stools black; tarry, sticky, smelly stools are different.”

Close the loop: action FIT and endoscopy results and confirm the referral was received. If investigation is declined, document the cancer risk explained and the safety-net given.
