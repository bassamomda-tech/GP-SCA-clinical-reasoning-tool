Red eye — acute presentation

For: acute red eye in general practice, face to face or remote. Not for: neonates — ophthalmia neonatorum → Same-day paediatric ophthalmology, swab urgently.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
RCOphth emergency eye care 2024NICE NG81College of Optometrists CMGsBASHHUKHSA
Core rule: pain + reduced vision = sight-threatening: never call it conjunctivitis — refer same day. Irrigate a chemical injury before anything else.

0Stop — call 999 now — do not delay for examination

  • Chemical splash (acid or alkali) → irrigate at once and continuously until ocular-surface pH is neutral; remove lenses and particles; do not delay for history or VA → emergency ophthalmology, 999 if severe.
  • Acute angle closure: severe unilateral pain, haloes, vomiting, cloudy cornea, fixed mid-dilated pupil → 999 / immediate ophthalmology. Do not delay for tonometry.
  • Open globe (grinding, hammering, teardrop pupil); sudden severe visual loss; hyphaema with visual compromise or high IOP; orbital cellulitis with systemic illness, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia or neurology → 999
  • While waiting: rigid shield without pressure; do not patch tightly, palpate the globe or remove an embedded foreign body. No IV acetazolamide from routine primary care.

2Screen — one row is enough

  • Keratitis / corneal ulcer: pain, photophobia, white opacity or fluorescein staining → Same-day ophthalmology; do not patch.
  • Anterior uveitis: ciliary flush, photophobia, deep ache, small irregular pupil → Same-day ophthalmology
  • Scleritis: severe deep boring pain, does not blanch → Same-day / ≤24 h ophthalmology
  • HSV dendrite, or HZO with V1 vesicles or ocular symptoms → Same-day ophthalmology; antiviral when indicated must not delay it.
  • Gonococcal: hyperpurulent, chemosis → Same-day ophthalmology + GUM
  • Hyphaema; recent eye surgery or injection with pain, redness or reduced VA; stuck foreign body or rust ring → Same-day ophthalmology

3Assess — once blocks 0–2 are answered

☐ VA first, each eye, with pinhole  ☐ Pupils  ☐ Injection pattern  ☐ Fluorescein  ☐ Lid eversion  ☐ Nodes, discharge  ☐ Eye movements  ☐ BP if subconjunctival haemorrhage

Not in primary care: palpating the globe for IOP; routine swabs, bloods, imaging. Swab only if gonococcal, resistant after 7 days, neonatal or immunocompromised.

4Classify — only if the screen is negative

  • Bacterial conjunctivitis — mucopurulent, lids stuck → many resolve in 5–14 days.
  • Viral — watery, pre-auricular node, recent URTI → 2–3 weeks. Allergic — bilateral itch, atopy.
  • Subconjunctival haemorrhage — painless, sharply demarcated → reassure; check BP.
  • Episcleritis — sectoral, mild, blanches with phenylephrine 2.5%; if not → think scleritis.
  • Blepharitis — lid crusting, worse mornings. Dry eye — gritty, worse evenings.
  • Uncertain — say so → community optometry, eye casualty or ophthalmology; never manage it remotely.

5Treat today — benign causes only; doses per BNF and local formulary

  • Bacterial: hygiene; mild often needs no or a delayed antibiotic; if appropriate, chloramphenicol per local formulary.
  • Allergic: sodium cromoglicate 2% four times daily; inadequate → olopatadine 0.1% twice daily. Viral: lubricants, cold compresses.
  • Blepharitis: warm compresses, lid massage and cleaning, long term. Dry eye: lubricants; benefit takes 4–6 weeks.
  • Lenses: stop until resolved + 24 h after antibiotics. Anticoagulant: check INR if relevant; do not stop reflexively.

6Refer routinely

  • Blepharitis or dry eye failing after 3 months → ophthalmology.
  • Recurrent or painful episcleritis → ophthalmology advice.
  • Subconjunctival haemorrhage 2+ in 6 months → FBC, clotting, BP.
  • Chronic follicular conjunctivitis + sexual exposure → GUM (chlamydia). Allergic not working → review at 4 weeks.

7Never reassure by

  • Normal VA — it does not exclude uveitis or early keratitis.
  • No discharge — it does not exclude serious eye disease.
  • A "conjunctivitis" label for a painful eye with reduced acuity.
  • Mild symptoms in a contact-lens wearer.
  • Steroid drops for an undiagnosed red eye.
  • A remote assessment of an undiagnosed red eye.

8Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Sudden severe eye pain with haloes or sickness, sudden loss of vision, an injury at speed — call 999. Chemical splash: wash with water continuously first.” Same day: “Vision drops, the eye turns painful or light-sensitive (lens wearers: take lenses out), a white spot, heavy pus, a forehead or nose rash, or worse despite treatment — contact the eye unit today.”

Remote or OOH: no fluorescein or VA testing and uncertain → optometry, eye casualty or ophthalmology. Infective: own towels; exclusion per UKHSA advice.
