Flashes and floaters — PVD, retinal tear and detachment

For: adults and children with new flashes and/or floaters, face to face or remote. Not for: a red eye without floaters, or visual loss without flashes or floaters (use the red eye or vision loss algorithm). Children go straight to block 2.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
College of OptometristsRCOphth 2024NICE NG128NG12CG150
Core rule: symptoms alone cannot exclude a retinal tear. Every new flash or floater needs a dilated peripheral retinal examination, usually within 24 hours; normal acuity, fields, red reflex or direct fundoscopy exclude nothing.

0Stop — act now — screen every patient by name for these

  • Shadow, curtain or new field defect; reduced vision or new persistent blur; sudden shower of floaters or red haze; poor or absent red reflex; RAPD; significant eye trauma → Same-day eye casualty.
  • Pain, photophobia or redness with floaters; recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection with pain, reduced vision or new floaters → Same-day, contacting the treating eye unit directly.
  • Visual loss with persistent neurological deficit → 999. Do not delay referral for BP, glucose or INR; do not stop anticoagulants; the patient does not drive.

1Triage — the history sets the speed

PresentationRoute

Transient lossMonocular or homonymous → TIA pathway, specialist ≤24 h (NG128)

2Screen — lower the threshold

  • Higher-risk eye: high myopia, previous tear or detachment, recent eye surgery, trauma, family history, Stickler, Marfan → ≤24 h, never routine.
  • Known proliferative diabetic retinopathy + new floaters → ≤24 h; shower, red haze or reduced vision → Same-day
  • Child, new floaters → ophthalmology ≤24 h; with visual loss, trauma or inflammation → Same-day
  • Child, absent or white red reflex → consider urgent retinoblastoma referral ≤2 weeks (NG12); plus new squint → Immediately (NG127)
  • First or atypical aura, persistent deficit, weakness, speech change, or older with no migraine history → consider urgent stroke or neurology assessment.

3Assess — triage only

☐ Acuity each eye, glasses or pinhole  ☐ Confrontation fields each eye  ☐ Pupils (RAPD)  ☐ Red reflex  ☐ Direct fundoscopy if competent  ☐ Anterior segment  ☐ Do symptoms stay in one eye when the other is covered?

BP, glucose, INR only if bleeding or retinopathy is suspected. Eye service: dilated exam, B-scan, OCT.

4Classify — the eye service confirms

  • PVD — one new ring or cobweb floater, brief peripheral flashes in one eye → still dilated exam ≤24 h; "uncomplicated" only after it.
  • Tear or detachment — many new dots, spreading shadow or curtain, reduced vision → same-day.
  • Vitreous haemorrhage — red haze, many dark floaters, poor red reflex → same-day, even if anticoagulated or diabetic.
  • Migraine aura — zig-zag in both eyes' fields, develops over ≥5 min, lasts 5–60 min, fully reversible → reassure only if typical.
  • Benign floaters — long-standing, unchanged, no flashes, normal acuity and fields. Any change → reassess.
  • Uncertain — treat as new flashes and floaters: dilated exam ≤24 h.

5Treat today — no drug treatment in primary care

  • Tear or detachment: laser, cryotherapy or surgery by the eye service only. The GP's job is speed and advice.
  • Anticoagulants and antiplatelets: do not stop for floaters or vitreous haemorrhage; the eye team and prescriber decide.
  • Gas in the eye after surgery: no flying and no nitrous oxide until the gas has gone; carry the wristband.
  • Uncomplicated PVD after dilated exam: flashes settle over weeks, floaters fade over weeks to months; give written warning symptoms.

6Refer routinely

  • Long-standing unchanged floaters, normal acuity and fields, no flashes or risk factors → routine optometrist.
  • Follow-up after PVD is set by the examining eye service.
  • Diabetes aged 12+ → NHS Diabetic Eye Screening. Migraine with aura → CG150; check contraception (UKMEC).

7Never reassure by

  • Normal acuity, confrontation fields or direct fundoscopy.
  • A normal red reflex or central fundus view.
  • Calling it "just a PVD" before a dilated examination.
  • One negative eye exam: a tear can develop in the following weeks.
  • Transient monocular loss treated as "just my eye": it is a TIA route.
  • A routine optometry slot in place of a prompt dilated exam.

8Safety-net — in writing, then document it

Same day “A shadow or curtain, a sudden shower of new floaters, more flashes, or blurred vision — go to eye casualty or call NHS 111 straight away, even at night or at the weekend. Don't wait for a GP appointment.” Same-day contact: symptoms in the other eye, floaters that change, or new distortion of straight lines → urgent eye assessment, usually ≤24 h.

Every patient: written warning signs and out-of-hours contacts; they apply to both eyes; no driving while vision or field is affected (DVLA). 999 for visual loss with a persisting neurological deficit.
