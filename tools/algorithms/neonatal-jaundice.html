1
Safety

Red Flags — Kernicterus, Biliary Atresia & Haemolysis Emergency

Any suspected or visible jaundice in the first 24 hours requires urgent medical review and urgent serum bilirubin measurement — refer to hospital urgently; use 999 if the baby is unwell, unstable, has neurological signs or cannot be transferred safely. Pale, chalky or white stools in a jaundiced infant require urgent same-day paediatric assessment, irrespective of the degree of visible jaundice.

Jaundice within 24 hours of birth Pathological until assessed — immune haemolysis (Rh/ABO), non-immune haemolysis (e.g. G6PD), sepsis, congenital infection, bruising/cephalhaematoma, polycythaemia, metabolic or liver disease. → urgent hospital referral (999 if unwell/unstable). NICE CG98: serum bilirubin within 2 hours; urgent medical review as soon as possible and within 6 hours; repeat every 6 hours until below threshold and stable/falling. Blood group, DAT, packed cell volume and further haemolysis tests are guided by the neonatal team.
Pale/chalky white or clay-coloured stools + jaundice Biliary atresia (obstructive cholestasis) — progressive liver destruction from fibrosis of biliary tree → urgent same-day paediatric assessment, irrespective of the degree of visible jaundice; contact the paediatric/hepatology team directly where possible. Earlier diagnosis and Kasai portoenterostomy are associated with better outcomes (timing and eligibility are specialist decisions) — do not wait for routine outpatient review. Use stool colour chart.
Jaundice + dark urine (conjugated bilirubin) Conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia — liver disease, biliary atresia, sepsis, metabolic disease (galactosaemia, tyrosinaemia). Any conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia needs specialist interpretation; conjugated bilirubin >25 μmol/L → prompt paediatric/hepatology advice (may indicate serious liver disease). Do not rely on a percentage-of-total threshold. Direct bilirubin stains the urine yellow-brown.
Neurological signs of kernicterus High-pitched cry, arching (opisthotonus), poor feeding, extreme lethargy, seizures, hyperthermia → 999. Acute bilirubin encephalopathy — permanent brain damage from bilirubin deposition in basal ganglia. Emergency transfer — exchange transfusion/intensified phototherapy is a neonatal-unit decision.
Jaundice + fever + systemically unwell neonate Neonatal sepsis (E. coli, GBS, Listeria, Staphylococcus) causing jaundice → 999 / same-day paediatrics. Neonates can deteriorate catastrophically within hours. Any sick jaundiced neonate = sepsis until proven otherwise. Temperature ≥38°C or <36°C needs urgent assessment for infection. Cultures and IV antibiotics in hospital per neonatal team/local antimicrobial protocol.
Jaundice persisting beyond 14 days (term) or 21 days (preterm, <37 weeks) Prolonged neonatal jaundice — NICE CG98: ask about pale stools and dark urine; measure conjugated bilirubin; FBC; maternal and infant blood group and DAT; consider urine culture only if UTI is clinically suspected; confirm routine newborn bloodspot screening (including congenital hypothyroidism) was completed. Conjugated >25 μmol/L → urgent paediatric/hepatology advice. Further liver/metabolic tests per local protocol or paediatric advice.
Jaundice + weight loss >10% + poor feeding Weight loss ≥10%, poor urine/stool output, ineffective feeding or clinical dehydration requires same-day feeding and neonatal assessment. Address feeding and hydration while continuing to monitor bilirubin according to the treatment chart. Establish feeding effectively — breastfeeding support specialist + consider supplementary feeding.
Rapidly rising bilirubin (>8.5 μmol/L/hr) Exchange transfusion threshold may be reached before phototherapy is effective. Same-day hospital/neonatal assessment; monitoring frequency per neonatal team and NICE CG98. Rate of rise is as important as absolute level.
Biliary atresia is the most time-critical diagnosis in neonatal jaundice — it is rare (1:10,000–15,000 births) but invariably fatal if untreated, and the outcome is critically time-dependent. The Kasai portoenterostomy (hepatic portoenterostomy) — an anastomosis between the intestine and the porta hepatis, bypassing the obliterated biliary tree — is associated with better outcomes when performed earlier — timing and eligibility are specialist decisions, so referral must be urgent. Children without successful bile drainage are likely to need liver transplantation. Stool colour charts help parents and clinicians recognise pale stools early, and NICE CG98 explicitly mandates that all neonates with jaundice beyond 14 days must have a split bilirubin performed to calculate the conjugated fraction. The most practical immediate test a GP can perform is urine dipstick — dark urine (conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and stains the urine) in a jaundiced neonate is a medical emergency requiring same-day referral to paediatric hepatology. Kernicterus from untreated severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia was historically common and caused high rates of cerebral palsy and sensorineural deafness — the introduction of phototherapy has made it rare but not extinct. The opisthotonus (arching of the back) and high-pitched cry in acute bilirubin encephalopathy are pathognomonic — any GP seeing these signs in a jaundiced neonate should call 999 immediately.

Diagnose

Age at Onset — The Primary Diagnostic Branch

Age of onset is the single most important clinical discriminator in neonatal jaundice. Use postnatal age in hours and days — not just "a few days old."

Jaundice <24 hours
Pathological until assessed — urgent hospital referral (999 if unwell). Causes include sepsis, congenital infection, bruising/cephalhaematoma, polycythaemia, metabolic/liver disease, and haemolysis: Rhesus incompatibility (anti-D haemolysis — most severe), ABO incompatibility (A or B baby, O mother — commonest haemolytic cause), G6PD deficiency (X-linked, common in African/Mediterranean populations — triggered by oxidative stress), hereditary spherocytosis.
Jaundice day 2–14 (physiological window)
Physiological jaundice most likely — but must measure bilirubin (TcB if ≥35 weeks and >24 hours where available, otherwise SBR) against the NICE CG98 treatment threshold chart. Physiological is a diagnosis of exclusion. Risk factors for severe physiological jaundice: prematurity (<38 weeks), bruising / cephalhaematoma, East Asian ethnicity, breastfeeding, sibling with neonatal jaundice, G6PD deficiency.
Jaundice day 4–10 (peak physiological)
Physiological jaundice peaks between day 3–5 in term infants, day 5–7 in preterm. Should be declining by day 10. Breastfed infants: higher peak, slower decline (breast milk jaundice — benign, do NOT stop breastfeeding).
Jaundice >14 days (term) or >21 days (preterm)
Prolonged neonatal jaundice. Most common benign cause: breast milk jaundice. NICE CG98: ask about pale stools/dark urine; conjugated bilirubin (biliary atresia); FBC; maternal and infant blood group + DAT; consider urine culture if UTI clinically suspected; confirm newborn bloodspot (including congenital hypothyroidism) completed. Galactosaemia is not part of routine bloodspot screening — consider clinically. No extensive liver/metabolic panel unless local protocol or paediatric advice.
Key history questions
Gestational age at birth (prematurity = higher risk) · Maternal blood group + rhesus status (booking record) · Previous sibling with neonatal jaundice / phototherapy · Ethnicity (G6PD risk — African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, South/South-East Asian) · Breastfeeding / formula feeding · Stool colour (pale = biliary atresia) · Urine colour (dark = conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia) · Birth injuries (bruising, cephalhaematoma — haematoma breakdown increases bilirubin load)
The 24-hour rule is the most important rule in neonatal jaundice — any visible jaundice within the first 24 hours of life is pathological by definition, as physiological jaundice cannot occur this early (the mechanisms that produce physiological jaundice — normal red cell breakdown after the high fetal haemoglobin production period — take at least 24 hours to generate significant bilirubin load). Visible jaundice before 24 hours may indicate haemolysis (immune or non-immune), sepsis, congenital infection, bruising, polycythaemia or metabolic/liver disease — haemolysis can make bilirubin rise faster than phototherapy can clear it. Exchange transfusion may be required within hours. The Rhesus incompatibility situation has been dramatically improved by routine anti-D prophylaxis to all Rh-negative pregnant women — but maternal records must be checked, as missed prophylaxis still occurs. ABO incompatibility (group O mother + group A or B baby) is now the most common cause of significant haemolytic disease of the newborn — it is unpredictable (no maternal sensitisation history) and presents with jaundice in the first 24 hours. G6PD deficiency is X-linked recessive (affects males primarily) and is common in populations from tropical and subtropical regions — it causes haemolytic crisis when the baby is exposed to oxidative stressors (infection, drugs like vitamin K if given in excessive doses, or in the neonatal period can be triggered without obvious trigger).

Diagnose

Classification — Unconjugated vs Conjugated

Unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia
Bilirubin not water-soluble — not excreted in urine. Urine normal colour. Causes: physiological, breast milk jaundice, haemolysis (Rh/ABO/G6PD/spherocytosis), polycythaemia (twin-to-twin transfusion, delayed cord clamping), hypothyroidism (reduced bilirubin conjugation), bruising/cephalhaematoma, Crigler-Najjar syndrome (severe UGT1A1 deficiency — rare). Treatment: phototherapy / exchange transfusion.
Conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia
Conjugated bilirubin >25 μmol/L — follow expert advice (may indicate serious liver disease); do not use a percentage-of-total threshold, and do not subtract conjugated from total bilirubin when plotting against NICE thresholds. Bilirubin water-soluble → dark urine, pale stools, steatorrhoea. Causes: biliary atresia (most important — surgical emergency), neonatal hepatitis (viral — CMV, hepatitis B), metabolic (galactosaemia, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency, PFIC), total parenteral nutrition cholestasis, sepsis. Always requires specialist assessment.
Physiological jaundice (diagnosis of exclusion)
Appears day 2–3. Peaks day 3–5. Resolves by day 10–14. Well baby, feeding normally, urine normal, stools yellow/green (NOT pale). SBR below treatment threshold on NICE CG98 chart. No risk factors for severe jaundice. Does not require treatment if below threshold. Continue breastfeeding.
Breast milk jaundice
Benign, prolonged unconjugated jaundice in exclusively breastfed infants. Persists beyond 14 days (occasionally to 12 weeks). Well, thriving, feeding well. Normal split bilirubin (conjugated normal). Mechanism: breast milk inhibitors of bilirubin conjugation (glucuronidase enzymes). Do NOT stop breastfeeding. Confirm the bilirubin is predominantly unconjugated, the infant is clinically well and feeding/growing appropriately, and follow the paediatric/community pathway. Repeat bilirubin only when clinically indicated or per the specialist plan.
Haemolytic jaundice
Onset <24 hrs, rises rapidly, SBR may exceed treatment threshold quickly. DAT positive (immune haemolysis: Rh, ABO) or negative (non-immune: G6PD, hereditary spherocytosis). FBC: anaemia, high reticulocyte count, spherocytes on film (ABO/spherocytosis), Heinz bodies (G6PD). A positive DAT alone does not establish clinically significant haemolytic disease. Phototherapy ± exchange transfusion per neonatal team. IVIG only as a neonatal-team adjunct (NICE CG98 criteria — see Step 6).
The distinction between unconjugated and conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia is the critical biochemical branch in neonatal jaundice — unconjugated jaundice (the common, physiological/haemolytic type) can cause kernicterus but is treatable with phototherapy; conjugated jaundice is always pathological and indicates liver or biliary tract disease requiring specialist assessment. The practical bedside test is urine examination — conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and stains the urine dark brown/orange. A urine dipstick positive for bilirubin in a jaundiced neonate is a same-day hospital emergency. Galactosaemia is a critical metabolic cause of conjugated jaundice — it is caused by deficiency of galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT enzyme) and presents in the first few days of life with jaundice, vomiting, hepatomegaly, hypoglycaemia, and E. coli sepsis (galactose accumulation impairs neutrophil function, predisposing to sepsis). It is not part of the routine UK newborn bloodspot screening programme (it is sometimes picked up incidentally), so it must be suspected clinically. Suspected galactosaemia is a metabolic emergency — contact the paediatric metabolic team immediately. If untreated, it causes liver failure, cataracts, intellectual disability, and ovarian failure in girls. Any sick jaundiced neonate with E. coli sepsis needs emergency paediatric/metabolic assessment — stop breast milk and lactose-containing feeds only on urgent specialist advice or per the local metabolic emergency protocol; do not select an alternative formula independently.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Clinical assessment of jaundice
Blanching test: press firmly on skin → if yellow on blanching = jaundice. Progression cephalocaudal: face → trunk → limbs → palms/soles (Kramer zones). Extent of jaundice may indicate more significant disease, but bilirubin must be measured — do not estimate severity from skin distribution. Jaundice may be harder to detect visually in darker skin tones (check sclerae, gums and blanched skin). Clinical estimation is unreliable — ALWAYS measure with transcutaneous bilirubinometer (TcB) or serum bilirubin (SBR). Do not manage neonatal jaundice clinically without measurement.
Transcutaneous bilirubinometer (TcB)
Non-invasive, immediate screening tool. NICE CG98: in babies ≥35 weeks and >24 hours, use TcB where available (SBR if TcB unavailable). Confirm with SBR if TcB >250 μmol/L or at/above the relevant treatment threshold; use SBR for all subsequent measurements once treatment thresholds are reached. There is no universal TcB cut-off of 200 μmol/L. Jaundice <24 hours → serum bilirubin within 2 hours. TcB unreliable after phototherapy (skin bleaching) — use SBR only. Not validated for gestation <35 weeks or <24 hrs old.
Serum bilirubin (SBR) — total and split
Total SBR: treatment threshold decision (plot on NICE CG98 gestational age-specific chart). Split bilirubin (direct/conjugated): mandatory if jaundice >14 days in term babies or >21 days in preterm babies (NICE CG98). Conjugated >25 μmol/L → prompt paediatric/hepatology advice; no percentage-of-total threshold; do not subtract conjugated from total when plotting. Heel-prick capillary or venous sample.
Clinical examination
Wellbeing: alert vs lethargic, tone (hypotonia = bilirubin encephalopathy), feeding. Colour: scleral icterus (most sensitive visible sign), skin. Hepatosplenomegaly (haemolytic disease, liver disease, congenital infection). Bruising / cephalhaematoma (increased bilirubin load). Dysmorphic features (chromosomal disorders associated with liver disease).
Investigations by clinical scenario
Significant hyperbilirubinaemia — core neonatal-unit assessment: SBR · Packed cell volume / FBC · Maternal + infant blood group + DAT · Targeted: Blood film (consider) · G6PD if relevant ethnicity, family history, rapid rise or unexplained haemolysis · Blood/urine/CSF cultures only if infection suspected · Liver tests + imaging if conjugated bilirubin raised or clinical signs of liver disease · If prolonged (>14 days term / >21 days preterm): Conjugated bilirubin · FBC · Blood group + DAT · Urine culture if UTI clinically suspected · Newborn bloodspot result check (congenital hypothyroidism; galactosaemia is not screened) · further liver/metabolic tests only per local protocol or paediatric advice
Stool colour chart
A stool colour chart (use one at the 6-week check, or earlier if jaundice is noted) — colours 1–3 (pale / white / clay) = abnormal → same-day referral. Colours 4–6 (yellow-green to dark brown) = normal. Parents should be taught to check stool colour at every nappy change in a jaundiced neonate.
NICE CG98 explicitly states that clinical assessment alone is insufficient for managing neonatal jaundice — visual (Kramer zone) assessment is unreliable for identifying neonates requiring phototherapy, particularly in darker skin tones. All neonates with visible jaundice should have their bilirubin measured with TcB (transcutaneous bilirubinometer) or SBR (serum bilirubin), not managed on clinical observation alone. Transcutaneous bilirubinometers (Dräger JM-103, Philips BiliChek) are now widely available in NHS community settings — they use spectrophotometry to estimate bilirubin through the skin and are validated for screening in infants ≥35 weeks' gestation. They allow safe, non-invasive, rapid assessment in any setting. GPs and community midwives who have access to a TcB should use it for jaundiced neonates ≥35 weeks and >24 hours old, within a verified local pathway. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) — also called the Coombs test — detects antibody-coated red cells. A positive DAT in a jaundiced neonate indicates antibody-coated red cells (Rh or ABO incompatibility), but a positive DAT alone does not establish clinically significant haemolytic disease. A negative DAT in a severely jaundiced neonate should prompt consideration of non-immune haemolysis: G6PD deficiency (red cell enzyme assay), hereditary spherocytosis (osmotic fragility test, ektacytometry), or pyruvate kinase deficiency.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day hospital (immediate)
999 / emergency transfer: neurological signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy (high-pitched cry, arching, extreme lethargy) · seizures, altered consciousness, marked lethargy or collapse · sick or shocked jaundiced neonate (sepsis) · respiratory or cardiovascular compromise · suspected exchange-transfusion emergency where safe transfer cannot be delayed. Urgent hospital referral: jaundice <24 hours old (999 if unwell/unstable) · SBR above exchange transfusion threshold.
Same-day paediatrics
Same-day hospital/neonatal assessment: jaundice in the first 24 hours · SBR at or above phototherapy threshold on NICE CG98 chart · Conjugated bilirubin >25 μmol/L · Rapidly rising SBR (>8.5 μmol/L/hr) · Pale or chalky stools · Dark urine staining the nappy with jaundice · Poor feeding with significant weight loss/dehydration · Fever or temperature instability · Suspected haemolysis or G6PD-related rapid rise · Jaundice + unwell neonate at any age
Paediatric hepatology (urgent)
Conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia in any neonate — specialist work-up (biliary atresia vs neonatal hepatitis). Urgent referral is required because earlier diagnosis and Kasai portoenterostomy are associated with better outcomes; timing and eligibility are specialist decisions — do not wait for routine outpatient review. Phone directly.
Community midwife / health visitor
Community pathway only if the infant is clinically well, feeding and hydration are satisfactory, bilirubin is below the treatment threshold by an appropriate margin, the local midwifery/neonatal pathway confirms monitoring arrangements, and parents have written safety-netting and a named contact. Repeat measurement per NICE timing (see Step 6) — GPs should not create an independent daily monitoring programme. Breastfeeding support if feeding concerns.
Prolonged jaundice >14 days
NICE CG98 work-up (ask stool/urine colour, conjugated bilirubin, FBC, blood group + DAT, urine culture only if UTI suspected, confirm newborn bloodspot) per the local prolonged-jaundice pathway; same-day paediatrics if conjugated >25 μmol/L, pale stools or dark urine; otherwise paediatric review per local pathway (breast milk jaundice likely but must be confirmed). Never reassure without split bilirubin.
The NICE CG98 threshold charts provide gestational-age-specific phototherapy and exchange transfusion thresholds — the treatment threshold is lower (more conservative) for preterm infants because the blood-brain barrier is less mature and kernicterus can occur at lower bilirubin levels. There are separate charts for infants ≥38 weeks, 35–37+6 weeks, and for infants with additional risk factors (haemolysis, previous sibling requiring exchange transfusion, albumin <25 g/L). The NICE CG98 chart is available as a free app and should be on every GP and midwife smartphone. Referral thresholds: if SBR is at or above the phototherapy line → same-day hospital for phototherapy. For clinically well babies ≥38 weeks: more than 50 μmol/L below the phototherapy threshold → no routine repeat measurement; within 50 μmol/L → repeat within 18 hours if risk factors, otherwise within 24 hours (follow the NICE chart and local pathway). The Kasai procedure timing is the most critical factor in biliary atresia outcome — national stool colour card screening (for example Taiwan's programme — international) has been shown to reduce diagnostic delay for biliary atresia. Every GP performing a 6-week check should ask specifically about stool colour and inspect the stool colour card. Pale stools in a jaundiced infant = same-day referral regardless of how well the baby appears — do not delay until the 6-week check.

Treat

Treatment Modalities

Phototherapy
Blue-green wavelength light (430–490 nm peak) converts unconjugated bilirubin to water-soluble photo-isomers excreted in bile and urine without conjugation. Single surface (overhead or underneath) or double surface (higher intensity — for higher levels or haemolytic disease). Neonatal-service treatment. Conventional phototherapy: short breaks for feeding, nappy changing and cuddles according to clinical judgement; intensified/multiple phototherapy: generally continuous, with enteral or IV feeds maintained. Do not start below the treatment threshold (depends on gestational age and postnatal age in hours). Repeat SBR 4–6 hours after starting, then every 6–12 hours once stable/falling; stop when SBR is at least 50 μmol/L below the phototherapy threshold; recheck rebound 12–18 hours after stopping. Do not arrange phototherapy outside the neonatal service unless a formal local home-phototherapy programme exists.
Exchange transfusion
Double-volume exchange transfusion is a neonatal intensive-care procedure performed when the NICE exchange threshold is reached or if there are clinical signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy. The local neonatal protocol determines volume, blood product specification and monitoring.
Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)
NICE CG98: IVIG 500 mg/kg over 4 hours as an adjunct to continuous intensified phototherapy, only in Rh or ABO haemolytic disease when SBR continues to rise by >8.5 μmol/L/hour. Initiated by the neonatal team — not in primary care and not merely on a positive DAT.
Adequate feeding
Feeding promotes gut motility → bilirubin excretion in stool. Continue breastfeeding throughout phototherapy (do NOT supplement with formula for physiological jaundice unless weight loss >10% or inadequate feeding confirmed). Breastfeed every 2–3 hours minimum. If truly inadequate intake: expressed breast milk supplement or formula supplement, not cessation of breastfeeding.
GP-community management (below threshold)
Well infant ≥38 weeks with bilirubin more than 50 μmol/L below the treatment threshold: usually no routine repeat bilirubin. Within 50 μmol/L of the threshold: repeat within 18 hours if risk factors, otherwise within 24 hours, via the verified local midwifery/neonatal pathway. Any worsening feeding, lethargy, rapidly deepening jaundice, temperature abnormality or parental concern → same-day assessment. Written safety-net: return immediately if feeding worsens, baby becomes lethargic, jaundice deepens or spreads to palms/soles. Do NOT discharge without a clear review plan.
The blue light of phototherapy (peak 460 nm) penetrates skin and converts unconjugated bilirubin IXα (the brain-toxic isomer) to lumirubin and configurational isomers that are water-soluble and can be excreted in bile without hepatic conjugation. This bypasses the conjugation defect of the immature neonatal liver. The efficiency of phototherapy is directly proportional to the irradiance (light intensity) and the skin surface area exposed — hence the use of double-surface phototherapy (baby between overhead lights and underneath billi blanket) for higher bilirubin levels. Home phototherapy (billi blankets) is used in some NHS trusts for stable, term infants with physiological jaundice, reducing hospitalisation and supporting breastfeeding. NICE CG98 does not endorse or prohibit home phototherapy — it acknowledges it as an option in some settings. The critical point is that phototherapy cannot be started in primary care without bilirubin measurement and threshold plotting — empirical phototherapy without measurement is not appropriate. The feeding message is critically important: stopping breastfeeding for jaundice is the single most common parental error — parents frequently stop breastfeeding because they have been told that breast milk "causes" jaundice. Breast milk jaundice (prolonged unconjugated jaundice in breastfed infants) is usually benign, but reassurance depends on the bilirubin plotted against the treatment chart, postnatal and gestational age, rate of rise, conjugated fraction and clinical condition — not a fixed level. Home phototherapy is only for a formal local programme. Breastfeeding must be actively supported and continued unless there is a medical reason to supplement.

Prolonged Jaundice & Specific Causes

Hypothyroidism
Congenital hypothyroidism causes prolonged unconjugated jaundice (reduced bilirubin conjugation). Check newborn bloodspot result — hypothyroidism screened at 5 days. If missed or borderline: urgent TFTs (TSH elevated + low T4). Levothyroxine replacement started immediately by paediatrician — early treatment prevents intellectual disability. Incidence 1:3000–4000 births.
UTI-associated jaundice
E. coli UTI is a well-recognised cause of prolonged neonatal jaundice — the mechanism is endotoxin-mediated cholestasis. NICE (2023 update): consider urine culture (clean catch) if there is clinical suspicion of UTI — not mandatory in every otherwise well infant with prolonged jaundice. Suspected UTI in a neonate → same-day paediatric assessment; antibiotics per paediatric team and local antimicrobial guidance.
Biliary atresia — GP role after referral
GP has no treatment role — this is a specialist surgical condition. GP's primary role: refer urgently the same day as soon as suspected (do not wait for a set age), coordinate follow-up, support family. Post-Kasai: monitoring for cholangitis (fever + rising bilirubin), fat-soluble vitamin supplementation (A, D, E, K), growth monitoring, liver transplant preparation if Kasai fails.
Galactosaemia
Clinical suspicion — not part of routine bloodspot screening. Jaundice, vomiting, hepatomegaly, hypoglycaemia, bleeding tendency or E. coli sepsis → emergency paediatric/metabolic assessment. Suspected galactosaemia is a metabolic emergency: contact the paediatric metabolic team immediately. Stop breast milk and lactose-containing feeds only on urgent specialist advice or per the local metabolic emergency protocol — do not select an alternative formula independently. Lifelong galactose-free diet (no dairy — strict). Monitor for intellectual impairment, cataracts, ovarian failure (premature menopause in girls).
Breast milk jaundice — management
Continue breastfeeding — do not interrupt breastfeeding solely because of jaundice. Confirm the bilirubin is predominantly unconjugated, the infant is clinically well and feeding/growing appropriately, and follow the paediatric/community pathway. Repeat bilirubin only when clinically indicated or per the specialist plan. Do not use a fixed bilirubin level to reassure — management depends on postnatal age, gestational age, total bilirubin, rate of rise, clinical condition, conjugated fraction and the treatment chart. Parental anxiety management — written information.
The GP's role in the 6-week check is particularly important for prolonged jaundice — it is the last formal clinical encounter before the baby is seen again at 8 weeks immunisations. NICE CG98 is explicit: if a baby is still visibly jaundiced at the 6-week check, a split bilirubin must be checked and the stool colour card reviewed. A baby who has been yellow since birth and whose parents "think it's breast milk jaundice" must not be dismissed without investigation. The consequences of missing biliary atresia at the 6-week check and reassuring parents without a split bilirubin are catastrophic — by the time the baby is seen at 8 weeks and jaundice is still present, the Kasai window may be closing. Check the newborn bloodspot result in the red book at the 6-week check. The NHS bloodspot screens for phenylketonuria (PKU), congenital hypothyroidism, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, MCADD, homocystinuria, isovaleric acidaemia, glutaric aciduria type 1, MSUD and tyrosinaemia type 1 — galactosaemia is not routinely screened, so a jaundiced, poorly feeding or septic infant still needs it considered clinically. GPs should confirm at early neonatal contacts that the heel prick was taken (day 5) and the result documented — if missing, contact the local newborn screening team promptly. Do not delay referral if pale stools, dark urine or persistent jaundice are identified before the 6-week check.

Lifestyle

Supporting Parents — Feeding, Observation & Wellbeing

Continue breastfeeding The most important lifestyle message in neonatal jaundice. Breastfeeding reduces neonatal jaundice risk through adequate hydration and gut stimulation. Interrupting breastfeeding for jaundice is NOT recommended and risks long-term breastfeeding failure. Ensure effective feeding: good latch, 8–12 feeds/24 hours, signs of adequate intake (6 wet nappies/day by day 5, regaining birth weight by 14 days).
Breastfeeding support access Any breastfeeding difficulty in a jaundiced neonate → same-day breastfeeding support (community midwife, infant feeding coordinator, peer support worker, National Breastfeeding Helpline 0300 100 0212). Poor latch / positioning → reduced milk transfer → dehydration → worsening jaundice. Address the feeding problem, not the jaundice in isolation.
Stool colour monitoring Teach parents to check stool colour at every nappy change throughout the jaundiced period. Keep a stool colour chart visible. Colours 1–3 (pale/white/clay) → contact GP or midwife same-day. Normal colours are yellow, green, or brown. Stools should be passing and transitioning from meconium (black-green) by day 4.
Weight monitoring Reweigh at 5 days and 10 days (or more frequently if jaundice worsening or feeding concerns). Weight loss ≥10%, poor urine/stool output, ineffective feeding or clinical dehydration → same-day feeding and neonatal assessment, while continuing to monitor bilirubin per the treatment chart. Target: regain birth weight by day 14. Weight gain of 20–30 g/day after milk comes in (day 3–4) is normal.
Sunlight — evidence and caution Filtered sunlight through glass does NOT provide effective phototherapy (glass blocks the therapeutic UV wavelengths). Placing baby in a sunny window is therefore not an effective treatment. However, outdoor sunlight exposure in warm, temperate weather may provide some benefit. NICE CG98 (updated 2016) advises not to use sunlight as treatment for hyperbilirubinaemia and it is not a substitute for measured phototherapy — do not advise parents to use sunlight in place of TcB monitoring and hospital phototherapy when indicated.
Parental anxiety support Neonatal jaundice causes significant parental distress — especially when hospitalisation for phototherapy is required in the first days of life. Acknowledge anxiety, explain jaundice clearly in plain language, reassure that physiological jaundice is very common (60% of term and 80% of preterm neonates) and that treatment is highly effective. Written information (NICE CG98 patient leaflet, NHS website).
Future pregnancy risk (haemolytic disease) Future pregnancies should have standard antenatal antibody screening. Previous clinically significant haemolytic disease (Rh or ABO) should be documented and discussed with maternity/neonatal teams so that early postnatal bilirubin and haemoglobin monitoring can be arranged; after Rh disease the maternity team plans anti-D and fetal medicine input as needed.
G6PD — parental education G6PD deficiency (X-linked) — lifelong avoidance of oxidative triggers: fava beans, certain antimalarials (primaquine, pamaquine), dapsone, high-dose aspirin, methylene blue, some antibiotics. Carry G6PD alert card. Genetic counselling and sibling testing per specialist genetic/neonatal advice. G6PD testing during or shortly after haemolysis may be falsely normal (reticulocytes have higher enzyme activity) — repeat testing may be required. G6PD deficiency is not part of the routine NHS newborn bloodspot screen.
The breastfeeding message in neonatal jaundice is one of the most common sources of parental misinformation — many parents are told by well-meaning relatives, and sometimes by healthcare professionals, to "stop breastfeeding for 24–48 hours to see if the jaundice improves." This "breastfeeding holiday" test has no evidence base and causes measurable harm: it disrupts breastfeeding establishment during the critical first two weeks, causes maternal engorgement and mastitis, reduces milk supply, and increases formula dependence. The only indication to temporarily supplement breastfeeding is objective evidence of inadequate intake (weight loss >10%, insufficient wet nappies) combined with a rising bilirubin — and even then, the supplement should be expressed breast milk first, formula second, and breastfeeding itself should continue simultaneously. G6PD deficiency is globally the most common enzyme deficiency (400 million affected worldwide) and is highly prevalent in populations from Africa, the Mediterranean, Middle East, and South/Southeast Asia. GPs seeing neonates from these backgrounds should have a lower threshold for G6PD testing in unexplained haemolytic jaundice. G6PD-deficient males can have catastrophic haemolytic crises when exposed to fava beans, certain drugs, or infections even in adult life — parental education at the time of diagnosis has lifelong benefit.

Safety

Follow-Up, Safety-Netting & The 6-Week Check

Community monitoring (below threshold)
Risk-stratified per NICE CG98, not a blanket daily rule: well infant ≥38 weeks more than 50 μmol/L below the treatment threshold → usually no routine repeat; within 50 μmol/L → repeat within 18 hours (risk factors) or 24 hours. Community monitoring only via a verified local midwifery/neonatal pathway with trained staff and equipment. Record each measurement + plot on NICE CG98 chart. Document clear escalation plan in notes: "If SBR rises to [X] μmol/L → same-day hospital."
After phototherapy discharge
SBR rebound check at 12–18 hours post-phototherapy (bilirubin can rise back after phototherapy lights removed — especially in haemolytic disease). Manage the rebound value according to the NICE treatment chart and neonatal-team advice (TcB is unreliable after phototherapy — use SBR). Confirm feeding established and weight gaining.
The 6-week check — biliary atresia screen
At the 6–8-week check (per local child-health documentation) — an opportunity, not a reason to delay referral: (1) Is the baby still visibly jaundiced? (2) What colour are the stools? (3) Is urine dark? (4) Show / inspect stool colour card. If jaundice still present → split bilirubin + same-day paediatrics if any conjugated element. If stool colour 1–3 → same-day paediatric hepatology regardless of jaundice severity. Do not delay referral if pale stools, dark urine or persistent jaundice are identified before the six-week check.
Newborn bloodspot check
Confirm heel prick was performed at day 5 and result is documented. Check for congenital hypothyroidism, PKU, sickle cell, CF, MCADD and other screened results (galactosaemia is not screened). If not done or result missing → contact the local newborn screening team promptly. Do not assume result was normal without documentation.
Post-exchange transfusion / severe jaundice
Specialist-led follow-up: audiological assessment (bilirubin neurotoxicity can affect hearing); neurodevelopmental surveillance; neurology/developmental paediatric review if abnormal tone, hearing, movement, gaze or developmental concerns. MRI only if clinically indicated or requested by the specialist team — not routine. Early intervention / SENCO referral if developmental concerns.
Recurrence in future siblings
Future pregnancies should have standard antenatal antibody screening. Previous clinically significant haemolytic disease (Rh or ABO) should be documented and discussed with maternity/neonatal teams so that early postnatal bilirubin and haemoglobin monitoring can be arranged. G6PD: X-linked — testing of future siblings per specialist genetic/neonatal advice; testing during or shortly after haemolysis may be falsely normal.
999 / same-day safety-net
Baby becoming difficult to rouse, high-pitched cry, arching, or seizures (acute bilirubin encephalopathy) · Jaundice deepening rapidly after discharge from phototherapy · Pale/white stools developing at any time · Dark urine (tea-coloured) · Baby stops feeding or is inconsolably irritable
Same-day GP / midwife
Jaundice visibly worsening between scheduled reviews · Baby losing weight or not feeding adequately · TcB approaching treatment threshold · Any parental concern about deterioration · Stool colour becoming paler than previous nappy · Temperature ≥38°C or <36°C in a jaundiced neonate → urgent assessment for infection (same-day hospital; 999 if clinically unwell)
The 6-week GP check is the last safety net for biliary atresia in the UK — after this point, the next routine contact is at 8 weeks immunisations, at which point the Kasai surgery window is closing. UK national audit data (BAPS-CASS) show that some biliary atresia cases are still diagnosed late, with correspondingly worse outcomes — so referral should never wait for the 6-week check if pale stools, dark urine or persistent jaundice are noticed earlier. The BAPS (British Association of Paediatric Surgeons) and the Children's Liver Disease Foundation (Yellow Alert campaign) have produced specific GP-targeted education on recognising biliary atresia at the 6-week check. GPs should treat a persistently jaundiced baby at the 6-week check as a potential biliary atresia until proven otherwise — the split bilirubin result and stool colour assessment are non-negotiable. The neurodevelopmental sequelae of kernicterus require long-term follow-up — kernicterus causes the classic tetrad: (1) athetoid/dyskinetic cerebral palsy, (2) sensorineural hearing loss (auditory neuropathy), (3) oculomotor palsy (particularly upgaze palsy), and (4) intellectual disability (variable). Early identification and intervention (cochlear implants for deafness, physiotherapy, SENCO) significantly improve outcomes. GPs should ensure neurodevelopmental review appointments are in place for any neonate who had severe hyperbilirubinaemia (e.g. at/above the exchange threshold) or signs of encephalopathy, as directed by the specialist team.
