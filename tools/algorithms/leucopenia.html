1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Neutropenic Sepsis and Bone Marrow Failure

Neutropenic sepsis is a medical emergency. Separate four situations: chemotherapy-related febrile neutropenia, chronic isolated neutropenia, drug-induced agranulocytosis, and pancytopenia/suspected marrow failure — they do not share the same thresholds. Follow the local Greater Manchester acute oncology/haematology contact pathway.

Neutropenic sepsis (anticancer treatment) NICE: ANC ≤0.5 × 10⁹/L with temperature >38°C or other clinically significant sepsis features → immediate referral to secondary care; do not delay for community investigations
Unwell, possibly neutropenic Do not rely on fever alone (attenuated in neutropenic or steroid-treated patients). Rigors, new cough or breathlessness, dysuria, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, mucositis, hypotension, confusion or unexplained deterioration → urgent assessment
Pancytopenia New pancytopenia, blasts, dysplastic cells, rapidly falling counts, significant bleeding, severe anaemia, fever or systemic illness → urgent same-day hospital/haematology discussion (aplastic anaemia, acute leukaemia, MDS)
Shock or rapid deterioration Hypotension, confusion, respiratory compromise → follow the local sepsis pathway and emergency resuscitation guidance while arranging 999 transfer
Mouth ulcers + sore throat Severe mucositis, inability to swallow, drooling → same-day (agranulocytosis, risk of aspiration pneumonia)
Receiving anticancer treatment Fever or other possible infection symptoms → immediate contact with the acute oncology service and hospital assessment; follow the patient's oncology instructions, even if temperature is below 38°C
Purpura or bleeding Petechiae, spontaneous bruising, mucosal bleeding → same-day haematology (DIC, aplastic anaemia, acute leukaemia)
Suspected drug-induced agranulocytosis Clozapine, carbimazole, sulfasalazine or other implicated drug + sore throat/fever or very low ANC → withhold the drug where clinically safe, urgent FBC, same-day assessment and contact the relevant specialist team

Neutropenic sepsis (neutrophils <0.5 × 10⁹/L + fever) is a medical emergency. Without neutrophils, infections can progress rapidly to septic shock; risk varies with cause, duration and nadir, mucosal injury, chemotherapy, comorbidity, age and steroid use, so no single mortality figure applies. NICE CG151 requires immediate referral to secondary/tertiary care, where empirical antibiotics are given immediately; treatment should not be delayed for community investigations.

Pancytopenia suggests bone marrow failure (aplastic anaemia, MDS, acute leukaemia). Urgent haematology assessment enables bone marrow examination and supportive care. NICE NG12 recommends a very urgent FBC when leukaemia is possible (unexplained fever, persistent fatigue, recurrent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, unexplained bruising, bleeding, petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly).

Drug-induced agranulocytosis is a recognised risk with clozapine, carbimazole and sulfasalazine. Management is drug-specific: clozapine follows the mandatory monitoring and emergency pathway via the clozapine/psychiatric team; carbimazole requires immediate discontinuation and urgent FBC/medical assessment; DMARDs are withheld and the initiating specialist/shared-care team contacted.

Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia: a patient on anticancer treatment who becomes unwell with fever or other possible infection symptoms needs immediate acute oncology contact and hospital assessment, even if they look well. Neutropenic sepsis is diagnosed in hospital using the clinical context, sepsis features, temperature and ANC; do not delay transfer while awaiting the ANC or cultures.

2
Diagnose

Confirm Leucopenia — Repeat FBC and Check Absolute Neutrophil Count

A low WCC should be interpreted using the local laboratory reference range and the differential count. The clinically important parameter for infection risk is the ANC. Assess whether the abnormality is isolated, persistent, falling, associated with other cytopenias or accompanied by infection/systemic symptoms. A single low WCC can be a lab error or transient viral infection.

Repeat FBC
Repeat FBC with differential to establish the trend; timing depends on the ANC, trend, other cytopenias and symptoms. Any fever, sepsis features or clinical deterioration → immediate hospital assessment without waiting for a repeat.
Absolute neutrophil count
Request full differential to calculate absolute neutrophil count (ANC). ANC is WCC × neutrophil %. ANC <1.5 = neutropenia, <0.5 = severe.
Leucopenia vs neutropenia
Leucopenia (WCC below the local reference range) can be due to low neutrophils, lymphocytes, or both. Neutropenia (ANC <1.5) is the clinically significant concern (infection risk).
Blood film
Request or discuss a blood film for persistent or unexplained neutropenia, abnormal automated flags, other cytopenias, macrocytosis, lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, constitutional symptoms, or suspected leukaemia, MDS or marrow infiltration. Identifies blasts, dysplasia, atypical lymphocytes.
Ethnic variation
Benign ethnic / Duffy-null-associated neutropenia: consider in a well patient with a stable, isolated neutropenia, normal haemoglobin and platelets, no recurrent infections and an unremarkable blood film. Do not diagnose it from ethnicity alone. If the ANC is persistently below 1.0, falling, associated with other abnormalities or accompanied by recurrent infection, seek haematology advice. Infection risk is generally not increased in confirmed stable cases.
Persistent leucopenia
Persistence and clinical significance depend on the ANC, trend, differential, associated abnormalities and patient context — not on WCC <4 on two occasions alone.

Transient leucopenia is common after viral infections (influenza, EBV, CMV). The count usually recovers as the infection settles. Repeating FBC avoids unnecessary investigations and anxiety.

Absolute neutrophil count is the key number. A patient with WCC 3.5, neutrophils 40% (ANC 1.4) has mild neutropenia with low infection risk. A patient with WCC 3.5, neutrophils 20% (ANC 0.7) has moderate neutropenia requiring investigation.

Benign ethnic / Duffy-null-associated neutropenia is a normal variant, not a disease. Some individuals have an ANC below 1.0 × 10⁹/L while remaining well; the diagnosis depends on long-term stability, no recurrent or severe infection, no other cytopenias, a normal blood film and no concerning symptoms or signs — not on ethnicity alone.

Blood film review can detect myelodysplasia (dysplastic neutrophils, hyposegmented nuclei) and early leukaemia (blasts). Automated counters can miss morphological abnormalities.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Identify Severity and Pattern of Cytopenia

Classify by severity of neutropenia and whether other cell lines are affected. This determines infection risk and urgency of referral.

Mild neutropenia
ANC 1.0-1.5 × 10⁹/L. Stable, isolated and asymptomatic: repeat and investigate selectively. Causes: benign ethnic/Duffy-null-associated neutropenia, viral infection, mild drug effect.
Moderate neutropenia
ANC 0.5-1.0 × 10⁹/L. Causes: Autoimmune (SLE, RA), drugs (azathioprine, MTX), chronic infection (HIV, TB). Persistent: urgent haematology advice/referral, timing according to trend and clinical context.
Severe neutropenia
ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L. High infection risk. Causes: Chemotherapy, aplastic anaemia, drug-induced agranulocytosis. New or unexplained: urgent same-day discussion with haematology or the acute medical service, even without fever — particularly if falling or with mucositis, recurrent infection, other cytopenias, abnormal cells or systemic illness. Do not automatically direct every stable chronic case to 999.
Isolated neutropenia
Low neutrophils only. Hb and platelets normal. Differential: BEN, autoimmune neutropenia, chronic idiopathic neutropenia, drug-induced.
Pancytopenia
Low WCC + Hb + platelets. Possible marrow failure or infiltration. Stable, mild, unexplained pancytopenia without red flags still needs urgent haematology referral. Causes: Aplastic anaemia, MDS, acute leukaemia, myelofibrosis, megaloblastic anaemia (B12/folate deficiency).
Lymphopenia
Lymphocytes <1 × 10⁹/L. Causes: HIV, steroids, chemotherapy, SLE, sarcoidosis. Less clinically significant than neutropenia unless HIV suspected.

Infection risk rises as the ANC falls, but also depends on cause, duration and nadir, mucosal barrier injury, chemotherapy, comorbidity, malignancy, age, corticosteroid treatment, previous infections and local microbiology. A stable chronic ANC of 0.8–1.0 in a well patient is not equivalent to a new falling ANC of 0.8 with fever, mucositis or pancytopenia.

Isolated neutropenia is more likely to be benign (BEN, autoimmune) than pancytopenia. If Hb and platelets are normal, the bone marrow is functioning. Pancytopenia suggests marrow failure or infiltration and requires urgent haematology assessment.

Lymphopenia is less clinically important than neutropenia. Lymphocytes <0.5 × 10⁹/L suggests immunodeficiency (HIV, chemotherapy, immunosuppressants). Check HIV serology if risk factors present.

Benign ethnic / Duffy-null-associated neutropenia is stable over time. If the ANC is falling, persistently below 1.0, associated with other abnormalities or recurrent infection, seek haematology advice.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Identify Infection, Splenomegaly, and Autoimmune Signs

Examine for active infection, organomegaly, and signs of underlying systemic disease causing neutropenia.

Vital signs
Fever → infection; any ANC with fever, sepsis features or deterioration → immediate hospital assessment. Fever may be absent in neutropenic or steroid-treated patients. Tachycardia, hypotension → sepsis (local sepsis pathway, 999 transfer).
Mouth and throat
Inspect oral mucosa. Ulcers, gingivitis, mucositis → agranulocytosis (severe neutropenia cannot mount inflammatory response). White patches → oral candidiasis (immunosuppression).
Lymph nodes
Palpate cervical, axillary, inguinal chains. Generalized lymphadenopathy → HIV, SLE, lymphoma. Tender nodes → viral infection (EBV, CMV).
Spleen
Percuss and palpate left upper quadrant. Splenomegaly → sequestration (Felty syndrome, portal hypertension), infiltration (lymphoma, myelofibrosis), chronic infection (EBV, malaria).
Skin
Petechiae, purpura → thrombocytopenia (aplastic anaemia, acute leukaemia). Malar rash, photosensitivity → SLE. Cellulitis, abscesses → infection in neutropenic patient.
Joints
Synovitis → RA (Felty syndrome: RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia). SLE arthritis (small joints, non-erosive).
Chest
Auscultate for crackles (pneumonia). Reduced air entry → effusion (SLE, lymphoma). Neutropenic patients may have minimal signs despite severe pneumonia.

Oral ulcers and gingivitis are hallmarks of severe neutropenia. Without neutrophils, the mouth cannot mount an inflammatory response to oral bacteria. Severe mucositis with ANC <0.5 increases aspiration pneumonia risk.

Splenomegaly in neutropenia suggests Felty syndrome (RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia). It usually occurs in long-standing RA. The spleen sequesters neutrophils, worsening neutropenia; management is specialist-led.

Malar rash + lymphopenia + neutropenia suggests SLE. Leucopenia is common in SLE. Autoimmune tests are indicated when symptoms or signs suggest SLE, RA, vasculitis or another autoimmune disease.

Absent infection signs in neutropenic patient does not exclude serious infection. Neutrophils are required for pus formation, fever, and inflammatory markers. CRP can be normal despite pneumonia or sepsis. Low threshold for imaging and empirical antibiotics.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — First-Line Bloods, Film and Targeted Tests

Investigate based on the pattern of cytopenia, severity of neutropenia, and clinical context. Target investigations to the likely differential.

Baseline bloods
Repeat FBC + differential and trend Blood film (dysplasia, blasts) Renal + liver function Medication review. Reticulocyte count and CRP where clinically indicated. Other tests guided by symptoms, travel, exposure and examination.
B12 and folate
Vitamin B12 Serum folate — megaloblastic anaemia causes pancytopenia. Check where clinically indicated, particularly with macrocytosis or other cytopenias.
Autoimmune screen
Only if symptoms/signs suggest SLE, RA, vasculitis or another autoimmune disease: ANA (SLE), RF (RA/Felty), Anti-dsDNA (SLE-specific) — not merely because ANC is below 1.0. Immunoglobulins if recurrent infections or lymphopenia suggest immune deficiency.
Viral serology
HIV test when clinically appropriate (lymphopenia, neutropenia). EBV/CMV serology only in a compatible acute illness, not as routine chronic-neutropenia screening. Hepatitis B/C if clinically indicated.
Drug review
Check all medications, particularly clozapine, carbimazole, sulfasalazine, azathioprine and methotrexate; consider other drugs (e.g. valproate, captopril, beta-lactams) in context, particularly with a temporal relationship and compatible film. If drug-induced neutropenia or agranulocytosis is suspected, withhold the likely causative drug where clinically safe, obtain an urgent FBC/differential and contact the relevant specialist team. For clozapine and other specialist-monitored medicines, follow the drug-specific monitoring and escalation pathway.
If pancytopenia
LDH (haemolysis, malignancy), Haptoglobin (low in haemolysis), Liver function (portal hypertension), TFTs (hypothyroidism). Urgent haematology referral; same-day discussion if red flags (bleeding, infection, severe anaemia, blasts, rapidly falling counts).
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT perform bone marrow biopsy in primary care. Do NOT request G-CSF without haematology input. Do NOT delay hospital transfer for suspected neutropenic sepsis, or referral to "complete investigations" if ANC <0.5 or pancytopenia.

B12/folate deficiency is a reversible cause of pancytopenia. Megaloblastic anaemia suppresses all cell lines due to ineffective haematopoiesis. Interpret B12 and folate results against the local laboratory range; replacement reverses the cytopenia.

Drug-induced neutropenia is a common, often immune-mediated cause. Clozapine requires mandatory FBC monitoring under its own monitoring and emergency pathway; patients on carbimazole should have been counselled to stop it and get an urgent blood test if sore throat or fever develops.

HIV testing should be offered in unexplained leucopenia/lymphopenia when clinically appropriate. HIV can cause lymphopenia and neutropenia.

Autoimmune neutropenia (SLE, RA, Felty syndrome) is suggested by positive ANA (SLE), RF (RA), or anti-neutrophil antibodies. Immunosuppressive treatment is specialist-led.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to Involve Haematology or Infectious Diseases

Use a risk-stratified approach based on the ANC, trend, other cytopenias, symptoms, anticancer treatment and local haematology/acute oncology policy (Greater Manchester contact pathway).

Immediate hospital assessment
Suspected neutropenic sepsis in a patient receiving anticancer treatment. Any ANC with fever, sepsis features or clinical deterioration. Sepsis, hypotension, confusion or respiratory compromise. ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L with systemic symptoms. Pancytopenia with bleeding, infection, severe anaemia or abnormal film. Suspected acute leukaemia or circulating blasts. Severe mucositis with drooling.
Urgent same-day haematology discussion
New or falling ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L, even if afebrile (stable chronic cases via local haematology policy, not 999). New pancytopenia, blasts, dysplasia or rapidly falling counts. Suspected drug-induced agranulocytosis.
Urgent haematology
Persistent ANC 0.5-1.0 × 10⁹/L (timing by trend and clinical context). Persistent unexplained WCC <3 × 10⁹/L. Abnormal blood film. Neutropenia with splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, recurrent infections or constitutional symptoms. Unexplained pancytopenia (stable, mild, no red flags).
Routine haematology
Persistent ANC 1.0-1.5 × 10⁹/L not explained by benign ethnic/Duffy-null-associated neutropenia, per local haematology guidance. Autoimmune neutropenia needing specialist input. Chronic idiopathic neutropenia (no cause found after initial investigations).
Primary care management
Stable, isolated mild neutropenia. Likely transient viral leucopenia that is improving. Confirmed stable benign ethnic/Duffy-null-associated neutropenia. Mild drug-related neutropenia after appropriate specialist advice and an improving count.
Infectious diseases referral
HIV positive: All HIV patients with cytopenia. Suspected TB: Night sweats, weight loss, lymphopenia. Persistent fever despite treatment in a neutropenic patient (via the admitting/specialist team).

New or unexplained ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L needs urgent same-day discussion even without fever, but the route depends on whether the patient is on chemotherapy, whether the result is new or established and stable, the trend, symptoms, film, comorbidity and local haematology policy. G-CSF is a specialist-led treatment.

Pancytopenia requires urgent bone marrow biopsy to differentiate aplastic anaemia (empty marrow), MDS (dysplastic marrow), and acute leukaemia (blast infiltration). Treatment of aplastic anaemia (immunosuppression or bone marrow transplant) is specialist-led.

Benign ethnic / Duffy-null-associated neutropenia does not require haematology referral once confirmed as stable and isolated with a normal film and no recurrent infection. Reassure the patient this is a normal variant. Routine annual FBC is not necessarily required; follow local haematology advice.

Drug-induced agranulocytosis requires the suspected drug to be withheld and urgent same-day assessment. Any G-CSF is specialist-led. Clozapine rechallenge decisions belong to the clozapine/psychiatric team and monitoring service.

Treat

Treatment — Treat Underlying Cause and Prevent Infection

Treatment targets the underlying cause. Primary care can manage benign causes; prophylaxis, G-CSF and immunosuppression are specialist-led.

Drug-induced neutropenia
Withhold suspected drug Drug-specific pathway
Withhold the likely causative drug where clinically safe, obtain an urgent FBC/differential and contact the relevant specialist team. Clozapine: follow the mandatory clozapine monitoring and emergency pathway; contact the clozapine/psychiatric team and arrange urgent assessment. Carbimazole: stop immediately and arrange urgent FBC/medical assessment. Sulfasalazine, azathioprine, methotrexate: withhold and contact the initiating specialist/shared-care team.
B12/folate deficiency
Replace deficiency Curative
Hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM (3× weekly for 2 weeks, then every 2–3 months; BNF). Folic acid 5 mg OD. Repeat FBC to confirm the cytopenia is resolving.
Benign ethnic neutropenia
No treatment Reassure
Explain this is a normal variant once confirmed stable and isolated; infection risk is generally not increased. No treatment needed. Routine annual FBC not necessarily required — follow local haematology advice. Provide written information to avoid repeated investigations.

Prophylaxis and specialist treatments: do not initiate antibiotic or antifungal prophylaxis solely because ANC is below 1.0 or 0.5 × 10⁹/L.

Antibiotic prophylaxis
Prophylaxis depends on anticipated duration of neutropenia, chemotherapy regimen, haematological malignancy, transplant status, corticosteroid/immunosuppressive treatment, CD4 count or specific immune deficiency and local resistance patterns. Co-trimoxazole is for selected Pneumocystis jirovecii risk groups, not neutropenia alone. Refer to haematology/oncology or infectious diseases protocols.
Antifungal prophylaxis
Specialist- and regimen-dependent; not initiated in primary care.
G-CSF (filgrastim)
Haematology-led only. G-CSF is a specialist-led treatment; do not initiate in primary care.
Autoimmune neutropenia
Corticosteroids, immunosuppression and rituximab are specialist-led treatments. Do not initiate them in primary care without haematology/rheumatology advice.
Infection treatment
Arrange immediate hospital transfer for suspected neutropenic sepsis. Do not delay transfer while awaiting the ANC or cultures. Hospital teams administer empirical antibiotics immediately according to the local antimicrobial pathway. If shocked or rapidly deteriorating, follow the local sepsis pathway and emergency resuscitation guidance while arranging 999 transfer.

Withholding the causative drug is the key step in drug-induced neutropenia, following the drug-specific pathway. Clozapine rechallenge after agranulocytosis is a specialist decision.

G-CSF (filgrastim) stimulates neutrophil production; dose and indication depend on the underlying disorder, body size, renal function, malignancy, chemotherapy protocol and response, so it is specialist-led.

Co-trimoxazole prophylaxis prevents Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) in selected risk groups. PJP risk relates to CD4 lymphopenia, prolonged high-dose steroids and specific chemotherapy regimens rather than to the neutrophil count alone; prophylaxis is specialist-directed.

Empirical antibiotics in neutropenic fever save lives and should not be delayed for culture results. NICE recommends piperacillin–tazobactam as initial empirical monotherapy for suspected neutropenic sepsis needing IV treatment unless patient-specific or local microbiological factors contraindicate it — this is a secondary-care protocol, not a GP prescription.

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Infection Prevention and Risk Reduction

Give proportionate advice: hand hygiene, prompt attention to fever or infection symptoms, safe food handling and routine oral/dental care. For most patients with stable isolated mild neutropenia or benign ethnic neutropenia, normal social contact and a normal diet are appropriate. Additional restrictions (prolonged profound neutropenia, stem-cell transplantation, intensive chemotherapy) should be individualised and follow the specialist oncology/haematology plan.

Hand hygiene Wash hands before eating, after toileting, after contact with pets. Alcohol gel if soap unavailable. Single most important intervention to prevent infection.
Avoid sick contacts Normal social contact is appropriate for stable mild neutropenia; avoid close contact with obvious infections such as chickenpox where practical. Routine mask use is not required solely because ANC is below 1.0.
Food hygiene Normal diet with ordinary safe food handling; no need for a "neutropenic diet" in stable mild neutropenia. Any additional food restrictions during intensive treatment follow the specialist plan.
Dental hygiene Brush teeth twice daily with soft toothbrush. Floss gently (avoid bleeding gums). Routine dental care should not be stopped routinely; dental work during profound or treatment-related neutropenia follows specialist advice.
Vaccinations Annual flu vaccine (inactivated). Pneumococcal vaccine per UKHSA Green Book chapter 25. Avoid live vaccines (MMR, yellow fever, BCG) because of immunosuppression or the underlying treatment, not an ANC threshold alone — follow the Green Book and specialist advice.
Pet safety Contact with healthy pets is usually acceptable with ordinary hygiene (wash hands after handling, litter trays and reptiles). Extra precautions only during profound or treatment-related neutropenia per specialist plan.
Gardening Usually acceptable with ordinary hygiene — gloves and hand washing. Extra precautions (e.g. compost) only during profound or treatment-related neutropenia per specialist plan.
Recognise infection early Fever, rigors or feeling unwell (especially on anticancer treatment) = urgent help; on chemotherapy, contact the acute oncology service immediately. Sore throat, mouth ulcers, dysuria, cough or breathlessness = same-day assessment. Educate patient on when to seek help.

Hand hygiene reduces infection transmission and is the core proportionate precaution.

Food hygiene prevents gastrointestinal infections (Salmonella, Campylobacter, Listeria). Ordinary safe food handling is sufficient for most patients; restrictive diets are reserved for specialist-defined high-risk periods.

Live vaccines (MMR, yellow fever, BCG) contain attenuated virus/bacteria. In immunosuppressed patients, live vaccines can cause disseminated infection; the decision depends on the immunosuppression or treatment, per the Green Book. Inactivated vaccines (flu, pneumococcal) are safe and recommended.

Early recognition of infection is life-saving. Neutropenic patients cannot mount typical signs (pus, high WCC, high CRP). Fever may be the only sign of serious infection — and may be absent in neutropenic or steroid-treated patients. Educate patients to seek help immediately for fever or feeling unwell.

9
Safety

Follow-Up — Monitor Neutrophil Count and Safety-Net for Infection

Monitor neutrophil trajectory, watch for infection, and re-refer if ANC falling or recurrent infections. Surveillance intensity depends on the cause, trend and local haematology advice.

1 week
Repeat FBC if drug-induced neutropenia, as agreed with the specialist team (should be recovering). If ANC still falling, same-day haematology discussion. Check patient asymptomatic (no fever, mouth ulcers).
2-4 weeks
Repeat FBC if transient viral leucopenia (should normalize). If WCC <3 persistent, refer haematology. Persistent ANC 0.5–1.0: urgent haematology advice/referral (timing by trend and context); new/falling ANC <0.5 or symptomatic: same-day discussion.
3 months
If benign ethnic/Duffy-null-associated neutropenia suspected: repeat FBC to confirm the ANC is stable and isolated with a normal film. If stable, reassure; routine annual FBC not necessarily required (local haematology advice). If falling or persistently below 1.0, seek haematology advice.
6-12 months
No routine FBC needed after a transient neutropenia that has resolved. For chronic idiopathic neutropenia, follow the haematology monitoring plan.
Safety-net immediate
Fever or feeling unwell while on anticancer treatment (contact acute oncology). Fever, rigors or sepsis features with known neutropenia. Confusion, breathlessness, collapse or rapid deterioration. Severe dysphagia with drooling (mucositis, aspiration risk).
Safety-net same-day
New mouth ulcers, sore throat, dysuria (infection). Cough + breathlessness (pneumonia). Diarrhoea or abdominal pain. Do not rely on fever alone. Any infection in a neutropenic patient needs same-day assessment.
Re-referral criteria
ANC falling despite stopping drugs. Recurrent infections judged by severity, site, unusual organisms, need for hospital treatment or evidence of immune deficiency. New pancytopenia (Hb or platelets dropping). Address patient anxiety with explanation, safety-netting and shared decision-making; refer on clinical findings.

ANC trajectory is more important than absolute value. A falling ANC (1.5 → 1.0 → 0.7 over 3 months) suggests evolving bone marrow failure or worsening autoimmune disease, even if each value is "mild." Rising ANC indicates recovery or response to treatment.

Benign ethnic / Duffy-null-associated neutropenia is stable over years without treatment. If ANC is falling, persistently <1.0 or associated with other abnormalities, seek haematology advice to investigate other causes (autoimmune, bone marrow failure). Follow local haematology advice on monitoring.

Neutropenic fever is a medical emergency regardless of how well the patient looks. NICE CG151 requires immediate referral to secondary care and immediate empirical antibiotics in hospital; do not delay transfer for community tests.

Recurrent infections in a neutropenic patient — judged by severity, site, unusual organisms and need for hospital treatment — warrant haematology review. May indicate inadequate neutrophil function despite acceptable count, or progression of underlying disease. G-CSF prophylaxis or immunoglobulin replacement may be needed.

Educational use only. Pathway based on NICE CG151 Neutropenic sepsis: prevention and management in people with cancer, NICE NG12 Suspected cancer: recognition and referral, the UKHSA Green Book (immunisation against infectious disease), and the local Greater Manchester acute oncology/haematology contact pathway. Always adapt to individual patient context, local guidelines, and specialist advice. Neutropenia is a laboratory finding — identify and treat the underlying cause while preventing infection.
