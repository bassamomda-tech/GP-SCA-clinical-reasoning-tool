Safety

Red Flags — Ectopic Pregnancy, Haemorrhage & Life-Threatening Causes

Scope: primary-care recognition, initial assessment and referral guide. Definitive management of ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, placenta praevia, abruption and GTD remains under specialist care. Local maternity, EPAU/EGU, ambulance and laboratory protocols take precedence — insert Bolton/Greater Manchester contact routes before use.

Vaginal bleeding in any woman of reproductive age = ectopic pregnancy until a negative pregnancy test excludes it. Call 999 for instability or collapse, suspected ruptured ectopic, major or ongoing haemorrhage, suspected abruption with maternal or fetal compromise, vasa praevia with fetal bradycardia, sepsis, or any patient deteriorating or unable to travel safely. Stable patients with minor or settled bleeding after 13 weeks need same-day maternity assessment, not automatically a 999 ambulance (NICE NG201).

GP actions — 1. Confirm pregnancy and gestation
Urine pregnancy test if status uncertain. Establish LMP, gestation, previous scan and maternity details.
2. ABC and vital signs
Pulse, BP, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature. Estimate bleeding, but do not rely on pad count alone.
3. Emergency features
Shock, collapse, severe pain, peritonism, rigid/tender uterus, major bleeding, sepsis or fetal compromise → call 999; do not delay for examination or blood tests.
4. First-trimester bleeding
Pain, ≥6 weeks, ectopic risk factors, significant bleeding or uncertain location → same-day EPAU/EGU. Selected painless bleeding <6 weeks without risk factors → local expectant pathway only.
5. Bleeding after 13 weeks
Same-day maternity/obstetric assessment. No digital vaginal examination if placenta praevia is possible. 999 if unstable, major bleeding, severe pain, suspected abruption or fetal compromise.
6. Document
Gestation, symptoms, observations, examination, advice, referral destination, clinician contacted and safety-netting.
Do NOT
Diagnose ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage using hCG alone · diagnose miscarriage from LMP alone · use endometrial thickness alone to diagnose complete or incomplete miscarriage · perform digital vaginal examination when placenta praevia has not been excluded · give progesterone before confirming an intrauterine pregnancy · prescribe methotrexate for pregnancy of unknown location · use old anti-D rules without checking the current NICE recommendation · delay emergency transfer while obtaining blood tests or anti-D.
Vaginal bleeding + lower abdominal pain + haemodynamic instability (pulse >100, BP <90/60, collapse) Ruptured ectopic pregnancy — haemoperitoneum. Can lose 1–2 litres rapidly into the peritoneal cavity. → 999 immediately. Do not delay transfer for examination; assess symptoms without deliberately provoking them. IV access en route. This is the most time-critical obstetric emergency in primary care. Mortality risk highest when diagnosis delayed. Ectopic must be excluded before any other diagnosis considered in first-trimester bleeding.
Any vaginal bleeding + positive pregnancy test + no confirmed intrauterine pregnancy on USS Ectopic pregnancy — unruptured or leaking. Location: fallopian tube (96%), ovary, cervix, scar (Caesarean scar ectopic — highest risk). Pain may be absent or minimal. Shoulder tip pain = diaphragmatic irritation from haemoperitoneum = ruptured ectopic. Same-day Emergency Gynaecology Unit (EGU)/EPAU. ANY bleeding in early pregnancy without a confirmed IUP = ectopic until USS excludes (selected painless bleeding <6 weeks without risk factors may follow a documented local expectant pathway).
Heavy bleeding in any trimester + haemodynamic instability Placenta praevia (low-lying placenta covering cervical os — typically painless bleeding, third trimester) OR placental abruption (painful hard rigid uterus, concealed or revealed haemorrhage, fetal distress — do not perform a digital vaginal examination when placenta praevia has not been excluded — may precipitate catastrophic haemorrhage). Bleeding may be concealed in abruption — apparent loss can underestimate severity. → 999. Left lateral position in later pregnancy, keep warm. Do not delay transfer for examination.
Any bleeding + signs of sepsis (fever >38°C, tachycardia, rigors) following miscarriage or termination Septic miscarriage / post-procedure infection — Streptococcus pyogenes, E. coli, Clostridium perfringens. Toxic shock possible. → 999 + IV broad-spectrum antibiotics urgently. Septic abortion mortality is high when treatment delayed. Any post-miscarriage patient with fever + offensive discharge + pain = septic miscarriage emergency.
Painless heavy vaginal bleeding in second or third trimester + known or suspected placenta praevia Major haemorrhage risk — placenta praevia. Do not perform a digital vaginal examination (risks catastrophic haemorrhage); speculum examination only in secondary care once praevia has been assessed or excluded. → 999 immediately + left lateral position + keep warm. Inform ambulance of suspected placenta praevia. Any antenatal patient with a low-lying placenta on scan should carry documentation of this fact and be advised to go straight to hospital with any bleeding.
Vaginal bleeding + hydatidiform mole features: uterus large for dates, hyperemesis, theca lutein cysts, very high beta-hCG (>100,000 IU/L) Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) — hydatidiform mole or choriocarcinoma. Snowstorm appearance on USS is suggestive but not definitive — diagnosis requires histology. All confirmed GTD is registered with a GTD centre (Charing Cross, Sheffield, Dundee). Highly chemotherapy-sensitive — excellent prognosis if treated. Beta-hCG is tumour marker for follow-up; persistent or rising hCG after a pregnancy event needs specialist assessment. Contraception advised until the GTD centre confirms surveillance is complete.
Ectopic pregnancy remains the leading cause of maternal mortality in the first trimester of pregnancy. The key failure mode in primary care is not thinking of ectopic pregnancy as a differential in a woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding, particularly when the patient has not mentioned or does not know she is pregnant (up to 30% of women with ectopic pregnancy do not know they are pregnant at presentation). The absolute clinical rule is: any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding must have a pregnancy test performed. A urine pregnancy test costs £0.50 and takes 3 minutes — the failure to perform it is the most common identifiable factor in delayed ectopic diagnosis. The 'no confirmed intrauterine pregnancy' principle is critical — it is not sufficient to know the pregnancy test is positive; what matters is whether an intrauterine pregnancy has been confirmed by ultrasound. A positive pregnancy test + pelvic pain + any bleeding without confirmed IUP on USS = treat as ectopic until proved otherwise. The vaginal examination contraindication in suspected placenta praevia is a fundamental obstetric safety rule — digital vaginal examination can disrupt the placenta overlying the cervical os and trigger catastrophic, immediately life-threatening haemorrhage. This rule applies even in primary care settings where vaginal examination might seem routine: if there is any possibility of placenta praevia (third trimester bleeding, known low-lying placenta from previous scan), no digital vaginal examination should be performed before USS confirmation of placental position; speculum examination belongs in secondary care once praevia has been assessed or excluded.

Diagnose

Gestational Age — Trimester-Based Differential

Early pregnancy (up to 13 completed weeks) — differential
Miscarriage (most common — 10–20% of all pregnancies): threatened (cervical os closed), inevitable (os open), incomplete (retained products), complete, missed. Ectopic pregnancy (1–2% of all pregnancies — must exclude). Implantation bleeding (around day 21–28 — light spotting, self-limiting, no pain). Cervical ectropion (post-coital or spontaneous — benign glandular tissue on ectocervix, more vascular in pregnancy). Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD — mole, rare but serious). Subchorionic haematoma — USS finding, often causes alarming bleeding, usually resolves.
After 13 weeks, before 24+0 — differential
Cervical incompetence / painless cervical dilation (late second trimester loss, often no warning). Late miscarriage (13–23 weeks). Placenta praevia (symptomatic as uterus grows). Placental abruption (painful, can occur any trimester). Cervical lesion (cervical polyp, ectropion, rarely SCC). Preterm labour (from 20 weeks). Vasa praevia (rare — fetal vessels traverse cervical os — rupture = rapid fetal exsanguination, fetal heart rate abnormalities).
From 24+0 weeks (antepartum haemorrhage) — differential
Placenta praevia — painless, typically recurrent bleeds, may be heavy. Placental abruption — painful, hard uterus, may be concealed (no external bleeding). Show (mucus plug + blood ahead of labour — normal). Preterm labour. Uterine rupture (rare — scar rupture in VBAC, prior myomectomy — sudden severe pain + cessation of contractions + fetal distress). Vasa praevia rupture — fetal blood loss → fetal bradycardia. → All unexplained bleeding after 13 weeks: same-day maternity assessment (NICE NG201); 999 if unstable, major bleeding, severe pain, suspected abruption or fetal compromise.
Later-pregnancy bleeding — key points
Bleeding may be concealed in placental abruption — apparent loss can underestimate severity. Ultrasound identifies placental location but does not exclude abruption. Assess fetal movements and fetal heart where gestationally appropriate, but do not delay transfer. Consider domestic abuse and abdominal trauma where relevant. Do not discharge from primary care without a documented maternity plan. Do not send an unassessed patient to a routine outpatient clinic.
Gestational age assessment
Last menstrual period (LMP) + cycle regularity. USS dating (crown-rump length at 8–12 weeks = most accurate). Uterine size on examination (symphysis-fundal height in cm ≈ gestational age in weeks from 20 weeks). Fetal heart: Doppler audible from approximately 10–12 weeks (Sonicaid). Absent fetal heart when expected = urgent USS.
The trimester-based differential is the most practical organising framework for vaginal bleeding in pregnancy because it immediately narrows the differential and determines urgency. The key principle is: all unexplained bleeding after 13 weeks needs same-day secondary-care review (NICE NG201), and any early-pregnancy bleeding without confirmed IUP is a potential ectopic until proved otherwise. Bleeding at 14–23 weeks is no less urgent than later bleeding — it includes diagnoses (late miscarriage from cervical incompetence, placenta praevia becoming symptomatic) that carry major implications for the current and future pregnancies. Vasa praevia deserves specific mention because it is rare but carries extremely high fetal mortality when undiagnosed — the rate of fetal death from vasa praevia rupture is approximately 50–75% without prior diagnosis vs less than 3% with antenatal diagnosis and planned Caesarean delivery. Risk factors for vasa praevia include: IVF conception, low-lying placenta, bilobed placenta, succenturiate lobe. The RCOG Green-top guideline on Vasa Praevia (No. 27b, 2018) does not support universal screening; targeted transvaginal colour Doppler may be considered when risk factors such as a low-lying placenta are found at the anomaly scan. GPs seeing women with these risk factors can flag them so the anomaly scan team can consider vasa praevia screening.

Diagnose

Clinical Assessment — History & Examination

Key history
Gestational age (LMP, booking USS) · Amount of bleeding (pads, clots, colour — pad counts are imprecise and do not override clinical assessment) · Pain: none (implantation, praevia, ectropion) vs unilateral sharp (ectopic) vs crampy bilateral (miscarriage, labour) vs sudden severe with rigid uterus (abruption) · Shoulder tip pain (ruptured ectopic — diaphragmatic irritation) · Recent intercourse (ectropion, cervical polyp) · Previous ectopic, IVF, IUCD, tubal surgery (ectopic risk factors) · Previous miscarriage number and gestation · Previous Caesarean (scar ectopic, placenta accreta/praevia risk) · Fever, offensive discharge (septic miscarriage) · Known placenta praevia (from scan)
Examination — what to do
Vital signs: pulse, BP, temperature, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate · Abdominal palpation: uterine size, fundal height, tenderness, guarding (peritonism = ruptured ectopic), uterine rigidity (abruption) · Uterine activity · Fetal heart and fetal movements where gestationally appropriate (do not delay transfer) · Speculum examination (early pregnancy only, haemodynamically stable; after 13 weeks leave to secondary care once praevia assessed or excluded): visualise cervix — os open (inevitable miscarriage) vs closed · Do NOT perform bimanual examination in first-line assessment of suspected ectopic (risk of rupture) · Ask about shoulder-tip pain — do not deliberately provoke it; maintain a position that supports ABC (usually left lateral in later pregnancy)
Examination — what NOT to do
Do NOT perform a digital vaginal examination when placenta praevia has not been excluded. Do NOT perform speculum examination if haemodynamically unstable or in later-pregnancy bleeding before praevia is assessed. Do NOT omit abdominal assessment (tenderness, guarding, uterine tenderness/rigidity, uterine activity, fundal height). Do NOT perform bimanual examination before USS in suspected ectopic. Do NOT delay 999 call to perform examination if the patient is collapsed or haemodynamically compromised — examination can happen in hospital.
Bedside investigations
Urine pregnancy test (mandatory in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain/bleeding) · Blood group and rhesus status (anti-D decision depends on gestation and management — see Anti-D below) · FBC (anaemia from blood loss — Hb may not fall immediately in acute haemorrhage — serial measurements) · Serum beta-hCG (serial hCG trends support specialist assessment of pregnancy of unknown location; they do not establish pregnancy location or viability in isolation — interpret only within the EPAU pathway. Do not use serum progesterone to diagnose viability or ectopic) · Clotting screen (if heavy loss or sepsis suspected) · Group and save (all significant bleeds)
Serum hCG does not determine pregnancy location. For a pregnancy of unknown location, two hCG measurements are taken approximately 48 hours apart and interpreted only within an EPAU/senior clinical pathway. NICE NG126: a rise of more than 63% over 48 hours suggests a developing intrauterine pregnancy, but ectopic pregnancy is not excluded — a rising hCG does not exclude ectopic. A fall of less than 50% or a rise of less than 63% requires early pregnancy review. Do not use serum progesterone to diagnose viability or ectopic pregnancy, and do not prescribe methotrexate for a pregnancy of unknown location. Shoulder-tip pain reflects blood tracking under the diaphragm (phrenic nerve, C3–C5) — ask about it, but do not deliberately provoke it; a woman with a positive pregnancy test, abdominal pain and shoulder-tip pain needs a 999 call.

Miscarriage Classification & Investigations

Threatened miscarriage
Bleeding + closed cervical os + fetal heart seen on USS. Pregnancy may continue. Confirmed IUP with fetal heartbeat and no previous miscarriage: if bleeding stops, routine antenatal care; if bleeding worsens or persists beyond 14 days, reassessment — routine repeat USS is not automatically required. There is no good evidence that intercourse, routine activity or exercise causes miscarriage; temporary avoidance of intercourse may be discussed if bleeding is heavy or for patient preference, but is not proven treatment. Progesterone (NICE NG126): offer vaginal micronised progesterone 400 mg BD when there is bleeding in the current pregnancy, a previous miscarriage, and an intrauterine pregnancy confirmed by USS (no need to wait for a heartbeat); if fetal heartbeat confirmed, continue to 16 completed weeks.
Inevitable miscarriage
Bleeding + open cervical os ± pain. Products at os visible or palpable. Imminent complete expulsion. Hospital assessment — may need surgical or medical management of retained products. Urgent EPAU referral.
Incomplete miscarriage
Bleeding + open os + retained products of conception (POC) on specialist USS assessment with the clinical picture (endometrial thickness alone is not diagnostic). Management: expectant (further bleeding expected), medical (misoprostol 600 micrograms single dose vaginal, oral or sublingual; 800 micrograms may be used to align local protocols; do not use mifepristone — NICE NG126), or surgical (manual vacuum aspiration or ERPC). EPAU-led decision.
Complete miscarriage
Bleeding now minimal + os closed + empty uterus on specialist USS + no products (endometrial thickness alone is not diagnostic). If no intrauterine pregnancy was previously confirmed, an apparent complete miscarriage may still be a pregnancy of unknown location or ectopic. Routine serum hCG to <5 IU/L is not required — home urine pregnancy test at 3 weeks. Emotional support and bereavement acknowledgement.
Missed miscarriage (embryonic / fetal demise)
TVUSS (NICE NG126): CRL <7.0 mm with no heartbeat → repeat scan after at least 7 days; CRL ≥7.0 mm with no heartbeat → second opinion and/or repeat scan after at least 7 days before diagnosis. MSD <25.0 mm with no fetal pole → repeat after at least 7 days; MSD ≥25.0 mm with no fetal pole → second opinion and/or repeat after at least 7 days. Do not use LMP alone to decide whether a heartbeat should be visible. Patient may be asymptomatic — diagnosed at booking scan. Management: expectant, medical (mifepristone 200 mg orally, then misoprostol 800 micrograms 48 hours later), or surgical (patient choice — NICE NG126). Sensitive counselling required.
USS confirmation (EPAU / EGU)
Early-pregnancy bleeding with pain, pregnancy ≥6 weeks, risk factors for ectopic pregnancy, diagnostic uncertainty or significant bleeding requires same-day EPAU/EGU assessment. Selected patients under 6 weeks with painless bleeding and no ectopic risk factors may be managed with a documented expectant pathway per local EPAU arrangements — repeat urine pregnancy test after 7–10 days and return if positive or bleeding continues; same-day EPAU review if pain develops, and 999 for severe pain, collapse or heavy bleeding (NICE NG126). Transvaginal USS (TVUSS) is more sensitive than transabdominal for early pregnancy assessment. Gestational sac visible from approximately 4.5–5 weeks · Yolk sac from approximately 5 weeks · Fetal pole from approximately 5.5–6 weeks · Fetal cardiac activity from approximately 6 weeks (TVUSS). Absence of expected structures: repeat scan after at least 7 days per NICE CRL/MSD criteria — do not judge by LMP alone.
Vaginal progesterone for threatened miscarriage is one of the most important recent evidence-based developments in early pregnancy care for GPs — the PRISM trial (2019, NEJM, n=4,153) provides the evidence (the earlier PROMISE trial, in women with recurrent miscarriage but no bleeding, showed no benefit) that vaginal progesterone 400 mg BD reduces miscarriage rates in women who present with first-trimester bleeding and have an intrauterine pregnancy confirmed on USS, with the greatest benefit in women who have had one or more previous miscarriages. NICE NG126 recommends offering vaginal micronised progesterone 400 mg BD to women with bleeding in the current pregnancy who have previously had a miscarriage, once an intrauterine pregnancy is confirmed on USS (no need to wait for a heartbeat), continuing to 16 completed weeks if a fetal heartbeat is confirmed. This is a GP-initiatable treatment — the GP does not need to wait for the EPAU to prescribe. The prescription: Cyclogest 400 mg pessaries, insert vaginally BD, from confirmation of an intrauterine pregnancy to 16 completed weeks (check GMMMG formulary and SPC). Endometrial thickness is not a reliable standalone criterion for complete or incomplete miscarriage — diagnosis rests on the clinical picture and specialist ultrasound assessment, including retained products where relevant.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 — immediate
Haemodynamic instability or collapse · Suspected ruptured ectopic (severe or worsening abdominal pain, peritonism, shoulder-tip pain, syncope or shock) · Major or ongoing haemorrhage · Suspected abruption with maternal compromise, severe pain, uterine rigidity or fetal compromise · Suspected vasa praevia rupture with fetal bradycardia or acute fetal compromise · Sepsis or suspected septic miscarriage with systemic illness · Uterine rupture · Any bleeding where the patient is deteriorating or cannot safely travel to hospital
Same-day EPAU / EGU
Any suspected ectopic pregnancy (positive test + no confirmed IUP + pain or bleeding) · Early-pregnancy bleeding with pain, ≥6 weeks, ectopic risk factors or diagnostic uncertainty · Significant first-trimester bleeding (clinical assessment — do not rely on pad count alone) · Inevitable miscarriage (open os on examination) · Suspected GTD (mole) · Any pregnancy bleeding where examination reveals open cervical os
EPAU within 24–48 hours
Threatened miscarriage (stable, closed os, light bleeding) — USS to confirm intrauterine pregnancy · Missed miscarriage confirmed on scan (patient stable, no bleeding) · Subchorionic haematoma on USS · Serum beta-hCG ordered — review result at 48 hours · Post-miscarriage follow-up (confirm complete)
Antenatal team / obstetrics (second and third trimester)
All unexplained vaginal bleeding after 13 weeks → same-day maternity/obstetric triage assessment (NICE NG201; not EPAU — EPAU manages early pregnancy only). This does not automatically require a 999 ambulance if stable and bleeding is minor or settled. Phone maternity triage directly. Do not send by routine referral letter or to a routine outpatient clinic. Arrange ambulance transfer if significant bleeding, instability, fetal concern, severe pain, suspected abruption or inability to travel safely. Suspected placenta praevia, abruption, preterm labour, or fetal compromise = hospital same day.
Anti-D immunoglobulin
Up to 11+6 weeks: NICE NG126 advises NOT offering anti-D for ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage or threatened miscarriage. 12+0 to 12+6 weeks: offer at least 250 IU with medical or surgical management of ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage, and consider it for threatened miscarriage with heavy or recurrent bleeding (NG126). Do not use a Kleihauer test for early-pregnancy events. From 13 weeks: unexplained bleeding needs secondary-care review; offer anti-D to RhD-negative patients at risk of isoimmunisation (NICE NG201). From 20 weeks: follow local obstetric/transfusion protocol for dose and fetomaternal-haemorrhage testing. Discuss that anti-D is a plasma-derived, filtered blood product. Check blood group if unknown. Do not delay emergency transfer while arranging anti-D.
Recurrent miscarriage
Three or more first-trimester miscarriages (clinical discretion to investigate after two where a pathological pattern is suspected — RCOG GTG 17, 2023) → recurrent miscarriage clinic. Investigations: antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant + anticardiolipin; consider β2-glycoprotein I), TFTs + TPO antibodies, uterine anatomy (ideally 3D USS), cytogenetics of pregnancy tissue, parental karyotype only if indicated. No routine inherited thrombophilia screen. Aspirin + heparin for confirmed APS — specialist-led; no empirical treatment for unexplained recurrent miscarriage.

Anti-D immunoglobulin administration in Rh-negative women with pregnancy-associated bleeding is one of the most important preventive interventions in obstetric primary care — failure to administer anti-D when indicated is one of the most common sources of maternal harm from GP prescribing errors in obstetrics. The mechanism: if a Rh-negative mother is sensitised to fetal Rh-positive red blood cells (which can enter maternal circulation during any pregnancy-related bleeding event), she develops anti-D antibodies that in subsequent pregnancies can cross the placenta and destroy fetal red blood cells, causing haemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN) — ranging from mild jaundice to hydrops fetalis and intrauterine death. Anti-D immunoglobulin given within 72 hours of the sensitising event prevents maternal sensitisation by destroying fetal red cells before the immune response develops. NICE NG126 has been updated: up to 11+6 weeks, anti-D is not offered for ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage or threatened miscarriage; at 12+0 to 12+6 weeks it is offered with medical or surgical management and considered for heavy or recurrent bleeding; from 13 weeks, unexplained bleeding needs secondary-care review and anti-D is offered to RhD-negative patients at risk of isoimmunisation (NICE NG201), with local obstetric/transfusion protocol from 20 weeks. Every GP practice should have a clear protocol for anti-D administration in early pregnancy bleeding — including the blood group result, dose (at least 250 IU at 12+0–12+6 weeks per NG126; local protocol thereafter), route (IM deltoid), timing (within 72 hours), and documentation. Recurrent miscarriage (three or more first-trimester losses) investigation should include antiphospholipid antibody testing because APS has a proven specialist-led intervention — LMWH + low-dose aspirin in women with antiphospholipid syndrome reduces miscarriage rate from approximately 90% to approximately 30% (NNT approximately 4 for a live birth), one of the most dramatic treatment effects in reproductive medicine.

Treat

Management by Diagnosis

Ectopic pregnancy — medical management (specialist)
First-line methotrexate (NICE NG126) if: no significant pain, unruptured tubal ectopic, adnexal mass <35 mm, no visible fetal heartbeat, hCG <1,500 IU/L, no intrauterine pregnancy on USS, able to attend follow-up. hCG 1,500–<5,000 IU/L: methotrexate or surgery after counselling. Dosing and contraindications per specialist protocol and SPC. hCG on days 4 and 7 after methotrexate, then weekly until negative; urgent review for pain or deterioration — rupture risk persists during treatment. Conception delayed per treating unit (commonly at least 3 months).
Ectopic pregnancy — surgical
Surgery first line if: follow-up not possible, significant pain, mass ≥35 mm, visible fetal heartbeat, or hCG ≥5,000 IU/L. Laparoscopic salpingectomy (ruptured or failed medical, or haemodynamically unstable — open surgery). Salpingotomy (if only tube — fertility-preserving, higher ectopic recurrence). Expectant management (specialist only): clinically stable and pain-free, tubal ectopic <35 mm, no visible heartbeat, hCG ≤1,000 IU/L (offer) or >1,000–<1,500 IU/L (consider), reliable follow-up; hCG on days 2, 4 and 7 then weekly, with at least a 15% fall between each measurement.
Miscarriage — management options (NICE NG126)
Expectant: first line for 7–14 days after confirmed miscarriage where clinically suitable. Medical — missed miscarriage: mifepristone 200 mg orally, then 48 hours later misoprostol 800 micrograms vaginally, orally or sublingually (unless the sac has already passed); incomplete miscarriage: misoprostol 600 micrograms single dose vaginally, orally or sublingually (800 micrograms may be used to align local protocols) — do not use mifepristone. Provide analgesia and anti-emetics; explain expected pain, bleeding, diarrhoea and vomiting. Surgical: manual vacuum aspiration (MVA) under local anaesthetic, or surgical management under general anaesthetic where clinically appropriate. Choice is influenced by patient preference, previous traumatic experience, haemorrhage risk and infection. Specialist/EPAU-led decisions — not GP prescribing instructions.
Threatened miscarriage
Progesterone if eligible (NICE NG126): Cyclogest 400 mg pessary BD vaginally — bleeding in current pregnancy + previous miscarriage + intrauterine pregnancy confirmed on USS; continue to 16 completed weeks if fetal heartbeat confirmed. Activity: no good evidence that intercourse, routine activity or exercise causes miscarriage; temporary avoidance of intercourse may be discussed if bleeding is heavy or for patient preference, but is not proven treatment. Follow-up: if bleeding stops, routine antenatal care; if it worsens or persists beyond 14 days, reassessment (repeat USS not automatically required). Emotional support: acknowledge the fear and uncertainty — "I know how worrying this must be."
Placenta praevia (antenatal management)
Timing and mode of birth are an obstetric-team decision based on symptoms, placental position and local guidance (RCOG GTG 27a, 2026, advises planned birth no later than 37+6 weeks for uncomplicated praevia — not a GP instruction). RCOG: pelvic rest (no intercourse), avoid vaginal examinations. Steroid cover if at risk of preterm birth. Cord blood banking consideration. Admit if heavy bleeding until stable. Iron supplementation for anaemia. Multidisciplinary planning for potential massive haemorrhage (blood bank, haematology, consultant anaesthesia).
GTD (hydatidiform mole)
Surgical uterine evacuation (suction curettage — NOT sharp curettage, risk of uterine perforation). Send tissue for histology. Refer to GTD centre (UK centres: Charing Cross London, Sheffield, Dundee) for beta-hCG surveillance and management of persistent GTD / choriocarcinoma. Contraception in line with the GTD centre plan until the centre confirms surveillance is complete. Surveillance duration differs between complete and partial mole; if chemotherapy was required, the GTD centre advises the interval before pregnancy (commonly 12 months).
The GTD surveillance programme in the UK (managed by three specialist centres — Charing Cross Hospital, Weston Park Hospital Sheffield, and Ninewells Hospital Dundee) is one of the most successful examples of centralised cancer surveillance in the world, achieving cure rates of over 98% for all forms of gestational trophoblastic disease including choriocarcinoma. The mechanism is that beta-hCG is a uniquely sensitive and specific tumour marker for GTD — persistent or rising beta-hCG after evacuation of a molar pregnancy requires specialist assessment for persistent trophoblastic disease. The surveillance programme monitors serial urine beta-hCG samples sent by post to the specialist centre, with treatment initiated promptly if levels plateau or rise. GPs play an important role: ensuring the patient is registered with the GTD centre after molar evacuation, actioning GTD-centre correspondence, providing contraception in accordance with the specialist plan until the centre confirms surveillance is complete, avoiding pregnancy-related investigations that complicate hCG surveillance, and urgently referring heavy bleeding, infection or symptoms suggestive of metastatic disease. The methotrexate treatment protocol for ectopic pregnancy requires specific follow-up that GPs may be involved in — the key post-injection monitoring parameters are: beta-hCG on day 4 and day 7 after injection (the hCG typically rises from day 1–4 before falling — so a rising hCG on day 4 is expected and should not trigger rescue surgery), and a fall of at least 15% from day 4 to day 7 indicates treatment success. Patients must be advised to return immediately if they develop worsening pain (indicating tubal rupture even during apparently successful treatment) and to avoid NSAIDs (which reduce methotrexate renal clearance and increase toxicity).

Treat

Anti-D, Emotional Support & Recurrent Miscarriage

Anti-D immunoglobulin — prescribing
Indication: Rh-negative woman with a potentially sensitising event at the gestations above (not before 12 weeks — NICE NG126). Dose: at least 250 IU (50 micrograms) at 12+0–12+6 weeks (NICE NG126); from 13 weeks offer to RhD-negative patients at risk of isoimmunisation (NICE NG201); from 20 weeks dose and fetomaternal-haemorrhage testing per local obstetric/transfusion protocol. No Kleihauer test for early-pregnancy events. Discuss that anti-D is a plasma-derived, filtered blood product. Timing: within 72 hours of bleed — never delay emergency transfer to give it. Route: intramuscular (deltoid preferred — avoid gluteal in pregnancy). Formulation: per local maternity protocol (e.g. Rhophylac 1500 IU, IM or IV). Confirm blood group first if unknown. Document in maternity notes and GP records. Not needed: previous sensitisation already confirmed, or woman already Rh-positive.
Emotional and psychological support after miscarriage
Miscarriage causes significant grief — acknowledge explicitly: "I'm so sorry for your loss." Avoid minimising: do not say "you can try again" or "it was very early" (dismissive). Validate: "This was your baby and your loss is real." Offer follow-up appointment in 2–4 weeks. Signpost to Miscarriage Association (www.miscarriageassociation.org.uk — helpline 01924 200799). Consider fitness for work certificate (2 weeks minimum for most patients — physical and emotional recovery). NHS Talking Therapies referral if depression or prolonged grief develops. Use clinical judgement to assess mental health, trauma, safeguarding and suicidal ideation where relevant. Partner involvement where appropriate.
Recurrent miscarriage investigation
After 3 or more first-trimester miscarriages (discretion after two if a pathological pattern is suspected — RCOG GTG 17, 2023): antiphospholipid antibodies — lupus anticoagulant + anticardiolipin antibodies; β2-glycoprotein I antibodies may also be considered (x2, ≥12 weeks apart). Thyroid function + thyroid peroxidase antibodies. Congenital uterine anomalies: ideally 3D ultrasound. Cytogenetic analysis of pregnancy tissue after the third and subsequent miscarriage, and after any second-trimester miscarriage. Parental karyotype only if tissue shows an unbalanced structural chromosomal abnormality, or tissue is unavailable/unsuccessful. Not routinely: inherited thrombophilia screen, ANA (unless clinically indicated), TORCH screen, NK-cell/cytokine/other immunological tests, endometrial biopsy or chronic endometritis testing outside specialist/research pathways, or aspirin/LMWH without confirmed APS or another indication.
Treatment of APS in pregnancy
LMWH + low-dose aspirin: enoxaparin 40 mg SC OD from positive pregnancy test throughout pregnancy + aspirin 75 mg OD from positive test. This combination reduces miscarriage rate in confirmed APS from approximately 90% to 30% (NNT ≈ 4 for live birth). Specialist-led — initiated by haematology/obstetric medicine; not empirical treatment for unexplained recurrent miscarriage (RCOG GTG 17). GP continues prescription with monitoring. Platelet count at baseline and monthly on LMWH. Stop aspirin at 36 weeks (haemorrhage risk at delivery).
The language used when a patient has had a miscarriage is one of the most important clinical communication skills a GP can develop — miscarriage is one of the most common and most emotionally devastating experiences that women and their partners can face, yet it is frequently trivialised or minimised by healthcare professionals. The most harmful phrases ('at least it was early,' 'you can try again,' 'it wasn't a real baby yet,' 'nature's way of dealing with abnormalities') are not medically inaccurate but are emotionally dismissive and can cause lasting psychological harm, including prolonged grief, complicated bereavement, and loss of trust in the healthcare system. The Miscarriage Association has published guidance on supportive communication that all GPs should be familiar with. The core elements are: acknowledge the loss directly and specifically, use the word 'baby' if the patient does (follow their language), allow and validate the emotional response, and avoid rushing to 'silver lining' or future-focused statements before the grief has been acknowledged. Many patients describe the GP's few words in the immediate aftermath of miscarriage as either the most healing or most harmful aspect of their entire experience. RCOG GTG 17 (2023) defines recurrent miscarriage as three or more first-trimester miscarriages, while allowing clinical discretion to investigate after two where a pathological rather than sporadic pattern is suspected.

Lifestyle

Recovery, Future Pregnancy & Wellbeing

Physical recovery after miscarriage Bleeding typically continues for 7–14 days after complete miscarriage (lighter than a period). Home urine pregnancy test 3 weeks after medical management, or 3 weeks after expectant management once bleeding and pain have resolved — if positive, EPAU review (retained products, ectopic or molar pregnancy). Persistent heavy bleeding, pain or fever needs assessment even if the test is negative. Next period expected in 4–6 weeks. Cervical screening should not be performed while bleeding. Sexual intercourse: safe to resume when comfortable (typically 2 weeks). No evidence that intercourse or activity causes miscarriage in subsequent pregnancy.
Timing of future pregnancy RCOG guidance: no medical evidence that waiting is beneficial (previously advised "wait 3 months" — not supported by evidence). WHO recommends waiting 6 months but evidence for this is weak. Current RCOG advice: try when physically and emotionally ready. Folic acid 400 mcg daily when planning pregnancy (5 mg where indicated by current national/local guidance — e.g. previous neural tube defect, diabetes, antiepileptic medicines). Refer to miscarriage clinic / obstetric medicine for planned future pregnancy if recurrent miscarriage or APS.
Smoking and alcohol cessation Smoking and heavy alcohol consumption increase pregnancy loss risk. Both should be addressed proactively at any pre-conception or post-miscarriage consultation. Refer pregnant smokers to the local Greater Manchester Smokefree Pregnancy service [insert referral route] (GMMMG tobacco dependency guidance). AUDIT-C screen. Offer very brief advice (VBA) at minimum.
Folic acid (pre-conception) Folic acid 400 mcg OD before conception. 5 mg OD where indicated by current national guidance and local prescribing — including previous neural tube defect, diabetes and antiepileptic medicines. For vitamin D and other dietary advice, see current NICE antenatal care guidance (NG201).
Emotional wellbeing and support Acknowledge that grief after miscarriage has no set timeline. Both partners may grieve differently — validate different responses. Some patients wish to mark the loss (memorial, naming, planting a tree) — support these choices. Miscarriage Association, Tommy's, SANDS (stillbirth) peer support. NHS Talking Therapies for depression or prolonged grief. Use clinical judgement to assess mood, trauma, safeguarding and suicidal ideation where relevant — routine PHQ-9/GAD-7 is not mandatory. Consider sick note: physical recovery + emotional impact typically warrants 2–4 weeks.
Ectopic pregnancy — future pregnancy planning After one ectopic: subsequent ectopic risk approximately 10% (vs 1% background). All future pregnancies: early USS, often around 6–7 weeks, to confirm IUP (EPAU self-referral advised). After methotrexate, delay conception per the treating unit (commonly at least 3 months). IUDs are UKMEC 1 after a previous ectopic (FSRH) — overall ectopic risk is lower than with no contraception, but if pregnancy occurs with a device in situ, exclude ectopic. Combined OCP or barrier methods are appropriate. After salpingectomy: reduced fertility (one tube) — consider early referral for assisted conception if not pregnant within 12 months of trying.
Information and consent for management options Miscarriage management is a patient choice (expectant vs medical vs surgical) — all three options have similar complete miscarriage rates at 3 months. Discuss the options, provide written information (NICE NG126 patient leaflet), allow time to decide (except haemodynamic instability). The feeling of control over how the miscarriage is managed is clinically important for psychological recovery. Do not rush decisions — the EPAU can see the patient again the following day.
Pre-conception health review Any patient with miscarriage, ectopic, or GTD presents an opportunity for pre-conception health optimisation: folic acid, cervical screening up to date, rubella immune, Hep B immunity, chlamydia screen (if <25), BMI review, diabetes/hypertension screening, thyroid function (undiagnosed hypothyroidism increases miscarriage risk), stop teratogenic medications (ACEi, statins, retinoids, valproate, methotrexate — VALPROATE PREGNANCY PREVENTION PROGRAMME).
The Valproate Pregnancy Prevention Programme (PPP) is a mandatory NHS programme that requires all women of childbearing potential prescribed sodium valproate to be on a formalised risk acknowledgement programme — this includes annual specialist review, contraception counselling, mandatory annual review by a specialist, and a signed acknowledgement of the teratogenicity risks. Valproate is associated with an approximately 11% risk of major congenital malformations (including neural tube defects) and a 30–40% risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in exposed children (MHRA). GPs who encounter a woman who is pregnant or planning pregnancy and taking valproate must immediately contact the prescribing specialist (psychiatry or neurology), as valproate is contraindicated in pregnancy unless there is absolutely no suitable alternative. If the patient is already pregnant on valproate, urgent referral to teratology services and the prescribing specialist is needed. Other teratogenic drugs that require pre-conception review: statins (all contraindicated), ACE inhibitors and ARBs (contraindicated in second/third trimester, caution in first), retinoids (isotretinoin — MHRA pregnancy prevention programme: negative pregnancy test before starting and highly effective contraception), warfarin (crosses placenta — switch to LMWH ideally before conception), mycophenolate (highly teratogenic — change to azathioprine if immunosuppression needed), thalidomide (absolute contraindication — strict pregnancy prevention programme), methotrexate (teratogenic — stop 3 months before conception). Every GP should perform a medication review for teratogenic drugs when a patient of childbearing age presents, particularly after a pregnancy event.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Threatened miscarriage
Bleeding stops → routine antenatal care; worsens or persists beyond 14 days → reassessment (repeat USS not automatically required). Check progesterone prescription in place if eligible (NICE NG126). Anti-D given and documented if Rh-negative and eligible by gestation (none up to 11+6 weeks — NICE NG126). Review bleeding pattern — worsening bleeding or new pain → EPAU same day.
After confirmed miscarriage
Home urine pregnancy test 3 weeks after medical management, or after expectant management once symptoms have resolved; positive → EPAU/specialist review (retained products, ectopic or molar pregnancy). Persistent heavy bleeding, pain or fever needs assessment even if the test is negative. Offer emotional support; use clinical judgement to assess mental health, trauma, safeguarding and suicidal ideation (routine PHQ-9/GAD-7 not mandatory). Contraception/future pregnancy discussion, sick note if needed. Recurrent miscarriage referral if this is the third first-trimester loss (discretion after two).
After ectopic
Follow the EPAU/gynaecology protocol — after methotrexate, hCG on days 4 and 7 then weekly until negative; expectant management, days 2, 4 and 7 then weekly. Urgent review for new pain or deterioration regardless of hCG. Delay conception after methotrexate per the treating unit (commonly at least 3 months). Counsel on future pregnancy: early USS, often around 6–7 weeks — give EPAU contact for self-referral. Contraception advice. Fertility counselling if single tube. Psychological support — ectopic carries significant emotional impact including fear of recurrence.
After GTD
GTD centre surveillance (Charing Cross, Sheffield, or Dundee) — serial urine beta-hCG. GP role: action GTD-centre correspondence promptly; provide contraception in accordance with the specialist plan until the centre confirms surveillance is complete; avoid initiating pregnancy-related investigations that complicate hCG surveillance; urgently refer heavy bleeding, infection or symptoms suggestive of metastatic disease. If beta-hCG plateau or rise on surveillance → GTD centre will initiate chemotherapy.
Return immediately
Collapse, fainting or severe dizziness · Severe or worsening abdominal or shoulder-tip pain · Heavy bleeding with weakness, breathlessness or palpitations · Fever, rigors, offensive discharge or severe pelvic pain after miscarriage or procedure · New pain while under ectopic surveillance · Any clinical deterioration, regardless of the hCG result. Do not rely on pad counts alone.
Same-day EPAU
New or worsening pain in known ectopic under conservative management · Home pregnancy test positive 3 weeks after miscarriage management · New bleeding at 12 weeks or more in an RhD-negative woman eligible for anti-D who has not yet received it · Symptoms of retained products (ongoing heavy bleeding + pain >2 weeks after miscarriage)
The safety-net for ectopic pregnancy under surveillance is arguably the most critical safety message in all of early pregnancy care — women managed expectantly (falling beta-hCG) or with methotrexate can rupture their ectopic at any point during the surveillance period, even when the beta-hCG is falling. The tubal rupture can occur without warning in a woman who appeared to be responding to conservative management. Every woman managed conservatively for ectopic must have a clear written safety-net: 'If you develop any significant pelvic pain at any time, call 999 or go to A&E immediately — do not wait to see your GP or phone the EPAU.' This instruction should be documented in the clinical record at every contact during ectopic surveillance. The GTD surveillance centre contact detail (Charing Cross Hospital GTD Centre: 020 3313 5894) is worth having in the practice system — if a GP has a patient who has had a molar pregnancy and loses contact with the surveillance programme, or if a patient presents with vaginal bleeding with a history of previous molar pregnancy, the centre can be contacted directly for advice.
