1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Neurological and Vascular Emergencies

Ask the critical question first: is the double vision monocular or binocular? Cover each eye in turn. Binocular diplopia resolves when either eye is covered — misalignment from a nerve, muscle, orbit or brainstem cause, the higher-risk group; all new binocular diplopia needs prompt specialist assessment, with urgency set by onset, examination and associated features. Monocular diplopia persists when the affected eye views the target alone and resolves when that eye is covered — usually an optical problem in that eye. Then screen for these emergencies:
Sudden severe headache + diplopia Thunderclap headache peaking within minutes (NICE NG228, 2022 describes 1–5 minutes) , with or without diplopia → 999 / immediate ED (suspected subarachnoid haemorrhage). Do not arrange outpatient MRI or rely on a normal primary-care examination
Third nerve palsy (ptosis ± dilated pupil) Dilated or poorly reactive pupil, severe headache, pain, reduced consciousness or other neurological deficit → Emergency hospital assessment via the emergency/neurovascular pathway for urgent vascular imaging (aneurysm until proven otherwise). Any acute 3rd nerve palsy, including pupil-sparing → same-day specialist/neurovascular assessment — do not assume microvascular
Horner's syndrome + neck pain New Horner's with ipsilateral neck, facial or orbital pain, recent neck trauma, manipulation or exertion → Same-day emergency vascular/neurological assessment (possible carotid dissection). Chronic painless Horner's → specialist assessment guided by age, smoking, symptoms and previous records (not automatically chest imaging)
Aged 50+ with new diplopia or other visual symptoms Consider GCA, particularly with new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication, constitutional or PMR symptoms or abnormal temporal arteries → start high-dose oral glucocorticoid now per the local Greater Manchester GCA pathway and arrange same day ophthalmology/rheumatology or acute assessment
Vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial or limb weakness, sensory loss Sudden binocular diplopia with these, severe imbalance or reduced consciousness → 999 via the local emergency stroke pathway (possible posterior circulation stroke — NICE NG128); absence of FAST signs does not exclude it
Painful ophthalmoplegia + fever / proptosis Chemosis, reduced vision, colour desaturation, RAPD or systemic illness → Same-day emergency hospital assessment; 999 if sepsis, reduced consciousness, airway compromise, rapidly progressive swelling or severe neurological deterioration (orbital cellulitis, cavernous sinus thrombosis). Distinguish from conjunctivitis, thyroid eye disease and non-infective orbital inflammation
Papilloedema, severe or progressive headache, persistent vomiting Or reduced consciousness, new focal deficit or marked ataxia; sixth nerve palsy may be a false-localising sign of raised intracranial pressure → Same-day emergency hospital assessment. A normal fundus does not exclude raised pressure
Diplopia or ptosis + bulbar or respiratory symptoms Dysphagia, dysarthria, weak cough, inability to handle secretions, neck flexor weakness, rapidly progressive generalised weakness or breathlessness → 999 / same-day emergency (possible myasthenic crisis)
Rapidly progressive, several cranial nerves Over days to weeks, or with areflexia and ataxia → Same-day neurology (Miller Fisher variant of Guillain–Barré, meningeal disease)
Diplopia after head or orbital trauma Restricted upgaze with orbital floor fracture, or a new fourth nerve palsy (severe orbital trauma or suspected orbital compartment syndrome → 999) → Same-day eye casualty / A&E
Child with a new squint or diplopia New diplopia, paralytic squint, abnormal eye movement or persistent head tilt → Urgent paediatric ophthalmology; paralytic squint → urgent neurological assessment; with headache, vomiting, ataxia or neurological signs → immediate acute paediatrics; abnormal/absent red reflex → immediate ophthalmology (NICE NG127). Adult microvascular-palsy advice does not apply
Progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function Only where emergency assessment is not needed → NICE NG12: consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (or CT if MRI contraindicated). Not for every progressive cranial-nerve palsy — new isolated diplopia or palsy goes via urgent ophthalmology, neuro-ophthalmology or neurology
An aneurysm pressing on the third nerve compresses the pupillary fibres that run on its outer surface, so a dilated pupil is a warning that the next event could be rupture. A normal pupil does not reliably exclude compression in partial, early, evolving or painful palsies. NICE NG228 (2022) treats a thunderclap headache as a red flag for subarachnoid haemorrhage: it describes a headache peaking within about 1–5 minutes, and notes that only a minority of such headaches turn out to be SAH. Giant cell arteritis can cause permanent visual loss, and the BSR 2020 guideline notes that most visual loss happens before treatment starts, so do not wait for blood results when the story fits. Posterior circulation strokes often present with diplopia and vertigo and are missed without a structured neurological examination. In an SCA case, name the emergency you are excluding and give a clear disposition: 999, same day, or urgent.

Diagnose

Monocular vs Binocular — First Diagnostic Step

This single question guides the whole pathway. Take a careful history of the images themselves before you examine.
Cover test
Cover one eye, then the other. Binocular: resolves when either eye is covered. Monocular: persists when the affected eye views the target alone and resolves when that eye is covered
Pinhole test
Monocular doubling that improves looking through a pinhole points to a refractive or corneal cause
Monocular diplopia
Uncorrected refractive error or astigmatism, cataract, dry eye, keratoconus, corneal scarring, lens displacement, macular disease → optometry/ophthalmology; urgent assessment if sudden visual loss, painful red eye, corneal opacity or trauma, proptosis, marked photophobia or neurological signs
Binocular — horizontal
Images side by side: sixth nerve palsy (worse looking towards the affected side and in the distance), convergence problems (worse for near), internuclear ophthalmoplegia, medial rectus restriction
Binocular — vertical or oblique
Images one above the other or tilted: fourth nerve palsy (worse looking down, reading and on stairs), thyroid eye disease (inferior rectus), orbital floor fracture, skew deviation (brainstem)
Binocular — any direction + ptosis
Third nerve palsy (eye "down and out", ptosis, ± dilated pupil); myasthenia (variable pattern, fatigable ptosis)
Time course
Sudden onset: vascular, microvascular or aneurysmal. Gradual and progressive: compressive lesion, thyroid eye disease. Variable through the day: myasthenia or decompensating squint
Associated symptoms
Pain around the eye (microvascular palsies can hurt, but so do aneurysms), headache, jaw claudication, gritty or bulging eyes, swallowing or speech difficulty, limb weakness, numbness
Background
Diabetes, hypertension, smoking, thyroid disease, known cancer, recent head or neck injury, previous squint or patching as a child. Ask about GCA symptoms (aged 50+), visual loss or colour desaturation, and whether the patient drives or works in a safety-critical role
Monocular diplopia is usually an optical problem in one eye. The pinhole test is a quick confirmation you can do in the room, and the patient can usually be seen by an optometrist rather than an ophthalmologist. Binocular diplopia means the two eyes are no longer aligned: a nerve, muscle, orbit or brainstem problem, so it needs a neurological and orbital assessment. Asking whether the images are side by side or one above the other, and in which direction of gaze they are furthest apart, helps describe the pattern before you examine — precise localisation is a specialist task. A childhood squint that decompensates later in life is a common, benign cause of intermittent double vision, but it should be a diagnosis of exclusion.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis by Cranial Nerve and Mechanism

Identify which nerve or muscle is affected, then look for the feature that separates the benign cause from the dangerous one.
Third nerve palsy
Eye down and out, ptosis, ± large pupil. Any acute third nerve palsy → urgent specialist/neurovascular assessment. Dilated or poorly reactive pupil, severe headache, pain, reduced consciousness or other deficit → emergency hospital assessment and urgent vascular imaging. A pupil-sparing palsy should not automatically be assumed to be microvascular
Fourth nerve palsy
Vertical diplopia, worse looking down and in; patient tilts the head away from the affected side. Causes: decompensated congenital palsy (old photographs show a head tilt), head trauma, microvascular
Sixth nerve palsy
Horizontal diplopia, worse in the distance and looking towards the affected side; eye cannot abduct. Causes: microvascular, raised intracranial pressure (false-localising), MS in younger adults, skull base tumour
Internuclear ophthalmoplegia
Adduction lag in one eye with nystagmus in the abducting eye → medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion: MS in young adults, stroke in older adults
Myasthenia gravis
Variable, fatigable ptosis and diplopia, worse later in the day and with sustained upgaze; pupils normal; may have bulbar or limb fatigue
Thyroid eye disease
Gritty, red or bulging eyes, lid retraction, restricted upgaze; can precede, follow or occur without abnormal thyroid function. Smoking worsens it
Orbital disease
Proptosis, pain, restriction in the direction of a mass or fracture; orbital cellulitis in the unwell patient
Decompensating squint
Long-standing small misalignment, often with a history of childhood squint; intermittent diplopia when tired or unwell; eye movements appear full
Giant cell arteritis
Aged 50+ with new diplopia or other visual symptoms — consider GCA, particularly with new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication, constitutional or PMR symptoms, or abnormal temporal arteries; diplopia can itself be an ischaemic symptom
An isolated sixth or fourth nerve palsy in someone over 50 with diabetes or hypertension is often microvascular, but this cannot be safely diagnosed in primary care — do not assume it because of vascular risk factors; all new binocular diplopia needs prompt specialist assessment. The same sign in a younger person, or in anyone with headache or papilloedema, may be the first sign of raised intracranial pressure or a skull base lesion. The "rule of the pupil" in third nerve palsy helps but is not absolute, because early or partial compressive palsies can spare the pupil. That is why specialist review is needed even when the pupil looks normal. Myasthenia is frequently missed because the examination can be normal when the patient is rested. Ask specifically about symptoms that are worse at the end of the day. Acetylcholine receptor antibodies are found in only about half of people with purely ocular myasthenia, compared with around 80–85% of those with generalised disease, so a negative test does not exclude it.

Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Visual acuity
Snellen chart for each eye with glasses, then with pinhole. Reduced acuity suggests an ocular cause or optic nerve involvement. Also confrontation visual fields, and colour vision if optic neuropathy is suspected
Pupils
Size in light and dark, reaction to light, relative afferent pupillary defect. Anisocoria greater in bright light = the larger pupil is abnormal (third nerve); greater in dim light = the smaller pupil is abnormal (Horner's)
Ocular motility
Test the "H" pattern and the primary position with a target held at arm's length. Ask where the images are furthest apart and describe the abnormal movement rather than over-localising — restriction may reflect cranial-nerve weakness, extraocular muscle disease, mechanical or orbital restriction, or supranuclear/internuclear disease. Look for nystagmus and adduction lag
Cover–uncover test
If competent to perform it: shows a manifest squint; the direction of the refixation movement tells you which way the eye was deviated
Eyelids
Ptosis (third nerve, Horner's, myasthenia), lid retraction and lid lag (thyroid), fatigability on sustained upgaze for about a minute (myasthenia)
Orbits
Proptosis (look from above), chemosis, periorbital swelling or redness, restricted movement with pain
Fundoscopy
Papilloedema (raised intracranial pressure), optic atrophy; a normal fundus does not exclude raised pressure
Full neurological examination
Level of consciousness; other cranial nerves including facial sensation and movement; speech and swallowing; limb power, sensation, reflexes, coordination and gait. Any additional deficit → same-day emergency assessment
Cardiovascular
BP and pulse rhythm (hypertension does not establish a microvascular cause), carotid bruit; temperature if infection is possible
Temporal arteries
Tenderness, thickening or reduced pulsation in anyone 50+ with new headache
The pupil examination can change management to emergency referral, so document it explicitly: size, symmetry and reaction. Examining eye movements with the patient telling you where the images are widest helps you describe the abnormality; precise localisation is a specialist task. A formal orthoptic assessment later measures the deviation. A full neurological examination picks up the brainstem signs that turn an "isolated" palsy into a stroke or demyelination. Temporal artery examination takes seconds and can prevent blindness from missed giant cell arteritis. Fundoscopy is worth attempting because papilloedema makes a sixth nerve palsy an emergency, but an undilated view in general practice is limited, so it cannot rule raised pressure out.

Investigations

Targeted tests only
No routine panel for all binocular diplopia. Possible microvascular palsy: BP, glucose or HbA1c + urgent specialist review. Stroke, aneurysm, dissection or orbital infection/inflammation: emergency pathway — do not delay for blood tests; imaging is secondary-care arranged. Lipids have no role in acute diplopia assessment. Monocular: optometry/ophthalmology assessment
Suspected GCA
ESR CRP FBC taken before steroids if this can be done immediately, but never delay treatment or same-day specialist assessment while awaiting results. Fast-track pathway for temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy (BSR 2020)
Suspected myasthenia
Prompt neurology/neuro-ophthalmology referral. AChR antibodies are a reasonable first-line test in a non-urgent presentation (per local arrangements) — a negative result does not exclude ocular myasthenia. MuSK/LRP4 antibodies, neurophysiology and thymic imaging are specialist-directed (ABN guidelines, Pract Neurol 2025)
Suspected thyroid eye disease
TSH, free T4 ; thyroid antibodies and orbital imaging usually specialist-led; refer even if thyroid function is normal
Urgent imaging
Do not arrange CT/MR angiography yourself — suspected pupil-involving or painful third nerve palsy goes to the emergency/neurovascular pathway, which arranges urgent vascular imaging. Do not wait for a routine MRI
Specialist-arranged MRI
Sixth nerve palsy without vascular risk factors or under 50; multiple cranial nerves; internuclear ophthalmoplegia; papilloedema; any palsy that progresses or fails to recover
Direct-access MRI (NICE NG12)
Only for progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function where emergency assessment is not required (CT if MRI contraindicated). New isolated diplopia or cranial-nerve palsy → urgent ophthalmology/neuro-ophthalmology/neurology pathway, not routine GP MRI
Not needed in primary care
Imaging for simple monocular diplopia. Routine investigation of a stable, long-standing decompensating squint beyond optometry/orthoptic assessment
ESR and CRP are needed quickly when GCA is possible in anyone over 50 with new binocular diplopia, but the BSR 2020 guideline is clear that treatment should not wait for results or biopsy when giant cell arteritis is strongly suspected. Biopsy or ultrasound should still be arranged promptly because steroids gradually reduce their yield. Vascular angiography (CT or MR) is how a compressive aneurysm is excluded. It belongs in the acute hospital pathway, not a routine outpatient request. Whether a presumed microvascular palsy in an older person with vascular risk factors needs immediate imaging varies between specialists. The safe primary care approach is to discuss or refer every new binocular diplopia and let the ophthalmologist or neurologist decide on imaging. Glucose/HbA1c and BP may be checked where a microvascular palsy is possible, but they do not establish the diagnosis; lipids belong to longer-term cardiovascular risk management.

Refer

Referral — Urgency by Diagnosis

Emergency / 999
Thunderclap headache or suspected SAH; acute focal neurological signs or suspected stroke; reduced consciousness; pupil-involving or painful third nerve palsy; suspected carotid dissection; suspected myasthenic crisis; painful proptosis with fever, reduced vision or systemic illness; papilloedema or severe raised-intracranial-pressure symptoms; severe orbital trauma or suspected orbital compartment syndrome
Same day (emergency)
Same-day hospital/eye/neurovascular assessment: any new third nerve palsy; acute painful binocular diplopia; new diplopia with ptosis or Horner's; suspected GCA with diplopia or other visual symptoms (start steroids first); progressive diplopia; multiple cranial nerve involvement; new diplopia after trauma; suspected orbital cellulitis or orbital inflammatory disease; significant uncertainty after examination
Urgent specialist (eye / neuro-ophthalmology / neurology)
New isolated binocular diplopia without emergency features; suspected fourth or sixth nerve palsy; suspected ocular myasthenia without bulbar or respiratory compromise; internuclear ophthalmoplegia; suspected thyroid eye disease with diplopia (thyroid eye clinic); persistent or recurrent unexplained diplopia — route per local pathway
NICE NG12
Progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function without emergency features → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (not for isolated diplopia or cranial-nerve palsy)
Optometrist
Monocular diplopia without red flags; suspected refractive, corneal, lens or macular cause; stable long-standing decompensated squint after urgent causes have been excluded
Orthoptist (via eye service)
Stable diplopia needing measurement, Fresnel prisms or occlusion advice
For giant cell arteritis with ischaemic or visual features, start high-dose oral glucocorticoid straight away per the local Greater Manchester GCA pathway; IV treatment is considered only through the specialist/acute pathway. Oral treatment should not be delayed while IV is arranged. Same-day specialist assessment is needed because the other eye is at risk. Many areas now route new binocular diplopia through an urgent eye clinic or neuro-ophthalmology service rather than general neurology, so know your local pathway. A new palsy should not simply be watched for 12 weeks in primary care without specialist contact.

Treat

GP Management — Microvascular Palsy and Bridging Care

Definitive treatment is usually specialist-led. The GP's role is to relieve symptoms once urgent causes have been considered, give driving and safety advice, and review risk factors after specialist confirmation.
Binocular diplopia (symptom relief)
Occlusion / prism
Temporary occlusion may relieve troublesome binocular diplopia while awaiting assessment — a patch or translucent tape in adults; it reduces binocular depth perception and does not treat the cause. Fresnel prisms are prescribed or fitted by orthoptics/ophthalmology. Lack of improvement, progression or any new sign → re-discuss with the specialist.
Suspected GCA
Start glucocorticoids now
Start high-dose oral glucocorticoid immediately using the local Greater Manchester GCA pathway. New visual loss, amaurosis fugax, diplopia or other ischaemic features → same-day ophthalmology/rheumatology or acute medical assessment; IV treatment only via the specialist/acute pathway. Taper is individualised and commonly prolonged (NICE materials describe about 18–24 months). Consider bone protection and gastroprotection.
Vascular risk factors
After specialist confirmation
If a specialist confirms a presumed microvascular cranial-nerve palsy, review diabetes, hypertension, smoking, lipid management and other cardiovascular risk factors according to the patient's existing disease-specific guidance. Targets (e.g. HbA1c) are individualised by age, treatment, comorbidity and hypoglycaemia risk.
Thyroid eye disease
Stop smoking + refer
Restore normal thyroid function, stop smoking, lubricants for exposure symptoms. Specialist treatment (steroids, selenium, surgery) is decided in the thyroid eye clinic.
Driving (statutory)DVLA People with diplopia must tell DVLA and must not drive. Group 1 driving can usually resume only once DVLA accepts the diplopia is controlled, for example by glasses or a patch the driver undertakes to wear. Patching is not acceptable for Group 2 licensing. Record the advice given (GOV.UK: Visual disorders — assessing fitness to drive)
Work and safetyAvoid working at height or with machinery, and cycling in traffic, if vision is unsafe. Issue a fit note if needed; notify occupational health where driving or visual safety is an essential job function. Document the advice
ChildrenDo not patch or prescribe prolonged occlusion in a child without orthoptic/ophthalmology advice. Occlusion in young children can cause amblyopia, and new diplopia in a child needs urgent specialist assessment anyway
Covering one eye removes double vision immediately. Patients are often relieved to be told this is safe in adults and does not harm the covered eye. The driving rules are a legal duty: GOV.UK states that drivers must tell DVLA about diplopia and can be fined up to £1,000 if they don't, and GPs should advise and document. GCA glucocorticoid dosing follows the local Greater Manchester GCA pathway. Vascular risk review after specialist confirmation follows the patient's existing disease-specific guidance.

Lifestyle

Patient Advice and Risk Factor Management

Driving Stop driving now and tell DVLA. Explain that this protects the patient and others, and that many people return to driving once the diplopia is resolved or controlled
Cardiovascular risk (after specialist confirmation) If a microvascular palsy is confirmed, review BP, diabetes, smoking and lipids per the patient's existing disease-specific guidance
Diabetes Agree an individual HbA1c target (NICE NG28); keep up diabetic eye screening; review medicines if hypoglycaemia is a risk while vision is impaired
Smoking cessation Offer referral to a stop-smoking service with behavioural support plus medication or a vaping option (NICE NG209, 2021). Smoking worsens thyroid eye disease and vascular risk
Occlusion technique Adults: patch or tape the glasses lens of whichever eye gives the most comfort; alternating is optional. Children: only on specialist advice
Falls and home safety Double vision and sudden monocular vision both affect depth perception. Take extra care on stairs and kerbs, remove trip hazards and use handrails; consider a falls assessment in older adults (NICE NG249, 2025)
Screen time and reading Near tasks may be worse with fourth nerve and convergence problems. Frequent breaks and good lighting help while waiting for recovery
Myasthenia Once diagnosed, follow specialist advice on medicines that may exacerbate myasthenia (check before prescribing) and carry an alert card; seek urgent help for swallowing, speech or breathing difficulty
Microvascular cranial nerve palsies share their risk factors with stroke and heart disease, so the consultation is an opportunity for cardiovascular prevention as well as symptom relief. Patients often underestimate how much double vision affects everyday tasks like stairs, pouring drinks and crossing roads. Practical safety advice and a falls assessment for older people are part of good care. In an SCA consultation, the driving conversation is often the hardest part. Explain the reason, acknowledge the impact on independence, and agree a plan for getting around in the meantime.

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Early review
Confirm specialist assessment has occurred within the timeframe specified by the referral pathway. Is the diplopia improving, stable or worse? Any new symptoms? Driving advice understood?
Escalate urgently
Diplopia worsens or becomes painful, ptosis or a pupil abnormality develops, another cranial nerve becomes involved, or any neurological, visual or systemic feature appears → same-day discussion/assessment
Not improving / atypical
Lack of improvement, progression or an atypical examination → re-discuss with ophthalmology/neuro-ophthalmology or neurology and consider imaging — do not wait three months if the diagnosis is uncertain or the course atypical. Follow-up timing is set by the specialist pathway
GCA
Shared care of an individualised, specialist-led taper (commonly prolonged); monitor symptoms, ESR/CRP, glucose, BP and bone health; relapse needs prompt review
999 safety-net
Sudden severe headache, a drooping eyelid with a large pupil, weakness, slurred speech, face drooping, difficulty swallowing or breathing, collapse
Same day
Any loss of vision, the double vision getting worse, a second nerve becoming involved, new eye pain or bulging, new numbness of the face
Safety-net script"Double vision like this needs checking by the eye or neurology team, who will work out the cause — sometimes it is a small blood vessel problem that settles, but we must not assume that. If you get a sudden severe headache, your eyelid drops or your pupil looks bigger, or you notice weakness, slurred speech or trouble swallowing, call 999. Please don't drive until DVLA says you can."
DocumentationMonocular or binocular; pupil findings; eye movement findings; red flags asked about; referral urgency; DVLA advice given
The key follow-up principle is that a confirmed microvascular palsy should improve, not worsen, and follow-up timing is set by the specialist pathway. Progression, a new nerve involved or a change in the pupil suggests a compressive or inflammatory cause that needs urgent imaging, even if the first assessment was reassuring. Giant cell arteritis needs long-term shared care because relapses are common during tapering, and both the disease and its treatment carry risks. Written or recorded safety-net advice with named symptoms is easier for patients to act on than a general instruction to return if worse. It is also the part of the record that matters most if things go wrong.
