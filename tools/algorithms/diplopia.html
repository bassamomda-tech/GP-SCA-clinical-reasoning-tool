1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Neurological and Vascular Emergencies

Ask the critical question first: is the double vision monocular or binocular? Cover each eye in turn. If the doubling persists with one eye covered, it is monocular (an optical problem in that eye). If it disappears with either eye covered, it is binocular (misalignment from a nerve, muscle, orbit or brainstem cause), which is the higher-risk group. Then screen for these emergencies:
Sudden severe headache + diplopia Thunderclap headache peaking within minutes (NICE NG228, 2022 describes 1–5 minutes) with a third nerve palsy → 999 (aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage)
Ptosis + dilated pupil Third nerve palsy with pupil involvement, painful or not → Emergency same day for CT or MR angiography: compressive aneurysm (usually posterior communicating artery) until proven otherwise
Horner's syndrome + neck pain Ptosis + small pupil after neck trauma or with neck or face pain → Same day (carotid dissection); chronic Horner's with smoking history → consider apical lung tumour
Aged 50+ with new headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness Diplopia can be an ischaemic feature of giant cell arteritis → start glucocorticoids and refer same day (BSR 2020)
Vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria, facial or limb weakness Diplopia with other brainstem signs → 999 (posterior circulation stroke — NICE NG128, 2019)
Painful proptosis + fever Red swollen lids, chemosis, painful or restricted eye movements → 999 / same day (orbital cellulitis, cavernous sinus thrombosis)
Headache worse lying down, vomiting, papilloedema Sixth nerve palsy as a false-localising sign of raised intracranial pressure → Same day imaging
Fatigable diplopia + bulbar or breathing symptoms Swallowing difficulty, slurred speech, breathlessness lying flat → 999 (myasthenic crisis)
Rapidly progressive, several cranial nerves Over days to weeks, or with areflexia and ataxia → Same-day neurology (Miller Fisher variant of Guillain–Barré, meningeal disease)
Diplopia after head or orbital trauma Restricted upgaze with orbital floor fracture, or a new fourth nerve palsy → Same-day eye casualty / A&E
Child with a new squint or diplopia New strabismus, abnormal eye movements or head tilt → Urgent ophthalmology / paediatrics; with headache, vomiting or unsteadiness → same day
Progressive, sub-acute neurological loss Gradually worsening cranial nerve deficit over weeks → NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (within 2 weeks) for CNS cancer
An aneurysm pressing on the third nerve compresses the pupillary fibres that run on its outer surface, so a dilated pupil is a warning that the next event could be rupture. NICE NG228 (2022) treats a thunderclap headache as a red flag for subarachnoid haemorrhage: it describes a headache peaking within about 1–5 minutes, and notes that only a minority of such headaches turn out to be SAH. Giant cell arteritis can cause permanent visual loss, and the BSR 2020 guideline notes that most visual loss happens before treatment starts, so do not wait for blood results when the story fits. Posterior circulation strokes often present with diplopia and vertigo and are missed without a structured neurological examination. In an SCA case, name the emergency you are excluding and give a clear disposition: 999, same day, or urgent.

Diagnose

Monocular vs Binocular — First Diagnostic Step

This single question guides the whole pathway. Take a careful history of the images themselves before you examine.
Cover test
Cover one eye, then the other. Doubling persists = monocular; disappears with either eye covered = binocular
Pinhole test
Monocular doubling that improves looking through a pinhole points to a refractive or corneal cause
Monocular diplopia
Uncorrected refractive error or astigmatism, cataract, dry eye, keratoconus, corneal scarring, lens displacement, macular disease → optometrist first; eye casualty only if painful, red or with sudden visual loss
Binocular — horizontal
Images side by side: sixth nerve palsy (worse looking towards the affected side and in the distance), convergence problems (worse for near), internuclear ophthalmoplegia, medial rectus restriction
Binocular — vertical or oblique
Images one above the other or tilted: fourth nerve palsy (worse looking down, reading and on stairs), thyroid eye disease (inferior rectus), orbital floor fracture, skew deviation (brainstem)
Binocular — any direction + ptosis
Third nerve palsy (eye "down and out", ptosis, ± dilated pupil); myasthenia (variable pattern, fatigable ptosis)
Time course
Sudden onset: vascular, microvascular or aneurysmal. Gradual and progressive: compressive lesion, thyroid eye disease. Variable through the day: myasthenia or decompensating squint
Associated symptoms
Pain around the eye (microvascular palsies can hurt, but so do aneurysms), headache, jaw claudication, gritty or bulging eyes, swallowing or speech difficulty, limb weakness, numbness
Background
Diabetes, hypertension, smoking, thyroid disease, known cancer, recent head injury, previous squint or patching as a child
Monocular diplopia is almost always an optical problem in one eye. The pinhole test is a quick confirmation you can do in the room, and the patient can usually be seen by an optometrist rather than an ophthalmologist. Binocular diplopia means the two eyes are no longer aligned: a nerve, muscle, orbit or brainstem problem, so it needs a neurological and orbital assessment. Asking whether the images are side by side or one above the other, and in which direction of gaze they are furthest apart, localises the weak muscle before you touch the patient. A childhood squint that decompensates later in life is a common, benign cause of intermittent double vision, but it should be a diagnosis of exclusion.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis by Cranial Nerve and Mechanism

Identify which nerve or muscle is affected, then look for the feature that separates the benign cause from the dangerous one.
Third nerve palsy
Eye down and out, ptosis, ± large pupil. Pupil involved or partial with other signs → compressive until imaged. Complete palsy with a normal pupil in an older person with vascular risk factors → often microvascular, but still needs specialist review and close follow-up
Fourth nerve palsy
Vertical diplopia, worse looking down and in; patient tilts the head away from the affected side. Causes: decompensated congenital palsy (old photographs show a head tilt), head trauma, microvascular
Sixth nerve palsy
Horizontal diplopia, worse in the distance and looking towards the affected side; eye cannot abduct. Causes: microvascular, raised intracranial pressure (false-localising), MS in younger adults, skull base tumour
Internuclear ophthalmoplegia
Adduction lag in one eye with nystagmus in the abducting eye → medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion: MS in young adults, stroke in older adults
Myasthenia gravis
Variable, fatigable ptosis and diplopia, worse later in the day and with sustained upgaze; pupils normal; may have bulbar or limb fatigue. Ice-pack test may improve ptosis
Thyroid eye disease
Gritty, red or bulging eyes, lid retraction, restricted upgaze; can precede, follow or occur without abnormal thyroid function. Smoking worsens it
Orbital disease
Proptosis, pain, restriction in the direction of a mass or fracture; orbital cellulitis in the unwell patient
Decompensating squint
Long-standing small misalignment, often with a history of childhood squint; intermittent diplopia when tired or unwell; eye movements appear full
Giant cell arteritis
Aged 50+, new headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, systemic upset; diplopia from ischaemia of nerves or muscles
An isolated sixth or fourth nerve palsy in someone over 50 with diabetes or hypertension is usually microvascular and tends to recover over weeks to a few months. The same sign in a younger person, or in anyone with headache or papilloedema, may be the first sign of raised intracranial pressure or a skull base lesion. The "rule of the pupil" in third nerve palsy helps but is not absolute, because early or partial compressive palsies can spare the pupil. That is why specialist review is needed even when the pupil looks normal. Myasthenia is frequently missed because the examination can be normal when the patient is rested. Ask specifically about symptoms that are worse at the end of the day. Acetylcholine receptor antibodies are found in only about half of people with purely ocular myasthenia, compared with around 80–85% of those with generalised disease, so a negative test does not exclude it.

Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Visual acuity
Snellen chart for each eye with glasses, then with pinhole. Reduced acuity suggests an ocular cause or optic nerve involvement
Pupils
Size in light and dark, reaction to light, relative afferent pupillary defect. Anisocoria greater in bright light = the larger pupil is abnormal (third nerve); greater in dim light = the smaller pupil is abnormal (Horner's)
Ocular motility
Test the "H" pattern and the primary position with a target held at arm's length. Ask where the images are furthest apart; the eye that fails to move in that direction is the weak one. Look for nystagmus and adduction lag
Cover–uncover test
Shows a manifest squint; the direction of the refixation movement tells you which way the eye was deviated
Eyelids
Ptosis (third nerve, Horner's, myasthenia), lid retraction and lid lag (thyroid), fatigability on sustained upgaze for about a minute (myasthenia)
Orbits
Proptosis (look from above), chemosis, periorbital swelling or redness, restricted movement with pain
Fundoscopy
Papilloedema (raised intracranial pressure), optic atrophy; a normal fundus does not exclude raised pressure
Full neurological examination
Other cranial nerves (especially V and VII), limb power, reflexes, coordination and gait, speech and swallow. Any additional deficit → urgent imaging
Cardiovascular
BP (hypertension supports a microvascular cause but doesn't exclude others), pulse rhythm, carotid bruit
Temporal arteries
Tenderness, thickening or reduced pulsation in anyone 50+ with new headache
The pupil examination changes management from watchful waiting to emergency imaging, so document it explicitly: size, symmetry and reaction. Examining eye movements with the patient telling you where the images are widest localises the weak muscle in a few seconds. A formal orthoptic assessment later measures the deviation. A full neurological examination picks up the brainstem signs that turn an "isolated" palsy into a stroke or demyelination. Temporal artery examination takes seconds and can prevent blindness from missed giant cell arteritis. Fundoscopy is worth attempting because papilloedema makes a sixth nerve palsy an emergency, but an undilated view in general practice is limited, so it cannot rule raised pressure out.

Investigations

Bloods — binocular diplopia
FBC ESR / CRP (aged 50+) HbA1c or glucose TFTs Lipids — plus BP
Suspected GCA
ESR CRP FBC taken before glucocorticoids if possible, but do not delay treatment for results. Fast-track pathway for temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy (BSR 2020)
Suspected myasthenia
AChR antibodies; MuSK antibodies if negative (usually requested by neurology). Once diagnosed, CT of the chest for thymoma is a specialist investigation (ABN myasthenia guidelines, Pract Neurol 2015)
Suspected thyroid eye disease
TSH, free T4 ± TSH receptor antibodies; refer even if thyroid function is normal
Urgent imaging
Pupil-involving or partial third nerve palsy → CT angiography or MR angiography the same day, arranged through the emergency or specialist team. Do not wait for a routine MRI
Specialist-arranged MRI
Sixth nerve palsy without vascular risk factors or under 50; multiple cranial nerves; internuclear ophthalmoplegia; papilloedema; any palsy that progresses or fails to recover
Direct-access MRI (NICE NG12, updated April 2026)
Consider an urgent direct-access MRI brain (within 2 weeks) for progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function where cancer is a concern
Not needed in primary care
Imaging for simple monocular diplopia. Routine investigation of a stable, long-standing decompensating squint beyond optometry/orthoptic assessment
ESR and CRP are needed quickly in anyone over 50 with new binocular diplopia, but the BSR 2020 guideline is clear that treatment should not wait for results or biopsy when giant cell arteritis is strongly suspected. Biopsy or ultrasound should still be arranged promptly because steroids gradually reduce their yield. Vascular angiography (CT or MR) is how a compressive aneurysm is excluded. It belongs in the acute hospital pathway, not a routine outpatient request. Whether a presumed microvascular palsy in an older person with vascular risk factors needs immediate imaging varies between specialists. The safe primary care approach is to discuss or refer every new binocular diplopia and let the ophthalmologist or neurologist decide on imaging. Glucose, HbA1c, lipids and BP are still worth checking, because they support the microvascular diagnosis and treating them reduces future vascular events.

Refer

Referral — Urgency by Diagnosis

999
Thunderclap headache; stroke signs; myasthenic crisis (breathing or swallowing difficulty); orbital cellulitis or cavernous sinus thrombosis with systemic illness
Same day (emergency)
Pupil-involving or painful third nerve palsy (angiography); Horner's with neck pain; papilloedema; diplopia after trauma; suspected GCA with visual symptoms (start steroids first); rapidly progressive or multiple cranial nerve palsies
Urgent — days (neurology / neuro-ophthalmology)
Suspected myasthenia without bulbar or breathing symptoms; internuclear ophthalmoplegia; sixth nerve palsy in anyone under 50 or without vascular risk factors
Urgent — eye service
New isolated presumed microvascular fourth or sixth nerve palsy, or pupil-sparing complete third nerve palsy, per local pathway; thyroid eye disease with diplopia (thyroid eye clinic)
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)
Progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain within 2 weeks
Optometrist
Monocular diplopia without pain or redness; intermittent diplopia with a known childhood squint and full eye movements
Orthoptist (via eye service)
Stable diplopia needing measurement, Fresnel prisms or occlusion advice
For giant cell arteritis with ischaemic or visual features, the BSR 2020 guideline supports high-dose glucocorticoids straight away: oral prednisolone, or IV methylprednisolone 500 mg–1 g daily for up to 3 days where there is acute or intermittent visual loss, given in hospital. Oral treatment should not be delayed while IV is arranged. Same-day specialist assessment is needed because the other eye is at risk. Many areas now route new binocular diplopia through an urgent eye clinic or neuro-ophthalmology service rather than general neurology, so know your local pathway. A new palsy should not simply be watched for 12 weeks in primary care without specialist contact.

Treat

GP Management — Microvascular Palsy and Bridging Care

Definitive treatment is usually specialist-led. The GP's role is to relieve symptoms, manage vascular risk and give the driving advice.
Presumed microvascular palsy (after specialist review)
Occlusion / prism
Frosted tape or an occluder on one spectacle lens, or a Fresnel prism from the orthoptist. Most recover over weeks to a few months; lack of improvement or any new sign means re-referral.
Suspected GCA
Start glucocorticoids now
BSR 2020: prednisolone 40–60 mg daily for uncomplicated GCA; with visual or ischaemic features, same-day specialist care and consideration of IV methylprednisolone. Taper over about 12–18 months if in remission. Consider bone protection and gastroprotection.
Vascular risk factors
Treat to guideline targets
BP: clinic target below 140/90 mmHg under 80, below 150/90 at 80+ (NICE NG136, 2019, updated 2023). HbA1c: individualised, typically 48–53 mmol/mol (NICE NG28). Statin: atorvastatin 20 mg for primary prevention if QRISK3 ≥10% (NICE NG238, 2023).
Thyroid eye disease
Stop smoking + refer
Restore normal thyroid function, stop smoking, lubricants for exposure symptoms. Specialist treatment (steroids, selenium, surgery) is decided in the thyroid eye clinic.
Driving (statutory)DVLA People with diplopia must tell DVLA and must not drive. Group 1 driving can usually resume only once DVLA accepts the diplopia is controlled, for example by glasses or a patch the driver undertakes to wear. Group 2 standards are stricter. Record the advice given
Work and safetyConsider work at heights, with machinery or driving for work. Issue a fit note if needed; occupational health for safety-critical roles
ChildrenNever patch a child's eye without orthoptic advice. Occlusion in young children can cause amblyopia, and new diplopia in a child needs urgent specialist assessment anyway
Covering one eye removes double vision immediately. Patients are often relieved to be told this is safe in adults and does not harm the covered eye. The driving rules are a legal duty: GOV.UK states that drivers must tell DVLA about diplopia and can be fined up to £1,000 if they don't, and GPs should advise and document. The BSR 2020 guideline sets a starting dose range of 40–60 mg prednisolone for uncomplicated giant cell arteritis. Vascular risk management matters beyond the palsy itself: the same risk factors predict stroke and heart attack. BP, HbA1c and statin targets on this page come from NICE NG136, NG28 and NG238, not from a generic "tighter is better" rule.

Lifestyle

Patient Advice and Risk Factor Management

Driving Stop driving now and tell DVLA. Explain that this protects the patient and others, and that many people return to driving once the diplopia is resolved or controlled
Blood pressure Home BP monitoring where helpful; lower salt, keep alcohol within 14 units a week, stay active and manage weight (NICE NG136)
Diabetes Agree an individual HbA1c target (NICE NG28); keep up diabetic eye screening; review medicines if hypoglycaemia is a risk while vision is impaired
Smoking cessation Offer referral to a stop-smoking service with behavioural support plus medication or a vaping option (NICE NG209, 2021). Smoking worsens thyroid eye disease and vascular risk
Occlusion technique Adults: patch or tape the glasses lens of whichever eye gives the most comfort; alternating is optional. Children: only on specialist advice
Falls and home safety Double vision and sudden monocular vision both affect depth perception. Take extra care on stairs and kerbs, remove trip hazards and use handrails; consider a falls assessment in older adults (NICE NG249, 2025)
Screen time and reading Near tasks may be worse with fourth nerve and convergence problems. Frequent breaks and good lighting help while waiting for recovery
Myasthenia Carry an alert card once diagnosed; some medicines can worsen weakness, so pharmacists and prescribers need to know
Microvascular cranial nerve palsies share their risk factors with stroke and heart disease, so the consultation is an opportunity for cardiovascular prevention as well as symptom relief. Patients often underestimate how much double vision affects everyday tasks like stairs, pouring drinks and crossing roads. Practical safety advice and a falls assessment for older people are part of good care. In an SCA consultation, the driving conversation is often the hardest part. Explain the reason, acknowledge the impact on independence, and agree a plan for getting around in the meantime.

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Within 1–2 weeks
Is the diplopia improving, stable or worse? Any new symptoms? Specialist appointment happened? Driving advice understood? BP and glucose results actioned
4–6 weeks
A microvascular palsy should be stable or improving. Worsening or a new sign → same-day discussion with the eye or neurology team
About 3 months
Not recovering → the specialist will usually image (if not done) and arrange orthoptic assessment, prisms or strabismus surgery later
GCA
Shared care of the steroid taper (about 12–18 months per BSR 2020); monitor symptoms, ESR/CRP, glucose, BP and bone health; relapse needs prompt review
999 safety-net
Sudden severe headache, a drooping eyelid with a large pupil, weakness, slurred speech, face drooping, difficulty swallowing or breathing, collapse
Same day
Any loss of vision, the double vision getting worse, a second nerve becoming involved, new eye pain or bulging, new numbness of the face
Safety-net script"We think this is caused by a small blood vessel problem affecting one of the eye-movement nerves, and most of these get better on their own over a few weeks to months. The eye team will confirm that. If you get a sudden severe headache, your eyelid drops or your pupil looks bigger, or you notice weakness, slurred speech or trouble swallowing, call 999. Please don't drive until DVLA says you can."
DocumentationMonocular or binocular; pupil findings; eye movement findings; red flags asked about; referral urgency; DVLA advice given
The key follow-up principle is that a microvascular palsy should improve, not worsen. Progression, a new nerve involved or a change in the pupil suggests a compressive or inflammatory cause that needs urgent imaging, even if the first assessment was reassuring. Giant cell arteritis needs long-term shared care because relapses are common during tapering, and both the disease and its treatment carry risks. Written or recorded safety-net advice with named symptoms is easier for patients to act on than a general instruction to return if worse. It is also the part of the record that matters most if things go wrong.
