1
Safety

Red Flags — Immediate Risk and Organic Emergencies

Always prioritise: Is the patient (and everyone else) safe right now? Then: is there an organic cause? Check glucose, observations and conscious level before assuming a psychiatric illness.
Command hallucinations to harm Voices telling the person to hurt themselves or others, especially with intent, a plan or distress about resisting → same-day crisis team or duty psychiatrist; 999 if imminent danger. Do not leave alone
Fever + hallucinations or confusion Encephalitis (viral or autoimmune) or meningitis — headache, neck stiffness, seizures, behaviour change → 999. Hospital will start IV aciclovir empirically if viral encephalitis is possible
Alcohol withdrawal Alcoholic hallucinosis (clear consciousness) or delirium tremens (confusion, tremor, sweating, tachycardia) → same-day admission: seizure risk, benzodiazepine regimen and parenteral thiamine (NICE CG100)
Visual hallucinations + new confusion Delirium — acute, fluctuating inattention → find and treat the cause; 4AT and the Delirium pathway
Recently started or changed medicine Levodopa and dopamine agonists, anticholinergics, opioids, steroids, mefloquine, aciclovir in renal impairment → urgent medication review
Severe agitation Unable to assess safely → withdraw, call 999 and request police support if there is immediate risk
Head injury Hallucinations or behaviour change after a head injury, particularly on an anticoagulant → same-day ED for CT head (NICE NG232, 2023)
Parkinson's or Lewy body disease New hallucinations may reflect medication, infection or disease progression → review drugs with the Parkinson's team; antipsychotics can cause severe sensitivity reactions (NICE NG97, 2018)
NG12 Progressive neurological loss Hallucinations (often olfactory or visual) with progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function — personality change, weakness, visual field loss, new seizures → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), rec 1.9.1)
Hallucinations are a symptom with a wide differential, and the dangerous causes sit on both sides of it: acute psychiatric risk (command hallucinations, suicidal or violent intent) and acute medical illness (encephalitis, delirium, withdrawal, intracranial lesions). Herpes simplex encephalitis carries a high mortality and outcome depends on starting aciclovir early, so fever with new psychiatric symptoms is a hospital problem. Alcohol withdrawal states can progress to seizures and delirium tremens. People with Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies can react severely to antipsychotics, which is why their hallucinations are managed with specialist advice rather than a standard antipsychotic prescription.
2
Diagnose

Characterise the Hallucination — Modality and Context

The modality is the most useful first question, followed by insight, timing and associated features. A hallucination is a perception without a stimulus; an illusion is a misperception of a real one.
Auditory
Voices talking to the person (second person), about them (third person, running commentary) or giving commands. Third-person voices and running commentary suggest schizophrenia; derogatory second-person voices fit psychotic depression; musical hallucinations are typical of older people with hearing loss
Visual
Complex (people, animals, faces) → Lewy body dementia, Parkinson's medication, Charles Bonnet syndrome, delirium, drugs. Simple (flashes, zigzags, shapes) → migraine aura, occipital seizures, retinal problems (new flashes and floaters need same-day eye assessment for retinal tear)
Tactile
Crawling under the skin (formication) → stimulants, alcohol withdrawal. Sexual or bodily interference with a delusional explanation → psychosis
Olfactory / gustatory
Brief stereotyped unpleasant smell or taste with déjà vu or a rising epigastric feeling → focal (temporal lobe) seizures. These modalities point towards an organic cause
Insight
Knows the experiences are not real (Charles Bonnet, hypnagogic, migraine) → lower acute risk. Believes them and acts on them (psychosis, delirium) → higher urgency
Timing
Only when falling asleep or waking (hypnagogic, hypnopompic) · only during substance use or withdrawal · evening worsening (delirium, Lewy body) · continuous through the day (psychosis)
Associated
Delusions, thought disorder, mood change, cognitive decline, parkinsonism, visual or hearing loss, headache, seizures, weight loss
Modality narrows the field quickly. Visual hallucinations in an older person are organic until proven otherwise, and in someone with poor vision, intact cognition and full insight Charles Bonnet syndrome is the likely explanation — but only after delirium, drugs and early Lewy body disease have been considered. Olfactory and gustatory hallucinations should prompt thought about focal seizures and structural lesions. Third-person voices and running commentary are classic features of schizophrenia. Hallucinations on falling asleep or waking are common in healthy people and, on their own, need explanation rather than investigation. Asking about insight and whether the person has acted on the experiences is also a risk question.
3
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis by Cause

Psychiatric
Schizophrenia and other primary psychoses (mainly auditory, with delusions), psychotic depression (mood-congruent voices of guilt or worthlessness), mania, severe PTSD (intrusive re-experiencing — distinguish from true hallucinations)
Neurological
Dementia with Lewy bodies (well-formed visual hallucinations, fluctuating cognition, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder), Parkinson's disease (medication-related, often at night), focal seizures, migraine aura, brain tumour, stroke, narcolepsy (sleep-onset hallucinations with daytime sleepiness and cataplexy)
Substance-related
Intoxication: cannabis (especially high-potency), cocaine, amphetamines, LSD, psilocybin, ketamine. Withdrawal: alcohol, benzodiazepines. Heavy alcohol use: Wernicke's encephalopathy
Delirium / metabolic
Infection, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hepatic or uraemic encephalopathy, thyroid disease
Drug-induced
Levodopa, dopamine agonists, amantadine, anticholinergics, opioids, corticosteroids, mefloquine, aciclovir or valaciclovir with renal impairment
Sensory / benign
Charles Bonnet syndrome (sight loss, formed images, insight retained), musical hallucinations (hearing loss), hypnagogic and hypnopompic experiences, bereavement (sensing, seeing or hearing the person who died)
Dementia with Lewy bodies deserves special attention because the diagnosis changes prescribing: NICE NG97 (2018) recommends donepezil or rivastigmine for mild to moderate disease and warns that antipsychotics can worsen motor features and cause severe sensitivity reactions. Bereavement experiences are common and usually comforting; they should be normalised, not medicalised, unless there are other features of psychosis or complicated grief. PTSD flashbacks are vivid re-experiencing of trauma, not new perceptions, and respond to trauma-focused psychological treatment. Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis typically presents in young adults, more often women, with psychiatric symptoms followed by seizures, dyskinesia or reduced consciousness; some cases are associated with ovarian teratoma, and it is treatable.
4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Mental state exam
Appearance, behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content (delusions, suicidal or violent ideas), perception, cognition, insight. Record verbatim what the voices say
4AT
Alertness (clearly abnormal = 4) + AMT4 (0 / 1 / 2) + months backwards (0 / 1 / 2) + acute change (yes = 4). ≥4 possible delirium ± cognitive impairment → Delirium pathway
Cognition
If gradual decline: structured test (6CIT, MoCA). Attention and visuospatial problems out of proportion to memory suggest Lewy body disease
Neurological
Parkinsonism (bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor), focal signs, visual fields, fundi (papilloedema), gait, nystagmus and ataxia (Wernicke's)
Observations
Temperature, pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, capillary glucose
Withdrawal signs
Tremor, sweating, tachycardia, agitation. A structured withdrawal scale (for example CIWA-Ar) supports assessment; confusion with ataxia or eye-movement signs → parenteral thiamine and admission (NICE CG100)
Vision and hearing
Visual acuity each eye, known eye disease (macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataract, diabetic retinopathy), hearing and wax
The mental state examination separates primary psychosis (clear consciousness, orientated, delusions) from delirium (fluctuating attention, disorientation) and from sensory syndromes (insight, normal cognition). The 4AT had a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.88 in a 2021 meta-analysis (Tieges et al., Age and Ageing), and uses the same scoring on the Delirium and Confusion algorithms. Wernicke's encephalopathy seldom shows the complete triad of confusion, ataxia and eye signs, so any of these in a heavy drinker or malnourished person warrants parenteral thiamine; NICE CG100 advises continuing it for at least 5 days unless Wernicke's is excluded. Parkinsonism in a person with visual hallucinations should raise Lewy body disease before any antipsychotic is considered.
5
Diagnose

Investigations

Bloods — new presentations
FBC U&E Calcium LFTs TFTs CRP Glucose B12 / folate; add syphilis and HIV serology where there are risk factors or atypical features
Urine drug screen
Useful in new-onset hallucinations, with consent. A positive result does not exclude a coexisting primary psychosis; a negative one does not exclude synthetic cannabinoids or other novel substances
Suspected encephalitis
Hospital work-up: MRI, LP, EEG, autoimmune antibodies (including anti-NMDA receptor). Arranged through emergency or neurology services, not from primary care
Suspected seizures
Stereotyped episodes with olfactory or gustatory aura, déjà vu or altered awareness → refer to a first-seizure or neurology clinic; EEG and MRI are arranged there. Advise not to drive until assessed (DVLA)
Neuroimaging
CT head same day for head injury or acute focal signs. NG12 progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (NG12 rec 1.9.1)
Not routinely
EEG, MRI or antibody screens in a straightforward first episode of psychosis without neurological features — the EIP team decides on further tests after assessment
Blood tests find the silent metabolic causes — hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hepatic and renal failure, thyroid disease and B12 deficiency — and give a baseline if an antipsychotic is later started. A drug screen informs the conversation about substance use but does not settle the diagnosis, because substance use and primary psychosis often coexist. Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is worth knowing because it presents first to GPs and psychiatrists and is treatable with immunotherapy; seizures, abnormal movements, autonomic instability or reduced consciousness in a young person with new psychosis should prompt emergency neurology input. Structural imaging is for focal, progressive or atypical presentations rather than routine for every hallucination.
6
Refer

Referral Pathway

999
Command hallucinations with intent and imminent risk, encephalitis or delirium with unsafe behaviour, withdrawal seizures, reduced consciousness, suspected Wernicke's
Same-day
New delirium without a clear treatable cause, alcohol withdrawal hallucinosis, suspected encephalitis, focal neurology, head injury, crisis team for high-risk psychotic presentations
Urgent — EIP
First presentation of psychotic hallucinations with no organic cause → Early Intervention in Psychosis, open to all ages with a first episode (NICE CG178, 2014). Treatment should start within 2 weeks of referral (NICE QS80, 2015). Under 18: urgent specialist CAMHS or EIP (from age 14) referral (NICE CG155, 2013, updated 2016)
NG12
Progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (NG12 rec 1.9.1)
Neurology
Suspected focal seizures, suspected Lewy body disease with prominent parkinsonism, narcolepsy, post-encephalitis follow-up
Old-age psychiatry / memory service
Dementia with hallucinations, suspected Lewy body dementia → specialist assessment before any antipsychotic
Ophthalmology / optometry
Charles Bonnet syndrome with untreated or changing sight loss; new flashes, floaters or field loss → same-day eye assessment
No referral
Hypnagogic or hypnopompic experiences alone, bereavement experiences without other psychotic features, established Charles Bonnet syndrome with insight — explain, reassure and safety-net
Charles Bonnet syndrome needs explanation more than referral: people often fear they are "going mad" and are relieved to hear it is the brain's response to reduced visual input; treating the eye condition where possible can reduce the hallucinations. Early Intervention in Psychosis services are designed to be accessed without delay, and the 2-week standard in QS80 reflects evidence that a shorter duration of untreated psychosis improves outcomes. Before any antipsychotic in dementia, specialist review is essential: the MHRA has warned (Drug Safety Update, March 2009) that antipsychotics are associated with increased cerebrovascular events and mortality in older people with dementia, and NG97 restricts their use to severe distress or risk of harm.
7
Treat

GP Management — Cause-Specific Treatment

Delirium
Treat the underlying cause
Glucose, antibiotics, fluids, stop culprit drugs, non-drug care. If dangerous distress persists: short-term haloperidol (usually ≤1 week, lowest clinically appropriate dose — NICE CG103), avoiding or using great caution in Parkinson's or Lewy body disease. Same approach as the Delirium algorithm.
Alcohol withdrawal
Hospital-based regimen
Withdrawal with hallucinations or confusion is managed in hospital. Delirium tremens: oral lorazepam first-line (NICE CG100). Parenteral thiamine at the upper end of the BNF range for suspected Wernicke's, for at least 5 days. Do not manage DTs at home.
Drug-induced
Stop or reduce the culprit
In Parkinson's disease, adjust dopaminergic drugs only with the Parkinson's specialist team (NICE NG71, 2017) — abrupt withdrawal risks a dangerous reaction. Review steroids and anticholinergics; check renal function for aciclovir. Hallucinations should settle over days to weeks.
PsychosisDo not start an antipsychotic for a first presentation of sustained psychotic symptoms in primary care unless it is done in consultation with a consultant psychiatrist (NICE CG178). The drug is chosen with the person, weighing side effects. GP role: risk, organic screen, baseline bloods and ECG, urgent referral. See the Psychosis algorithm.
Focal seizuresAntiseizure medicine is started by the specialist after diagnosis. Valproate is subject to MHRA pregnancy prevention requirements and should not be started by a GP.
Lewy body dementiaDonepezil or rivastigmine for mild to moderate disease (NICE NG97, 2018), started on specialist advice; they may reduce hallucinations. Antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk of harm, with specialist input.
Charles BonnetExplanation, optimise vision and lighting, coping techniques (moving the eyes, changing activity or lighting). No medication usually needed.
The cause dictates the treatment: an antipsychotic helps primary psychosis but can harm someone with Lewy body disease and does nothing for Charles Bonnet syndrome or withdrawal. NICE CG178 is explicit that GPs should not start an antipsychotic for a first presentation of sustained psychosis without a consultant psychiatrist's involvement, because the choice of drug and monitoring plan belong to the specialist team. In Parkinson's disease, hallucinations often reflect medication load, but the dopaminergic regimen should be changed only with the specialist team. Thiamine is given parenterally in suspected Wernicke's because absorption is unreliable in heavy drinkers, and CG100 recommends doses at the upper end of the BNF range.

Lifestyle

Patient and Carer Support

Explanation Name the experience and its likely cause. For Charles Bonnet, grief or sleep-onset experiences: "Your brain is filling in gaps — this is not a sign that you are losing your mind."
Cannabis Daily use of high-potency cannabis was associated with nearly five-fold odds of psychotic disorder (Di Forti et al., Lancet Psychiatry, 2019). Brief intervention and referral to local drug services
Other substances Stimulants and hallucinogens cause hallucinations in intoxication and withdrawal. Harm-reduction approach and community drug and alcohol service
Sleep Severe sleep loss lowers the threshold for perceptual disturbance. Regular routine; CBT for insomnia rather than hypnotics
Carer support Hallucinations in dementia are distressing for carers. Explain, advise not to argue with the experience, offer a carer's assessment and signpost to the Alzheimer's Society or Lewy Body Society
Safe environment Good lighting (shadows trigger misperceptions), less clutter, familiar people, reduced noise; avoid restraint
Vision and hearing Eye review and treatment, low-vision services, hearing aids for musical hallucinations; these can reduce or stop the experiences
Support lines Mind infoline 0300 123 3393; Samaritans 116 123 (24 hours); Hearing Voices Network groups; RNIB for Charles Bonnet information
Many people do not volunteer hallucinations for fear of being thought mad; a matter-of-fact question and a clear explanation often relieve most of the distress, especially in Charles Bonnet syndrome and bereavement. Cannabis is the most important modifiable risk factor for psychosis in young people: in the EU-GEI case-control study, daily high-potency use carried an adjusted odds ratio of about 4.8 (Di Forti et al., 2019). Improving lighting and vision addresses the trigger in sensory-deprivation syndromes. Carers of people with dementia who hallucinate need practical advice — acknowledging the experience without arguing about it — and support for themselves, because carer breakdown is a common route to crisis admission.

Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

1–2 weeks
Are the hallucinations settling? Cause treated? New features? Reassess risk. Confirm the EIP, memory or neurology referral has been received and acted on
4–6 weeks
Drug-induced: resolved after the change? Delirium: fully cleared? If not, rethink the diagnosis and consider further assessment
Ongoing (psychosis)
Shared care with CMHT/EIP — physical health monitoring at least annually in primary care (NICE CG178), side effects, adherence. See Psychosis algorithm
Lewy body dementia
Specialist advice for any agitation or distressing hallucinations; non-drug measures first; antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk (NICE NG97)
Driving
Possible focal seizures or unexplained episodes of altered awareness: advise not to drive until assessed and explain the person's duty to tell the DVLA if a seizure diagnosis is made. Active psychosis also affects fitness to drive
Documentation
Record the modality, content (in the patient's words), insight, risk assessment, capacity where relevant, who was told, and the agreed follow-up date
999 safety-net
Voices telling them to harm themselves or others and they feel they might act, fever with confusion, fit, collapse, sudden loss of vision
Same-day GP
Hallucinations worsening, new weakness or unsteadiness, unable to look after self, carer unable to cope, new confusion
Wording
"If the voices start telling you to hurt yourself or anyone else, or you feel you can't keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. You can also call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option, or the crisis number we've given you."
The key follow-up question after stopping a suspected culprit drug is whether the hallucinations resolved; if not, a primary neurological or psychiatric cause is more likely and needs pursuing. Risk is dynamic, so it should be reassessed at every contact rather than documented once. Wernicke's encephalopathy can develop during any period of poor intake in a heavy drinker, including after admission, so recurrence of confusion, unsteadiness or eye signs needs same-day review. For the SCA, examiners reward explicit, specific safety-netting with named routes (999, NHS 111 mental health option, crisis line), a follow-up date, and attention to the carer.
