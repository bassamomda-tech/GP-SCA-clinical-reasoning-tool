1
Safety

Exclude emergencies — septic arthritis, malignancy, NAI, fracture

Screen for these first — delay in septic arthritis can rapidly result in cartilage destruction, joint damage, permanent disability and sepsis. Normal temperature, WCC, CRP or ESR do not exclude septic arthritis.

🔴 Fever + hot swollen joint Fever with a hot, swollen or severely painful joint, particularly with refusal to weight-bear, is septic arthritis until proven otherwise → Same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment Use 999 if the child is toxic, haemodynamically unstable, has airway/breathing concerns, altered consciousness or cannot be transferred safely
🔴 Unwell / toxic appearance Septic, rigors, high fever, tachycardia, poorly perfused → 999 Septicaemia / haematogenous osteomyelitis
🔴 Persistent / unexplained bone pain Persistent or unexplained bone pain, particularly pain at rest/night, unexplained limp or swelling → urgent clinical assessment. NICE NG12 FBC within 48 hours where leukaemia is a possibility; very urgent X-ray (48 hours) where bone sarcoma is a possibility. Do not delay immediate referral if the child is unwell or has petechiae/hepatosplenomegaly. Night pain alone does not require 999. Malignancy (osteosarcoma, Ewing's, leukaemia)
🔴 Unexplained bruising / marks Inconsistent history, unexplained bruising, delayed presentation or injury incompatible with developmental ability, child fearful → Safeguarding assessment Contact the practice safeguarding lead, paediatric team or children's social care according to the level of concern; do not discharge with significant concerns until senior/safeguarding assessment has occurred
🔴 Bilateral leg weakness / back pain Saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention, bilateral symptoms → 999 Spinal cord compression / cauda equina
🔴 Newborn / infant refuses to move limb Pseudo-paralysis, crying on nappy change, fever or no fever → Same-day paediatric assessment Septic arthritis / osteomyelitis (fever may be absent in neonates)
🔴 Weight loss / pallor / fatigue Constitutional symptoms with bony pain, lymphadenopathy → Very urgent FBC (48 h) Leukaemia / lymphoma (leukaemia can present with musculoskeletal pain or limp, and blast cells may be absent initially)
🔴 Acute trauma with deformity Mechanism of injury, deformity, inability to weight-bear, crepitus → Same-day A&E Fracture — including toddler's fracture (subtle spiral)
Septic arthritis is a surgical emergency — delay can rapidly result in cartilage destruction, joint damage, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, permanent disability and sepsis. The Kocher criteria (fever, non-weight-bearing, raised ESR, raised WBC) are a risk-stratification tool, mainly validated in children with suspected hip septic arthritis; published estimates vary by population and setting. They may support risk stratification but do not exclude septic arthritis — clinical concern overrides the score. A child who is systemically unwell, cannot weight-bear, has severe pain, fever or a hot joint requires same-day specialist assessment regardless of the score.

Malignancy: Leukaemia can present with musculoskeletal pain or limp, and blast cells may be absent on the initial blood film. Persistent or unexplained bone pain, pain at rest/night and constitutional symptoms are the alarm features. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): very urgent FBC (within 48 hours) for pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, persistent or unexplained bone pain, unexplained bruising or bleeding; immediate specialist assessment for unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly; very urgent direct-access X-ray (within 48 hours) for unexplained bone swelling or pain where bone sarcoma is a concern, and very urgent referral if the X-ray suggests bone sarcoma.

NAI: Fractures are a recognised feature of physical abuse. A limp with no clear explanation, an inconsistent history, bruising in pre-mobile infants, delayed presentation or injury incompatible with developmental ability warrants safeguarding assessment — this does not automatically require same-day hospital admission in every case; act according to the level of concern. Document carefully; do not confront carers alone.
2
Diagnose

Apply age-based differential — the single most powerful diagnostic filter

Age narrows the differential dramatically. Ask the age first, then build your differential.

0–2 years
Developmental dysplasia of hip (DDH), toddler's fracture (subtle spiral tibia), septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, NAI — always consider abuse in non-walking children
2–5 years
Transient synovitis (common in this age group — must not reduce vigilance for infection), septic arthritis, toddler's fracture, leukaemia, Perthes disease (commonly ~4–10 yr), developmental dysplasia (may present late — a limp in a walking child still needs assessment for other diagnoses)
4–10 years
Perthes disease (avascular necrosis of the femoral head; commonly ~4–10 years but the age range is broader), transient synovitis, Köhler disease (navicular osteonecrosis), JIA, reactive arthritis
10–16 years
SUFE (slipped upper femoral epiphysis — commonly early adolescence; associated with obesity but obesity is not required; atypical age or body habitus → consider endocrine/renal disease), Osgood-Schlatter, overuse injury, JIA, Scheuermann's, hypermobility, malignancy
Any age
Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, discitis, fracture (including toddler's fracture), reactive arthritis post-infection, NAI, malignancy, haemarthrosis in haemophilia, vaso-occlusive crisis in sickle-cell disease, abdominal, pelvic and genitourinary causes (including testicular/ovarian torsion where clinically relevant)
Duration of symptoms
Acute (<48 hr)
Trauma, septic arthritis, transient synovitis, fracture (including toddler's)
Subacute (days–weeks)
Perthes, reactive arthritis, osteomyelitis, JIA onset, SUFE
Chronic (>6 weeks)
JIA, DDH (missed), malignancy, hypermobility, Scheuermann's, Osgood-Schlatter
Age-related prevalence determines pre-test probability before any examination or investigation. Transient synovitis is a common cause of acute hip pain in younger children, but it is a diagnosis of exclusion — septic arthritis must be considered first on clinical assessment (Kocher criteria do not exclude it). SUFE is an important hip disorder in adolescents and may present with knee or groin pain — beware the referred pain pattern; the knee is examined but the hip is the problem. Delay in diagnosing SUFE leads to avascular necrosis. Perthes disease runs a prolonged course — early specialist referral supports management.
3
Diagnose

Targeted history — onset, fever, preceding illness, joint distribution, morning stiffness

Systematic history directs the differential — never skip preceding illness or trauma history.

Onset & mechanism
Sudden (trauma/fracture) vs gradual (Perthes/JIA) vs insidious (malignancy). Ask: "Did anything happen, even a small bump?" — toddler fractures often have trivial mechanisms
Fever history
Temperature at home, timing, associated rigors → Fever + hot, swollen or severely painful joint = septic arthritis until proven otherwise; a normal temperature does not exclude it. Low-grade fever may accompany transient synovitis or JIA
Preceding illness
URTI / gastroenteritis 1–4 weeks prior → Reactive arthritis or transient synovitis. Sore throat + joint symptoms → post-streptococcal reactive arthritis or rheumatic fever. If rheumatic fever is clinically suspected, arrange same-day paediatric assessment and follow the local protocol; do not use ASO titres as a screening test
Pain character
Persistent or unexplained bone pain, particularly at rest/night = urgent clinical assessment — FBC within 48h where leukaemia is possible, urgent X-ray where bone sarcoma is possible (NICE NG12). Activity-related = mechanical. Morning stiffness >30 min = JIA / inflammatory. Pain at rest = infection/malignancy
Pain location
Hip → knee referred pain common in SUFE and Perthes — always examine the hip if knee pain. Groin pain = hip pathology until proven otherwise. Heel = Sever's disease
Joint distribution
Monoarthritis = septic/reactive/Perthes/SUFE. Polyarthritis = JIA, reactive arthritis, viral arthritis, leukaemia
Weight bearing
Refuses to weight-bear entirely = serious pathology (septic arthritis, fracture, osteomyelitis). Antalgic gait = pain-avoidance limp. Trendelenburg gait = proximal hip weakness
Family / PMH
JIA (family history), sickle cell disease, haemophilia, psoriasis (psoriatic arthritis), IBD-related arthritis. Ask about prior episodes
Social / safeguarding
Who was with the child? Does history match the injury? Delayed presentation? Previous attendances? Inconsistent accounts? Injury incompatible with developmental ability? → Safeguarding assessment according to the level of concern (practice safeguarding lead, paediatrics or children's social care)
Referred pain is the most dangerous diagnostic pitfall: SUFE can present with isolated knee pain and a normal knee examination — clinicians who only examine the knee miss the diagnosis. Always examine the hip when a child complains of thigh or knee pain.

Morning stiffness >30 minutes is a JIA hallmark — children are often described as "warming up" over the morning. JIA requires 6 weeks of symptoms before formal diagnosis; early referral to paediatric rheumatology avoids joint damage.

Safeguarding: The limp consultation is a key opportunity — physically abused children may present with limb symptoms. The RCGP safeguarding competency requires GPs to consider abuse when injury doesn't fit the story.
4
Diagnose

Targeted examination — gait, hips, full joint survey, spine, lymph nodes

Watch the child walk first. Never force a child to walk — if they refuse, this is highly significant.

General
Temp, HR (tachycardia = infection/pain), appearance (toxic = 999), weight/growth centile (obesity → SUFE risk), pallor (leukaemia)
Observe gait
Antalgic (short stance phase on painful side) = pain. Trendelenburg (pelvis drops on swing side) = hip abductor weakness (DDH, Perthes). Stiff-knee = quad weakness / knee pain
Hip examination
Log-roll test: pain or reduced internal rotation in early SUFE/Perthes. FABER test (hip flexion-abduction-external rotation). Measure true & apparent leg length. Marked or painful restriction of hip movement, particularly internal rotation, requires urgent assessment and imaging according to age, clinical context and local paediatric orthopaedic pathway
Knee examination
Effusion (patellar tap, bulge sign), tenderness at tibial tuberosity (Osgood-Schlatter), patellar grind, MCL/LCL laxity. Remember: knee pain may be referred from hip
Spine
Lumbar range of movement, sciatic stretch tests. Scoliosis (may cause limp). Tenderness over vertebral bodies (disc/infection)
Lower leg & foot
Sever's disease: squeeze calcaneum → pain. Köhler's: midfoot tenderness over navicular. Plantar fascia tenderness. Toddler's fracture: spiral tibia tenderness on compression
Lymph nodes & abdomen
Lymphadenopathy (leukaemia, lymphoma, viral). Splenomegaly (leukaemia). Psoriatic plaques, nail pitting (psoriatic arthritis). Visual symptoms/acuity (JIA uveitis — GP examination cannot replace slit-lamp screening)
Neurology
Power, tone, reflexes, sensation in both legs. Bilateral findings or upper motor neurone signs → urgent neurological referral
Log-roll test: The hip is the single most important examination in a limping child. Loss of internal rotation is the earliest sign of Perthes disease and SUFE — often before the child is in severe pain. Marked or painful restriction of hip movement, particularly internal rotation, requires urgent assessment and imaging (AP and lateral hip) according to age, clinical context and local paediatric orthopaedic pathway.

Trendelenburg gait in a young child suggests hip dysplasia (DDH) or early Perthes — both require urgent paediatric orthopaedic referral.

Fever >38.5°C + restricted hip movement + refusal to weight-bear: Treat as suspected septic arthritis and arrange same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment — aspiration and definitive diagnosis are specialist-led. Do not be reassured by a normal inflammatory marker — early septic arthritis can have normal bloods.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — bloods, X-ray, ultrasound, MRI (stratified by urgency)

Investigations are guided by the clinical picture — urgency depends on the child's appearance, weight-bearing, examination and red flags. If septic arthritis is suspected, do not delay transfer for imaging or blood tests — normal bloods do NOT exclude it.

Same-day (if red flags — usually as part of same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment)
FBC + differential
Bloods Supports assessment; a raised WBC is not a diagnostic threshold. Blast cells may be absent in early leukaemia — repeat if clinical suspicion. Thrombocytopenia + anaemia → haematology referral
CRP / ESR
Bloods Inflammatory markers support assessment but cannot establish the diagnosis — clinical assessment remains paramount. Normal CRP does NOT exclude septic arthritis
Plain X-ray
Imaging AP pelvis + frog-leg lateral hip: SUFE (posterior slip of femoral head — best seen on lateral), Perthes (sclerosis, fragmentation, flattening), fracture. Normal X-ray does not exclude early Perthes or SUFE
Hip ultrasound
Imaging Detects effusion (present in transient synovitis AND septic arthritis — cannot distinguish). Aspiration and definitive diagnosis are specialist-led. If septic arthritis is suspected, refer same-day — do not delay transfer for imaging
Urgent within 48 hours
MRI hip/pelvis
Imaging Gold standard for Perthes (early avascular necrosis before X-ray changes), osteomyelitis, soft tissue infection, malignancy. Request via paediatric orthopaedics
Blood cultures
If febrile Same-day, as part of specialist assessment: blood cultures and inflammatory markers before antibiotics if this does not delay transfer. Do not delay transfer to take cultures
Suspected rheumatic fever
Same-day Acute rheumatic fever is a clinical diagnosis (revised Jones criteria) supported by evidence of preceding streptococcal infection. If clinically suspected, arrange same-day paediatric assessment and follow the local protocol; do not use ASO titres as a screening test (titres may remain elevated after previous infection)
Routine (if chronic/subacute, no emergency features)
Suspected JIA
If persistent synovitis or a clinical pattern suggestive of JIA is present, refer to paediatric rheumatology. Blood tests may support assessment but should not delay referral and no blood test excludes JIA; normal inflammatory markers do not exclude JIA. ANA is not a diagnostic test for JIA; RF/anti-CCP are not required for every child with joint pain; HLA-B27 is relevant mainly to selected enthesitis-related or axial presentations — specific tests guided by the specialist or phenotype. Urine dip (haematuria → HSP/vasculitis)
Bone profile / LDH
Malignancy concern Not routine for every chronic limp. ALP is frequently physiologically elevated during growth and is not a useful standalone marker of bone metastasis or Ewing sarcoma. LDH and uric acid should be guided by FBC, examination and paediatric/haematology advice
Knee X-ray
Osgood-Schlatter (tibial tuberosity fragmentation — incidental finding, clinical diagnosis), Blount's disease, knee effusion
When NOT to investigate
Sever's disease
Clinical diagnosis — heel pain in 8–12 year active children. No X-ray needed (calcaneal apophysitis is normal growth variant)
Osgood-Schlatter
Clinical diagnosis in active adolescents. X-ray only if atypical features or not settling. MRI not routinely indicated
The Kocher criteria (fever >38.5°C, non-weight-bearing, ESR >40 mm/h, WBC >12; Caird later added CRP >20) are a risk-stratification tool, mainly validated in suspected hip septic arthritis; published estimates vary by population and setting. They are not a standalone rule-in or rule-out test — a score of 2 indicates significant risk requiring assessment, not an automatic diagnosis. Clinical appearance and examination remain more important.

X-ray limitations: Early Perthes and SUFE may have normal plain films. A normal X-ray in a child with restricted hip movements and pain requires MRI. SUFE can only be confidently excluded on the frog-leg lateral view — an AP view alone can miss a slip.

Avoiding unnecessary radiation: Children are more radiation-sensitive than adults. Ultrasound is preferred for effusion detection; MRI for soft tissue and bony detail. Don't X-ray a toddler's "limp" if clinical features clearly point to Sever's or Osgood-Schlatter.
6
Refer

Referral pathway — match urgency to clinical picture

Children with red flags need same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment; reserve 999 for the toxic, shocked or unsafe-to-transfer child. Don't manage septic arthritis or SUFE in primary care.

999
Toxic or shocked child / severe sepsis. Suspected spinal cord compression with neurological deficit or bladder/bowel dysfunction. Neurovascular compromise of limb. Child who cannot be safely transferred
Same-day A&E
Same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment (not automatically 999): suspected septic arthritis; fever plus limp/non-weight-bearing; clinically unwell child, severe pain, hot joint or marked restriction of movement. Kocher criteria may support urgency but do not replace clinical assessment. Suspected osteomyelitis. Suspected fracture — including toddler's fracture. Suspected SUFE (adolescent, restricted internal rotation, knee/groin pain) — avoid weight-bearing. Infant with pseudo-paralysis / refuses to move limb. Significant safeguarding concern. Clinically significant diagnostic uncertainty
Same-day safeguarding
Suspected NAI — contact the practice safeguarding lead, paediatric team and children's social care/police according to the level of risk. Do NOT discharge a child with significant safeguarding concerns until an appropriate senior/safeguarding assessment has occurred. Ask about safeguarding privately where possible; do not use family members as interpreters; do not promise confidentiality where a child may be at risk. Document objectively using a body map and contemporaneous description — exact statements, observed injuries, developmental ability, mechanism and discrepancies. Clinical photographs only with consent and according to local safeguarding/forensic policy, using approved equipment and secure storage
Suspected cancer: bone
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), children and young people: unexplained bone swelling or pain → very urgent direct-access X-ray (within 48 hours), and very urgent referral (within 48 hours) if the X-ray suggests bone sarcoma; pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever or infections, generalised lymphadenopathy, persistent unexplained bone pain or unexplained bruising → very urgent FBC (within 48 hours); unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist referral. Persistent/unexplained bone pain (particularly at rest/night), unexplained limp and constitutional symptoms raise suspicion; LDH/ALP are not routine screening tests.
Urgent paeds ortho (<2 weeks)
Perthes disease on X-ray with restricted hip rotation. Suspected Perthes with a normal radiograph: urgent specialist discussion or further imaging if clinical suspicion persists. Suspected SUFE is not for this route — same-day ED/orthopaedic assessment, avoid weight-bearing; confirmed SUFE → paediatric orthopaedics, admission and operative management according to the specialist team
Routine paeds ortho
DDH suspected (click/clunk, positive Barlow/Ortolani in infant). Leg length discrepancy >1 cm. Persistent limp >6 weeks without diagnosis
Paeds rheumatology
Persistent synovitis or a clinical pattern suggestive of JIA (e.g. persistent joint swelling, inflammatory morning stiffness) — blood tests should not delay referral and no blood test excludes JIA. Enthesitis pattern. Psoriatic arthritis. Systemic JIA (quotidian fever, salmon rash, arthritis)
Primary care management
Only when the child is systemically well; able to weight-bear, or has a clearly benign explanation; without fever, marked restriction, significant trauma, safeguarding concerns or concerning systemic features; and given a clear review plan and written safety-netting. Transient synovitis (diagnosis of exclusion, after septic arthritis and other serious causes have been considered). Osgood-Schlatter. Sever's disease. Overuse injury. Hypermobility-related pain
SUFE is a surgical emergency once confirmed: the child must be non-weight-bearing from the moment of suspicion and assessed same day; admission and operative management are according to the specialist team. Any delay risks further slip and avascular necrosis. Do not send home to await an outpatient appointment.

Perthes disease: Outcome correlates strongly with age of onset and degree of femoral head involvement. Younger children generally have a better prognosis; surgical decisions are specialist-led. Early referral preserves function.

Transient synovitis: A diagnosis of exclusion — do not label it as such where the child is febrile, toxic, non-weight-bearing or has marked restriction of movement. Manage in primary care only if systemically well, able to weight-bear and without red flags. Review within 24–48 hours; persistent or worsening symptoms require same-day paediatric/orthopaedic reassessment.
7
Treat

Primary care management — transient synovitis, mechanical pain, Osgood-Schlatter, Sever's

Only manage in primary care once serious pathology is excluded. Escalate immediately if not improving as expected.

Condition-specific primary care treatment
Transient synovitis (irritable hip)
Analgesia + rest First-line
Diagnosis of exclusion — not if febrile, toxic, non-weight-bearing or marked restriction of movement. Weight-based paracetamol or ibuprofen per BNFC/local formulary. Relative rest from sport. Review within 24–48 hours. Persistent, worsening or recurrent symptoms require reassessment
Osgood-Schlatter disease
Load management + physio First-line
Reduce high-impact activity (not complete rest). Ibuprofen or paracetamol for pain. Options: ice after activity, quadriceps strengthening and flexibility work. Return to sport gradually. Refer physio if persistent. Usually settles with skeletal maturity, but symptoms may persist — persistent, atypical, night or systemic symptoms should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis
Sever's disease (calcaneal apophysitis)
Activity modification + heel raise First-line
Relative load reduction rather than complete rest. Options: heel raise/cushioned insole, calf stretching and strengthening. Reduce running/jumping temporarily; return to sport gradually. Analgesia: ibuprofen or paracetamol as needed. Usually settles with skeletal maturity, but symptoms may persist — persistent, atypical, night or systemic symptoms should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis
Hypermobility-related pain
Physiotherapy First-line
Proprioception and strengthening exercises. Avoid extreme positions. Pacing advice. Consider Beighton score. If recurrent joint dislocations — refer to paeds rheumatology to exclude hEDS
Analgesia for acute limb pain (primary care) — doses per BNFC/local formulary
Step 1 Paracetamol or ibuprofen — weight-based dosing according to the current BNFC and product information. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate period. Consider the child's hydration, renal function, asthma, gastrointestinal risk and suspected diagnosis
Step 2 Ibuprofen — dose by age/weight per BNFC. Avoid in dehydration; use caution in chickenpox or renal disease. Infection in general is not a blanket contraindication
Step 3 Moderate/severe or inadequately controlled pain — seek same-day clinical review rather than constructing a complex staggered regimen (routine alternating paracetamol and ibuprofen increases dosing errors)
Step 4 Refer to A&E — strong opioids require hospital setting in children. Codeine is CONTRAINDICATED in children under 12 and post-tonsillectomy — follow current MHRA advice and current paediatric prescribing guidance (BNFC)
Specialist-led management (for context — not GP treatment instructions)
Septic arthritis
Hospital admission under paediatrics/orthopaedics: aspiration, antibiotics according to local antimicrobial guidance, and surgical washout where indicated
Perthes disease
Conservative (activity restriction, physiotherapy) for mild/young cases. Containment surgery (femoral or pelvic osteotomy) for severe/older cases. Managed by paeds orthopaedics
SUFE
Admission and operative management according to the paediatric orthopaedic team. No weight-bearing. The other hip is assessed, as SUFE can be bilateral
JIA
Treatment (e.g. NSAIDs, intra-articular corticosteroids, methotrexate, biologics) is initiated and directed by paediatric rheumatology
Ibuprofen in children: Anti-inflammatory and analgesic — useful for musculoskeletal pain. Regular dosing is not appropriate for every child or every pain presentation; use the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate period, dosed by age/weight per BNFC. Ibuprofen is not contraindicated in infection generally; avoid in dehydration and use caution in chickenpox or renal disease.

Codeine is contraindicated in children under 12 (MHRA restriction following deaths in codeine-morphine ultra-rapid metabolisers). Follow current MHRA advice and current paediatric prescribing guidance.

Transient synovitis can recur, and a small proportion of children later prove to have Perthes disease — this is why persistent or recurrent symptoms need reassessment. Natural history of TS is benign, but follow-up is mandatory.
8
Lifestyle

Non-pharmacological interventions — activity guidance, physio, school, nutrition

Lifestyle is treatment — especially for overuse injuries, hypermobility, and chronic musculoskeletal conditions.

Activity modification (not rest) For overuse injuries (Sever's, Osgood-Schlatter): reduce load, not eliminate. Swimming and cycling tolerated. Return to sport gradually — "pain is the guide" approach. Complete rest causes deconditioning
Physiotherapy referral Refer for strengthening, proprioception training, gait re-education. Essential for JIA, hypermobility, post-fracture rehabilitation, DDH. Reduces recurrence of transient synovitis in hypermobile children
Footwear assessment Cushioned trainers for Sever's. Heel raise insoles may be an option. Avoid flip-flops in children with flat feet or hypermobility. Paediatric orthotics referral if persistent biomechanical issues
Weight management SUFE risk directly correlates with obesity (BMI >95th centile). Reduce load on developing epiphyses. Refer to dietitian / MEND programme if BMI >91st centile. Supports Perthes outcomes too
School communication Provide "fit note equivalent" for schools: PE exemption letter, stair access, seating arrangements. Children with JIA or Perthes may need adapted PE plans — coordinate with SENCO. Fatigue management for JIA
Vitamin D supplementation Supplementation depends on age, season, diet and risk group — follow current NHS/UK government guidance and local prescribing arrangements. Do not routinely test 25-OH vitamin D in every child with a limp or overuse injury; test only where there is a specific clinical indication
Calcium-rich diet Dairy or fortified alternatives for bone health. Adequate calcium intake is important during rapid growth phases. Important for children on long-term corticosteroids (JIA)
Mental health & chronic pain Chronic pain affects school attendance and mental health. Screen for anxiety and depression in children with JIA, hypermobility, or persistent limb pain. Refer to CAMHS / pain psychology if needed. Involve family in pain management approach
Activity modification vs rest: Complete rest worsens outcomes in overuse injuries — bone requires mechanical loading to remodel. The British Journal of Sports Medicine recommends relative load management over complete cessation. Children respond better and faster than adults to structured physiotherapy.

Obesity and SUFE: SUFE is associated with childhood obesity, although obesity is not required — atypical age or body habitus should prompt consideration of endocrine/renal disease. Weight reduction reduces shear forces on the capital femoral epiphysis and may delay contralateral slip.

Vitamin D: Supplementation advice depends on age, season, diet and risk group — follow current NHS/UK government guidance and local prescribing arrangements. Do not routinely test vitamin D in every limping child; consider deficiency (e.g. rickets) only where there is a specific clinical indication.
9
Safety

Follow-up, safety-netting, and monitoring — never discharge without a safety net

All children managed in primary care for limping must have explicit safety-netting. Document this in the notes.

Transient synovitis: 24–48 hr
Phone review or same-day review if not improving. If worsening, fever develops, or child now refuses to weight-bear → Same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment. If improving: review at 2 weeks, then discharge if resolved
Transient synovitis: 2 weeks
Should have full resolution. If persistent limp at 2 weeks → plain X-ray hip + urgent paeds orthopaedic referral (exclude early Perthes). Do not discharge a child still limping at 2 weeks
Osgood-Schlatter: 6 weeks
Review response to load management. Refer to physiotherapy if not improving. Re-examine to exclude other pathology. X-ray only if atypical. Usually settles with skeletal maturity, but symptoms may persist — reassess persistent, atypical, night or systemic symptoms
Sever's disease: 4–6 weeks
Review response to load modification. Bilateral Sever's does not automatically require vitamin D testing or paediatric orthopaedic referral. Persistent, atypical, night or systemic symptoms → reconsider the diagnosis; refer if structural concern
JIA monitoring
Children with suspected or confirmed JIA require paediatric rheumatology referral and ophthalmology uveitis screening according to the risk-stratified local/RCPCH ophthalmology protocol (JIA subtype, ANA status, age at onset, disease duration, previous uveitis) — uveitis can be asymptomatic. GP role: ensure attendance and respond promptly to visual symptoms. Joint disease review by rheumatology 3-monthly. Monitor for MTX toxicity (LFTs, FBC). Annual growth monitoring
Post-septic arthritis
Paeds orthopaedic follow-up according to the specialist plan for avascular necrosis surveillance. Parents advised: any return of pain/limp → same-day assessment
Perthes / SUFE
Managed entirely by paeds orthopaedics. GP role: ensure attended follow-up, monitor pain, screen for contralateral SUFE (can be bilateral — same-day assessment if new hip/groin/knee pain)

The 2-week review for transient synovitis: true transient synovitis should be settling. Any child still limping at 2 weeks has a diagnosis other than transient synovitis until proven otherwise — Perthes, reactive arthritis, and early JIA are the commonest alternatives. A normal X-ray at 2 weeks does not exclude early Perthes — MRI is needed.

Contralateral SUFE risk: SUFE can be bilateral (sequential or synchronous). Children who have had one SUFE fixed must be counselled to return immediately with any new hip, groin, or thigh pain. GP follow-up has a critical role in detecting the contralateral slip early.

JIA uveitis: Anterior uveitis in JIA is often painless and can cause visual loss if undetected. Screening frequency is risk-stratified (JIA subtype, ANA status, age at onset, disease duration, previous uveitis) according to the local/RCPCH paediatric ophthalmology protocol — not a universal interval. GPs must ensure ophthalmology follow-up is active. This is an area where GP-initiated safety-netting prevents preventable visual loss.
