1
Safety

Red Flags — exclude the limb- and life-threatening causes

Most thigh pain is muscular, but first clear the vascular, infective, compartment and bone-cancer emergencies — and a hip fracture in the older patient who has fallen.
Proximal DVT Unilateral thigh/calf swelling, tenderness, warmth, pitting oedema, with VTE risk (recent surgery, immobility, cancer, COCP, pregnancy) → two-level Wells, same-day DVT pathway (not 999 if no PE features). Suspected PE → use the PE Wells/PE pathway, not the DVT-only pathway; 999 if haemodynamic compromise, syncope, severe hypoxia or marked deterioration. Consider haematoma, cellulitis, muscle injury, ruptured Baker's cyst and venous compression.
Acute limb ischaemia Sudden pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis or marked coldness (6 Ps) is an acute vascular emergency → 999 and notify the vascular service. Assess both limbs; don't rely on palpated pulses alone (Doppler may be needed); no compression bandaging; keep the limb flat, not elevated; don't delay referral for bloods or imaging; document time of onset.
Compartment syndrome Pain out of proportion, worse on passive stretch, tense swollen compartment — after trauma, fracture or reperfusion → immediate ED/orthopaedic assessment. Present pulses do not exclude it; clinical diagnosis may be difficult. Do not wait for pulses to disappear or for compartment pressure measurement in primary care.
Necrotising fasciitis / sepsis Severe pain out of proportion to skin signs, rapidly spreading erythema, systemic toxicity, septic shock, crepitus or skin necrosis → 999
Hip fracture (NOF) Older / osteoporotic patient after a fall, unable to weight-bear, shortened externally-rotated leg, groin/thigh/knee pain → urgent ED with analgesia and safe transfer (999 ambulance if unstable, neurovascular compromise, major trauma or cannot be transferred safely). AP pelvis + lateral hip X-rays; if negative but suspicion remains → MRI (CT if MRI unavailable within 24 hours or contraindicated).
Bone sarcoma — NICE NG12 Adults: unexplained persistent or worsening deep bone pain or a bony mass → X-ray; if it suggests bone sarcoma → Suspected cancer pathway referral. Children and young people: unexplained bone pain or swelling → very urgent direct-access X-ray (within 48 hours) and specialist assessment. Age, night pain or a raised ALP alone do not set the referral route; a normal X-ray does not exclude bone malignancy or stress fracture — reassess if symptoms persist. NICE NG12
Soft-tissue sarcoma — NICE NG12 Unexplained lump increasing in size, deep to fascia, painful, fixed/immobile, approximately >5 cm, or recurrent after excision → urgent direct-access ultrasound; if it suggests sarcoma or is uncertain with persistent concern → Suspected cancer. Do not inject, biopsy, excise or aggressively manipulate.
Cauda equina / progressive deficit Acute bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia or progressive neurological deficit → 999 / emergency. Document neurological findings before referral.

Thigh pain spans four emergencies that a muscular label can hide: venous (proximal DVT, which can throw a fatal PE), arterial (acute ischaemia — time-critical, with urgency set by limb viability on vascular assessment), pressure (compartment syndrome) and infective (necrotising fasciitis). Each has a simple discriminator — unilateral swelling with VTE risk, the 6 Ps, pain out of proportion, and systemic toxicity — and each needs emergency or same-day action rather than analgesia and review.

NICE NG12 makes bone and soft-tissue sarcoma a primary-care responsibility. The femur is a classic site for osteosarcoma in younger patients. Persistent deep bone pain — particularly waking the patient at night — or a bony mass warrants an X-ray (very urgent direct-access within 48 hours in children and young people), an enlarging soft-tissue lump an urgent ultrasound, and an X-ray suggestive of sarcoma triggers a suspected cancer pathway referral (within 48 hours for children and young people); a normal X-ray with ongoing suspicion still needs repeat imaging or referral.

2
Diagnose

Focused history — localise the origin and screen for systemic disease

Four questions sort most thigh pain: how did it start, what is the pain like and where does it travel, is there a claudication pattern, and are there systemic or risk features?
Onset & trauma
Sudden during sport (muscle tear), insidious (OA, stress fracture, tumour), after a fall (hip fracture), or spontaneous on an anticoagulant (haematoma).
Pain character
Deep boring bone pain, worse at night (sarcoma/mets); sharp radicular pain down the front of the thigh (L2–L4); or burning/tingling over the outer thigh (meralgia paraesthetica — lateral femoral cutaneous nerve).
Claudication
Cramping buttock/thigh pain on walking a fixed distance, relieved by rest → aorto-iliac peripheral arterial disease. Erectile dysfunction + buttock claudication suggest proximal arterial disease but are not diagnostic of Leriche syndrome.
Systemic / cancer
Fever, weight loss, night sweats, night pain, known malignancy (bone metastasis), or a growing lump.
Risk factors
VTE risk (surgery, immobility, cancer, COCP/HRT, pregnancy); diabetes (proximal amyotrophy — painful quadriceps wasting); training error / RED-S (stress fracture).
Medications
Anticoagulant (spontaneous haematoma), statin (myalgia/myositis), long-term bisphosphonate (prodromal thigh pain of an atypical femoral fracture).

The history alone usually localises thigh pain to one of five compartments — vascular, neurogenic, muscular, referred (hip/spine) or bone — which is what determines the right investigation. A radicular description points to the lumbar spine and an MRI, a claudication history to the arteries and an ABPI, and a deep night-pain history to bone and an X-ray.

Two drug-specific traps are worth a deliberate question. Long-term bisphosphonate use can cause an atypical femoral fracture, classically heralded by weeks of dull thigh or groin pain before the bone breaks; and statins cause a spectrum from benign myalgia to rare necrotising myositis, so a CK is part of the work-up when muscle pain is diffuse.

3
Diagnose

Examination — look, feel, move, and check pulses and nerves

Look
Compare both limbs. Swelling, bruising or haematoma (particularly if anticoagulated), erythema, deformity, quadriceps wasting, an obvious mass, or a shortened externally-rotated leg (hip fracture).
Feel
Point of maximal tenderness, compartment tension, skin temperature and capillary refill, and femoral, popliteal and pedal pulses bilaterally. Measure and compare thigh and calf circumference to document asymmetry — this does not diagnose DVT or replace the Wells pathway and ultrasound. Palpate inguinal nodes and for a femoral/inguinal hernia.
Move
Active and passive hip and knee range; pain on resisted knee extension (quadriceps) or flexion (hamstring) and resisted adduction (adductor strain).
Neuro (L2–L4)
Hip flexion / knee extension power, knee-jerk reflex, sensation over the anterior thigh; femoral stretch test. Meralgia paraesthetica gives an oval patch of altered sensation over the anterolateral thigh with normal power and reflexes. Document neurological findings before referral if radiculopathy or cauda equina is suspected.
Vascular
Cool, pale, pulseless limb → ischaemia (compare both limbs; use Doppler signals if pulses are hard to palpate). Reduced/absent pulses + bruit → PAD. Warm, swollen, tender calf → consider DVT.
Always check
The joint above and below — hip and knee, even when pain is reported in the thigh — and the lumbar spine, because thigh pain is so often referred; examine the abdomen, groin and testicles where clinically relevant.

Because the thigh refers pain from the spine above and the hip and knee below, the examination must deliberately include all three plus the nerves and vessels that cross it. A normal local muscle examination with pain reproduced on hip movement reclassifies the problem entirely, as does an absent pulse or a focal neurological deficit.

Measuring limb circumference and assessing compartment tension are quick, objective steps that help distinguish a benign strain from the two pressure-related emergencies — a swelling DVT and an evolving compartment syndrome — where the diagnosis turns on asymmetry and pain out of proportion rather than on the appearance of the skin. Circumference documents asymmetry but does not diagnose DVT — the Wells pathway and ultrasound do.

4
Diagnose

Differential diagnosis — by anatomical origin

Vascular
Emergency or urgent Proximal DVT, acute or critical limb ischaemia, intermittent claudication (aorto-iliac), spontaneous haematoma (anticoagulant).
Neurogenic
Lumbar radiculopathy (L2–L4), meralgia paraesthetica (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve entrapment), proximal diabetic amyotrophy.
Muscular / soft tissue
Quadriceps, hamstring or adductor strain or tear, myositis, delayed-onset muscle soreness, iliopsoas or trochanteric pathology.
Referred
Hip osteoarthritis or fracture, knee pathology, femoral or inguinal hernia.
Bone
Do not miss Femoral neck/shaft stress fracture (runners, RED-S), atypical femoral fracture (long-term bisphosphonate), bone sarcoma / metastasis, osteomyelitis.
Skin / other
Cellulitis, superficial thrombophlebitis, lipoma or soft-tissue sarcoma.

Grouping the differential by tissue of origin keeps the dangerous diagnoses visible next to the common ones: every column contains both a benign cause (strain, meralgia, claudication you can manage) and a red-flag (sarcoma, ischaemia, fracture), so the framework itself prompts you not to anchor on muscle.

Two under-recognised diagnoses sit specifically in the thigh: meralgia paraesthetica, a purely sensory entrapment of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve that needs reassurance rather than imaging, and diabetic amyotrophy, a painful proximal motor neuropathy with quadriceps wasting that is easily mistaken for a structural hip or spine problem.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — targeted, not routine

Suspected DVT
First Two-level Wells score. DVT likely (≥2): proximal leg-vein ultrasound with the result within 4 hours if possible; if not, D-dimer, interim therapeutic anticoagulation if appropriate, and ultrasound within 24 hours. DVT unlikely (≤1): D-dimer within 4 hours; if positive, proximal leg-vein ultrasound; if the D-dimer cannot be obtained promptly, consider interim anticoagulation while awaiting it. A negative D-dimer does not override a high clinical probability where the pathway requires imaging. If PE is suspected, use the PE Wells/PE investigation pathway instead.
Bloods (targeted)
FBC/CRP/ESR if infection or inflammatory disease is suspected, persistent unexplained bone pain, possible malignancy or systemic symptoms. CK if diffuse myalgia, weakness, suspected myositis or statin-related muscle symptoms. HbA1c if diabetic amyotrophy is suspected or there are diabetes symptoms/poor control. Not a routine panel for every patient.
Myeloma screen
Not for every older patient — consider with persistent unexplained bone pain, anaemia, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia, raised ESR/plasma viscosity, unexplained fracture or other suggestive features.
PSA
Not a routine test for male thigh pain — only where prostate cancer is clinically suspected, after discussing the implications of testing.
X-ray
Persistent or night bone pain, after trauma, suspected stress or atypical femoral fracture, or suspected sarcoma (request urgently). Suspected hip fracture: AP pelvis and lateral hip. A normal X-ray does not completely exclude bone malignancy or stress fracture.
Vascular studies
ABPI ± arterial duplex for claudication or suspected PAD.
MRI
Not usually needed for uncomplicated radiculopathy without progressive deficit. Emergency same-day MRI via the emergency department for suspected cauda equina. Via an urgent pathway for progressive neurological deficit, suspected occult fracture, suspected stress fracture with a normal X-ray, suspected malignancy or osteomyelitis, or an unexplained mass needing specialist imaging.
Soft-tissue USS
An unexplained enlarging lump in an adult → urgent direct-access ultrasound as the initial imaging test (NICE NG12).
Do NOT
Image a simple, improving muscle strain, send routine blood panels or PSA, or order a D-dimer when the clinical probability of DVT is high — go straight to ultrasound.

Investigation follows the localisation rather than scatter-gun testing: a Wells score directs the DVT pathway, an ABPI confirms PAD, a plain X-ray screens bone, and MRI is reserved for progressive neurological deficit, cauda equina, occult fracture, suspected malignancy or infection, or a mass needing specialist imaging. The single most important rule is that a high clinical probability of DVT goes straight to ultrasound — a D-dimer cannot exclude it.

A normal X-ray never excludes a sarcoma or an early stress fracture. If deep bone pain persists, MRI (or referral) is the next step, because both diagnoses are radiographically silent in their early weeks and the cost of a missed bone tumour is measured in stages.

6
Refer

Referral criteria

999 / emergency
Acute limb ischaemia (call 999 and notify the vascular service); necrotising infection or septic shock; suspected PE with haemodynamic compromise, syncope, severe hypoxia or marked deterioration; cauda equina syndrome with acute bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia or progressive neurological deficit.
Immediate ED / same-day
Suspected compartment syndrome (immediate ED/orthopaedics); suspected hip fracture (urgent ED, analgesia, safe transfer — 999 ambulance if unstable or unsafe to transfer); suspected DVT without PE features (same-day local DVT pathway); suspected osteomyelitis or septic arthritis; acute severe pain with a tense swollen thigh; suspected pathological fracture; severe pain with an enlarging mass or systemic illness.
Suspected cancer — bone sarcoma NICE NG12
An X-ray suggesting bone sarcoma → suspected cancer pathway referral (adults); very urgent specialist referral within 48 hours (children and young people, who should have a very urgent direct-access X-ray within 48 hours for unexplained bone pain or swelling). Persistent unexplained bone pain or a bony mass with a normal X-ray → reassess, repeat imaging or specialist referral.
Suspected cancer — soft-tissue sarcoma NICE NG12
An unexplained soft-tissue lump that is increasing in size → urgent direct-access ultrasound (within 48 hours in children and young people); suspected cancer pathway referral if the scan suggests sarcoma, or is uncertain and concern persists. Red flags: increasing size, deep to fascia, painful, fixed/immobile, approximately over 5 cm, recurrence after previous excision. Do not inject, biopsy, excise or aggressively manipulate a suspected sarcoma outside the specialist pathway.
Vascular
Intermittent claudication → cardiovascular risk management and supervised exercise, with vascular referral if lifestyle-limiting; chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (rest pain, tissue loss) → urgent vascular referral; acute limb ischaemia → 999.
Orthopaedics / MSK
Stress fracture, atypical femoral fracture (urgent), refractory strain, a tear with significant functional deficit, suspected tendon rupture or large haematoma, or hip/knee pathology.
Spinal
Radiculopathy managed per NICE NG59; urgent referral if not settling or with a progressive deficit; emergency if any cauda equina features.
Local pathways
Use the verified local (Bolton) DVT, vascular, MSK and sarcoma referral pathways and check current local criteria.

The referral routes mirror the red-flag list: vascular and infective emergencies bypass investigation entirely, while the two NG12 sarcoma pathways (bone via urgent X-ray then referral — suspected cancer pathway for adults, 48 hours for children and young people — soft tissue via urgent ultrasound, then a suspected cancer pathway referral if it suggests sarcoma) are the cancer safety net that primary care owns for limb pain.

Suspected atypical femoral fracture deserves urgent orthopaedic discussion in its own right: the prodromal thigh-pain phase is the window to protect the bone (stop the bisphosphonate, restrict loading, image both femora) before a complete, poorly-healing fracture occurs.

7
Treat

Management of the common, non-emergency causes

Muscle strain / tear
Relative rest + graded loading
Relative rest from aggravating activity, early comfortable movement, progressive loading, simple analgesia and a graded return to activity. Ice may help short-term; avoid compression/elevation if fracture, compartment syndrome or vascular compromise is possible. Refer according to functional deficit, suspected tendon rupture, haematoma, location and sporting requirements.
Meralgia paraesthetica
Reassure + offload the nerve
Explain it is a purely sensory neuropathy that often settles spontaneously; loosen tight belts/clothing, address weight where relevant, avoid prolonged hip extension. Consider neuropathic medication or nerve injection only if persistent or disabling. Recheck the diagnosis if there is weakness, reflex loss, back pain or symptoms below the knee.
Claudication (PAD)
Risk-factor + exercise
Supervised exercise programme (core intervention), smoking cessation, BP and diabetes control; antiplatelet and statin once PAD is established, following the local NICE pathway. Vascular referral if lifestyle-limiting; urgent if chronic limb-threatening ischaemia; 999 if acute ischaemia.
Analgesia
Paracetamol ± topical or short-course oral NSAID (caution in PAD/renal/elderly); reserve opioids for short-term severe pain. Establish the cause before treating neuropathic pain — do not automatically prescribe amitriptyline or gabapentin for unexplained thigh pain; meralgia may need conservative measures only, and lumbar radiculopathy follows the NICE low back pain and sciatica pathway (NG59).
Diabetic amyotrophy
Optimise glycaemic control and physiotherapy; analgesia and further management via specialist/local pathway advice; usually improves over many months — explain the slow course.
Do NOT
Inject, biopsy, excise or aggressively manipulate an undiagnosed mass, or treat presumed strain/sciatica with escalating opioids without re-examining for red flags.

Most thigh pain is mechanical and self-limiting, and the evidence supports active management — graded loading for muscle injury and a supervised exercise programme for claudication — over rest and escalating analgesia. Opioids have a small, short-term role and no place in the chronic management of mechanical or neuropathic limb pain.

Naming the diagnosis changes the treatment: meralgia paraesthetica responds to offloading the nerve and reassurance rather than imaging or surgery, and diabetic amyotrophy needs glycaemic control and time rather than orthopaedic referral. Setting realistic timelines — months, not weeks — prevents repeat presentations and inappropriate escalation.

8
Lifestyle

Rehabilitation, risk factors & self-care

Graded rehabilitation Progressive strengthening and load management beat rest for muscle and tendon injury; physiotherapy guidance and a clear return-to-activity plan reduce recurrence.
Training & bone health Address training error, footwear and energy availability (RED-S) in runners with stress fractures; ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D and review bone health.
Vascular risk Smoking cessation, statin, blood-pressure and glucose control and walking exercise are the foundation of claudication care and reduce cardiovascular events.
VTE awareness Advise on hydration and mobility around surgery and long travel for at-risk patients, and on the symptoms of DVT/PE that mean urgent review.
Weight & ergonomics Weight loss and avoiding tight waistbands or prolonged hip extension help meralgia paraesthetica and load-related pain.
Diabetes Good long-term glycaemic control aids recovery from amyotrophy and reduces neuropathic pain.

The lifestyle measures are not generic advice — each maps onto a specific cause: load management for tendons and muscle, energy availability and bone health for stress fractures, and the cardiovascular risk-factor package for claudication, where the leg is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis and the priority is preventing MI and stroke.

Equipping at-risk patients to recognise DVT and PE symptoms shortens the time to treatment for the one thigh-pain diagnosis most likely to kill, and is a high-value, low-effort piece of every relevant consultation.

9
Safety

Safety-netting & follow-up

Vascular / VTE
999 New swelling with breathlessness, chest pain, syncope, coughing blood or marked deterioration (PE); a suddenly cold, pale, numb or weak leg (ischaemia). Same-day New or increasing calf/thigh swelling without PE symptoms → same-day DVT pathway.
Infection / pressure
Immediate ED Severe pain out of proportion with tense swelling or pain on passive stretch (compartment syndrome); spreading redness, fever or rapidly worsening pain (necrotising infection — 999 if systemically unwell).
Bone red flags
Review Worsening or persistent bone pain (including despite a normal X-ray), a new or enlarging lump, or unexplained weight loss → reassess, re-image or refer on the sarcoma pathway.
Neurology
999 / emergency Progressive leg weakness or numbness, or any bladder/bowel disturbance or saddle numbness (cauda equina).
Expected course
Most uncomplicated strains improve over 2–6 weeks with graded loading. If pain is not improving, is worsening, or function remains substantially limited, reassess for fracture, haematoma, tendon rupture, infection, DVT, malignancy or referred pain rather than simply escalating analgesia.
Documentation
Record the working diagnosis, red flags checked, the plan, the safety-net advice given, and the review interval.

Specific, symptom-level safety-netting is what makes a probable-strain label safe: the patient leaves knowing exactly which changes (a swelling or breathless episode, a cold leg, spreading redness and fever, new weakness or bladder symptoms) mean same-day or 999 review, rather than a vague instruction to come back if no better.

A planned review interval matched to the expected recovery turns safety-netting into active follow-up. Pain that fails to settle on the expected trajectory is a prompt to re-open the differential — re-examine, re-image, reconsider sarcoma or stress fracture — not to escalate analgesia on an unconfirmed diagnosis.
