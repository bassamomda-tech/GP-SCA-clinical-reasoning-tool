1
Safety

Red flags — sight-threatening emergencies requiring same-day ophthalmology

New flashes and floaters usually mean a posterior vitreous detachment (PVD). A minority have a retinal tear that can progress to detachment, which is treatable if caught early and a cause of permanent sight loss if missed. Screen every patient for the features below before deciding how fast they need a dilated retinal examination.
Shadow, curtain or veil in the vision Any new field defect, especially one spreading from the periphery → Same-day eye casualty (retinal detachment)
Reduced vision New blur or drop in acuity with flashes/floaters → Same day. Patient-reported reduced vision is the strongest single symptom linked to a tear (likelihood ratio about 5; Hollands et al., JAMA 2009)
Shower of floaters Sudden "soot", "cloud" or many new dots, or a red haze → Same day (vitreous haemorrhage or pigment from a tear)
Central distortion Straight lines looking wavy, a central gap → Same day (macula involved or threatened; also consider wet macular degeneration)
Higher-risk eye High myopia, previous retinal tear or detachment in either eye, recent cataract or other intraocular surgery, eye trauma, family history of detachment, Stickler or Marfan syndrome → Lower threshold for same-day review
Painful red eye with floaters Floaters with pain, photophobia or redness → Same day (uveitis, endophthalmitis after surgery or injection)
Recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection New floaters, pain or reduced vision within days to weeks → Same day via the treating eye unit (endophthalmitis)
Transient loss, not flashes Monocular "curtain" that clears in minutes = amaurosis fugax; homonymous field loss in both eyes = cortical event → manage as suspected TIA per NICE NG128 (2019) — specialist within 24 h; persisting deficit → 999
Child with floaters or visual change Unusual in children — consider trauma, uveitis, retinal disease → Urgent ophthalmology. An absent red reflex in a child → NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider urgent referral (within 2 weeks) for retinoblastoma
A meta-analysis of patients presenting with acute flashes or floaters (Hollands et al., JAMA 2009) found a retinal tear in about 14%. The history features that most raised the chance of a tear were subjective reduction in vision and a large number of new floaters. The key surgical distinction is whether the macula is still attached. A "macula-on" detachment is operated on urgently to protect central vision; once the macula detaches, visual recovery is less complete. That is why a patient describing a shadow or curtain goes to eye casualty today, not to the next routine clinic. In an SCA consultation, ask about each red flag by name; patients rarely volunteer "a shadow" unless asked.
2
Diagnose

Confirm presentation — characterise flashes and floaters

The description of the symptoms largely decides urgency. Take a precise history for each eye.
Floaters
One new ring, cobweb or "fly" that moves with eye movement = typical PVD (the ring is the detached vitreous at the optic disc) | Many new dots or a "shower" = blood or pigment — higher risk of a tear | Long-standing, unchanged spots = benign vitreous opacities
Flashes
Brief arcs or lightning streaks, usually at the edge of vision, in one eye, more noticeable in dim light = vitreoretinal traction (PVD or tear) | Zig-zag, shimmering, spreading pattern affecting both eyes' field, lasting minutes = migraine aura
Onset
Sudden (hours to days) = acute PVD, tear, haemorrhage | Gradual or unchanged for months = benign floaters
Duration of flashes
PVD: a second or less, repeated. Typical migraine aura: develops over at least 5 minutes and lasts 5–60 minutes (NICE CG150, 2012)
One eye or both
Symptoms that stay in one eye when the other is covered = eye cause. Visual phenomena present with either eye covered, or affecting the same half of vision in both eyes = brain (migraine aura, occipital lesion)
Visual field and acuity
Any shadow, curtain, missing area or blur — ask directly
Risk factors
Age over about 50, short-sightedness, previous eye surgery or injury, previous tear or detachment, diabetes (vitreous haemorrhage), anticoagulation (bleeding into the vitreous)
Other eye
Previous PVD or detachment in the fellow eye. Detachment in one eye raises the risk in the other
A single new ring-shaped floater with brief peripheral flashes is the classic story of an uncomplicated PVD. A sudden "shower" of dots is more worrying, because blood or pigment released into the vitreous is strongly associated with a retinal tear. In the Hollands meta-analysis, new onset of 10 or more floaters or a shower carried likelihood ratios of roughly 8 to 36. Migraine aura is distinguished by its slow spread, zig-zag pattern, effect on both eyes' fields and duration of minutes. It needs no eye referral if typical, but a first aura over 50 or one with persisting deficit needs a stroke assessment. Asking whether the symptoms persist with each eye covered separates an eye problem from a brain one within seconds.
3
Diagnose

Classification — benign vs sight-threatening

Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD)
The commonest cause of new flashes and floaters from middle age onwards; the vitreous gel separates from the retina. Uncomplicated: normal acuity, no field loss, no shower, and a dilated examination shows no tear
Retinal tear
Occurs in a minority of symptomatic PVDs (about 14% of acute presentations in the Hollands 2009 meta-analysis). Suggested by a shower of floaters, reduced vision, or pigment or blood in the vitreous. Treated with laser or cryotherapy to seal the tear
Retinal detachment
Fluid passes through the tear and lifts the retina: a spreading shadow or curtain, ± reduced vision. Needs urgent vitreoretinal surgery; timing depends on whether the macula is still attached
Vitreous haemorrhage
Sudden "red haze", many dark floaters, reduced vision; poor or absent red reflex. Causes: retinal tear, proliferative diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, anticoagulation
Posterior uveitis / vitritis
Floaters with blurred vision ± pain, photophobia, red eye; may be linked to systemic inflammatory disease or infection
Benign vitreous floaters
Long-standing, stable spots or strands; normal vision and fields; no flashes. Reassure, but any change in character means reassessment
Migraine aura
Positive spreading visual disturbance affecting both eyes' fields, usually followed by headache; fully reversible (NICE CG150)
Other mimics
Occipital lobe lesions and seizures (stereotyped coloured shapes), retinal migraine (monocular, diagnosis of exclusion), phosphenes on eye movement from optic neuritis
A retinal tear cannot be excluded without a dilated examination of the peripheral retina, which is why every new PVD needs one: from an optometrist with the right equipment, a community urgent eye service or the hospital eye service, depending on local arrangements. The College of Optometrists guidance on flashes and floaters says a patient with a confirmed PVD after dilated examination can be managed in practice, provided they are well informed about the symptoms that mean a tear or detachment has developed. Sealing a tear with laser or cryotherapy is a quick outpatient procedure, whereas a detachment needs surgery. The earlier this pathway is triggered, the more treatment options remain.
4
Diagnose

Targeted examination in primary care

Visual acuity Essential
Snellen chart each eye separately, with glasses (or pinhole). Record the result (e.g. 6/6, 6/12). If no chart: count fingers, hand movements, perception of light
Visual fields
Confrontation for each eye separately, all four quadrants. Any defect → same-day referral. An Amsler grid (or a straight line such as a door frame) for central distortion
Pupils
Swinging light test for a relative afferent pupillary defect — present with a large detachment or optic nerve disease and a reason for same-day referral
Red reflex
Through the direct ophthalmoscope at arm's length: a dim or absent reflex suggests vitreous haemorrhage or a dense cataract
Fundoscopy
Direct ophthalmoscopy shows the disc and central retina only. Look for haemorrhage, a grey elevated retina, or disc swelling. A normal view does not exclude a tear, which is usually peripheral
Anterior segment
Redness, photophobia, irregular pupil (uveitis); signs of recent surgery or trauma
BP and glucose
Especially with a vitreous haemorrhage or if diabetic; severe hypertension can cause retinal haemorrhages and disc swelling
Examination in primary care is for triage: acuity, fields and the red reflex identify who needs to be seen today. Retinal tears are usually in the far periphery. Seeing them needs pupil dilation and a slit lamp with a handheld lens or an indirect ophthalmoscope, so a normal direct fundoscopy by a GP should never be used to reassure. Documenting acuity and fields at the first contact also gives the eye service a baseline and protects you if things change.
5
Diagnose

Investigations

Dilated retinal examination Essential
Slit-lamp biomicroscopy with a fundus lens or indirect ophthalmoscopy ± scleral indentation to see the peripheral retina, by an optometrist, community urgent eye service or hospital eye service per local pathway
Vitreous pigment ("tobacco dust")
Seen on slit lamp in the anterior vitreous. Its absence makes a tear less likely (negative LR about 0.23 in Hollands 2009); its presence prompts a very careful search
B-scan ultrasound
When a vitreous haemorrhage or dense cataract blocks the view — shows whether the retina is attached. Performed in the eye service
OCT (optical coherence tomography)
Cross-sectional imaging of the macula: vitreomacular traction, macular hole, macular oedema. Many community optometrists now have OCT
In primary care
Acuity, fields, pupils, red reflex, BP; glucose/HbA1c if diabetic retinopathy is a possible source of haemorrhage; INR if on warfarin with vitreous haemorrhage
Neuroimaging
Only if symptoms are cortical rather than ocular (homonymous field loss, other neurology), arranged through the stroke or neurology pathway
Not routine
Fluorescein angiography, electrodiagnostics — specialist investigations
The one investigation that matters is a dilated peripheral retinal examination by someone equipped and trained to do it. In many areas this is now done in the community by optometrists working in a commissioned urgent eye service, with onward referral if a tear or detachment is found. Vitreous pigment granules were the best-studied examination finding in the Hollands meta-analysis. When pigment is absent a tear is much less likely, but not ruled out, so safety-netting still applies. Ultrasound matters when a haemorrhage hides the retina, because an unseen detachment behind the blood changes the urgency completely.
6
Refer

Referral criteria — emergency eye department or urgent eye service

Same-day eye casualty
Shadow or curtain / field loss | reduced acuity | shower of floaters or suspected vitreous haemorrhage (poor red reflex) | RAPD | pain or redness | recent intraocular surgery or injection | high-risk eye (high myopia, previous tear or detachment) with new symptoms
Urgent dilated exam (usually within 24 hours)
New flashes and/or floaters with no red flags — to an urgent community eye service or optometrist able to dilate, or eye casualty, per local pathway. This is local clinical practice rather than a single national standard
Stroke / TIA pathway
Transient monocular loss or homonymous field loss — NICE NG128: specialist assessment within 24 hours for suspected TIA; 999 if deficit persists
Urgent ophthalmology
Suspected uveitis without severe pain or visual loss (same day if severe); children with new floaters; known proliferative diabetic retinopathy with new floaters
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)
Child with an absent red reflex → consider urgent referral (appointment within 2 weeks) for ophthalmological assessment for retinoblastoma
Routine optometrist
Long-standing, unchanged floaters with normal vision and no flashes. Referral to ophthalmology only if symptoms severely affect quality of life despite reassurance
Reassure and safety-net
Typical migraine aura (gradual spread, both eyes' fields, 5–60 minutes, full recovery) with normal examination
Urgency follows risk. A shadow, reduced vision or a shower of floaters raises the probability of a tear or detachment enough to need same-day assessment. An isolated new floater with flashes still needs a dilated examination soon, because symptoms alone cannot exclude a tear. Most local pathways aim for within about 24 hours. The College of Optometrists guidance advises referral when relevant symptoms occur with any of the examination signs of a break or detachment, and supports managing a confirmed uncomplicated PVD in the community with good advice. Know your local urgent eye service: it is often the fastest route to a dilated examination.
7
Treat

Treatment — condition-specific

Uncomplicated PVD
No treatment. Flashes usually settle over weeks; floaters become less noticeable over weeks to months as the brain adapts and the opacity moves. Normal activities can continue unless the eye service advises otherwise. Give clear written warning symptoms
Retinal tear
Laser retinopexy or cryotherapy by the eye service, usually as an outpatient, to seal the edges of the tear and prevent fluid entering. Follow-up as directed
Retinal detachment
Surgery: vitrectomy with gas or oil, scleral buckle, or pneumatic retinopexy, chosen by the surgeon. After gas: posture as instructed, no flying and no nitrous oxide anaesthesia until the gas has gone (the patient should carry the warning wristband)
Vitreous haemorrhage
Treat the cause: laser for proliferative diabetic retinopathy, anti-VEGF injections in some cases; vitrectomy if it fails to clear or the retina is threatened. Anticoagulation is usually continued — ask the eye team before stopping
Uveitis
Specialist-led topical or systemic treatment; investigate for associated systemic disease
Persistent troublesome floaters
Most people adapt. Vitrectomy for floaters is reserved for severe, persistent symptoms because of its risks (cataract, retinal tear); laser vitreolysis is a specialist option with limited evidence
Migraine aura
Manage migraine per NICE CG150; combined hormonal contraception is contraindicated with migraine with aura (UKMEC 4)
Treatment of tears and detachments is entirely specialist-led. The GP's contribution is speed of referral and good advice. The practical points that matter in primary care are that people with intraocular gas must not fly or have nitrous oxide until the gas has absorbed, and that anticoagulants should not be stopped without discussion. Reassurance for uncomplicated PVD works best when it is paired with specific warning symptoms. "Nothing to worry about" on its own can delay a patient returning when a tear develops later.

Lifestyle

Prevention and risk reduction for high-risk patients

Diabetic eye screening Make sure everyone with diabetes aged 12+ attends the NHS Diabetic Eye Screening Programme (annual, or 2-yearly for those at low risk). Proliferative retinopathy is a preventable cause of vitreous haemorrhage
Glycaemic and BP control Individualised HbA1c targets (NICE NG28 / NG17) and BP targets (NICE NG136) reduce progression of diabetic retinopathy
High myopia Regular optometrist reviews; teach the warning symptoms (shadow, curtain, shower of floaters) and where to go out of hours
After cataract surgery The risk of detachment is raised after intraocular surgery; reinforce the eye unit's discharge advice about sudden floaters, flashes or a shadow
Eye protection Protective eyewear for racquet sports, DIY, power tools and contact sports, especially in myopic eyes or after previous retinal problems
Smoking cessation Smoking is a risk factor for age-related macular degeneration and vascular eye disease; offer a stop-smoking service referral (NICE NG209, 2021)
Driving Drivers must meet the DVLA visual acuity and field standards; anyone whose vision is significantly reduced should not drive until reassessed, and some conditions must be notified to DVLA
Regular sight tests NHS sight tests are free for many groups, including people aged 60+ and people with diabetes or glaucoma
Most PVDs cannot be prevented, so prevention here means preventing the sight-threatening complications of other conditions and making sure high-risk patients present early. Diabetic eye screening detects retinopathy before symptoms develop, and good glycaemic and BP control slows progression. The best protection against losing sight from a detachment is a patient who recognises the warning symptoms and knows where to go out of hours. Driving advice matters because a reduction in acuity or field may fall below the DVLA standard.

Follow-up and safety-netting

After the eye assessment
Record the findings and plan in the notes; make sure the patient understands whether follow-up is booked and why
The next 6 weeks
A tear can develop after a normal first examination. In the Hollands 2009 meta-analysis about 3.4% of those initially found to have an uncomplicated PVD had a tear within 6 weeks. Any new symptoms → re-examine the same day
Planned review
Some eye services re-examine high-risk PVDs (e.g. with vitreous haemorrhage or pigment) at a few weeks; follow the plan they give
Same day / out of hours
"A shadow or curtain, a sudden shower of new floaters, more flashes, or blurred vision — go to eye casualty or call NHS 111 straight away, even at night or at the weekend. Don't wait for a GP appointment."
Re-assess promptly
Symptoms starting in the other eye; floaters that change in character; new distortion of straight lines
Fellow eye
A PVD in one eye is commonly followed by one in the other eye, and a detachment in one eye raises the risk in the other. Explain that the same warning signs apply to both eyes
Written advice
Give written or recorded safety-netting with the local eye casualty contact details and document that you did
Hollands and colleagues (JAMA 2009) found a small but real rate of tears in the weeks after an apparently uncomplicated PVD, about 3.4% within 6 weeks. That is why the safety-net matters more than any single follow-up appointment. The College of Optometrists guidance makes good patient information a condition of managing PVD in the community. Naming the exact symptoms (shadow, curtain, shower, blur) and where to go out of hours gives the patient a plan they can act on. Documenting that advice is the medicolegal safeguard.
