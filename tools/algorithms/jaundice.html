Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Life-Threatening Causes First

Jaundice is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Always exclude emergency and malignant causes before proceeding.

Suspected acute liver failure Acute liver injury with prolonged INR plus new encephalopathy (confusion, drowsiness, asterixis), hypoglycaemia, acidosis, rapidly worsening jaundice or multiorgan dysfunction → 999 + urgent liver/critical-care discussion
Ascending cholangitis Fever/rigors + RUQ pain + jaundice → same-day hospital; 999 if hypotension, confusion, severe sepsis, hypoxia or instability
Haemodynamic instability Hypotension, poor perfusion or altered consciousness — judged on the whole picture → 999
Paracetamol overdose Any suspected overdose → ED now (999 if unwell) + TOXBASE/NPIS advice. Do not delay acetylcysteine for bloods if late, uncertain-timing or staggered — staggered ingestion is not plotted on the nomogram
Jaundice at 40 or over Suspected cancer: pancreatic Suspected cancer pathway referral whether or not there is weight loss (NICE NG12); weight loss, pale stools, itch or a palpable gallbladder (Courvoisier — suggestive, not diagnostic) add concern
Pruritus + pale stools + dark urine Obstructive pattern → urgent USS/HPB assessment; at 40+ the jaundice itself meets the NG12 pancreatic criterion → Suspected cancer
Coagulopathy / bleeding Raised INR needs urgent interpretation (exclude warfarin/DOAC, vitamin K deficiency, chronic disease); spontaneous bruising or GI bleed → Same-day ED (999 if bleeding actively)
Pregnant patient Jaundice in pregnancy → urgent same-day obstetric/medical; 999 if unstable, confused, hypotensive, bleeding, severely unwell or suspected acute liver failure/HELLP/AFLP
Acute liver failure is a medical emergency. Ascending cholangitis (Charcot's triad) progresses to Reynold's pentad (septic shock + confusion) rapidly. Pancreatic head cancer causes obstructive jaundice and has a poor prognosis — an early suspected cancer pathway referral may enable surgical resection. Paracetamol toxicity must be identified within the window for N-acetylcysteine to be effective. AFLP is life-threatening if missed.

Diagnose

Confirm Jaundice — Pattern Recognition and History

Confirm true jaundice (scleral icterus visible when bilirubin >35 µmol/L), then use history to direct the differential.

Confirm jaundice
Scleral icterus (best seen in natural light); check palms, skin. Pseudo-jaundice (hypercarotenaemia) spares sclera
Onset and speed
Sudden onset suggests haemolysis or acute hepatitis; gradual onset suggests obstruction or chronic liver disease
Dark urine + pale stools
Conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia → obstructive or hepatocellular. Absent in prehepatic (haemolytic) jaundice
Pain character
Colicky RUQ = gallstone; Constant epigastric/back = pancreatic; No pain = malignancy, cholestasis, haemolysis
Drug and OTC history
DILI is common. Ask: statins, antibiotics (co-amox, nitrofurantoin), herbal remedies, supplements, new medications in last 3 months
Alcohol history
Units/week; CAGE score; pattern (binge vs daily). Alcoholic hepatitis can present acutely
Travel + exposure
Hep A/E — recent foreign travel, shellfish, water. Hep B/C — IVDU, sexual contacts, tattoos, healthcare worker
Family history
Gilbert's syndrome (benign, fasting-precipitated); haemolytic anaemias; haemochromatosis; Wilson's disease
Systemic features
Weight loss, anorexia, fatigue → malignancy. Fever → infection/cholangitis. Arthralgia → autoimmune hepatitis
History directs the differential more than any single test. The triad of dark urine, pale stools, and pruritis suggests obstructive jaundice, although hepatocellular disease can also cause dark urine and pale stools. Drug-induced liver injury is the most commonly missed cause of jaundice — always take a full drug history including OTCs, herbal remedies, and supplements. Gilbert's syndrome is a common benign cause precipitated by fasting, illness, or exercise — important to recognise to avoid unnecessary investigation.
3
Diagnose

Classify Jaundice Type — Pre-hepatic / Hepatic / Post-hepatic

Classify into one of three mechanistic categories to direct investigation and referral.

Pre-hepatic (haemolytic)
Unconjugated bilirubin ↑; LFTs usually normal (secondary changes or coexisting liver disease possible); dark urine absent; anaemia, splenomegaly. Causes: haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell, G6PD, Gilbert's
Hepatic (hepatocellular)
Mixed bilirubin ↑; raised AST/ALT (often >10× ULN in acute); ALP mildly raised. Causes: viral hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, DILI, autoimmune, cirrhosis, acute liver failure
Post-hepatic (obstructive)
Conjugated bilirubin ↑; ALP/GGT markedly raised; AST/ALT mildly raised; dark urine + pale stools + pruritus. Causes: gallstones, cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic cancer, primary sclerosing cholangitis
LFT pattern guide
Hepatitic: ALT >3× ULN, ALP <3× ULN. Cholestatic: ALP >3× ULN, ALT <3× ULN. Mixed: both elevated. Gilbert's: isolated unconjugated bilirubin, normal LFTs
Key discriminator
Describe the predominant pattern by the relative rise of ALT vs ALP; for suspected DILI use the R-ratio (ALT/ULN ÷ ALP/ULN: ≥5 hepatocellular, 2–5 mixed, ≤2 cholestatic). GGT with ALP suggests a biliary (not bone) source. Conjugated bilirubin is not synonymous with obstruction
Classification is essential because management pathways diverge completely. Hepatocellular jaundice rarely requires imaging as first-line — virology and autoimmune screen drive diagnosis. Cholestatic jaundice almost always requires urgent USS. Haemolytic jaundice needs haematological workup. Getting the pattern right from LFTs saves time and prevents over-investigation. LFTs are the first investigation for all new jaundice presentations.
4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Examination should take <3 minutes and directly modifies the differential and urgency of referral.

Vital signs
Fever + jaundice raises concern for cholangitis — same-day hospital. HR/BP for haemodynamic stability. SaO₂ if unwell
Scleral icterus
Confirm jaundice. Yellow tinge to conjunctiva. Best seen in natural light or with bright examination light
Abdomen
RUQ tenderness = biliary/hepatic pathology. Murphy's sign (cholecystitis). Epigastric mass = pancreatic cancer. Hepatomegaly (smooth = congestion/hepatitis; nodular = cirrhosis/metastases). Splenomegaly = portal hypertension / haemolysis
Courvoisier's sign
Palpable non-tender gallbladder + jaundice raises suspicion of malignant obstruction (not diagnostic) → urgent imaging; at 40+ the jaundice meets the NG12 pathway
Stigmata of CLD
Spider naevi (>5 = significant), palmar erythema, leukonychia, Dupuytren's, parotid enlargement, gynaecomastia, caput medusae, testicular atrophy → suggests chronic liver disease/cirrhosis
Lymphadenopathy
Supraclavicular (Virchow's), axillary — haematological malignancy or metastatic disease
Skin
Scratch marks = cholestatic pruritus. Xanthelasma = chronic cholestasis (PBC). Tattoos/tracks = hepatitis risk
Mental state
Asterixis (liver flap), confusion, drowsiness → hepatic encephalopathy → acute liver failure → 999
A palpable non-tender gallbladder with jaundice (Courvoisier's sign) raises suspicion of malignant obstruction but is not diagnostic — arrange urgent imaging and referral; at 40 or over the jaundice itself meets the NG12 pancreatic criterion. Stigmata of chronic liver disease (especially >5 spider naevi) suggest portal hypertension and require hepatology involvement. A positive Murphy's sign with fever warrants same-day surgical assessment. Asterixis is a critical finding indicating Grade II+ encephalopathy requiring emergency hospital admission.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — Bloods and Imaging

Initial bloods for all new jaundice; imaging urgency set by sepsis, rate of rise, pain, duct dilatation and malignancy concern.

First-line bloods All
Bilirubin (fractionated), ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, albumin, PT/INR, FBC + platelets, U&E, glucose; CRP if infection suspected; TFTs, calcium, CK by the picture
BSG standard aetiology screen All unexplained
Liver USS, HBsAg, HCV antibody (RNA/core antigen if positive), ferritin + transferrin saturation, AMA, ANA, ASMA, immunoglobulins. Acute hepatitis: hep A IgM and hep E (even without travel); EBV/CMV selectively. HBsAg positive → specialist referral and HBV staging
Autoimmune screen Hepatitic/cholestatic
ANA, ASMA, AMA, IgG (in the standard screen at any age) ± LKM-1; a positive antibody alone does not establish autoimmune disease
Metabolic screen
Ferritin + transferrin saturation (haemochromatosis); serum caeruloplasmin if <40 (Wilson's disease); alpha-1-antitrypsin if <50 with no obvious cause
USS abdomen Cholestatic pattern
First-line imaging for suspected obstruction; urgency by clinical picture and local access. Suspected cholangitis → hospital, not outpatient USS
MRCP
Via the specialist/HPB pathway if USS is non-diagnostic but obstruction, PSC or stricture remains suspected. Better than USS for CBD stones and biliary strictures
Haemolytic screen Pre-hepatic
Blood film, reticulocyte count, haptoglobin (low = haemolysis), LDH (raised), Coombs' test if immune haemolysis suspected
Do NOT routinely order
CA 19-9 without imaging evidence of malignancy (high false positive rate). CEA, AFP without specialist guidance. Random biopsy without diagnosis
LFT pattern guides the investigation pathway — ALP-predominant pattern needs prompt abdominal USS (clinical practice); jaundice at age 40 or over meets the NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) suspected cancer pathway criterion for pancreatic cancer. INR/PT is the most sensitive marker of acute hepatic synthetic function — a raised INR in a jaundiced patient needs urgent interpretation but is not automatically acute liver failure — consider anticoagulants, vitamin K deficiency and chronic liver disease. Autoimmune hepatitis is eminently treatable with steroids but missed if screen not requested — include the autoimmune markers in the standard screen. Haemochromatosis is the most common genetic liver condition in the UK (1 in 200 homozygous) and is highly treatable with venesection.
6
Refer

Referral Criteria — Urgency and Destination

Almost all new jaundice in adults requires specialist input. The key decision is urgency of referral.

999
Suspected acute liver failure (encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia); cholangitis with sepsis or instability; shock; active GI bleeding; unwell after overdose; pregnancy with systemic illness
Same-day ED
New jaundice with coagulopathy, renal dysfunction, fever, significant pain, rapidly rising bilirubin, inability to maintain intake or suspected decompensation; suspected paracetamol overdose → ED now, not later in the day; suspected alcoholic hepatitis (steroids for Maddrey DF ≥32 are specialist-only) — no single bilirubin or INR number decides
Suspected cancer: pancreatic / upper GI
Jaundice at 40 or over (with or without weight loss) → pancreatic suspected cancer pathway (NG12) — do not wait for imaging, and a normal ultrasound does not exclude pancreatic cancer; obstruction or duct dilatation → urgent HPB assessment, suspected cancer pathway referral if criteria met or imaging suggests malignancy. NICE NG85: pancreatic-protocol CT before biliary drainage unless drainage is urgent
Urgent (1–2 weeks)
Cholestatic LFTs + normal USS (PSC/PBC suspected); HBsAg or HCV RNA positive; positive autoantibodies supported by pattern, raised IgG or persistent derangement
Routine Hepatology
Chronic viral hepatitis (stable); haemochromatosis; Wilson's disease; PBC (stable); cirrhosis monitoring; unexplained persistent LFT derangement >6 months
Primary care manage
Gilbert's syndrome (isolated unconjugated bilirubin, normal ALT/ALP/GGT/albumin/INR, haemolysis excluded); fatty liver without jaundice and with low fibrosis risk — new jaundice from suspected alcoholic hepatitis needs urgent specialist assessment
The Maddrey discriminant function (4.6 × [PT - control PT] + bilirubin µmol/L ÷ 17) predicts 30-day mortality in alcoholic hepatitis — a score ≥32 identifies severe disease needing hospital and specialist consideration of prednisolone consideration. PSC carries an increased lifetime risk of cholangiocarcinoma and needs specialist-led surveillance. PBC has excellent outcomes with UDCA if treated early. Hepatitis C is now curable in 8–12 weeks with direct-acting antivirals — urgent hepatology referral is essential.

Treat

Treatment Pathway by Cause

Treatment is cause-specific. Most definitive treatments are secondary care — primary care role is stabilisation and specific manageable conditions.

Gilbert's Syndrome
Reassurance Benign
Confirm isolated unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (FBC/retic/LDH if haemolysis possible; not with abnormal ALT, ALP, GGT, albumin or INR). No treatment or routine monitoring once confirmed.
Suspected Alcoholic Hepatitis
Urgent specialist assessment
New jaundice → urgent hospital/hepatology, especially with coagulopathy, renal dysfunction, infection or encephalopathy. Corticosteroids are specialist-only after infection, GI bleeding and renal impairment are addressed. Thiamine if at risk (IV Pabrinex in hospital if Wernicke's suspected); no abrupt cessation in dependence without assisted withdrawal; alcohol services.
Drug-Induced Liver Injury
Review the suspected drug
Stop a suspected non-essential drug after weighing indication and withdrawal risk (specialist advice for essential medicines). Monitor by severity and trend — jaundice, coagulopathy, renal dysfunction, encephalopathy or worsening → urgent specialist. A diagnosis of exclusion; Yellow Card.
Pruritus (cholestatic)
Colestyramine 4–8 g daily (BNF)
First-line for cholestatic itch; separate from other medicines by at least 4 hours (product information). Rifampicin only on specialist advice (hepatotoxicity, interactions). Antihistamines do not treat the cholestatic mechanism, though sedating ones may aid sleep.
PBCSuspected on a persistent cholestatic pattern, compatible imaging and AMA/specialist serology → hepatology. UDCA (about 13–15 mg/kg/day) is usually specialist-initiated; second-line treatment is specialist-led (obeticholic acid carries MHRA liver-injury warnings).
Hep CDirect-acting antivirals via the hepatology/ODN pathway — regimen and duration depend on cirrhosis, prior treatment, renal function, interactions and commissioning. Screen for hepatitis B first (reactivation risk).
Hep BHBsAg positive → specialist referral and HBV staging; any antiviral choice is specialist-led (HBV DNA, ALT, fibrosis, pregnancy, renal and bone health). Vaccinate household contacts; notify per local public-health rules.
HaemochromatosisConfirm iron overload and genotype via hepatology; venesection targets and frequency are specialist-set. Primary care: avoid iron supplements, address alcohol and comorbid liver disease; offer family testing (HFE C282Y).
UDCA for PBC improves transplant-free survival; assessment of treatment response and second-line therapy are specialist-led. Hepatitis C is now a curable disease; failure to refer is a significant clinical governance issue. Rifampicin for cholestatic pruritus is off-label but supported by BSG guidelines when first-line fails — requires hepatology oversight due to hepatotoxicity risk. Venesection in haemochromatosis, if started before cirrhosis, normalises life expectancy. In DILI, rechallenge is generally contraindicated due to risk of severe reaction.

Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions — Essential Adjuncts

Lifestyle interventions are treatment, not optional add-ons. In alcoholic and NAFLD-related disease, lifestyle may be the only effective intervention.

Alcohol cessation Complete abstinence in established alcohol-related liver disease or cirrhosis — but no abrupt stopping in dependence without an assisted-withdrawal plan. Refer to alcohol services; relapse-prevention drugs per specialist advice (naltrexone generally avoided in significant liver disease).
Weight management Gradual, sustained weight loss where overweight, with physical activity and cardiovascular-risk management.
Dietary hepatoprotection Mediterranean-style diet; limit sugary processed foods. Coffee may be discussed but is not a treatment.
Vaccination Offer Hep A + Hep B vaccines to all patients with chronic liver disease. Flu and pneumococcal vaccination per the current Green Book. Check immunity before commencing immunosuppressants.
Medication review Avoid hepatotoxic drugs: NSAIDs, sedatives (precipitate encephalopathy); tailor paracetamol dose to severity, weight, nutrition and alcohol; statins are usually continued unless specialist-advised. Review and document all supplements.
Exercise 150 minutes moderate aerobic/week reduces hepatic fat. Resistance training improves insulin sensitivity. Reduces NAFLD progression independent of weight loss.
Salt restriction If ascites: moderate no-added-salt diet with specialist dietary advice.
Infection vigilance Cirrhosis raises infection risk: prompt treatment of infections; SBP prophylaxis only per hepatology criteria (not routine for all ascites — NG50).
NAFLD is common and a leading cause of cirrhosis referral — lifestyle intervention is the primary treatment as no licenced pharmacotherapy exists for NASH. The impact of alcohol cessation on liver disease is dramatic — even compensated cirrhosis can show hepatic functional recovery. Coffee consumption is one of the most consistent lifestyle hepatoprotective factors in the literature, with data across multiple prospective cohorts. Salt restriction in decompensated cirrhosis reduces readmission rates and diuretic requirements (EASL guidelines 2018, international).
9
Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring and Safety-Netting

All patients with new jaundice need structured follow-up. The interval depends on cause and severity.

1–2 weeks
Repeat LFTs + FBC in selected stable patients — not instead of urgent assessment or referral. Check response to any stopped drug (DILI). Confirm specialist appointment made. Review alcohol intake
4 weeks
LFTs improving? If not, escalate urgency of referral. Check medication side effects (cholestyramine constipation/malabsorption)
3 months
LFT trend: falling is reassuring but does not exclude malignancy, chronic disease or persistent obstruction. Static/rising = escalate. NAFLD without advanced fibrosis: retest for advanced fibrosis every 3 years (NICE NG49); hepatology if fibrosis markers raised (FIB-4 / ELF)
Cirrhosis
Cirrhosis: liver USS every 6 months ± AFP per cause and hepatology protocol — never substitute annual AFP for imaging (NG50). Variceal screening per hepatology
FIB-4 score
Age × AST ÷ (platelet × √ALT). <1.3 low risk under 65 (about 2.0 if over 65); intermediate → ELF/elastography; high (BSG commonly >3.25) → specialist — follow the local GM hepatology thresholds. Use to triage NAFLD/AFLD monitoring in primary care
Safety-net 999
Confusion / drowsiness (encephalopathy); haematemesis or melaena; severe abdominal pain; marked pallor; collapse
Safety-net same-day
Deepening jaundice; new fever; inability to eat/drink; bruising without injury; worsening abdominal distension (ascites)
Patient information
Provide written safety-net advice. Alcohol advice leaflet if relevant. NAFLD patient information. BSG / EASL patient info links
The FIB-4 score is validated for risk-stratification of liver fibrosis in primary care (AUC 0.86 for advanced fibrosis) and is widely used first-line (clinical practice); NICE NG49 (NAFLD/MASLD) recommends considering the ELF test to assess for advanced fibrosis. USS surveillance for HCC every 6 months is recommended in cirrhosis (NICE NG50). Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) presents with subtle signs in cirrhosis — any unexplained deterioration should prompt same-day assessment. Hepatic encephalopathy progresses from Grade I (mild confusion) to Grade IV (coma) rapidly and should always be treated as emergency.
