1
Safety

Red flags — is this leg threatened today?

Stable claudication is a primary-care problem. A leg with rest pain, tissue loss or a sudden loss of circulation is not, and the distinction is made clinically, before any ABPI result. Ask and look for each of these at every PAD contact.
Acute limb ischaemia — the 6 Ps Sudden Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Perishing cold, Paraesthesia, Paralysis. Sensory loss or weakness means the limb is already threatened → 999 / emergency vascular. Hours matter: do not arrange an outpatient test.
Rest pain Burning pain in the forefoot or toes at night or at rest, worse lying flat and eased by hanging the leg over the bed or sleeping in a chair. Persisting beyond 2 weeks it defines chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI) → urgent vascular, telephone the on-call team.
Tissue loss Non-healing ulcer (painful, punched-out, on toes, heel or pressure points) or dry/wet gangrene → urgent vascular. With spreading infection or sepsis → same-day admission.
Diabetic foot with ischaemia Ulcer plus ischaemia, gangrene, suspected deep infection or sepsis is limb-threatening under NICE NG19 (2015): refer immediately to acute services and inform the multidisciplinary foot service.
Sudden deterioration Walking distance collapsing over days to weeks, a newly cold foot or new pulse loss → acute-on-chronic occlusion; discuss with vascular the same day.
Aortic or cardiac emergency Sudden back or abdominal pain with collapse or absent femoral pulses (aortic occlusion, dissection or ruptured AAA), or chest pain / stroke symptoms → 999.
The single most important split is stable claudication versus limb-threatening ischaemia, and it is made on the history and a look at the foot, not on the ABPI. Acute limb ischaemia is a time-critical emergency: muscle and nerve become irreversibly damaged within hours, so sensory loss or weakness means a same-hour decision. Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (rest pain, ulceration or gangrene) needs revascularisation assessment quickly; the Vascular Society of Great Britain and Ireland PAD Quality Improvement Framework (2019, updated 2022) sets targets of revascularisation within 5 days for admitted patients and within 14 days for those managed as outpatients, which is only possible if the GP referral is made by telephone rather than by letter. In a person with diabetes, neuropathy may remove the pain that would otherwise announce rest pain, so the foot must be looked at, not just asked about.
2
Diagnose

Is it really claudication? — the history that discriminates

Intermittent claudication is reproducible, exercise-induced muscle pain (ache, cramp or tightness) that comes on at a fairly consistent distance and settles within a few minutes of standing still. Build the diagnosis from the history before touching the Doppler.
Classic features
Calf ache or cramp on walking, earlier uphill or at pace; never present at the start of walking; relieved by standing still within about 10 minutes (no need to sit or bend); comes back at a similar distance. The Edinburgh Claudication Questionnaire formalises this (Leng and Fowkes, J Clin Epidemiol 1992: sensitivity about 91%, specificity about 99% against a physician diagnosis).
Site maps to level
Buttock / hip → aorto-iliac disease (ask men about erectile dysfunction: buttock claudication + impotence + absent femoral pulses = Leriche pattern). Thigh → iliac or common femoral. Calf → superficial femoral / popliteal (commonest). Foot arch → tibial disease, typical of diabetes.
Record the distance
Distance or time to onset of pain and to having to stop, on the flat. Ask what it stops the person doing (work, shopping, dog-walking). This is the baseline you will judge exercise therapy and referral against.
Risk-factor history
Smoking (pack-years and current status), diabetes, hypertension, lipids, CKD, known coronary or cerebrovascular disease, family history of premature vascular disease, previous DVT (venous claudication), sport and age (young athletes: entrapment, compartment syndrome).
Severity staging
Fontaine: I asymptomatic · IIa claudication at more than 200 m · IIb claudication at less than 200 m · III rest pain · IV ulceration or gangrene. Stages III and IV are CLTI and belong in Step 1.
Differentials and the feature that separates them
Neurogenic claudication (lumbar spinal stenosis)
Pain, numbness or weakness down both legs on standing as well as walking; relieved only by sitting or bending forward; walking uphill, pushing a trolley or cycling are easier; pulses and ABPI normal. See the sciatica pathway.
Venous claudication
Bursting thigh pain after previous iliofemoral DVT, swollen leg, relieved slowly by elevation rather than standing still.
Hip or knee osteoarthritis
Pain at the start of walking, variable distance, joint-line pain on passive movement, eases slowly, worse with weather and weight-bearing, often present at rest.
Chronic exertional compartment syndrome
Young, athletic, tight bursting calf after a set amount of running, slow to settle; pulses normal at rest.
Popliteal artery entrapment
Young athlete with calf claudication; foot pulses weaken with active plantar flexion. Refer to vascular.
Other
Baker's cyst, radicular pain (sciatica — dermatomal, cough or straight-leg-raise provoked), painful neuropathy (burning at rest, glove-and-stocking), statin myalgia (symmetrical, not distance-related).
The commonest diagnostic error is labelling lumbar spinal stenosis as vascular claudication, or the reverse. Two questions separate them well: does standing still relieve it (vascular) or do you have to sit or bend forward (neurogenic), and can you cycle or walk uphill pushing a trolley without pain (neurogenic)? Mapping the site of pain to the arterial level matters because buttock and thigh claudication with weak femoral pulses points to aorto-iliac disease, which is more often suitable for endovascular treatment and can also cause erectile dysfunction that the patient will not volunteer. Recording a walking distance at the first consultation is what allows you to show later that exercise therapy has worked, or that deterioration warrants referral.
3
Diagnose

Cardiovascular risk — PAD is established atherosclerotic disease

A diagnosis of symptomatic PAD places the person in the secondary-prevention group for cardiovascular disease. The legs are the presenting complaint; the heart and brain are where most of the mortality lies.
Do not calculate QRISK3
Risk calculators are for primary prevention. NICE NG238 (2023) advises not using a risk assessment tool for people who already have CVD — which includes symptomatic PAD. Treat as secondary prevention straight away.
Other vascular beds
Ask about exertional chest pain or breathlessness, TIA or stroke symptoms, and listen for carotid and femoral bruits. Polyvascular disease carries the highest event rate and changes antithrombotic decisions (see the protocol).
Diabetes
HbA1c if not known; if known, current control, neuropathy and foot-risk status (NICE NG19). Diabetes plus PAD is the group at highest risk of amputation.
CKD
eGFR and urine ACR. CKD, like diabetes, stiffens tibial arteries and makes the ABPI unreliable (Step 5), and matters for any contrast imaging.
Smoking
Current smoking is the strongest modifiable driver of progression and of failure after revascularisation. Record status and readiness at this visit.
AAA
PAD raises the chance of an aortic aneurysm. NICE NG156 (2020): encourage men aged 66 or over with coronary, cerebrovascular or peripheral arterial disease (or COPD) who have not been screened to self-refer to the NHS AAA screening programme; consider aortic ultrasound in women aged 70 or over with risk factors including PAD.
Patients understand tablets for leg pain as optional; tablets that reduce heart attack and stroke are not. Framing PAD as the same disease as coronary heart disease, affecting the leg arteries, is both accurate and the best adherence strategy. The practical corollaries are that no risk score is needed (NG238), the statin, antiplatelet and blood pressure decisions follow secondary-prevention rules, and every PAD patient should be on the practice CVD register and reviewed annually. Checking for an unscreened AAA is quick and is directly supported by NG156 for men over 66 and women over 70 with vascular disease.
4
Diagnose

Targeted examination

Both legs, shoes off
Colour, temperature gradient, hair and nail changes, shiny atrophic skin. Look between the toes, at the heels and over pressure points for ulcers or fissures — NICE CG147 (2012, updated 2020) asks for the legs and feet to be examined for evidence of critical ischaemia.
Pulses
Femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial on both sides; record present, weak or absent. A single absent foot pulse is common in healthy people; a pattern of absent pulses in the symptomatic leg is not.
Buerger's test
Elevate the leg with the patient supine: pallor on modest elevation, slow return of colour and then dependent rubor (dusky red foot when hanging down) indicate severe ischaemia. A dependent-rubor foot can look deceptively healthy.
Capillary refill
Prolonged refill in the toes supports poor perfusion but is non-specific in a cold room.
Bruits and the aorta
Femoral and carotid bruits; palpate for an expansile abdominal mass (Step 3 on AAA).
Neurological
Monofilament and vibration sense in diabetes — neuropathy masks rest pain and ulceration. Straight-leg raise and lower-limb neurology if a spinal cause is on the list.
ABPI
Measure with a hand-held Doppler at rest in both arms and both ankles (Step 5).
Examination has two jobs: to confirm arterial disease and grade it, and to catch the patient whose claudication story hides critical ischaemia. The foot inspection is the step most often skipped and the most important, because a small heel ulcer or a fissure between the toes changes the referral from routine to urgent. CG147 frames diagnosis as clinical assessment plus ABPI, not ABPI alone, and specifically warns against excluding PAD in diabetes on the basis of a normal or high ABPI. Neuropathy testing belongs here because a person who cannot feel their feet will not report rest pain or an early ulcer.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — ABPI and when it misleads

ABPI Essential
Highest ankle pressure (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial) in each leg ÷ the higher of the two brachial pressures. Recommended for diagnosis in NICE CG147.
Reading the number
Over 1.3 or incompressible → unreliable (calcified vessels). 0.9–1.3 → normal at rest. Below 0.9 → PAD. 0.5–0.9 fits claudication. Below 0.5 → severe disease, consistent with critical ischaemia if symptoms fit. These bands are the ones used on the protocol page.
Normal ABPI, classic story
Do not reassure yet. In diabetes or CKD ask for toe pressures / toe-brachial index or Doppler waveforms. Where the resting ABPI is normal but the history is typical, a vascular lab can repeat the ABPI after exercise.
Bloods
HbA1c, full lipid profile (non-HDL cholesterol is the NICE NG238 monitoring measure), FBC (anaemia worsens symptoms; polycythaemia or thrombocytosis are rare causes of thrombosis), U&E and eGFR, urine ACR in diabetes, liver transaminase as a statin baseline (NG238), and TSH if the lipids are abnormal.
ECG
Atrial fibrillation (an embolic source and an anticoagulation decision) and silent ischaemic changes.
Imaging — not in primary care
Duplex ultrasound is the first-line imaging for anyone being considered for revascularisation (CG147); contrast-enhanced MR angiography, or CT angiography where MRA is unsuitable, is arranged by the vascular team. Imaging only changes management if the person is willing to consider an intervention.
ABPI is the core primary-care test, but it has one important blind spot: medial arterial calcification in diabetes and CKD makes the tibial arteries incompressible, so the cuff pressure and therefore the ABPI read falsely high. CG147 is explicit that PAD should not be excluded in people with diabetes on a normal or raised ABPI alone. Toe pressures work because digital arteries calcify less. The investigations are chosen to find the modifiable drivers (glucose, lipids, renal function) and the embolic source (AF), not to image the arteries; imaging is a specialist decision tied to whether revascularisation is being considered.
6
Refer

Referral — how fast and to whom

999 Emergency
Acute limb ischaemia (6 Ps, especially new sensory loss or weakness) · suspected aortic emergency · sepsis from a foot.
Urgent — phone today
Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia: rest pain for more than 2 weeks, ulceration or gangrene, usually with ABPI below 0.5 or low toe pressures. CG147: people with critical ischaemia should be assessed by a vascular multidisciplinary team before treatment decisions. A diabetic foot ulcer with ischaemia, gangrene or deep infection → acute services immediately (NG19).
Vascular — soon
Rapidly worsening claudication; suspected aorto-iliac disease in a younger patient; suspected popliteal entrapment; diagnostic uncertainty (classic story with a normal or incompressible ABPI).
Routine vascular
Claudication that still limits lifestyle after supervised exercise and optimised risk factors, in someone who would consider angioplasty or bypass (CG147).
Primary care
Stable claudication: secondary prevention, supervised exercise programme referral (via the local vascular or community route), smoking cessation, foot care, annual review.
Full referral wording and letters: see the peripheral arterial disease protocol.
Referral speed follows the limb, not the ABPI number. CG147 positions supervised exercise and risk-factor management before angioplasty for claudication, so a routine referral for intervention is appropriate once those have been tried and the person still has lifestyle-limiting symptoms and wants to consider a procedure. Critical ischaemia is different: the Vascular Society framework expects revascularisation within 14 days of referral for outpatients, so the call should be made the same day. In diabetes, NG19 treats ulceration with ischaemia as limb-threatening and asks for immediate referral to acute services. Older referral rules based on a fixed ABPI or a walking distance of 200 m are not in CG147 and have been removed from this step.
7
Treat

Medical management — summary (detail in the protocol)

This pathway is for diagnosis and triage. The full prescribing detail sits in the peripheral arterial disease protocol; the headline decisions are below so the diagnosis leads straight into action.
Statin
Atorvastatin 80 mg for established CVD (NICE NG238, 2023). Aim for LDL cholesterol 2.0 mmol/L or less, or non-HDL 2.6 mmol/L or less; escalate (e.g. ezetimibe) if not reached.
Antiplatelet
Clopidogrel 75 mg daily is recommended for symptomatic PAD (NICE TA210, 2010); aspirin if clopidogrel is unsuitable.
Blood pressure
NICE NG136 (2019) clinic targets: below 140/90 mmHg under 80 years, below 150/90 mmHg at 80 and over. Type 2 diabetes without CKD uses the same targets. Do not withhold a beta-blocker needed for another indication because of claudication.
Diabetes
Individualised HbA1c target per NICE NG28, reviewed alongside foot risk.
Claudication drug
Naftidrofuryl oxalate is the only drug recommended (NICE TA223, 2011), for people for whom supervised exercise has not helped enough and who prefer not to be referred; review at 3–6 months and stop if no benefit (CG147). Cilostazol and pentoxifylline are not recommended (TA223).
Dual pathway
Rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily plus aspirin is an option for symptomatic PAD at high risk of ischaemic events after a bleeding-risk assessment (NICE TA607, 2019) — usually a specialist-guided decision.
The treatment table has been reduced to decisions, with sources, because the protocol holds the prescribing detail and a pathway that duplicates it risks the two drifting apart. Three corrections are worth knowing. First, the blood-pressure target in PAD with or without diabetes is the NG136 one (below 140/90 under 80; below 150/90 at 80 and over), not the lower European target sometimes quoted. Second, cilostazol is not recommended for claudication (NICE TA223); naftidrofuryl is the only recommended drug, and only after exercise. Third, lipid targets follow NG238 (LDL 2.0 or less, non-HDL 2.6 or less), not the lower European figure.

Lifestyle

Exercise and risk-factor change — the treatment for the walking pain

Supervised exercise programme First-line for intermittent claudication in NICE CG147: 2 hours of supervised exercise a week for 3 months, encouraging walking to the point of maximal pain. Explain that pushing into the ache and resting is what builds collateral flow.
Unsupervised walking Where no programme is available or it is declined: walk most days, to near-maximal pain, rest until it settles, then continue; keep a simple walking diary to see progress.
Stop smoking The single biggest lever for the leg and the heart. Offer behavioural support with varenicline, combination NRT or a nicotine-containing e-cigarette per NICE NG209 (2021); refer to the local stop-smoking service.
Diet and weight Cardioprotective, Mediterranean-style diet; the PREDIMED trial (Estruch et al., NEJM 2018) showed fewer major cardiovascular events with this pattern. Support weight loss where BMI is raised.
Alcohol Keep within the UK Chief Medical Officers' low-risk guidance of 14 units a week, spread over several days.
Foot care Daily inspection, well-fitting shoes, never walk barefoot, prompt treatment of minor injuries; in diabetes, foot-risk classification and podiatry per NICE NG19.
Patients are usually told to rest a painful leg, so exercise therapy needs explaining: repeated walking into claudication pain, then resting, improves walking distance and is recommended by CG147 before angioplasty is considered. Supervised programmes work better than advice alone, but many areas have limited access, so a structured home walking plan is the fallback. Smoking cessation is where primary care has the greatest leverage — continued smoking drives progression, graft and stent failure and amputation — and NG209 supports combining behavioural support with pharmacotherapy or e-cigarettes. Foot care is the cheapest amputation-prevention measure available.

Safety

Follow-up, monitoring and safety-netting

At 3 months
Walking distance and diary; exercise programme attended? Smoking status; statin and antiplatelet tolerated; BP and lipids moving towards target. If naftidrofuryl was started, review benefit at 3–6 months (CG147).
Annually
CVD-register review: BP, lipids (non-HDL), HbA1c, renal function, smoking, medication adherence; foot inspection; repeat ABPI if symptoms change.
Lifestyle-limiting despite all this
Offer routine vascular referral if the person would consider a procedure (Step 6).
999 safety-net
"If your leg suddenly becomes painful, pale, cold, numb or weak, call 999 — do not wait for an appointment." Chest pain or stroke symptoms: 999.
Contact us urgently
Pain in the foot at night or at rest, especially if eased by hanging the leg out of bed · any new sore, ulcer, black or blue area on the foot · walking distance falling quickly over days or weeks.
Documentation
Record the walking distance, ABPI (and whether reliable), pulses, foot findings and the safety-net given — the baseline for the next clinician.
Review schedule and patient wording: peripheral arterial disease protocol.
Follow-up has two separate aims. The cardiovascular aim is to get and keep the secondary-prevention bundle at target, which is what reduces heart attack and stroke. The limb aim is to detect progression early: a shrinking walking distance, new rest pain or a new foot lesion moves the patient from routine to urgent. Safety-netting is most effective in the patient's own words — pain at night, a new sore that will not heal, and a leg that suddenly turns pale, cold and numb — because these are the three events that should bring someone back the same day. Recording the baseline walking distance and ABPI is what makes deterioration visible.
