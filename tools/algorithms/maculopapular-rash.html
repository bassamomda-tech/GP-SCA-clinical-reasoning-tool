1
Safety

Red Flags — Meningococcaemia, SJS/TEN & Vasculitis

Most maculopapular rashes are viral exanthems or drug reactions — benign and self-limiting. The red flags below are dermatological or systemic emergencies that must be identified in the first 60 seconds of assessment.

First 60 seconds — assess: sick or well appearance · airway/breathing/circulation · temperature and blood pressure · non-blanching rash · mucosal involvement · skin pain, blistering or detachment · facial oedema · lymphadenopathy · systemic symptoms · recent drugs and timing · travel, sexual and vaccination history.

Maculopapular rash + non-blanching petechiae/purpura + fever + headache + photophobia Meningococcal septicaemia (Neisseria meningitidis). → 999. Do not wait for petechiae or purpura. A seriously ill patient with fever, meningococcal features or rapidly evolving rash requires immediate emergency treatment even if the rash blanches or is absent. Assess consciousness, respiratory effort, capillary refill, blood pressure, limb perfusion, neck stiffness, rapidly progressive rash, severe limb pain and cold extremities. A glass test is not sufficiently reliable to exclude meningococcal disease. If meningococcal disease is strongly suspected and there is a clinically significant delay in transfer, give pre-hospital IV/IM ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin according to the current local/UKHSA/NICE protocol. Do not delay transfer to administer antibiotics. Check severe beta-lactam allergy. Contact the local Health Protection Team as soon as meningococcal disease is suspected.
Drug rash + blistering + skin detachment + mucosal involvement (eyes, mouth, genitals) + fever Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS, <10% BSA) or Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN, >30% BSA). Typically 7–14 days after first exposure or within 3 days of re-exposure (NICE CG183). → Stop the suspected culprit drug immediately + 999. Emergency admission to a specialist dermatology/burns/critical-care service according to the local pathway. Arrange urgent ophthalmology review if there is ocular involvement. Common causes: allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, sulfonamides, oxicam NSAIDs.
Widespread erythematous rash + mucosal involvement (strawberry tongue, lip cracking, conjunctivitis) + fever >5 days + lymphadenopathy in a child <5 years Kawasaki disease (consider incomplete Kawasaki disease, particularly in infants and young children, and MIS-C). → Any child with persistent fever and mucocutaneous features suggestive of Kawasaki disease requires same-day paediatric assessment. Do not delay referral while waiting for blood tests or echocardiography. Treatment with IVIG, aspirin and echocardiographic monitoring is paediatric-specialist led.
Widespread drug eruption + fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia/atypical lymphocytes or organ involvement (liver, kidney, lung, heart), usually 2–6 weeks after a new drug (can be longer) DRESS syndrome. Eosinophilia may be delayed or initially absent. → Stop the suspected culprit and arrange same-day hospital assessment (admit or discuss urgently with dermatology/acute medicine).
Palpable purpura (raised purpuric lesions) + arthralgia + abdominal pain + haematuria in child or adult IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura) or ANCA-associated vasculitis. Check blood pressure, urine for blood/protein and renal function. In children, repeat urine and blood-pressure monitoring because renal involvement may occur after the initial rash. Same-day hospital assessment if severe abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, acute kidney injury, hypertension, neurological involvement, significant testicular pain/swelling or systemic illness. Do not refer directly to rheumatology without first assessing for renal and gastrointestinal complications.
Maculopapular rash + arthralgia + lymphadenopathy + sore throat in an adult after potential tick exposure or travel Secondary syphilis (copper-coloured rash including palms/soles — suggestive but not diagnostic), HIV seroconversion illness, or EBV/CMV. Syphilis serology through the local sexual-health pathway; HIV fourth-generation test when clinically indicated or per local opt-out policy; EBV/CMV testing only if the clinical syndrome warrants it; travel-specific tests only according to travel history. GUM same week.
The Nikolsky sign is a critical bedside test for distinguishing SJS/TEN from other blistering disorders — lateral pressure applied to normal-appearing skin adjacent to a blistered area causes skin detachment (positive Nikolsky sign) in SJS/TEN (and pemphigus vulgaris), but not in bullous pemphigoid or erythema multiforme. Any patient with a new widespread rash and blistering should have the Nikolsky sign tested: gently press and slide a finger on apparently normal skin — if the epidermis slides off or wrinkles, this indicates epidermal-dermal separation (positive = SJS/TEN or pemphigus). The causative drug principle in SJS/TEN: the 'suspect window' is 1–8 weeks before the rash onset for most drugs. Allopurinol is now the most common cause of SJS/TEN in the UK. The HLA-B*5801 allele (common in South/East Asian patients) dramatically increases allopurinol SJS/TEN risk — pharmacogenomic screening before allopurinol in high-risk patients is recommended by some guidelines but not yet universally implemented in the UK.

Classification — Causes of Maculopapular Rash

Viral exanthems (most common)
Roseola infantum (HHV-6): fever 3–5 days → defervescence → rash (age <3). Rubella: fine pink macules, cephalocaudal spread, lymphadenopathy, arthralgia. Measles: Koplik spots (buccal mucosa) → fever + cough + conjunctivitis → rash cephalocaudal day 3–4. EBV (infectious mononucleosis): sore throat + cervical lymphadenopathy + splenomegaly + rash (especially post-amoxicillin — a drug–virus interaction, not penicillin allergy). Fifth disease (erythrovirus B19): "slapped cheek" → reticular rash trunk/limbs. HIV seroconversion: acute febrile illness + maculopapular rash ± oral ulcers 2–4 weeks after exposure.
Drug reactions
Maculopapular drug eruption (most common drug rash): symmetrical erythematous macules + papules, starts trunk, spreads to limbs. Typically 6–10 days after first exposure or within 3 days of re-exposure (NICE CG183). Pruritic. Commonly: penicillin/ampicillin (most common antibiotic cause — amoxicillin rash does NOT mean penicillin allergy in non-EBV context), sulphonamides, allopurinol, antiepileptics, NSAIDs. Not all drug rashes = drug allergy — must distinguish hypersensitivity from idiosyncratic reaction.
Systemic infections
Secondary syphilis: maculopapular rash involving PALMS + SOLES (suggestive but not diagnostic) + copper-coloured. Condylomata lata. Mucosal patches. Generalised lymphadenopathy. Syphilis serology via local sexual-health pathway. GUM. Rickettsial diseases (tick typhus, Rocky Mountain spotted fever — returning travellers): fever + rash. Dengue fever: biphasic fever + maculopapular rash + thrombocytopenia (travel). Typhoid: rose spots (faint maculopapular) on trunk.
Inflammatory / immune
SLE ("butterfly rash" — photosensitive, malar distribution, spares nasolabial folds) · Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) — salmon-coloured evanescent rash with fever (systemic JIA) · Dermatomyositis (Gottron's papules over knuckles + heliotrope eyelid discolouration) · Kawasaki disease · Drug reaction
The amoxicillin-EBV rash interaction is one of the most frequently misunderstood phenomena in primary care — a large proportion of patients with infectious mononucleosis (EBV) who are given amoxicillin or ampicillin develop a widespread maculopapular rash. This is NOT a penicillin allergy — it is a drug-virus interaction where EBV-infected B-cells produce a specific immune response that makes ampicillin/amoxicillin immunogenic. Patients who develop this rash can safely receive other beta-lactams in the future and should NOT have penicillin allergy recorded in their medical notes. The clinical importance: (1) if a patient with EBV-confirmed mononucleosis develops a rash after amoxicillin, do NOT label them as penicillin-allergic — perform allergy testing before labelling if uncertain; (2) the best way to avoid this rash is to not prescribe amoxicillin/ampicillin for pharyngitis without excluding EBV (monospot test or EBV serology) — prescribing amoxicillin for what appears to be bacterial pharyngitis but is actually EBV is a common cause of amoxicillin rash presentations in primary care.

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

Key history
Onset and progression — separate immediate reactions (minutes to hours: urticaria/anaphylaxis) from non-immediate exanthems (typically 6–10 days after first exposure or within 3 days of re-exposure; NICE CG183). Drug history — all recently started drugs with start dates (SJS/DRESS window) + antibiotic history. Travel history (dengue, typhoid, rickettsial). Sexual history (secondary syphilis, HIV seroconversion). Vaccination status (measles, rubella). Contact with sick people (viral). Fever and systemic symptoms. Mucosal involvement (mouth, eyes, genitals — SJS/DRESS flag).
Examination
Distribution (cephalocaudal spread = measles; palms + soles = secondary syphilis/hand-foot-mouth; butterfly = SLE; extensor surfaces = dermatomyositis). Blanching test (glass test): press glass firmly on rash — blanches = vasodilation (most viral/drug rashes); does NOT blanch = haemorrhage into skin (purpura/petechiae = vasculitis/meningococcaemia). A glass test is not sufficiently reliable to exclude meningococcal disease. Mucosal assessment (mouth, conjunctiva). Lymphadenopathy. Splenomegaly (EBV). Nikolsky sign (only in blistering cases). Temperature. Koplik spots (buccal mucosa — measles).
Investigations
Phenotype-led, when indicated: FBC + differential (eosinophilia = DRESS; lymphocytosis + atypical lymphocytes = EBV; thrombocytopenia = dengue/ITP) · LFTs (DRESS, EBV hepatitis) · Renal function · ESR/CRP · Urine dipstick (if vasculitis/systemic disease suspected) · Targeted syphilis/HIV testing · EBV testing (compatible syndrome only) · travel-specific tests (e.g. malaria, dengue) only when travel/exposure and fever pattern warrant it. Avoid routinely: EBV/CMV serology in every rash, Monospot in all patients, ANA/complement for nonspecific rashes, throat swab for a typical non-pharyngitic exanthem. For possible drug allergy, serum-specific IgE is not a general non-specialist test.
The glass (tumbler) test for non-blanching rash is one of the most important clinical skills in primary care — meningococcal disease begins as a blanching maculopapular rash and evolves to non-blanching purpura as the disease progresses (from sepsis + DIC). Early treatment (before purpura develops) dramatically improves outcome. GPs must check for non-blanching at every febrile rash assessment: press a clear glass firmly against the rash — if the rash disappears (blanches) under pressure, it is caused by vasodilation and is not purpura. If the rash persists under glass pressure (does not blanch), it represents haemorrhage into the skin and is a purpuric or petechial rash — in a febrile patient, this is meningococcal disease until proved otherwise. A glass test is not sufficiently reliable to exclude meningococcal disease — a seriously ill patient with fever, meningococcal features or a rapidly evolving rash needs immediate emergency treatment even if the rash blanches or is absent. In the context of fever + rash + ill patient, a non-blanching result should trigger 999, with pre-hospital antibiotic per current protocol if transfer is delayed.

Secondary syphilis is the classic 'great imitator' — it can mimic virtually any skin condition and is frequently missed in primary care. The clinical clue is the combination of: (1) involvement of the palms and soles (extremely few rashes involve palms and soles — secondary syphilis, hand-foot-mouth disease, and palmoplantar psoriasis are the main differentials); (2) generalised lymphadenopathy; (3) a history of potential sexual exposure (genital ulcer in the past few months — primary syphilis chancre, which may have healed unnoticed); (4) condylomata lata (flat moist wart-like lesions at mucosal junctions — perianal and vulval); (5) oral mucosal patches ('snail-track' ulcers). Secondary syphilis is contagious — the skin lesions teem with Treponema pallidum and direct contact can transmit infection. Any patient with a palms-and-soles maculopapular rash should have syphilis serology through the local sexual-health pathway, regardless of whether syphilis seems likely.

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day emergency
Suspected meningococcal disease (do not wait for a non-blanching rash; pre-hospital antibiotic per current protocol only if transfer is delayed) · SJS/TEN (blistering + mucosal involvement + skin detachment) · DRESS with systemic involvement · Kawasaki disease (child + persistent fever + mucocutaneous features) · Purpura with renal, abdominal or systemic features · Rapidly progressive blistering/erosive rash · Rash with shock, altered consciousness or respiratory compromise
Dermatology (urgent — within 1 week)
Diagnostic uncertainty in a systemically well patient · Persistent or recurrent unexplained rash · Suspected drug allergy needing specialist assessment. (Suspected DRESS, blistering/erosive rash and purpura with systemic features need same-day hospital assessment; urgency of a purpuric rash depends on systemic features, platelet count and renal involvement.)
GUM (same week)
Suspected secondary syphilis · HIV seroconversion illness · Any STI-associated rash
Rheumatology / paediatrics
Suspected SLE · Kawasaki disease · JIA · Dermatomyositis · ANCA vasculitis
GP management
Only manage in primary care if the patient is systemically well, the rash is blanching, there is no mucosal involvement, no skin pain, blistering or purpura, no facial oedema or organ symptoms, and reliable follow-up is available. Provide symptomatic treatment, clear safety-netting and review if the diagnosis is uncertain. Viral exanthem (typical features, self-limiting): reassure + symptomatic. Drug rash (mild, no mucosal involvement, no eosinophilia): consider stopping the suspected drug, antihistamine + emollient, monitor. EBV-related amoxicillin rash: stop amoxicillin, reassure (not penicillin allergy), treat EBV symptomatically.
Kawasaki disease diagnostic criteria require the presence of fever for ≥5 days PLUS at least 4 of: (1) bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis; (2) changes in lips/oral mucosa (strawberry tongue, lip erythema/cracking); (3) changes in peripheral extremities (erythema/oedema of hands/feet acutely, periungual desquamation in convalescent phase); (4) polymorphous rash (erythematous, maculopapular, targetoid — not vesicular); (5) cervical lymphadenopathy (typically unilateral, >1.5 cm). Incomplete Kawasaki disease (fewer than 4 criteria) is increasingly recognised, particularly in infants — any child under 1 year with fever >5 days and even 2–3 features should be assessed for Kawasaki disease. Coronary artery aneurysms are the feared complication, and prompt specialist treatment reduces the risk — making prompt diagnosis genuinely protective. Consider MIS-C and other serious differentials according to the clinical context. Do not delay same-day paediatric referral while waiting for blood tests or echocardiography; echocardiographic monitoring is paediatric-specialist led.

Treat

Management by Cause

Viral exanthem
Supportive only
Antipyretics (paracetamol/ibuprofen), antihistamine for itch (e.g. cetirizine; use age-appropriate doses per current BNF/SPC; avoid routine sedating antihistamines, particularly in children, older adults or those who drive), emollient for dryness. Measles: notifiable to the UKHSA Health Protection Team. Monitor for complications: bacterial superinfection, pneumonia (measles), encephalitis. Rubella exposure in pregnancy: contact the local Health Protection Team/obstetric or infectious-disease service for advice (congenital rubella syndrome).
Drug maculopapular rash
Consider stopping suspected drug
Consider stopping the suspected drug after assessing the indication and availability of alternatives. If the drug is essential, seek specialist advice rather than automatically discontinuing it. Assess for fever, facial oedema, mucosal symptoms, skin pain, blistering, purpura, lymphadenopathy, organ symptoms and eosinophilia. For uncomplicated drug exanthem, provide symptomatic treatment (e.g. cetirizine for itch at age-appropriate dose per current BNF/SPC; emollient) and review. Systemic corticosteroids require specialist advice and are not routine treatment. Monitor for evolution to SJS/DRESS. Document drug and reaction in records.
Secondary syphilis
Refer to sexual health (GUM)
Refer suspected secondary syphilis to sexual health for confirmation, staging, treatment and partner notification. Treatment per current BASHH guideline is GUM-led because of stage confirmation, pregnancy assessment, neurological/ocular symptoms, partner notification, follow-up serology, Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction (fever + rash flare — not allergy) and HIV testing.
SLE
Refer to rheumatology
Suspected SLE requires rheumatology assessment. Hydroxychloroquine and other immunomodulatory treatment, dosing and retinal monitoring are specialist-led. Sunscreen + sun avoidance in the meantime.
Kawasaki disease
Same-day paediatric assessment
Treatment with IVIG, aspirin and echocardiographic monitoring is paediatric-specialist led. Do not prescribe aspirin or arrange IVIG from primary care without discussion with the paediatric team.
The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction after penicillin treatment for syphilis is important to explain to patients before giving the first dose — it is common in secondary syphilis patients treated with penicillin, typically within hours of the first injection. It consists of: fever, rigors, myalgia, headache, and transient worsening of the skin rash. It is caused by the sudden release of bacterial antigens from dying treponemes, triggering an immune reaction. It is NOT an allergic reaction to penicillin and does NOT mean the patient is penicillin-allergic. Patients should be warned explicitly: 'In the next 12 hours after the injection, you may develop fever, chills, and muscle aches, and your rash may temporarily look worse. This is a sign the treatment is working — it means the antibiotic is killing the bacteria. It will pass within 24 hours. Take paracetamol if uncomfortable.' Mistaking the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction for penicillin allergy and switching to an inferior antibiotic is a recognised error in syphilis management.

Treat

SJS/TEN, DRESS & Severe Drug Reactions

SJS / TEN — immediate management
Stop the suspected culprit drug immediately (especially allopurinol, antiepileptics, sulfonamides, NSAIDs) and urgently review all recently started medicines with dermatology/toxicology/acute medical support. Avoid re-exposure to the suspected culprit. Do not stop essential medicines automatically without specialist advice unless the clinical situation requires it. → 999 + emergency admission to a specialist dermatology/burns/critical-care service according to the local pathway. Arrange urgent ophthalmology review if there is ocular involvement. Do not initiate systemic corticosteroids, ciclosporin, IVIG or other immunomodulatory treatment in primary care. These are specialist decisions after emergency admission.
DRESS — immediate management
Suspect DRESS in a patient with a widespread drug eruption plus fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia/atypical lymphocytes or organ involvement. Eosinophilia may be delayed or initially absent. Stop the suspected culprit and admit or discuss urgently with dermatology/acute medicine (same-day hospital assessment). Systemic corticosteroids are commonly used for significant organ involvement but should be initiated under specialist guidance. Symptoms may persist or worsen weeks after drug cessation. Arrange specialist follow-up for delayed autoimmune complications. Monitoring should be individualised according to organs involved and may include thyroid function, glucose and other tests over subsequent months. Document all suspect drugs as contraindicated in the medical record.
Drug allergy documentation
After any drug rash document (NICE CG183): generic and proprietary drug name; formulation, route and dose where known; indication; date and time; number of doses/days before reaction; signs, symptoms and severity; treatment required; drugs or classes to avoid; and whether it is a suspected allergy or an adverse drug reaction (amoxicillin-EBV: NOT allergy). Record drug allergy separately from non-allergic adverse effects, using SNOMED-CT allergy codes correctly (penicillin allergy is systematically over-coded). Refer for specialist drug-allergy assessment when confirmation would materially affect future treatment options or when the reaction was severe, immediate, anaphylactic or otherwise uncertain. Do not undertake drug challenge in primary care unless operating within an approved specialist protocol with appropriate resuscitation facilities.
The penicillin allergy over-labelling problem is one of the most significant antibiotic stewardship issues in UK primary care — many people in the UK are labelled as 'penicillin allergic,' but most labelled patients do not have true IgE-mediated penicillin allergy. The most common reasons for false penicillin allergy labelling: (1) amoxicillin-EBV rash (as above); (2) maculopapular drug rash that was incorrectly attributed to penicillin (rather than the true cause — virus, another drug); (3) family history of allergy mistakenly applied to the patient; (4) vague childhood rash for which no documentation exists. The consequences of false penicillin allergy labelling: patients receive inferior second-line antibiotics for serious infections (with higher C. difficile risk from broad-spectrum alternatives), higher cost, and reduced treatment options. NHS penicillin allergy delabelling programmes have been developed — drug challenge should only be undertaken within an approved specialist protocol with appropriate resuscitation facilities, not ad hoc in primary care; refer for specialist drug-allergy assessment when confirmation would materially affect future treatment.

Lifestyle

Public Health, Prevention & Patient Education

Measles notification Measles is a notifiable disease — any clinical suspicion must be reported to the local Health Protection Team (HPT) immediately by phone (before laboratory confirmation). Document the notification in the medical record. Isolate a suspected case promptly on arrival — do not let them sit in a communal waiting room. Avoid school/work for 4 full days after rash onset. Arrange oral-fluid/laboratory testing through UKHSA pathways. Post-exposure advice for contacts as directed by the HPT (UKHSA measles guidance).
Vaccination — measles prevention Two doses of measles-containing vaccine; check the current schedule and catch-up advice in the UKHSA Green Book (chapter 21, measles) rather than relying on a fixed schedule here. Contraindicated: immunocompromised, pregnancy. Measure MMR coverage in practice population (QOF indicator) and identify and vaccinate unvaccinated individuals opportunistically.
Sun protection for photosensitive rashes SLE, PMLE (polymorphous light eruption), dermatomyositis, drug-induced photosensitivity (doxycycline, thiazides, amiodarone, NSAIDs, some statins): UVA/UVB SPF50+ sunscreen applied 30 min before sun exposure + reapplied every 2 hours. UVA-protective clothing (broad-spectrum protection). Avoid midday sun. Sun protection for SLE patients is not cosmetic — UV exposure can trigger systemic lupus flares including nephritis.
Sexual health after secondary syphilis Partner notification for all sexual contacts in the past 2 years (secondary syphilis). Follow current BASHH/GUM advice on when to resume sexual activity. Annual syphilis screening for sexually active MSM or high-risk individuals. HIV PrEP consideration. GUM-led follow-up serology per current BASHH guideline.
Drug allergy documentation and communication After any drug rash: document in the clinical record, add allergy coding, and communicate to the patient in writing what drug caused the reaction and what to avoid in the future. Patient-held allergy card. Medical alert bracelet if SJS/TEN history (life-threatening allergy). Notify hospital records for multi-site access.
Kawasaki disease — long-term cardiac education Families of children with coronary artery aneurysms: contact sport restrictions (if large aneurysms). Annual cardiology review. Aspirin therapy education (Reye syndrome risk with viral infections — paediatrician advises on management during viral illness). MedicAlert bracelet documenting aneurysm status.
Sun care and drug photosensitivity Common photosensitising drugs: tetracyclines (especially doxycycline), thiazides, amiodarone, fluoroquinolones, sulphonamides, NSAIDs (piroxicam — high risk), vemurafenib. Counsel at prescription: wear SPF50+ sunscreen and protective clothing when outdoors. Photosensitivity reactions range from mild sunburn-type reactions to severe blistering.
Rubella in pregnancy Rubella (German measles) in early pregnancy can cause congenital rubella syndrome (deafness, cataracts, cardiac defects, microcephaly). Pregnant woman exposed to a rubella-like rash: contact the local Health Protection Team/obstetric or infectious-disease service for advice on exposure assessment and appropriate testing (rubella IgM can be falsely positive and should be interpreted with specialist/public-health advice). There is no routine role for MMR as post-exposure prophylaxis. Do not administer MMR during pregnancy (Green Book chapter 28).
The measles notification responsibility is a legal requirement under the Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010 — measles is a notifiable disease in England (similar lists in Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland). GPs must notify the local Health Protection Team by telephone (not just by submitting the lab form) when they suspect measles — clinical notification is required, and laboratory confirmation is a separate process. The reason telephone notification is required (not waiting for the lab): measles is highly contagious, and rapid contact tracing and post-exposure prophylaxis (MMR within 72 hours or HNIG — human normal immunoglobulin — within 6 days for immunocompromised contacts) can prevent secondary cases. A single missed notification in a community with low vaccine coverage can allow a cluster to become an outbreak. The UKHSA HPT telephone number for the local area is available on the gov.uk HPT directory.

Follow-Up, Notifications & Safety-Netting

Viral exanthem follow-up
Not routine for mild cases. Return if: rash not improving at 1 week, systemic features develop (high fever, joint swelling, lymphadenopathy), rash becomes non-blanching. Measles: notification + isolation advice + check vaccination status of close contacts.
Drug rash follow-up
Review at 1 week: is rash improving (typical drug rash improves 1–2 weeks after drug stopped)? New mucosal lesions? Fever? If any concern for evolution to SJS/DRESS → hospital immediately. Document drug and reaction type in medical record.
Secondary syphilis
GUM-led follow-up serology, partner notification and HIV testing per current BASHH guideline.
Suspected SLE
Refer to rheumatology. Treatment (including hydroxychloroquine) and its monitoring are specialist-led. Sunscreen and sun avoidance.
Return immediately
Non-blanching rash developing in febrile patient → 999 (meningococcaemia) · Blistering or mucosal involvement with any rash → SJS/DRESS → 999 + stop the suspected culprit drug · Rash + severe abdominal pain + blood in stool → HSP/vasculitis → hospital
Same-day review
Drug rash worsening despite stopping drug after 48h · Fever developing with drug rash · Lymphadenopathy appearing with rash + LFT elevation · Child with persistent fever + rash + mucocutaneous changes → same-day paediatric assessment (Kawasaki)
The SJS/TEN drug documentation principle is one of the most important medico-legal and safety actions after a patient survives Stevens-Johnson syndrome or TEN — the causative drug must be: (1) documented as a severe allergy in the patient's medical record immediately; (2) communicated to the patient in writing with a clear 'never take this drug or related drugs again' instruction; (3) entered as an allergy in all NHS electronic records (GP system, Summary Care Record, hospital records); and (4) the patient should be advised to carry a medical alert card or wear a MedicAlert bracelet. Re-exposure to the causative drug after SJS/TEN can cause the condition to recur with potentially fatal consequences. The cross-reactivity patterns are important: sulfonamide allergy → avoid all sulfonamide-containing drugs (dapsone, co-trimoxazole, furosemide has sulfonamide structure but clinical cross-reactivity is rare). Carbamazepine → may cross-react with other aromatic antiepileptics (phenytoin, phenobarbital, lamotrigine — but cross-reactivity is unpredictable). Always document the specific drug, not just the class, when recording SJS/TEN allergy.
