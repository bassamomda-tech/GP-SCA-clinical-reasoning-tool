1
Safety

Red Flags — Hypereosinophilic Syndrome & Organ Damage

Descriptive categories: mild approximately 0.5–1.5, moderate 1.5–5.0, severe >5.0 × 10⁹/L — but the count does not reliably predict organ damage. Significant disease can occur with lower counts, while some patients with very high counts remain well. Hypereosinophilia generally means an absolute eosinophil count ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L confirmed on at least two occasions, usually at least 4 weeks apart. Persistent eosinophilia ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L warrants investigation (BSH), and any eosinophilia with organ damage needs urgent evaluation without waiting for duration criteria.

Eosinophilia + cardiac symptoms (chest pain, dyspnoea, syncope, palpitations, heart failure symptoms or thromboembolism) Eosinophil-associated cardiac injury / endomyocardial disease (Löffler endocarditis) — eosinophil-mediated cardiac damage: endomyocardial fibrosis, thrombus formation, restrictive cardiomyopathy. Same-day hospital assessment: ECG, troponin and echocardiography. Call 999 if haemodynamic instability, severe chest pain, syncope, acute heart failure or respiratory compromise. Haematology + cardiology. Organ damage from eosinophilia is the key emergency — cardiac involvement is the leading cause of death in hypereosinophilic syndrome.
Hypereosinophilia with eosinophil-attributable organ or tissue damage Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) — requires persistent or recurrent hypereosinophilia, evidence of eosinophil-attributable organ or tissue damage, and exclusion of an alternative cause. Do not wait for a duration threshold (4 weeks, or the traditional 6 months) before urgent assessment or treatment where there is cardiac, neurological, respiratory or other organ damage. Causes: lymphocytic variant (Th2 cytokine-driven), myeloproliferative variant (FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion — imatinib-sensitive), idiopathic. Urgent haematology.
Eosinophilia + adult-onset or difficult asthma + chronic rhinosinusitis/nasal polyps + neuropathy/mononeuritis multiplex + purpura Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA, formerly Churg-Strauss) — ANCA-associated vasculitis; also pulmonary infiltrates, renal, cardiac or gastrointestinal involvement. ANCA (MPO) is positive in only a minority (approximately 40%) — many patients are ANCA-negative, so ANCA must not be used to exclude the diagnosis. Organ-threatening. Urgent rheumatology; treatment is specialist-led.
Eosinophilia + marked weight loss + fever + drenching night sweats + lymphadenopathy Haematological malignancy (Hodgkin lymphoma — reactive eosinophilia is a classic paraneoplastic feature; T-cell lymphoma with IL-5 production; myeloid neoplasm with eosinophilia). FBC + blood film + LDH + LFTs; imaging as directed by haematology or the suspected cancer pathway. Urgent haematology advice; consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly (lymphoma, NICE NG12).
Eosinophilia + peripheral neuropathy + skin lesions + travel history Strongyloides stercoralis hyperinfection (in immunosuppressed — particularly steroid-treated patients from tropical regions) — can cause life-threatening disseminated strongyloidiasis. Strongyloides IgG serology (stool microscopy has limited sensitivity). Suspected hyperinfection or sepsis → same-day hospital and urgent infectious diseases/tropical medicine — prolonged specialist treatment (often daily ivermectin until parasitological clearance) plus management of bacterial sepsis.
Drug-induced eosinophilia + pulmonary infiltrates + fever DRESS syndrome (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms) or eosinophilic pneumonia from drug (NSAIDs, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, allopurinol, antiepileptics). Stop the suspected culprit drug. Same-day hospital/dermatology assessment if systemic features. DRESS = life-threatening.
Eosinophilia + new peripheral neuropathy, mononeuritis multiplex, focal deficit, confusion or seizures Urgent hospital/specialist assessment — do not simply arrange outpatient ANCA testing.
Eosinophilia + hypoxia, significant breathlessness, pulmonary infiltrates or haemoptysis Same-day hospital assessment — a chest radiograph alone is insufficient where severe disease is suspected.
Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) cardiac involvement (Löffler endocarditis) is the most feared complication of sustained hypereosinophilia — eosinophils degranulate and release toxic proteins (major basic protein, eosinophil cationic protein) that directly damage cardiac myocytes, endocardium, and heart valves. The pathological sequence is: acute necrotic phase (eosinophil infiltration + granule release → myocyte death) → thrombotic phase (mural thrombus formation on damaged endocardium) → fibrotic phase (endomyocardial fibrosis → restrictive cardiomyopathy). Each phase can cause sudden death or permanent cardiac impairment. Cardiac assessment (ECG, troponin, echocardiography) is therefore needed when eosinophilia is significant or persistent, or there are cardiac symptoms — catching endomyocardial involvement in the early phase before fibrosis develops is critical. EGPA (formerly Churg-Strauss syndrome) is a fascinating disease with a characteristic three-phase presentation that GPs should recognise: Phase 1 — allergic phase (asthma + allergic rhinitis — can precede vasculitis by years); Phase 2 — eosinophilic phase (blood and tissue eosinophilia, eosinophilic pneumonia, gastroenteritis); Phase 3 — vasculitic phase (mononeuritis multiplex, skin purpura, pericarditis, renal involvement). The triad of asthma + peripheral eosinophilia + mononeuritis multiplex is EGPA until proved otherwise.

Classification — Causes of Eosinophilia

Mild eosinophilia (0.5–1.5 × 10⁹/L)
Atopic disease (most common in the UK — asthma, eczema, allergic rhinitis, food allergy): usually mild; may persist despite good symptom control — interpret the count independently of symptom control. Drug reactions: NSAIDs, antibiotics (ampicillin, cephalosporins), antiepileptics, allopurinol, ACEi. Worm infestation: Toxocara, Toxoplasma, Ascaris. Mild parasitic infection.
Moderate eosinophilia (1.5–5.0 × 10⁹/L)
Helminth infections (tissue-invasive): Strongyloides (IgG serology; stool PCR/specialised culture via tropical medicine/microbiology), Toxocara (ELISA), Ascaris (stool microscopy), hookworm, Trichinella, Filaria, Schistosoma (travel history). EGPA (Churg-Strauss). Reactive to malignancy (Hodgkin lymphoma). Eosinophilic pneumonia. Adrenal insufficiency (Addison's — cortisol normally suppresses eosinophils).
High eosinophilia / hypereosinophilia (>5.0 × 10⁹/L)
Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES): FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion (imatinib-sensitive) or lymphocytic variant (IL-5-driven). Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia. Eosinophilic granulomatosis (EGPA). T-cell lymphoma (IL-5 secreting). Tropical eosinophilia (Filaria).
Interpreting the count
Categories are descriptive only — the count does not reliably predict organ damage. Confirm an unexpected result with a repeat FBC with differential and blood film, and review previous counts and laboratory reference ranges.
The helminth infection-eosinophilia connection is critically important in primary care because UK GPs increasingly see patients from countries where tissue-invasive helminths are endemic, and because immunosuppressive treatment in a patient with undiagnosed strongyloidiasis can cause life-threatening disseminated disease. The key principle: any patient from a tropical/subtropical region (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, South America, Southeast Asia, Southern Europe — Strongyloides is present in some Southern European areas) who requires systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy should be screened for Strongyloides serology first, based on exposure geography and clinical risk (UKHSA: Strongyloides can persist for decades). Corticosteroids reduce eosinophil-mediated killing of Strongyloides larvae and allow unchecked autoinfection → hyperinfection syndrome → gram-negative bacteraemia from larvae carrying gut bacteria through the intestinal wall → potentially fatal sepsis. Ivermectin (commonly 200 micrograms/kg, regimen per current tropical medicine/local protocol) is used for uncomplicated infection; patients who are immunosuppressed or need urgent immunosuppression should be discussed urgently with infectious diseases/tropical medicine rather than relying on a simple two-dose regimen. This screening principle should be part of the standard pre-immunosuppression workup for patients from endemic regions.

Diagnose

Assessment — History & Investigations

History
Geographic/travel history (tropical travel → helminths) · Atopy history (asthma, eczema, hayfever) · Drug history (NSAIDs, antibiotics, allopurinol, antiepileptics — all can cause eosinophilia) · Systemic symptoms (weight loss, fever, night sweats — malignancy/vasculitis) · Pulmonary symptoms (asthma, cough, dyspnoea — EGPA, eosinophilic pneumonia) · Neurological symptoms (neuropathy, CN palsy — EGPA, HES) · Skin changes (rash, urticaria, angioedema, facial oedema, fever) · Organ-damage symptoms (chest pain, palpitations, syncope, thromboembolism, haemoptysis, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, renal symptoms) · Migration and soil/freshwater exposure · Previous FBCs · Examination: lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, rash, respiratory, cardiac and neurological findings
Initial tests (persistent unexplained or ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L, or systemic illness)
Repeat FBC with differential · Blood film (confirm morphology, exclude blast cells) · Renal function + eGFR · LFTs · Bone profile · LDH · ESR and/or CRP · Vitamin B12 · medication and travel review (BSH)
Investigations — targeted
Strongyloides IgG serology (relevant exposure to endemic areas, before immunosuppression — key initial test) · Stool microscopy / PCR / specialised culture (limited sensitivity — via tropical medicine/microbiology; three routine samples are not a universal requirement) · Toxocara IgG ELISA (child, dog contact) · ANCA (only if EGPA or another ANCA-associated vasculitis is clinically suspected; a negative ANCA does not exclude EGPA) · ECG, troponin, echo (significant/persistent eosinophilia or cardiac symptoms — not indiscriminately in every mild case) · IgE (if atopy, allergic disease or a lymphocytic variant suspected — not required for every patient) · TSH (only if clinically indicated) · CXR (eosinophilic pneumonia, EGPA) · Bone marrow + molecular testing (haematology-led: persistent unexplained ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L, abnormal film, cytopenias, splenomegaly, constitutional symptoms or marked eosinophilia)
The blood film is mandatory for any unexpected or significant eosinophilia before proceeding to further investigations — an unexpected result should be confirmed with a repeat FBC and blood film. The blood film allows manual examination of eosinophil morphology, confirmation of the count, identification of blast cells (leukaemia), hypersegmented eosinophils (HES), and reactive changes. Before arranging tropical parasitology serology or ANCA testing, confirming that the eosinophilia is real on film is a key quality step. Total IgE is not required for every patient; where atopy, allergic disease or a lymphocytic variant is suspected, a markedly elevated total IgE supports an atopic or parasitic cause, while a normal IgE with significant eosinophilia suggests a non-allergic cause (EGPA, HES, malignancy, drug reaction). Specific IgE to common aeroallergens (grass pollen, HDM, cat) can confirm atopic aetiology.

Diagnose

Severity Assessment & Differential Summary

Mild eosinophilia + atopy history + no systemic symptoms
Likely reactive — atopic disease or drug reaction. Review all medications. Primary care review is reasonable for a well patient with a single mild elevation, clear atopy, no systemic symptoms and a normal examination. Optimise atopy treatment (antihistamines, nasal steroids for rhinitis, ICS for asthma). Repeat testing guided by the count, persistence, symptoms and local haematology advice — not automatically at 3 months or annually.
Mild eosinophilia + travel history + no atopy
Strongyloides IgG serology if relevant exposure to an endemic area (key initial test); other helminth serology guided by exposure. Stool microscopy has limited sensitivity — stool PCR or specialised culture via tropical medicine/microbiology. Toxocara ELISA if dog contact. If positive → treat and recheck eosinophil count (should normalise within 3 months).
Eosinophilia + pulmonary symptoms + asthma + sinusitis + any neuropathy
EGPA until proved otherwise. ANCA + CXR + urinalysis (haematuria/proteinuria) → urgent (same-week) rheumatology; same-day hospital if organ-threatening (neurological deficit, hypoxia, cardiac or renal involvement). ANCA positivity is not required.
Eosinophilia + weight loss + lymphadenopathy + night sweats
Haematological malignancy screen: LDH, LFTs, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, urgent haematology advice; suspected cancer pathway referral if unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly (lymphoma).
Persistent unexplained ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L, marked or rising eosinophilia, abnormal film, cytopenias, splenomegaly or constitutional symptoms
Urgent haematology advice. ECG, troponin and echo for cardiac assessment. Molecular testing and bone marrow assessment coordinated by haematology. Do not use >5.0 × 10⁹/L as the only urgent criterion — a count of 2.0 × 10⁹/L with cardiac or neurological involvement may be more urgent than an asymptomatic count of 8.0 × 10⁹/L.
The drug eosinophilia timeline is practically important — drug-induced eosinophilia typically appears 1–4 weeks after starting the causative drug, and resolves within 3 weeks of stopping it. If eosinophilia persists beyond 3–4 weeks after drug cessation, an alternative or additional cause should be sought. Allopurinol is one of the most common causes of drug-induced eosinophilia in primary care (given its widespread use for gout), and can also cause the more severe DRESS syndrome — a patient starting allopurinol who develops eosinophilia should be checked for rash, fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis, renal injury and respiratory symptoms; stop the drug if clinically safe and recheck the count. Eosinophilia alone is not always a reason to stop a necessary drug — seek specialist advice when the drug is essential or the diagnosis is uncertain. If DRESS is suspected (eosinophilia + fever + skin rash + organ involvement) — same-day hospital assessment.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day hospital (999 if unstable)
Eosinophilia with chest pain, dyspnoea, syncope, acute heart failure or thromboembolism · Suspected DRESS with systemic involvement · Hyperinfection/sepsis in a patient at risk of Strongyloides · Acute neurological deficit or rapidly progressive neuropathy · Severe pulmonary infiltrates or hypoxia
Haematology (urgent)
Persistent eosinophilia ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L without an obvious cause · Eosinophilia with abnormal blood film, blasts, cytopenias, splenomegaly or constitutional symptoms · Marked eosinophilia, particularly if rising · Any evidence of eosinophil-attributable organ injury · Suspected HES or chronic eosinophilic leukaemia
Rheumatology (same week)
Same-week or urgent referral for suspected EGPA, especially with neuropathy, pulmonary infiltrates, renal disease, purpura or cardiac disease. ANCA positivity is not required.
Tropical medicine / ID
Relevant parasite risk or confirmed/suspected tropical helminth infection (Strongyloides, Filaria, Schistosoma, Visceral larva migrans) · Pre-immunosuppression screening positive · Immunosuppressed or needing urgent immunosuppression with Strongyloides risk
Cardiology
Echo showing endomyocardial changes + eosinophilia · HES with cardiac involvement → specialist cardiac management
GP management
Reasonable for a well patient with a single mild eosinophil elevation, clear atopy, no systemic symptoms and no abnormal examination: optimise allergy treatment. Drug-induced (uncomplicated, asymptomatic): stop the suspected drug if clinically safe + recheck. Simple helminth (Toxocara/Ascaris): treat + recheck. Repeat testing guided by the count, persistence, symptoms and local haematology advice — not automatically at 3 months or annually.
The Tropical Medicine/Infectious Diseases referral for suspected strongyloidiasis before immunosuppression is a GP-level decision — any patient from an endemic region (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia) with eosinophilia who requires corticosteroids, rituximab, anti-TNF therapy, or other immunosuppression should be screened for Strongyloides BEFORE immunosuppression starts. If Strongyloides serology is positive, discuss with infectious diseases/tropical medicine and complete treatment per current protocol before initiating immunosuppression. Consider empiric treatment only when urgent immunosuppression cannot wait for testing, exposure risk is substantial, testing is unavailable or likely falsely negative, or on specialist advice — not routinely for every patient with a remote travel history. This is a straightforward, life-saving protocol that GPs initiating immunosuppressive therapy should know. Regional infectious diseases/tropical medicine units and the UKHSA Rare and Imported Pathogens Laboratory provide specialist advice on tropical parasite diagnosis and treatment for GPs unfamiliar with these agents.

Treat

Treating Specific Causes

Atopic eosinophilia
Optimise atopy treatment
Loratadine/cetirizine 10 mg OD. ICS for asthma. Intranasal steroid for rhinitis. Dupilumab for severe atopic eczema/asthma (specialist). Eosinophilia may persist despite good asthma/eczema control — interpret the count independently of symptom control, and do not attribute persistent or progressive eosinophilia to atopy without reconsidering other causes.
Strongyloides
Ivermectin — regimen per tropical medicine/local protocol
Uncomplicated infection in an immunocompetent patient: ivermectin commonly 200 micrograms/kg, with the exact regimen determined by the current tropical medicine/local protocol. Albendazole is less effective for Strongyloides. Immunosuppressed or needing urgent immunosuppression: discuss urgently with infectious diseases/tropical medicine — do not rely on a simple two-dose regimen. Hyperinfection: hospital, prolonged specialist treatment (often daily ivermectin until parasitological clearance) and management of bacterial sepsis. Follow-up of serology and eosinophil count per specialist advice.
Toxocara
Albendazole 400 mg BD × 5 days
May repeat if eosinophilia persists. Add prednisolone if ocular toxocariasis or severe visceral larva migrans (ophthalmology + ID co-management). Recheck FBC and serology at 3 months.
EGPA
Urgent rheumatology — specialist-led treatment
Suspected EGPA requires urgent rheumatology assessment. Do not initiate cyclophosphamide or rituximab in primary care. Corticosteroid treatment may be required urgently when there is organ-threatening disease, but should be discussed with the receiving rheumatology/acute medical team unless delay would be dangerous. Mepolizumab is not standard NHS treatment on the basis of NICE TA845 — that appraisal was terminated and NICE was unable to make a recommendation.
HES (FIP1L1-PDGFRA+)
Haematology-led (imatinib-sensitive)
FIP1L1-PDGFRA is an imatinib-sensitive fusion. Dose, monitoring and treatment duration are haematology-led and depend on the molecular lesion, disease burden and organ involvement. Molecular testing (FISH/PCR) and bone marrow assessment are coordinated by haematology. Any patient with persistent unexplained eosinophilia ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L, marked eosinophilia, abnormal blood film, splenomegaly, cytopenias, constitutional symptoms or possible organ injury requires urgent haematology advice.
Drug-induced
Stop causative drug
Stop the suspected drug if clinically safe and document the suspected reaction. Check for rash, fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis, renal injury and respiratory symptoms. Do not rechallenge if DRESS, SJS/TEN, hepatitis, nephritis or a significant systemic reaction is suspected. Seek specialist advice when the drug is essential or the diagnosis is uncertain — eosinophilia alone is not always a reason to stop a necessary drug. If DRESS: same-day hospital; systemic corticosteroids are specialist-led. Recheck FBC after stopping.
Mepolizumab (anti-IL-5 monoclonal antibody) may be used by specialists for refractory EGPA, but it is not standard NHS treatment on the basis of NICE TA845 (that appraisal was terminated without a recommendation because no evidence submission was made) — it blocks IL-5, the primary cytokine driving eosinophil production and survival, and achieves significant reduction in relapse rates and steroid requirements in patients with EGPA not controlled by standard treatment. This is relevant for GPs managing patients with EGPA on long-term systemic steroids — awareness of mepolizumab as an available option means appropriate specialist referral (rheumatology or respiratory) for EGPA patients still on significant steroid doses. The imatinib-PDGFRA mechanism is a landmark discovery in precision oncology: the FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion oncogene (which drives the myeloproliferative variant of HES) is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that is highly sensitive to imatinib. Dose, monitoring and treatment duration are haematology-led.

Treat

Specific Scenarios & DRESS

DRESS syndrome management
Recognise: recent drug exposure (often 2–8 weeks previously), fever, widespread rash or facial oedema, eosinophilia or atypical lymphocytosis, lymphadenopathy, internal-organ involvement (particularly liver, kidney, lung or heart). Stop the suspected culprit drug and any non-essential potentially causative medicines after careful medication review — do not stop essential medicines indiscriminately without considering the indication and withdrawal risks. Seek urgent hospital/dermatology assessment. Hospital assessment: FBC and film, renal function and urinalysis, LFTs, CRP, ECG/troponin if cardiac symptoms or marked systemic illness, chest imaging if respiratory symptoms, viral/reactivation testing per specialist advice. Systemic corticosteroids are specialist-led after infection assessment; severe DRESS can require prolonged, carefully supervised tapering. Common causative drugs: allopurinol (HLA-B*58:01 — see allopurinol safety), carbamazepine (HLA-B*15:02 in Han Chinese — screen before initiation), lamotrigine, sulfonamides, vancomycin, abacavir. Document as severe drug allergy. Never rechallenge.
Pre-immunosuppression Strongyloides screen
Relevant exposure to an endemic area (South/Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, South America) + requiring immunosuppression: Strongyloides IgG serology (stool microscopy has low sensitivity). If positive: discuss with infectious diseases/tropical medicine and treat per current protocol before starting immunosuppression — do not rely on a simple two-dose regimen in immunosuppressed patients. Consider empiric treatment only when urgent immunosuppression cannot wait for testing, exposure risk is substantial, testing is unavailable or likely to be falsely negative, or on specialist/tropical medicine advice — not routinely for every patient with a remote travel history.
Eosinophilic pneumonia (Löffler syndrome)
Transient pulmonary infiltrates + eosinophilia (Löffler syndrome) — often parasitic (Ascaris/hookworm larval migration through lungs). Self-limiting in 1–3 weeks. Chest X-ray: fleeting bilateral infiltrates. Stool tests may be negative during the pulmonary phase. Treat with albendazole if strongly suspected. Hypoxia, significant breathlessness or haemoptysis → same-day hospital. Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia: bronchoalveolar lavage eosinophilia + bilateral perihilar infiltrates → specialist-led oral corticosteroids (dramatic response — "photographic negative of pulmonary oedema" on CXR).
HLA-B*58:01 carriage increases the risk of severe allopurinol-induced DRESS/SJS/TEN. The risk is particularly relevant in Han Chinese, Korean, Thai and some other south-east Asian populations, and may also be relevant in some individuals of African ancestry — do not imply that all people of South Asian ancestry require testing. UK practice does not universally mandate screening; follow current MHRA, CPIC and local allopurinol prescribing guidance. Similarly, HLA-B*15:02 testing before carbamazepine in patients of Han Chinese or Southeast Asian ancestry is recommended by the MHRA.

Lifestyle

Parasite Prevention, Allergy Management & Monitoring

Helminth prevention for travellers Pre-travel: TravelHealthPro (NaTHNaC) and UKHSA Green Book vaccination advice (hepatitis A/B, typhoid, malaria prophylaxis). During travel: safe water (boil/filter), food hygiene (avoid raw vegetables washed in contaminated water), avoid walking barefoot on soil/sand (hookworm, Strongyloides percutaneous penetration), wear long sleeves in mosquito-endemic areas. Post-travel: Strongyloides and other helminth serology guided by exposure for unexplained eosinophilia; stool tests via microbiology/tropical medicine.
Food safety for toxocariasis prevention Toxocara canis/cati infection: prevent dog/cat faecal soil contamination of food. Regular anthelmintic treatment of household pets (every 3 months for dogs). Wash hands after contact with soil or pets. Wash raw vegetables thoroughly. Children: discourage soil eating (pica).
Atopy control for eosinophilia reduction Optimised ICS for asthma, nasal steroids for rhinitis, antihistamine for urticaria — eosinophilia may persist despite good control; reconsider other causes if persistent or progressive. Allergen immunotherapy (SLIT/SCIT) for significant atopic eosinophilia where allergen identified.
Allopurinol initiation safety HLA-B*58:01 risk is particularly relevant in Han Chinese, Korean, Thai and some other south-east Asian populations, and possibly some individuals of African ancestry — follow current MHRA/CPIC/local allopurinol guidance. Low-dose starting strategy with slow titration. FBC monitoring is not a standard universal strategy for preventing hypersensitivity — advise patients to report rash or fever. Document allergy if eosinophilia + rash occurs.
Drug history review at each visit Ask about OTC NSAIDs, herbal supplements, recently started prescription drugs — all can cause drug eosinophilia. Any new drug started within 8 weeks of eosinophilia discovery should be considered causative until excluded. Drug timeline matched to eosinophilia timeline.
Repeat FBC monitoring Unexplained eosinophilia: recheck FBC after any intervention (treat parasites, stop drug, optimise atopy), timed by the count, persistence, symptoms and local haematology advice. No routine annual FBC for uncomplicated asthma, eczema or allergic rhinitis solely because of a previous mild eosinophilia. EGPA/HES on treatment: monitoring per specialist plan.
The pre-travel helminth education is a genuinely preventive intervention that GPs can provide at travel consultations — Strongyloides stercoralis, hookworm, and Toxocara are avoidable with straightforward behavioural measures. The percutaneous penetration route of Strongyloides (larvae in contaminated soil penetrate intact skin on bare feet or in contact with contaminated soil) means that the simplest prevention is wearing shoes or sandals when walking in soil in endemic areas. This message is not consistently communicated at travel consultations. GPs providing travel advice should include: always wear footwear outdoors in tropical/subtropical areas, wash all fruit and vegetables with clean water, and reattend for a post-travel check including stool examination and serology if any new symptoms (unexplained eosinophilia, abdominal symptoms, skin rash) within 3 months of return.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

After treatment for parasites
Recheck FBC + eosinophil count at 3 months — should normalise. Recheck serology at 3 months for Strongyloides (serology takes months to decline even after successful treatment — use eosinophil count as primary treatment monitoring tool). If eosinophilia persists → ID/tropical medicine review (retreatment per specialist advice).
Atopy-related eosinophilia
Repeat testing guided by the count, persistence and symptoms — not automatically. If eosinophilia persists or progresses despite good atopy control → investigate for additional cause (drug, parasites, EGPA, haematological).
Drug-induced eosinophilia
Recheck FBC after stopping the suspected drug. If not normalised → investigate for other causes (parasites, vasculitis, haematological). Do not rechallenge after DRESS, SJS/TEN, hepatitis, nephritis or significant systemic reaction.
On EGPA/HES treatment
Monitoring per specialist plan: FBC (eosinophil count response), ESR/CRP, organ function. Steroid-related monitoring (BP, glucose, bone density). Inhaled steroids at maximum dose to reduce oral steroid exposure.
Same-day hospital / 999 if unstable
New cardiac symptoms (dyspnoea, palpitations, chest pain, syncope) + known eosinophilia → same-day ECG, troponin and echo; 999 if haemodynamic instability, severe chest pain, syncope, acute heart failure or respiratory compromise · New peripheral neuropathy, focal deficit, confusion or seizures + eosinophilia → urgent hospital/specialist assessment (not just outpatient ANCA) · Hypoxia, significant breathlessness or haemoptysis → same-day hospital · Drug rash + fever + eosinophilia → stop the suspected culprit + same-day hospital (DRESS) · Immunosuppressed with Strongyloides risk and sepsis → same-day hospital
GP same week
Eosinophilia not responding to treatment at review · New systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss) appearing during follow-up of simple eosinophilia · Pre-immunosuppression Strongyloides positive → urgent infectious diseases/tropical medicine discussion and treatment per protocol before immunosuppression proceeds
The 3-month serology persistence after Strongyloides treatment is an important patient communication point — Strongyloides IgG serology can remain positive for 6–12 months after successful treatment because the immunological memory persists. Using the eosinophil count (not the serology) as the primary treatment monitoring tool is more accurate: if eosinophilia resolves within 3 months of ivermectin treatment, treatment is likely successful regardless of serology. Persistent serology positivity without eosinophilia does not require retreatment. Persistent eosinophilia 3 months after treatment should prompt Tropical Medicine/infectious diseases review (retreatment per specialist advice) and reconsideration of other causes.
