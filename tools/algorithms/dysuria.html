1
Safety

Red Flags — Urosepsis, Malignancy & Retention

Scope: adults in primary care. Separate considerations apply to pregnancy, catheterised patients, men, suspected STI, sepsis/systemic UTI, recurrent UTI, haematuria and urinary retention. Children under 16 are not covered — use a paediatric pathway. Use with the current Greater Manchester antimicrobial guideline, local microbiology advice and Bolton laboratory reporting.

Dysuria has several causes — cystitis, pyelonephritis/systemic UTI, urethritis/STI, prostatitis, vulvovaginitis/genitourinary syndrome of menopause, stone disease, bladder pain syndrome and retention/obstruction. Exclude these emergencies first.

Dysuria + fever >38°C + rigors + loin pain + haemodynamic instability Urosepsis / ascending pyelonephritis with bacteraemia. → Assess for sepsis using clinical assessment and local NEWS2/sepsis pathway. Any patient who is systemically unwell, hypotensive, confused, hypoxic, unable to take oral treatment or deteriorating requires urgent hospital assessment. Obtain cultures and give antibiotics in accordance with the receiving/hospital pathway; do not delay emergency transfer.
Dysuria + haematuria (macroscopic) + age 45+ Bladder/urothelial carcinoma. → urology suspected cancer pathway referral. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): aged 45+ with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or visible haematuria that persists or recurs after successful UTI treatment → suspected cancer pathway. Treat the UTI, then recheck — do not let a 'UTI' explanation close the question.
Dysuria + inability to void + suprapubic pain + palpable bladder Acute urinary retention. → Urgent same-day assessment and bladder decompression by an appropriately trained clinician. Send to ED/urology if unable to catheterise, severe pain, AKI, sepsis, haematuria/clot retention, neurological features or suspected urethral injury. Causes: BPH, urethral stricture, clot retention (haematuria), neurogenic, drug-induced (anticholinergics, opioids).
Dysuria + urethral discharge + recent sexual exposure STI (gonorrhoea, Chlamydia, NGU, herpes). Ask sensitively about sexual exposure. Offer STI testing (first-void urine NAAT for Chlamydia/GC) when urethritis, discharge, genital lesions, pelvic/testicular symptoms or relevant sexual risk is present. Do not delay treatment of a clinically suspected bacterial UTI, prostatitis or severe infection while awaiting STI results. Refer or discuss with GUM according to local pathway.
Dysuria + suprapubic pain + fever + tender prostate in a man Acute bacterial prostatitis. → Antibiotic per NICE NG110: 14 days then review (for example ciprofloxacin 500 mg BD, noting MHRA January 2024 fluoroquinolone restrictions; trimethoprim if unsuitable). Hospital if systemically unwell. If examination is required, perform a gentle DRE; do not massage the prostate. Avoid repeated examinations. MSU culture before antibiotics.
Dysuria + genital ulcers/vesicles + inguinal lymphadenopathy Herpes simplex (HSV-1/2) genitalis. Refer suspected first-episode genital herpes to GUM; antiviral choice and duration should follow the current BASHH/local GUM protocol. Syphilis chancre: painless ulcer — but syphilis can mimic many conditions.
Other red flags Obstructed infected system or severe loin pain/suspected stone · pregnancy with fever, loin pain or systemic symptoms · acute scrotal pain (suspected testicular torsion) · immunocompromised or other high-risk patient → urgent same-day assessment.
Dysuria in a young man is categorically different from dysuria in a woman — UTI in younger men is less common, and when it does occur, it should prompt investigation for a structural cause (urethral stricture, BPH in older men, prostatitis, reflux) or an STI. A recognised pitfall is prescribing nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim for 'UTI' in a sexually active young man without performing an STI screen — this misses gonorrhoea or Chlamydia, delays appropriate STI treatment, misses partner notification, and may provide partial cover for GC that selects for resistance. Ask sensitively about sexual exposure and perform STI NAAT (first-void urine) when urethritis or sexual exposure is plausible; send a pre-treatment MSU in men with suspected lower UTI. Do not delay treatment of a suspected bacterial UTI or prostatitis while awaiting STI results. UTI in men requires 7-day treatment (not 3 days) because of the risk of subclinical prostatic involvement.

Classification — Causes of Dysuria

Women — common causes
Bacterial UTI (cystitis): E. coli (most common), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (young women), Klebsiella, Enterococcus. Dysuria + frequency + urgency ± haematuria ± suprapubic pain. Urethritis: STI (Chlamydia, GC, NGU) or chemical (soaps, lubricants). Vulvovaginitis: Candida (itch + white discharge), BV (offensive discharge), trichomoniasis. Atrophic vaginitis (post-menopausal): dysuria + dryness — responds to topical oestrogen. Herpes genitalis: vesicles → ulcers, extremely painful urination.
Men — causes
Urethritis (STI): Chlamydia (often asymptomatic), GC (urethral discharge), NGU (Mycoplasma genitalium, Ureaplasma). Prostatitis (acute bacterial — fever + tender prostate; chronic/CPPS — perineal pain). UTI: uncommon in young men — investigate for structural cause. Urethral stricture: poor stream + dysuria — history of GC, catheterisation, instrumentation.
Both sexes
Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome: chronic pelvic pain + frequency + urgency + dysuria without infection — difficult diagnosis of exclusion. Radiation cystitis: previous pelvic radiotherapy (prostate, cervical, rectal cancer). Drug-induced cystitis: cyclophosphamide (haemorrhagic cystitis — mesna prophylaxis). Renal calculi: ureteric stone passing through ureterovesical junction causes dysuria + haematuria + loin pain.
Differentiating UTI from urethritis
UTI: frequency + urgency + suprapubic pain + cloudy urine. Urethritis (STI): dysuria predominantly, often without frequency/urgency/suprapubic pain, may have urethral discharge. Urine pH does not reliably distinguish cystitis from urethritis. A negative culture may reflect prior antibiotics, low bacterial count, sampling problems, fastidious organisms, vaginitis, urethritis or non-infective disease. Dipstick: leucocytes positive in both UTI and urethritis (urethral inflammation).
The differentiation between UTI and urethritis is a clinically important distinction that determines whether the patient needs antibiotics, STI screening, or both. The key clinical discriminators: UTI produces a symptom triad of dysuria + frequency + urgency (the bladder is inflamed and contracting against urine), while urethritis produces predominantly dysuria (the urethra is inflamed) without significant frequency/urgency. Urethral discharge strongly suggests urethritis. The urine dipstick is unhelpful in differentiating the two conditions — leucocytes are positive in both UTI (inflammatory cells in bladder urine) and urethritis (inflammatory cells in urethra contaminate the urine sample). MSU culture helps, but a negative culture may reflect prior antibiotics, low bacterial count, sampling problems, fastidious organisms, vaginitis, urethritis or non-infective disease. A patient with dysuria and a positive dipstick but a negative MSU culture should be considered for Chlamydia/GC testing according to sexual risk.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Dipstick & MSU

History
Onset, duration, severity. Frequency/urgency (UTI) vs dysuria alone (urethritis). Suprapubic vs loin pain (pyelonephritis). Vaginal discharge/itch (candida, BV, trichomoniasis). Urethral discharge (STI). Sexual history (if urethritis possible). Post-menopausal vaginal dryness. Previous UTIs — frequency, organisms. Haematuria (timing: at start = urethral; throughout = bladder; at end = bladder neck/prostate). Drug history (cyclophosphamide). Catheter.
Urine dipstick interpretation
Leucocytes + nitrites positive: strongly suggests bacterial UTI in symptomatic non-pregnant women under 65. Treat empirically in this group without sending MSU. Leucocytes positive, nitrites negative: possible UTI (Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Enterococcus — nitrite-negative organisms) OR urethritis/vaginitis (contamination). Send MSU. Blood positive only: haematuria — UTI or non-infective cause. Negative dipstick in symptomatic woman: UTI less likely — consider urethritis, vaginitis, bladder pain syndrome.
Dipstick — by population
Symptomatic non-pregnant women under 65: dipstick may support diagnosis according to local policy. Adults over 65: avoid dipsticks (GM guidance) — assess clinically and send MSU if UTI is suspected. Catheterised patients: do not use dipsticks to diagnose CAUTI; culture only when symptomatic. Men and pregnancy: send a pre-treatment MSU — a negative dipstick does not exclude UTI.
MSU — when to send
Always: men, children, pregnancy, recurrent UTI, treatment failure, pyelonephritis, catheter UTI. Selected women: atypical presentation, immunocompromised, suspected resistant organism, elderly with non-specific symptoms. Not needed: uncomplicated cystitis in otherwise healthy non-pregnant women with classic symptoms + positive dipstick.
Minimum investigation by patient group
Healthy non-pregnant woman with classic uncomplicated cystitis: clinical assessment ± dipstick according to local policy; MSU usually unnecessary · Man: pre-treatment MSU; assess for prostatitis and STI · Pregnancy: pre-treatment MSU; treat according to culture · Pyelonephritis: MSU before antibiotics; blood tests/hospital assessment according to severity · Catheterised patient: do not use dipstick; culture only when symptomatic · Recurrent/atypical infection: culture-confirmed episodes and targeted investigation · Suspected urethritis: first-void urine NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhoea; consider other STI tests according to risk and local sexual-health guidance.
Additional investigations
STI screen: FVU for Chlamydia/GC NAAT (first-void urine — not MSU) when urethritis, discharge, genital lesions, pelvic/testicular symptoms or relevant sexual risk is present. Renal USS (hydronephrosis, stones) if obstruction/stone is suspected, infection is recurrent or atypical, persistent haematuria, renal impairment or no improvement within 48 hours — not routinely after uncomplicated pyelonephritis. Flexible cystoscopy (haematuria, recurrent unexplained UTI). Urodynamics (overactive bladder, interstitial cystitis).
The nitrofurantoin and MSU before prescribing principle is set out in NICE NG109 and the UKHSA diagnostic tool — for uncomplicated cystitis in otherwise healthy non-pregnant women under 65 with classic symptoms (dysuria + frequency + urgency) and a positive dipstick (leucocytes + nitrites), MSU culture before prescribing is NOT required. The diagnostic accuracy of the clinical presentation + dipstick combination is sufficient to treat empirically. However, the 'test and treat' or 'dipstick + culture' approach remains appropriate for: men, pregnant women, children, patients with recurrent UTI (to identify organisms and sensitivities), immunocompromised patients, those with atypical symptoms, and elderly patients in whom the diagnosis of UTI is more uncertain (atypical presentations are common in elderly — confusion or falls without classic urinary symptoms can represent UTI in this group, but avoid dipsticks in adults over 65, in line with GM guidance, because asymptomatic bacteriuria is common). NICE NG109 advises considering local resistance data before prescribing trimethoprim empirically; follow the current GM antimicrobial guideline.

Special Situations — Men, Pregnancy & Recurrent Dysuria

Men with dysuria
Pre-treatment MSU culture. Ask sensitively about sexual exposure; FVU Chlamydia/GC NAAT when urethritis or sexual exposure is plausible — do not delay treatment of suspected bacterial UTI or prostatitis while awaiting results. If positive bacterial culture: 7-day antibiotic course. If prostatitis is suspected and examination is required: gentle DRE only — do not massage the prostate; avoid repeated examinations. Urology referral if: recurrent, structural cause suspected, or first UTI without clear precipitating cause.
Pregnant women
Send MSU before antibiotics for suspected UTI in pregnancy. Treat confirmed asymptomatic bacteriuria and symptomatic lower UTI according to culture and susceptibility, using a 7-day regimen. First-line (NICE NG109): nitrofurantoin 100 mg MR BD × 7 days if eGFR ≥45 (avoid at term — follow the current local maternity/GM antimicrobial policy for the gestational-age cut-off); second-line: amoxicillin (only if culture-sensitive) or cefalexin 500 mg BD × 7 days. Trimethoprim: avoid first trimester. Review results and liaise with maternity services regarding persistent, recurrent or upper-tract infection. Follow local obstetric policy regarding test-of-cure.
Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI)
Bacteriuria in long-term catheter is near-universal — treat ONLY if symptomatic (fever, new suprapubic pain, systemic unwell, rigors). Do NOT treat positive MSU in asymptomatic catheterised patients. Do not use dipsticks. If symptomatic, consider removing or changing a catheter that has been in place for more than 7 days, without delaying antibiotics. Obtain a culture using the local catheter-sampling protocol. 7-day antibiotic course if symptomatic.
Recurrent UTI (≥2 episodes in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months)
Culture-confirm episodes. Investigate and refer according to risk, not routinely: imaging/urology referral if atypical features, haematuria, suspected obstruction or stones, recurrent upper UTI, unusual organisms or no identifiable cause; STI testing according to sexual risk. Urine cytology is not a routine investigation. Management (NICE NG112, shared decision-making with review): behavioural/self-care advice → vaginal oestrogen in post-menopausal women where appropriate → single-dose trigger-based antibiotic prophylaxis → methenamine hippurate (eligible non-pregnant women and people with a female urinary system) or daily antibiotic prophylaxis. Do not use alkalinising urinary sachets with methenamine. Consider bladder pain syndrome if cultures are consistently negative.
Bladder pain syndrome (interstitial cystitis) is a diagnosis of exclusion. Suspect it when dysuria, frequency, urgency or bladder-related pelvic pain persist and urine cultures are repeatedly negative. Avoid repeated antibiotic courses when cultures are negative. Before attributing symptoms to bladder pain syndrome, assess for haematuria, pelvic pathology, vaginal causes and red flags, and refer according to the local urology/gynaecology pathway.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Urgent / same-day
Suspected sepsis/systemic UTI (assess using NEWS2/local sepsis pathway) · Acute urinary retention — same-day assessment and bladder decompression; ED/urology if unable to catheterise, AKI, sepsis, clot retention, neurological features or suspected urethral injury
Suspected cancer · urology
Aged 45+: unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or persisting/recurring after successful UTI treatment · Aged 60+: unexplained non-visible haematuria with dysuria or raised white cell count (NICE NG12)
Urology (urgent)
First UTI in a man under 50 (structural cause) · Recurrent UTI with haematuria · Suspected urethral stricture (poor stream + dysuria history) · Post-void residual >100 ml
GUM clinic
Suspected STI (urethral discharge, dysuria without frequency/urgency, sexual risk history) — refer or discuss according to local pathway
Gynaecology / urogynaecology
Chronic dysuria with negative cultures (interstitial cystitis pathway) · Recurrent UTI in women with prolapse/incomplete emptying
GP management
Uncomplicated UTI in healthy non-pregnant women: treat empirically with nitrofurantoin 100 mg MR BD × 3 days (or 7 days if recurrent/complicated). Suspected first-episode genital herpes: refer to GUM; antiviral choice and duration per current BASHH/local GUM protocol. Atrophic vaginitis: topical oestrogen.
The NICE NG12 haematuria referral rule is one of the most important cancer safety-netting rules in primary care — it is frequently violated by attributing haematuria to UTI and not following up after treatment. The correct approach when a patient presents with dysuria AND macroscopic haematuria: (1) check for UTI and treat if confirmed; (2) if there is no UTI, refer on the suspected cancer pathway now (aged 45+); (3) if there was a UTI, confirm the haematuria has cleared after treatment and refer if it persists or recurs (NICE NG12). Bladder cancer can co-present with UTI, and treating the infection does not treat the cancer. A patient who had haematuria with a 'UTI' that appeared to resolve should still be referred if they had not had a urology assessment. GPs should have a system for checking that suspected cancer referrals for haematuria are not inadvertently dropped when haematuria 'resolves' with antibiotics.

Treat

Antibiotic Treatment by Scenario

Uncomplicated lower UTI — non-pregnant women
Nitrofurantoin 100 mg MR BD × 3 days
First-line per NICE NG109 (2018). eGFR ≥45 required. Culture not usually needed. Review if worsening or not improving within 48 hours. Alternative: pivmecillinam 400 mg initial dose then 200 mg TDS for a total of 3 days (NG109). Trimethoprim 200 mg BD × 3 days (check local resistance).
UTI in men
Trimethoprim 200 mg BD × 7 days
7-day course. Nitrofurantoin 100 mg MR BD × 7 days (eGFR ≥45) is an alternative only if prostate involvement is not suspected. MSU culture before starting. STI NAAT if urethritis or sexual exposure is plausible. If prostatitis features: 14 days then review per NICE NG110. Recheck if no improvement at 48h.
Pyelonephritis (outpatient, well)
Cefalexin 500 mg BD–TDS × 7–10 days (NICE NG111)
Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BD × 7 days only when other commonly recommended antibiotics are inappropriate (MHRA January 2024); co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg TDS × 7–10 days or trimethoprim only if culture confirms susceptibility. Do not use nitrofurantoin, pivmecillinam or oral fosfomycin (inadequate renal-tissue concentrations). MSU culture before starting. Hospital if vomiting, confused, or haemodynamically unstable. Reassess if worsening or not improving within 48 hours; review culture and change treatment if resistant.
Herpes genitalis — first episode
Refer to GUM — antiviral per current BASHH/local GUM protocol
First episodes may require longer treatment and specialist review. Reduces duration and severity. Does not prevent future recurrences. GUM referral for typing, partner notification, suppressive therapy counselling.
Atrophic vaginitis dysuria
Vaginal oestrogen (cream or pessary)
Regimens are product-specific — check product SPC and local formulary. Use for recurrent UTI prevention is off-label for some products. History of breast cancer: NICE NG23 (2024) — non-hormonal moisturisers/lubricants first, then consider vaginal oestrogen; if on an aromatase inhibitor, decide with the breast cancer specialist (specialist/shared decision-making). Addresses root cause of dysuria + recurrent UTI in post-menopausal women.
Lower UTI — pregnancy
Nitrofurantoin 100 mg MR BD × 7 days (eGFR ≥45)
MSU before antibiotics; treat according to culture and susceptibility. Avoid at term — follow local maternity/GM policy for the gestational-age cut-off. Review culture results; liaise with maternity services if persistent, recurrent or upper-tract infection (NICE NG109 [1]).
Recurrent UTI prophylaxis (NICE NG112 [4])
Self-care → vaginal oestrogen → single-dose trigger prophylaxis → methenamine hippurate or daily antibiotic prophylaxis
Shared decision-making with planned review. Methenamine is an antibiotic-sparing option in eligible non-pregnant women and people with a female urinary system. Do not use alkalinising urinary sachets with methenamine. Do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in non-pregnant adults or catheterised patients.
⚠️ Nitrofurantoin safety (MHRA [7])
Pulmonary and hepatic reactions
Nitrofurantoin can rarely cause pulmonary and hepatic adverse reactions, including serious or long-term disease. Ask patients to report new cough, dyspnoea, chest pain, jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus or unexplained systemic symptoms. Consider the risk particularly with prolonged or repeated exposure, and review the current MHRA advice and product information. Check renal function and dose appropriateness, previous nitrofurantoin pulmonary/hepatic reaction, possible G6PD-related haemolysis, interactions and adverse effects, and product-specific contraindications in the current SPC.
⚠️ Fluoroquinolone safety (MHRA [8])
Only when other recommended antibiotics are inappropriate
Systemic fluoroquinolones must only be prescribed when other commonly recommended antibiotics are inappropriate — for example because of resistance, contraindication, treatment-limiting adverse effects or treatment failure. Document the rationale. Counsel about tendon, muscle, joint, neurological, psychiatric and sensory adverse effects. Stop treatment and seek urgent clinical advice at the first serious reaction.
Aciclovir for first-episode genital herpes has several important clinical nuances that GPs should know: (1) it must be started within 5 days of lesion onset to be effective (after this, viral replication has largely ceased and antiviral treatment has minimal benefit on the current episode); (2) the antiviral reduces the duration and severity of the episode but does not affect the establishment of latency in the sacral ganglia (herpes will likely recur); (3) patients should be counselled that they will have lifelong HSV infection that can reactivate; (4) suppressive therapy (regimen per current BASHH/GUM protocol) is available for patients with frequent recurrences (≥6/year) and reduces recurrence frequency — this is a GUM clinic discussion; (5) asymptomatic shedding (when the virus is infectious without any visible lesions) occurs intermittently and is responsible for much HSV transmission between sexual partners. GUM referral after the first episode is important to ensure appropriate counselling, partner testing, and long-term management planning.

Treat

Bladder Pain Syndrome, Prostatitis & Special Situations

Bladder pain syndrome / interstitial cystitis
A diagnosis of exclusion. Avoid repeated antibiotics when cultures are negative. Assess for haematuria, pelvic pathology, vaginal causes and red flags. Refer according to the local urology/gynaecology pathway.
Chronic prostatitis / CPPS
Category III prostatitis (negative cultures, perineal pain + dysuria + ejaculatory pain + lower urinary symptoms). Treatment: alpha-blocker (tamsulosin 0.4 mg OD — reduces urethral/prostatic smooth muscle tone → improves voiding), pelvic floor physiotherapy, CBT (central sensitisation component). Antibiotics only on specialist advice — fluoroquinolones only when other commonly recommended antibiotics are inappropriate (MHRA). Refer urology/urology-pain clinic if not improving.
Acute bacterial prostatitis
NICE NG110 (2018): ciprofloxacin 500 mg BD (or trimethoprim 200 mg BD if a fluoroquinolone is unsuitable) for 14 days, then review — stop if resolved, continue a further 14 days if not. MHRA (January 2024): fluoroquinolones only when other recommended antibiotics are inappropriate — document the indication, alternatives considered, susceptibility information, counselling and safety-netting; warn about tendon, muscle, joint, neurological, psychiatric and sensory adverse effects; stop and seek advice at the first serious reaction. MSU before antibiotics. Hospital if systemically unwell (rigors, confusion, haemodynamic instability, unable to take oral treatment). If examination is required, perform a gentle DRE; do not massage the prostate. Avoid repeated examinations. Reassess if worsening or not improving within 48 hours — consider prostatic abscess (urology/imaging).
Radiation cystitis
Haematuria + dysuria + urgency after pelvic radiotherapy. Management: hydration, bladder instillation (hyaluronic acid, chondroitin), hyperbaric oxygen (specialist). Urology referral. Exclude malignant recurrence (biopsies).
NICE NG110 (2018) sets a 14-day course for acute bacterial prostatitis followed by review, extending to 28 days only if symptoms persist, because antibiotic penetration into the prostate is poor (blood-prostate barrier) and undertreatment risks relapse and chronic bacterial prostatitis. Ciprofloxacin has the best prostate tissue penetration of available oral antibiotics (lipophilic — crosses the blood-prostate barrier effectively). The clinical monitoring during the course: if the patient is not improving within 48 hours or is worsening, reconsider the diagnosis (prostatic abscess — requires USS/MRI + drainage) or antibiotic choice (culture sensitivity — if organism resistant to ciprofloxacin, switch based on culture). Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) is indicated if there is no improvement at 1 week of appropriate antibiotics — a prostatic abscess requires surgical drainage (transperineal or transrectal).

Lifestyle

Prevention, Hygiene & Patient Education

Fluids and voiding Advise adequate fluid intake to avoid dehydration and avoid delaying urination (NICE NG112). Pale straw-coloured urine suggests adequate intake.
Post-coital voiding Void soon after intercourse — mechanically flushes periurethral bacteria that have been displaced into the urethra during intercourse. A simple self-care measure that can be discussed for recurrent post-coital UTI; it should not be presented as a proven intervention.
Perineal hygiene Wipe front to back after defaecation (prevents faecal bacteria reaching the urethra). Warm water only for external genital washing — no soap, intimate washes, or douches (disrupt the protective Lactobacillus microbiome). These personal-hygiene measures are reasonable self-care advice but are not proven to prevent UTI.
Spermicide avoidance Nonoxynol-9 (spermicide in diaphragms and many condoms) disrupts normal vaginal flora and significantly increases UTI susceptibility. Women with recurrent UTI using spermicide-containing contraception should switch to non-spermicide barrier or alternative contraceptive method.
Topical oestrogen (post-menopausal) An option for recurrent UTI prevention in post-menopausal women (NICE NG112) — estriol cream or oestradiol vaginal tablets restore vaginal Lactobacillus colonisation, reduce pH, and reduce E. coli periurethral colonisation. Consider before antibiotic prophylaxis. Regimens are product-specific — check product SPC and local formulary.
STI prevention Consistent condom use reduces STI transmission. Annual STI screen for sexually active adults under 25 (NCSP). HPV vaccination (Gardasil 9 — reduces HPV-related dyspareunia and condylomata). Advise: "Regular testing is responsible, not shameful." Free home Chlamydia test kits for under-25s.
Herpes management education After first HSV episode: asymptomatic shedding occurs intermittently. Condoms reduce transmission but not completely (skin-to-skin transmission outside condom coverage). Suppressive aciclovir significantly reduces shedding and transmission risk. Partner should be informed and tested. British Association for Sexual Health resources (bashh.org).
Cranberry and D-mannose Optional self-care only — evidence is uncertain or variable; not an established treatment (NICE NG112).
Bladder habits Void every 2–3 hours during the day — avoid holding urine for long periods (stasis promotes bacterial growth). Double-void technique (void, wait 30s, void again) if incomplete emptying suspected. Treat constipation (faecal reservoir adjacent to urethra increases E. coli colonisation).
The genital herpes disclosure dilemma is one of the most sensitive psychosocial aspects of managing first-episode HSV — patients often struggle with the question of when and how to inform sexual partners, and some experience significant shame, anxiety about future relationships, and fear of rejection. Proactive, empathetic GP counselling at the first HSV consultation significantly improves patient outcomes. Key messages: HSV is extremely common; most people with genital herpes do not know they have it (majority of acquisition is from asymptomatic shedding by a partner who did not know they were infected); having genital herpes does not mean you or your partner has been unfaithful (the infection can be dormant for years before first presentation); the infection is manageable with suppressive treatment; and long-term relationships with HSV-positive partners are possible with appropriate precautions. GUM referral provides specialist counselling support beyond what can be delivered in a primary care consultation.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Uncomplicated UTI — review
Not routine. Advise: return if not significantly improved within 48 hours. If no improvement at 48h: MSU culture result if sent (check sensitivity), change antibiotic according to culture susceptibility and local guidance. If still no improvement at 5 days → urine culture + exclude pyelonephritis (examination + CRP).
Pyelonephritis follow-up
Safety-net all patients. Reassess, usually within 48 hours if symptoms are not improving or if risk factors are present. Review culture results and change treatment if resistant. Routine post-treatment culture is not required if clinically well, unless pregnancy, persistent symptoms, recurrence or local policy indicates. Do not routinely arrange ultrasound after an uncomplicated, clinically improving episode; investigate if obstruction/stone is suspected, infection is recurrent or atypical, there is persistent haematuria, renal impairment, or no clinical improvement within 48 hours.
After first HSV episode
GUM referral for: HSV type confirmation, partner testing, suppressive therapy if ≥6 recurrences/year, psychosexual support. PHQ-9 at follow-up (depression and anxiety common after diagnosis). Document diagnosis and GUM referral made.
After STI diagnosed
Partner notification within PN period (Chlamydia 4 weeks; GC 3 months). Test of cure after gonorrhoea treatment (TOC at 1–2 weeks — NAAT + culture). 3-monthly STI screen if MSM with multiple partners. HIV retest at 6 weeks if high-risk exposure.
Safety-net (all patients)
Seek further assessment if symptoms worsen rapidly, the patient becomes systemically unwell, treatment is not beginning to work within 48 hours, vomiting prevents oral treatment, or new loin pain, visible haematuria or urinary retention develops. Do not arrange routine test-of-cure cultures or renal ultrasound unless clinically indicated or required by local maternity policy.
Return immediately
Unable to void (retention) → same-day assessment · Systemically unwell, confused, rapidly worsening or unable to take oral treatment → urgent hospital assessment (999 if critically unwell) · Aged 45+ with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or visible haematuria persisting/recurring after successful UTI treatment → suspected cancer pathway (NICE NG12) · Aged 60+ with unexplained non-visible haematuria plus dysuria or raised WCC → suspected cancer pathway · Unexplained or persistent visible haematuria at any age still needs explanation
Within 48 hours
UTI symptoms not improving after 48h of appropriate antibiotics → MSU culture result + consider step-up · Pyelonephritis worsening or not improving within 48 hours → reassess (in person if clinically indicated)
Routine test-of-cure culture and renal ultrasound are not required after an uncomplicated, clinically improving episode of pyelonephritis (NICE NG111). Reassess if symptoms worsen or do not improve within 48 hours, review culture results and change treatment if the organism is resistant. Investigate (ultrasound/urology) if obstruction or stone is suspected, infection is recurrent or atypical, there is persistent haematuria or renal impairment, or there is no clinical improvement within 48 hours. Repeat culture is appropriate in pregnancy, with persistent symptoms or recurrence, or when local policy requires it.
Educational use only. Adults in primary care only. Use with the current Greater Manchester antimicrobial guideline, local microbiology advice and Bolton laboratory reporting. Doses may require modification for renal function, allergy, age, pregnancy, weight and local microbiology advice — check the BNF and current SPC.
