1
Safety

Red Flags — Cancer, Systemic Disease & Safeguarding

Itch with bleeding, altered bowel habit, anaemia or a mass 2WW Anal itch itself is not an NG12 trigger — the accompanying symptom is. Offer quantitative FIT per NICE NG12 (unexplained change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, abdominal mass, rectal bleeding in the relevant age/symptom groups); FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → colorectal suspected cancer pathway. A rectal mass, or unexplained anal mass or ulceration, is referred without FIT; a low, unavailable or unreturned FIT must not delay referral if concern remains. Do not attribute bleeding to haemorrhoids without examination.
Persistent asymmetric, scaly, crusted, pigmented, indurated or ulcerated plaque Consider extramammary Paget disease (rare), anal intraepithelial neoplasia/Bowen disease or invasive malignancy, especially if treatment-resistant → urgent assessment and biopsy via the clinically appropriate service (dermatology, colorectal or anogenital neoplasia pathway).
Generalised itch + jaundice Cholestatic liver disease (PBC, PSC, bile duct obstruction). LFTs + bilirubin — reserved for jaundice, cholestatic symptoms or significant generalised itch, not uncomplicated anal itch.
Generalised itch + systemic symptoms Lymphoma (Hodgkin's — severe nocturnal itch), CKD (uraemic pruritus), polycythaemia vera (aquagenic pruritus after bathing), thyroid disease. FBC + U&E + TFTs + LFTs.
Perianal itch in child + safeguarding Consider threadworm, irritant dermatitis, eczema, psoriasis, lichen sclerosus and infection as well as safeguarding. Warts, genital lesions, trauma, bleeding, disclosure, STI or concerning context → careful assessment under local safeguarding/paediatric protocols. Lichen sclerosus does not exclude abuse; warts alone do not establish it.
New perianal warts (condylomata acuminata) HPV. Sexual-health assessment for new, multiple, recurrent, atypical or symptomatic warts or relevant exposure; HIV/STI testing by risk. HPV typing is not routinely required. Biopsy if atypical, indurated, ulcerated, pigmented, bleeding or treatment-resistant. HIV-positive and other high-risk groups may need specialist assessment.
Extramammary Paget's disease (EMPD) of the anus is one of the most important diagnoses not to miss in chronic perianal itch — it presents as a well-demarcated, erythematous, weeping, eczematous plaque that fails to respond to standard eczema treatments. It is often misdiagnosed as contact dermatitis or eczema for years. A proportion of perianal EMPD is associated with an underlying internal malignancy. Atypical, persistent, treatment-resistant or diagnostically uncertain perianal dermatitis needs specialist review and biopsy. Aquagenic pruritus (intense itching after bathing, not restricted to perianal area) is a classic presenting symptom of polycythaemia vera — check FBC for elevated haemoglobin and haematocrit.

Diagnose

History — Pattern & Context

Age and household
Child or entire household itching (especially nocturnal, around anus/perianal area) → threadworms (Enterobius vermicularis). Common. Treat household members simultaneously according to age, pregnancy and local protocol.
Nocturnal vs daytime itch
Nocturnal perianal itch → threadworms (females emerge at night to lay eggs). Daytime + contact history → contact/irritant dermatitis. Constant → skin disease (eczema, lichen sclerosus, psoriasis, EMPD).
Dietary triggers
Coffee, tea, cola, alcohol, spicy food, citrus or chocolate may contribute in some people — an individual 2–4-week trial, not a universal exclusion list. Diarrhoea / fecal soiling (from haemorrhoids, loose stool, fistula) → moisture maceration → itch.
Hygiene habits
Over-washing (soap disrupts perianal skin barrier → pH change → itch). Under-washing (fecal residue). Wet wipes (preservatives — contact dermatitis). Perfumed products. Tight synthetic underwear. All common iatrogenic contributors.
Skin disease history
Personal/family history of eczema, psoriasis, lichen sclerosus. Psoriasis frequently involves perianal skin (inverse psoriasis — glazed erythematous plaque without scaling). Lichen sclerosus can extend from vulva/penis to perianal skin.
Also ask
Bleeding, pain, discharge, lump, ulceration, altered bowel habit, faecal leakage; all creams, wipes, haemorrhoid preparations and antiseptics; sexual history and STI risk; pregnancy/breastfeeding; IBD.
Systemic disease
Diabetes (candida, impaired skin barrier), renal failure (uraemic pruritus), liver disease (cholestatic itch), haematological malignancy, HIV (susceptibility to infections, prurigo nodularis)
Idiopathic pruritus ani (no identifiable cause despite thorough investigation) accounts for 50–90% of cases — it is believed to be related to fecal soiling of the perianal skin combined with excessive moisture, creating an alkaline microenvironment that disrupts the perianal epithelial barrier and triggers itch. The itch-scratch cycle worsens the barrier damage. Dietary triggers (coffee being the most consistently implicated) increase fecal leakage and perianal moisture. An individual 2–4-week trial reducing suspected triggers helps some patients. Over-washing with soap is a key iatrogenic cause — many patients, embarrassed by the itch, wash the area multiple times daily with soap, progressively damaging the skin barrier. Water-only washing with gentle patting dry is the first-line behavioural intervention.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Threadworms (Enterobius)
Most common cause in children. Nocturnal perianal itch (worse at night — female worm lays eggs on perianal skin). Household members affected. Worms visible at night around anus. A morning adhesive-tape test can be considered if the diagnosis is uncertain; stool microscopy is not the preferred test.
Idiopathic pruritus ani
Normal examination (or minor excoriations). No identifiable cause. Associated with coffee, spicy food, loose stools, excessive sweating, tight underwear. Most common cause in adults. Hygiene modification + barrier cream ± low-potency topical steroid.
Contact / irritant dermatitis
Erythematous, weeping, ill-defined perianal rash. Cause: wet wipes (preservatives), fabric softener, soaps, anal creams and topical antibiotics. Patch testing (dermatology) identifies contact allergen.
Perianal candidiasis
White satellite lesions, erythema, itch. Associated with antibiotics, diabetes, immunosuppression. Scraping/culture if uncertain, recurrent or treatment-resistant. Topical imidazole (e.g. clotrimazole 1%) per product information; review the diagnosis if not improving.
Psoriasis (perianal / inverse)
Well-demarcated glazed erythematous plaque — no silvery scaling in flexures (inverse psoriasis). Check elbows, knees, scalp, nails for classic psoriasis. Treat as flexural psoriasis: short course of a mild/moderate topical steroid; exclude candidiasis if atypical. Dermatology if uncertain or persistent.
Lichen sclerosus
White atrophic plaques, fissuring. Usually a clinical diagnosis; biopsy if uncertain or suspicious areas. Clobetasol for confirmed LS only (BAD). Ongoing review — SCC risk is increased, mainly described in vulval disease. Perianal-only, male, paediatric or uncertain cases → specialist.
Haemorrhoids / fecal soiling
Haemorrhoidal prolapse → mucus leakage → moisture → itch. Grade III–IV haemorrhoids, fistula, or loose stools. Treat underlying cause. Barrier cream (zinc oxide, Sudocrem) to protect perianal skin.
Neomycin contact allergy is one of the most common causes of perianal contact dermatitis — ironically caused by over-the-counter haemorrhoid creams that contain neomycin as an antiseptic component (e.g., Anusol Plus, Proctosedyl). Patients apply the cream for itch → develop neomycin contact allergy → itch worsens → apply more cream. This cycle can persist for years without the cause being identified. Stopping the implicated product often settles the dermatitis; patch testing confirms the allergen if it persists. GPs should review all over-the-counter products being used when assessing pruritus ani and specifically ask about haemorrhoid creams.
4
Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Perianal inspection
Skin appearance: erythema (candida, eczema), white atrophic plaque (lichen sclerosus), glazed/shiny red (inverse psoriasis), lichenification/excoriation (chronic itch-scratch), satellite lesions (candida), vesicles (herpes), warts (HPV), ulcer (EMPD, cancer, herpes).
Also examine (with consent ± chaperone)
Genitalia if lichen sclerosus, candidiasis or STI possible · abdomen · DRE in adults with bleeding, pain, mass, altered bowel habit, discharge or suspected anorectal disease · fissure, fistula, abscess, prolapse, soiling, perianal Crohn's.
Full skin examination
Look for associated skin disease elsewhere: psoriasis plaques (elbows, knees, scalp), eczema, lichen planus (wrists, ankles). Systemic itch: jaundice, signs of liver disease, lymphadenopathy (lymphoma).
Investigations
No routine bloods for typical localised pruritus with a normal examination. · HbA1c (recurrent or extensive candidiasis) · Tape test (threadworm, if uncertain) · Swab / scraping (suspected bacterial or uncertain fungal infection) · Patch testing (persistent dermatitis — dermatology) · FBC/ferritin, U&E, LFTs, TFTs (only if itch is generalised, persistent, unexplained or systemic)
Biopsy (via dermatology/colorectal)
Atypical, persistent, treatment-resistant or diagnostically uncertain lesions → biopsy. A typical irritant dermatitis that is improving does not automatically need biopsy; do not continue empirical treatment indefinitely without reassessing the diagnosis.
The sticky tape test for threadworms (Sellotape test / cellophane tape swab) is highly sensitive (90%) when performed correctly: apply a strip of clear adhesive tape to the perianal skin early morning before the patient washes or opens bowels, remove, apply to a glass slide, and send to microbiology for microscopy for Enterobius eggs. Stool microscopy (OCP) is less sensitive for threadworms because the eggs are not passed in stool — they are deposited on perianal skin by the female worm at night. Blood count may show eosinophilia (>0.4 × 10⁹/L) in parasitic infection. Perianal skin disease that fails appropriate treatment should be reassessed and biopsied if atypical — EMPD in particular is often delayed by repeated empirical treatment.
5
Refer

Referral Pathways

2WW · NICE NG12
Colorectal symptoms meeting NG12 with FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g · rectal mass, or unexplained anal mass or ulceration (no FIT needed) · use the local Greater Manchester lower-GI pathway. Suspicious non-healing plaque (EMPD/AIN/SCC) → urgent dermatology, colorectal or anogenital neoplasia assessment for biopsy
Dermatology
No meaningful improvement after 4–6 weeks of appropriate treatment · suspected lichen sclerosus or psoriasis (confirmation + specialist management) · suspected contact allergy (patch testing) · undiagnosed perianal skin condition
Colorectal
Haemorrhoid-related soiling causing itch (haemorrhoidal banding/haemorrhoidectomy) · fistula-in-ano causing moisture/soiling · persistent pruritus after colorectal investigation
GUM
New, multiple, recurrent or atypical perianal warts · STI-related perianal disease · HIV-positive with perianal lesions · STI testing (including rectal NAAT) by symptoms, exposure and local protocol
Patch testing for contact dermatitis is a specialist dermatology investigation — it is performed over 3 visits (application, reading at 48 hours, reading at 96 hours) using a standardised European Baseline Series of 28 allergens plus additional specific allergens relevant to the patient's history. For perianal contact dermatitis, the Anogenital Series is also applied. Identifying the specific allergen (e.g., neomycin, methylchloroisothiazolinone in wet wipes) allows targeted allergen avoidance. Without patch testing, patients continue to be exposed to the causative allergen and chronic treatment is ineffective.

Treat

GP Treatment

Threadworms
Mebendazole 100 mg single dose (licensed from 2 years)
Repeat after 2 weeks if advised locally or reinfection likely. Treat household members simultaneously by age and pregnancy status. GM guidance: from 6 months off-label below 2 years (individual risk–benefit). Pregnancy (especially first trimester): hygiene measures alone where possible, seek advice if severe; under 6 months: hygiene alone for 6 weeks. Hygiene: handwashing, nails short and clean, morning perianal wash, close-fitting underwear at night, wash sleepwear and bedding, vacuum and wipe surfaces.
Idiopathic / irritant dermatitis
Zinc oxide cream (Sudocrem) + hygiene
Barrier protection: zinc oxide or petroleum jelly applied after washing and drying. Water-only cleaning. Soft white toilet paper (unscented). Loose cotton underwear. Individual dietary-trigger trial. Clear inflammatory dermatitis (infection and suspicious lesions excluded): hydrocortisone 1% once or twice daily for up to 7–14 days; no prolonged or repeated unsupervised use; review if not improving.
Perianal candidiasis
Topical imidazole, e.g. clotrimazole 1%
Only when the diagnosis is plausible (moist erythema with satellite papules/pustules), per product information. Keep the area dry; treat the underlying cause; HbA1c if recurrent or extensive. Persistent or recurrent → scraping/culture and review the diagnosis rather than routine oral fluconazole (pregnancy, hepatic disease, QT and warfarin interactions).
Inverse psoriasisShort course of a mild/moderate topical steroid as a flexural site. Vitamin D analogues irritate flexures — not routine first-line perianally. No long-term potent steroids. Tacrolimus is off-label and specialist-led here. Dermatology if not controlled or uncertain.
Lichen sclerosusClobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment — tapering protocol (OD × 4 weeks → EOD × 4 weeks → twice weekly × 4 weeks). For confirmed LS (BAD 2018); biopsy first if uncertain. Maintenance per specialist plan. Ongoing review of response, scarring and fissuring; new ulceration, induration, nodule or persistent change → reassess ± biopsy. Emollient (50:50 white soft paraffin).
Treating only the symptomatic patient often fails because of household re-infection, so household members are treated simultaneously according to age and pregnancy status. The combination of pharmacological treatment + hygiene measures (hand washing, nail cutting, hot laundry) is required for eradication. The two-dose regimen (day 1 and day 14) is important — mebendazole kills adult worms but not eggs, which hatch after 2–4 weeks. The second dose kills the worms that hatched from those eggs. Zinc oxide (e.g. Sudocrem) forms an effective occlusive barrier that prevents fecal moisture contact with perianal skin — it is the cornerstone of idiopathic pruritus ani management and is available OTC. For moisture-related itch a barrier usually matters more than a steroid.

Treat

Breaking the Itch-Scratch Cycle

Anti-itch strategies
Cool compress (wet flannel) to reduce itch sensation acutely. 1% hydrocortisone cream BD × 2 weeks (short course only) for acute inflammatory component. A sedating antihistamine at night may reduce nocturnal scratching but is not an evidence-based antipruritic — avoid or use with great caution in older adults (anticholinergic, falls), with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines or driving, and in children without age-appropriate advice.
Nail hygiene
Keep nails short and filed (reduces skin trauma from scratching). Cotton gloves at night (prevents scratching during sleep). Scratching causes excoriation → secondary infection → worsened itch cycle.
Barrier protection
Apply zinc oxide cream or petroleum jelly (Vaseline) to clean dry perianal skin after washing and after defecation. Prevents moisture contact. Use at bedtime to prevent nocturnal scratching skin damage.
The itch-scratch cycle in pruritus ani is self-perpetuating — scratching causes physical skin barrier damage, which releases histamine and pro-inflammatory mediators (substance P, IL-31), which worsen the itch. Breaking this cycle requires simultaneous treatment of the itch stimulus (topical steroid for inflammation, barrier cream for moisture), reduction of scratching behaviour (gloves, short nails, sedating antihistamine), and treatment of the underlying cause (eliminate contact allergen, treat threadworms, improve stool consistency). Addressing all three simultaneously is more effective than each alone. A sedating antihistamine does not treat pruritus ani itself and carries sedation and falls risk, so it is a selective, short-term adjunct at most.

Lifestyle

Perianal Hygiene & Diet Modification

Water-only cleaning Wash perianal area with lukewarm water only after defecation — no soap, no wet wipes, no shower gels. Avoid antiseptics, fragranced products and abrasive rubbing. Pat dry gently or use a cool hairdryer.
Dietary modification (2-week trial) Eliminate: coffee, tea, cola, alcohol, spicy food, tomatoes, citrus fruits, chocolate. These increase perianal moisture and alter stool pH. An individual 2–4-week trial; reintroduce one at a time to identify personal triggers.
Stool consistency Firm, well-formed stool reduces perianal soiling. High-fibre diet (25 g/day) + adequate fluids. Avoid frequent loose stools. If faecal leakage or loose stool contributes, address the cause first; loperamide may suit selected adults without red flags (not in bloody diarrhoea, fever, suspected infective colitis, IBD flare or ileus; follow product dose limits). Paradox: constipation also worsens itch via straining → haemorrhoids → mucus.
Underwear and clothing Loose-fitting white cotton underwear. Avoid synthetic fibres (trap moisture and heat). Change underwear daily. Avoid tight-fitting trousers/leggings. No panty liners (occlusive, traps moisture). No thongs (fecal spread, friction).
Avoid wet wipes Avoid leave-on wet wipes, especially fragranced or preserved (methylisothiazolinone) products — often the cause of the itch. Water cleansing is preferable; if unavailable, a genuinely fragrance-free, preservative-free product used sparingly.
Manage excess moisture / sweating Excess perianal sweating worsens maceration. Cotton undergarments. Avoid talc and cornstarch powders (they cake and worsen itch); use a thin layer of petroleum jelly or zinc oxide as a barrier. Weight loss reduces intergluteal sweating. Sleep in loose cotton shorts.
The dietary connection to pruritus ani is mechanistically well understood — caffeine and certain foods cause relaxation of the internal anal sphincter (caffeine is a phosphodiesterase inhibitor that relaxes smooth muscle), leading to small amounts of fecal leakage onto the perianal skin. This microsoiling, combined with the alkaline pH of feces, disrupts the perianal skin acid mantle (normal pH 4–5) and activates protease-activated receptors (PARs) that trigger itch. Responses vary, so present it as a personal experiment: "Try cutting out coffee for 2–4 weeks and see if the itch improves — if it does, you may have found a trigger."

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

4–6 weeks
Review at 2–4 weeks if treatment started or symptoms significant; by 4–6 weeks, meaningful improvement? If not → consider secondary cause (patch testing, skin scraping, biopsy). Review all topical products the patient is using — contact allergen?
No improvement
No meaningful improvement after 4–6 weeks of appropriate conservative treatment → escalate investigation and refer to dermatology, colorectal or GUM by findings. Biopsy atypical lesions. Do not continue empirical topical treatment indefinitely.
Threadworm eradication
Treat household simultaneously; repeat at 2 weeks if advised locally. Symptoms should resolve within 2 weeks of treatment + hygiene measures. Re-treat in 3 months if recurrence (especially children in nursery/school — frequent re-exposure).
Lichen sclerosus review
Confirmed LS needs ongoing review and patient self-examination. New persistent ulceration, induration, nodule, hyperkeratosis, bleeding or treatment-resistant change → reassess, biopsy if indicated. Vulval/penile LS: examine perianal skin at review.
999 safety-net
Anaphylaxis to mebendazole (rare) — new rash + angioedema + wheeze after dose → 999.
Same-day GP
Perianal itch + new rectal bleeding, rapidly spreading perianal skin change, new lump or ulcer developing, systemic symptoms developing (fever, weight loss, lymphadenopathy)
Perianal "eczema" that fails appropriate treatment, or looks atypical, needs its diagnosis reassessed — usually with biopsy. The biopsied diagnoses discovered at this stage include EMPD, Bowen's disease (anal SCC in situ), lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, and Crohn's disease — all of which require specialist management and all of which have significant clinical consequences if missed. Document the review and the reason for escalation when treatment fails.
