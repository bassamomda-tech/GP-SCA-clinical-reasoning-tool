1
Safety

Red Flags — the renal & haematological emergencies

Nephrotic syndrome A clinical syndrome, not a urine number alone: heavy proteinuria (nephrotic range approximately PCR >300 or ACR >250 mg/mmol) with hypoalbuminaemia and oedema (periorbital, leg, ascites), ± hyperlipidaemia; frothy urine. Risk of VTE and infection — new nephrotic syndrome → urgent nephrology discussion; same-day hospital assessment if severe or pulmonary oedema, AKI, thrombosis, infection, severe hypertension or clinical instability.
Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis / AKI Proteinuria + visible or dysmorphic haematuria + rising creatinine + hypertension ± systemic features (rash, haemoptysis). A nephritic emergency — also pulmonary haemorrhage with renal impairment, oliguria, severe hyperkalaemia, acidosis or fluid overload → same-day assessment.
Myeloma — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Aged 60+ with persistent bone pain (especially back pain) or unexplained fracture → offer FBC, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains (urine Bence-Jones protein if serum free light chains unavailable). If these suggest myeloma → suspected cancer pathway referral. In proteinuria, also consider a myeloma screen when protein is disproportionate to ACR, or with unexplained anaemia, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia, bone pain, weight loss or raised ESR/PV. Same-day if AKI, symptomatic hypercalcaemia or suspected spinal cord compression.
Haematuria with proteinuria — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Aged 45+ with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or visible haematuria persisting or recurring after a treated UTI; or aged 60+ with unexplained non-visible haematuria and either dysuria or a raised blood white cell count → Suspected cancer: urology (bladder/renal cancer), alongside the renal work-up. Haematuria with significant proteinuria at any age also suggests glomerular disease; significant proteinuria itself is not a suspected cancer referral. Confirm persistent non-visible haematuria and investigate renal and urological causes in parallel — do not delay urgent renal assessment because a urological referral is also indicated.
Pre-eclampsia (pregnant) New proteinuria (PCR ≥30 or ACR ≥8 mg/mmol — not a first-morning sample) + new hypertension ≥140/90 after 20 weeks → urgent maternity assessment; same-day particularly with BP ≥160/110, neurological symptoms, epigastric/right-upper-quadrant pain, pulmonary oedema, AKI, thrombocytopenia or abnormal liver tests. Stop ACEi/ARB promptly in pregnancy and offer an alternative.
Malignant hypertension Proteinuria with BP ≥180/120 and retinal haemorrhages or papilloedema, or life-threatening symptoms (new confusion, chest pain, heart failure, AKI) → same-day specialist assessment (NICE NG136).
Hyperkalaemia on RAS blockade Potassium ≥6.0 mmol/L in someone with CKD on an ACEi/ARB → review and stop other contributors, stop the ACEi/ARB and seek same-day advice; check for AKI.
Proteinuria is usually a chronic marker, but a handful of presentations are emergencies hiding behind a dipstick result. Rapidly progressive nephritic presentations and proteinuria with acute kidney injury need same-day assessment because the kidney is being actively damaged; new nephrotic syndrome needs urgent nephrology discussion, and same-day assessment if severe or complicated. The examiner-critical point is myeloma: standard urine dipsticks detect albumin, not the monoclonal free light chains (Bence Jones protein) of myeloma, so an older patient with bone pain, anaemia, hypercalcaemia or a high ESR needs a deliberate myeloma screen (serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains) — the proteinuria may be "overflow", and the dipstick can be falsely reassuring. For haematuria the NG12 age thresholds matter: visible haematuria at 45 or over, and non-visible haematuria at 60 or over with dysuria or a raised white count, are the suspected cancer triggers; younger patients with haematuria and proteinuria are more likely to have glomerular disease and need the renal route rather than a cancer pathway.
2
Diagnose

History — transient vs persistent, and the systemic clues

Transient triggers
Recent fever, UTI, vigorous exercise, heart failure, or an acute illness can all cause transient proteinuria — re-test when recovered before labelling it chronic.
Orthostatic pattern
In younger patients, proteinuria present when upright but absent in a first-morning sample is benign orthostatic proteinuria — confirm with an early-morning ACR.
Diabetes & hypertension
Duration of diabetes, glycaemic and BP control — diabetic nephropathy and hypertensive nephrosclerosis are the commonest causes of persistent proteinuria.
Glomerular clues
Frothy urine, oedema, recent throat/skin infection (post-strep), haemoptysis or rash (vasculitis/SLE), NSAID use.
Systemic / red-flag
Bone pain, weight loss, night sweats, recurrent infection (myeloma); known cancer; family history of renal disease (Alport, polycystic).
Drugs
NSAIDs, gold/penicillamine, lithium, bisphosphonates, some biologics (e.g. anti-VEGF agents); check OTC and herbal use. (ACE inhibitors reduce proteinuria; they are a treatment, not a cause.)
Pregnancy
Ask about pregnancy in women of child-bearing age — new proteinuria after 20 weeks has its own thresholds and urgency (NICE NG133) and is managed through obstetric pathways, not the CKD algorithm.
Symptoms of CKD
Usually none until advanced — fatigue, itch, nocturia, breathlessness or leg swelling suggest more advanced disease or fluid overload.
Ideas and concerns
Many people hear "protein in the urine" as kidney failure — ask what they understand and explain that most proteinuria found in primary care is a risk marker to be managed, not a sign of imminent dialysis.
The first decision in proteinuria is whether it is real and persistent. Fever, urinary infection, strenuous exercise and decompensated heart failure all produce transient proteinuria that resolves, so a single positive result is repeated once the patient is well. In younger people, orthostatic proteinuria — present on standing, absent in the first-morning sample — is benign and needs only reassurance. The history then sorts the persistent causes into the common metabolic ones (diabetes, hypertension) and the glomerular and systemic ones that change the urgency and the work-up.
3
Diagnose

Quantify — ACR is the standard, not the dipstick

Use ACR
Urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR) is the recommended test for proteinuria in CKD, in preference to PCR, because it is more sensitive at low levels (NICE NG203, 2021). Do not use reagent strips to detect proteinuria unless they measure albumin at low concentrations and express it as an ACR. PCR remains useful for non-albumin proteinuria and in pregnancy.
Confirm persistence
An initial ACR 3–70 mg/mmol should be confirmed on a repeat early-morning sample (NICE NG203); an initial ACR ≥70 needs no repeat to confirm. CKD is defined by abnormalities (ACR >3 or eGFR <60) persisting for more than 3 months.
Thresholds
ACR <3 = A1 (normal to mildly increased); 3–30 = A2 (moderately increased); >30 = A3 (severely increased). PCR and ACR measure different components and are not reliably interchangeable; use PCR mainly where non-albumin proteinuria is suspected. Stage every patient by both eGFR (G1–G5) and ACR (A1–A3).
Haematuria check
Test for haematuria with a reagent strip rather than microscopy; if 1+ or more, confirm and investigate persistent non-visible haematuria alongside the proteinuria (NICE NG203).
Pregnancy thresholds
After a positive dipstick in pregnancy, quantify with PCR (significant ≥30 mg/mmol) or ACR (≥8 mg/mmol). Do not use a first-morning sample and do not routinely use a 24-hour collection; if at or above threshold with clinical uncertainty, repeat on a new sample alongside clinical review (NICE NG133).
Exclude UTI
Always send a urine culture / exclude infection before attributing proteinuria to renal disease.
Non-albumin proteinuria
If dipstick protein is positive but ACR is low, request PCR and consider tubular or overflow (light-chain) proteinuria → myeloma screen.
Quantification matters because the number drives both staging and referral. The albumin:creatinine ratio on an early-morning urine (outside pregnancy) is the standard, removing the variability of a dipstick and the inconvenience of 24-hour collections. A mismatch — dipstick-positive but ACR low — is an important clue, because it points away from albumin (glomerular) proteinuria towards tubular or overflow light-chain proteinuria, and should prompt a PCR and a myeloma screen rather than reassurance. Staging by both eGFR and ACR matters because albuminuria independently predicts cardiovascular events and progression: a patient with a normal eGFR but an ACR of 40 carries more risk than the eGFR alone suggests.
4
Diagnose

Differential by mechanism

Glomerular
Diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, glomerulonephritis (IgA, membranous, FSGS, lupus, post-infectious). Usually albumin-predominant; heavy proteinuria = nephrotic range.
Tubular
Tubulointerstitial disease, drugs (NSAIDs, lithium), reflux nephropathy. Lower-grade, often non-albumin.
Overflow
Do not miss Monoclonal free light chains in myeloma — dipstick-negative, detected by electrophoresis/free light chains.
Transient / benign
Fever, exercise, orthostatic, heart failure — resolve on retest.
Post-renal / contamination
UTI, menstrual contamination, vaginal discharge — exclude before work-up.
Clues that separate them
Diabetes: long duration, retinopathy, gradual rise in ACR, bland urine. Glomerulonephritis: haematuria with red-cell casts, hypertension, rapid change, systemic features. Hypertensive: long-standing poorly controlled BP, modest ACR. Polycystic kidney disease: family history, loin pain, palpable kidneys, cysts on ultrasound. Myeloma: older age, anaemia, high calcium or ESR, low ACR relative to total protein.
Sorting proteinuria by mechanism keeps the dangerous overflow cause visible alongside the common glomerular ones. Glomerular proteinuria (diabetes, hypertension, the glomerulonephritides) is albumin-predominant and well captured by ACR. Tubular and overflow proteinuria are not — and overflow proteinuria is the footprint of myeloma. Holding these categories in mind prevents the two classic errors: investigating benign orthostatic proteinuria as if it were renal disease, and reassuring an older patient with overflow proteinuria whose ACR looks unremarkable.
5
Diagnose

Investigations

Urine
ACR (early-morning; repeat if 3–70) ± PCR if non-albumin proteinuria suspected; dipstick for blood; sediment examination only if glomerulonephritis suspected or per local laboratory pathway; culture if UTI possible.
Bloods
Creatinine/eGFR, urea, electrolytes, bicarbonate, HbA1c/glucose, FBC, lipids, bone profile (calcium), albumin. BP (home or ambulatory where relevant); medication, OTC NSAID and supplement review.
Myeloma screen
When protein is disproportionate to ACR (dipstick/PCR positive with low ACR), or with unexplained anaemia, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia, bone pain, weight loss or raised ESR/PV: ESR/plasma viscosity, serum protein electrophoresis + serum free light chains; urine Bence Jones only if serum free light chains unavailable. A normal ACR does not exclude myeloma.
Immunology
If glomerulonephritis suspected: ANA/anti-dsDNA, ANCA, complement, immunoglobulins, anti-GBM, hepatitis B/C, HIV — usually with urgent renal advice.
Imaging
Renal ultrasound — size, obstruction, polycystic kidneys, asymmetry.
Do NOT
Diagnose CKD on a single eGFR or proteinuria on a single dipstick; both need confirmation.
Kidney failure risk
For people with CKD G3–G5, calculate the 4-variable Kidney Failure Risk Equation (age, sex, eGFR, ACR) and record the 5-year risk (NICE NG203, 2021). Do not use during AKI, unstable renal function or active UTI; interpret cautiously in unusual primary renal diseases.
Ultrasound indications
Accelerated progression, visible or persistent non-visible haematuria, symptoms of obstruction, family history of polycystic kidney disease (age over 20), eGFR <30, or when a biopsy is being considered.
Investigation is tiered by what the history and ACR suggest. Everyone gets the core panel — eGFR, HbA1c, FBC, lipids and a confirmed ACR — that stages CKD and identifies the common drivers. A glomerulonephritis picture (haematuria, casts, systemic features, falling eGFR) triggers the immunology screen and prompt referral, while an older patient or a non-albumin pattern triggers the myeloma screen. Renal ultrasound checks structure and obstruction and is targeted by indication rather than done for everyone. The Kidney Failure Risk Equation turns eGFR and ACR into a 5-year probability of needing dialysis, which is both a referral trigger and a practical way to explain risk to a patient. The recurring rule is confirmation: neither CKD nor proteinuria is diagnosed on a single reading.
6
Refer

Referral criteria

Myeloma — NG12
Protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains or Bence-Jones test suggesting myeloma → suspected cancer pathway referral to haematology (NICE NG12). Same-day if AKI, symptomatic hypercalcaemia or suspected spinal cord compression. A small paraprotein without other features (possible MGUS) → discuss with haematology rather than reassure.
Urology — NG12
Unexplained visible haematuria at 45 or over (without UTI, or persisting after UTI treatment), or unexplained non-visible haematuria at 60 or over with dysuria or a raised white cell count → suspected cancer pathway referral for bladder/renal cancer (NICE NG12, updated April 2026).
Nephrology (routine/urgent)
ACR ≥70 mg/mmol (unless known to be caused by diabetes and already appropriately treated); ACR >30 with haematuria; sustained decrease in eGFR of 25% or more with a change in G category within 12 months, or of 15 ml/min/1.73 m² or more within 12 months; 5-year KFRE risk >5%; hypertension poorly controlled despite 4 or more antihypertensives; known or suspected rare or genetic cause; suspected renal artery stenosis; renal outflow obstruction needing specialist management (NICE NG203, 2021).
Before referring
Include the confirmed ACR values, eGFR trend, BP, HbA1c, urine dip for blood, ultrasound result if done, medication list and the KFRE score. Many renal units offer advice and guidance for borderline cases.
Same-day / emergency
AKI or rapidly progressive renal dysfunction with proteinuria/haematuria · proteinuria + haematuria + hypertension + systemic features (RPGN) · pulmonary haemorrhage/haemoptysis with renal impairment · severe hyperkalaemia, acidosis, oliguria or fluid overload · new nephrotic syndrome with significant oedema, thrombosis, infection or severe hypoalbuminaemia · suspected spinal cord compression or severe hypercalcaemia in possible myeloma · severe hypertension with retinal changes, neurological symptoms, chest pain, pulmonary oedema or AKI · suspected pre-eclampsia. Otherwise, new nephrotic syndrome → urgent nephrology discussion.
Obstetric
New proteinuria + hypertension (≥140/90) after 20 weeks → urgent maternity assessment for pre-eclampsia, same-day particularly with severe hypertension (≥160/110), neurological symptoms, epigastric/right-upper-quadrant pain, pulmonary oedema, AKI, thrombocytopenia or abnormal liver tests (NICE NG133). Proteinuria in pregnancy follows obstetric, not CKD, pathways.
The referral thresholds are deliberately concrete: an ACR of 70 or above, or above 30 with haematuria, defines the renal disease that benefits from nephrology, as does any sustained fall in eGFR. Around these sit the two cancer pathways — a positive myeloma screen to haematology, and visible or qualifying non-visible haematuria to urology under NG12 — and the emergencies (rapidly progressive nephritic disease, AKI, complicated nephrotic syndrome) that bypass all of this for same-day assessment. In pregnancy, new proteinuria with hypertension is pre-eclampsia until proven otherwise. For patients who do not meet the thresholds, the GP remains the long-term manager: most people with diabetic or hypertensive CKD are never referred, so the renoprotection bundle in step 7 is the main intervention.
7
Treat

Slow progression — the renoprotection bundle

Diabetes + ACR ≥3; hypertension + ACR >30; or any ACR ≥70 — offer and refer (NICE NG203)
ACE inhibitor or ARB
Titrate to the maximum tolerated dose — the antiproteinuric effect, not just BP, is the goal. Check potassium and eGFR before starting and 1–2 weeks after starting/each dose increase (do not change the dose if eGFR falls by less than 25% or creatinine rises by less than 30%). Do not routinely start if potassium is above 5.0 mmol/L; stop if potassium reaches 6.0 mmol/L or more, after reviewing and stopping other contributors to hyperkalaemia. If creatinine rises by ≥30% or eGFR falls by ≥25%, investigate volume depletion, NSAIDs, obstruction and renovascular disease. Do not combine ACEi + ARB. No diabetes with ACR 30–70: monitor, and seek renal advice if ACR rises or eGFR declines — not for every low-level albuminuria result. Stop promptly if pregnancy occurs and offer an alternative.
eGFR 20 to <45; or eGFR 45–90 with ACR ≥22.6 mg/mmol or type 2 diabetes (NICE TA1075, 2025; TA942, 2023)
SGLT2 inhibitor
Dapagliflozin/empagliflozin as add-on to optimised standard care, including the highest tolerated licensed ACEi/ARB dose unless contraindicated — follow the individual TA, product licence and local GM formulary. Not for CKD treatment in type 1 diabetes. Assess volume status (diuretics, frailty, low BP); counsel about genital candidiasis and rare euglycaemic DKA; withhold during significant acute illness, vomiting, dehydration and before major surgery per local protocol; avoid initiation during ketoacidosis or suspected AKI; expect a modest early eGFR fall — seek advice if excessive or with clinical deterioration; foot-care advice in diabetic foot or peripheral vascular disease.
Cardiovascular risk
Atorvastatin 20 mg
Offer atorvastatin 20 mg for primary or secondary prevention of CVD in adults with CKD (NICE NG238) — do not use QRISK3 to decide eligibility in CKD; for established CVD follow secondary-prevention recommendations. If non-HDL cholesterol falls by less than 40% and eGFR is 30 or more, increase the dose where clinically appropriate; discuss higher doses with the renal team if eGFR is below 30. Lipids 2–3 months after starting or changing; CK only if muscle symptoms (unless indicated at baseline). Contraindicated in pregnancy; avoid when breastfeeding unless specialist advice. Antiplatelet only for established vascular disease.
Blood pressure
CKD with ACR ≥70: aim for systolic 120–129 and diastolic below 80. CKD with ACR below 70: systolic 120–139 and diastolic below 90 (NICE NG203). ACEi/ARB first-line where albuminuria thresholds are met.
Glycaemic control
Optimise HbA1c in diabetic kidney disease; SGLT2 inhibitor preferred where eligible.
Treat the cause
Immunosuppression for specific glomerulonephritides (specialist-led); stop nephrotoxins (NSAIDs).
Type 2 diabetes
Other kidney-protective options are specialist-guided: a GLP-1 receptor agonist can help weight and glycaemia, and finerenone may be added for people with type 2 diabetes and CKD already on optimised ACEi/ARB and SGLT2 inhibitor where eligible — dose and eligibility per NICE TA877 and the BNF.
Medication review
Adjust renally cleared drugs (e.g. metformin, DOACs, gabapentinoids, nitrofurantoin) to eGFR — dose per BNF.
Do NOT
Start ACEi/ARB without a follow-up U&E, or continue NSAIDs in significant proteinuric CKD.
For most proteinuric kidney disease the disease-modifying treatment is a bundle, not a single drug. Blocking the renin–angiotensin system with an ACE inhibitor or ARB titrated to the maximum tolerated dose reduces proteinuria and slows progression, and an SGLT2 inhibitor added on top now has strong evidence for protecting the kidney and the heart. Around these sit blood-pressure and lipid targets, glycaemic control in diabetes, and removal of nephrotoxins. The safety rule is the post-initiation U&E: a modest eGFR dip is expected and acceptable, but it must be checked. In the SCA, explain the dip in advance ("your kidney blood test may change slightly when we start this — that's expected and a sign the tablet is working, so we'll check it in a fortnight") to avoid alarm and non-adherence.
8
Lifestyle

Risk-factor & self-management

Salt & diet Reducing dietary salt enhances the antiproteinuric effect of ACEi/ARB and lowers BP; a balanced diet without excessive protein is advised — avoid very high-protein regimens. Do not offer low-protein diets (below 0.6–0.8 g/kg/day) to people with CKD (NICE NG203). Dietetic referral for advanced CKD, malnutrition risk or nephrotic syndrome.
Smoking cessation Smoking accelerates CKD progression and multiplies cardiovascular risk — the single highest-yield change.
Weight & activity Weight loss reduces proteinuria and BP; regular activity improves cardiovascular and metabolic risk.
Glycaemic self-care In diabetes, good glucose control and adherence to renoprotective drugs slow nephropathy.
Avoid nephrotoxins Counsel against routine NSAID use and unregulated herbal remedies; individualised advice for ACEi/ARB/SGLT2i/diuretics during vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration or acute illness.
Vaccination Review vaccination status in all patients with clinically significant CKD. Offer seasonal influenza and pneumococcal vaccination according to the current Green Book schedule; consider hepatitis B vaccination in advanced CKD, particularly where dialysis is anticipated; review COVID-19 vaccination per current national eligibility. Patients on immunosuppression may need additional or specialist advice (UKHSA Green Book, chapter 7).
Know your numbers Encourage patients to know their ACR, eGFR stage and BP target, and to ask for these at each review.
Lifestyle in proteinuric CKD is genuinely disease-modifying rather than supportive. Salt restriction unlocks the full antiproteinuric benefit of RAS blockade, smoking cessation slows progression and protects the heart, and weight loss lowers both proteinuria and blood pressure. Equally important is what to avoid — routine NSAIDs and unregulated herbal products — and individualised advice for the renoprotective drugs, which may need pausing during significant dehydrating illness to prevent acute kidney injury.
9
Safety

Follow-up & safety-netting

Monitor
Monitor eGFR at least at the minimum frequency in NICE NG203 Table 2 (about once a year in early stages, rising to 2–4 times a year at G4–G5 and with heavier albuminuria), tailored to cause, ACR, eGFR trend, comorbidity and medication changes; individualise ACR monitoring. Track BP and target, potassium, HbA1c, cardiovascular risk treatment, medication doses and KFRE where applicable.
Acute deterioration
Same-day New oedema with frothy urine (nephrotic), visible haematuria, rapidly rising creatinine, or oliguria.
Myeloma safety-net
Persistent unexplained proteinuria with bone pain, anaemia, hypercalcaemia or high ESR → complete/repeat the myeloma screen — do not let a normal ACR close the question.
Drug safety
Recheck U&E after each ACEi/ARB/diuretic change and during intercurrent illness; give individualised advice for dehydrating illness.
Pregnancy
Any new proteinuria + hypertension after 20 weeks → urgent maternity assessment (same day if severe features).
Document
Record the confirmed ACR, CKD G/A stage, cause, treatment, targets, and review interval; code CKD so that the patient enters the CKD register and recall system.
Audit checklist
ACR confirmed (repeat if 3–70) · eGFR trend recorded · BP and target documented · potassium checked after starting/uptitrating ACEi/ARB · KFRE recorded (G3–G5) · statin prescription · SGLT2 inhibitor eligibility assessed · referral decision documented · safety-netting documented.
SCA safety-net wording
"The test shows a small amount of protein leaking from the kidneys. It doesn't mean your kidneys are failing, but it's a signal to protect them and your heart. If you notice swelling of your ankles or face, very frothy urine, blood in your urine or you pass much less urine, contact us the same day."
Follow-up does two things: it tracks the trajectory of the kidney (serial ACR and eGFR, with monitoring frequency scaled to CKD stage) and it keeps the dangerous causes under review. The myeloma safety-net never fully closes in older patients with unexplained proteinuria, and any drift towards nephrotic features, visible haematuria or a rising creatinine warrants same-day reassessment. Each change to a renoprotective drug — and every dehydrating illness — is a cue to recheck renal function. Monitoring intervals rise with both worsening eGFR and heavier albuminuria, which is why every patient needs both numbers coded.
