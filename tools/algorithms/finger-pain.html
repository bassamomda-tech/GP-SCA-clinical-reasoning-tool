Safety

Red Flags — Septic Arthritis, Malignancy & Vascular Emergency

Acutely swollen hot tender finger joint + fever + inability to move joint + systemically unwell Suspected septic arthritis of a finger, MCP or PIP joint → same-day hospital/hand-surgery assessment. Do not delay transfer for community investigations. The receiving service arranges aspiration (microscopy, Gram stain, culture) where feasible; antibiotics follow the local microbiology pathway (allergy, likely organisms, resistance, bite/water exposure, immunosuppression) and are given promptly after cultures unless the patient is septic or unstable.
Finger pain + progressive sensory loss + wasting of intrinsic hand muscles + Horner syndrome (ptosis + miosis) Pancoast tumour (superior sulcus — invades brachial plexus C8-T1 + sympathetic chain). → urgent CXR (NICE NG12) + CT chest; lung suspected cancer pathway referral if imaging suggests lung cancer.
Acute painful digital ischaemia — cold, pale/blue digit, rapidly progressive colour change, sensory loss or absent Doppler signals Assess capillary refill, temperature and colour against the other hand, Doppler if available, sensory and motor status; the radial pulse may remain palpable in digital artery occlusion. Causes: embolism, vasculitis, arterial trauma, hypothenar hammer syndrome, severe Raynaud's → immediate ED/vascular or hand-surgery assessment; 999 if the digit or hand is threatened, the patient is systemically unwell or there is major trauma/vascular compromise. Do not delay transfer for Allen's testing.
Fusiform swelling of entire finger + tenderness along flexor tendon sheath + finger held in slight flexion + pain on passive extension Suspected pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis (Kanavel signs) → same-day ED/hand-surgery assessment. Treatment may involve operative washout, IV antibiotics per local microbiology guidance, elevation and splinting. Delay may result in tendon necrosis, adhesions, stiffness and loss of function.
Rapidly spreading erythema + lymphangitis (red streak to forearm) + fever from finger wound or paronychia Septic digital infection spreading to hand/forearm → same-day ED/hand-surgery assessment (999 if septic or unstable). Antibiotics per local microbiology pathway (consider bite or water exposure). Ascending lymphangitis = systemic sepsis risk.
Persistent unexplained focal bone pain or swelling, lytic lesion or pathological fracture Arrange X-ray and act on the findings → suspected-cancer (sarcoma) pathway referral if the X-ray suggests bone sarcoma (NICE NG12).
Kanavel's four cardinal signs of flexor tenosynovitis are one of the most important surgical emergency frameworks in hand surgery — they must be memorised by every GP who examines hands: (1) Finger held in slight flexion at rest (the position of minimum tenosynovial tension); (2) Uniform fusiform swelling of the entire finger ('sausage finger' — the swelling follows the tendon sheath from distal palm to fingertip); (3) Intense pain on passive extension of the finger (as the inflamed sheath is stretched); (4) Tenderness along the course of the flexor tendon sheath on palpation (palmar surface from A1 pulley to fingertip). Suspected pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis needs same-day ED/hand-surgery assessment — not every case needs a 999 ambulance. The flexor tendon sheath is a closed space with poor blood supply, and delay may result in tendon necrosis, adhesions, stiffness and loss of function; treatment may involve operative washout, IV antibiotics chosen by local microbiology guidance, elevation and splinting.

Classification of Finger Pain — Joint, Tendon & Soft Tissue

Arthritis affecting fingers
Osteoarthritis (OA): DIP (Heberden's nodes) and PIP (Bouchard's nodes) joints most common; insidious onset; bony swelling; activity-related pain; morning stiffness usually no more than 30 minutes; typical hand OA is a clinical diagnosis. First CMC (thumb-base) OA: thumb-base pain and reduced pinch strength. MCP swelling: consider RA, gout, CPPD or haemochromatosis rather than assuming OA. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): symmetrical MCP + PIP involvement (spares DIP); morning stiffness >1h; warm soft swelling (synovitis); ulnar deviation; anti-CCP antibody; RF. Psoriatic arthritis: DIP joint involvement (distinctive — RA spares DIP); associated nail changes (pitting, onycholysis, oil-drop); dactylitis (sausage digit). Gout: acute urate crystal arthritis — classically the first MTP joint, but can affect the wrist, MCP, PIP and DIP joints; intensely painful, erythematous, hot, tender. A hot swollen finger joint has a broad differential (septic arthritis, CPPD, psoriatic arthritis, trauma).
Tendon and pulley disorders
Trigger finger (stenosing tenosynovitis): locking or clicking of finger in flexion on active movement — thickened A1 pulley traps flexor tendon; most common ring finger; palpable nodule at A1 pulley (proximal flexor crease). De Quervain's tenosynovitis: first dorsal compartment (APL + EPB) at radial styloid — pain on Finkelstein's test (thumb adducted into fist then wrist ulnarly deviated) — not diagnostic alone; consider first CMC OA, intersection syndrome, scaphoid pathology and superficial radial nerve irritation. Mallet finger: avulsion of extensor tendon at DIP → drooping DIP joint — splint (urgent hand assessment if open injury, volar subluxation, large bony avulsion or inability to maintain extension). Suspected boutonnière injury needs a different splinting position — do not use the mallet pathway.
Soft tissue and structural
Ganglion cyst: smooth, transilluminable swelling at dorsum of wrist or dorsal DIP joint (mucous cyst). Dupuytren's contracture: ring and little finger most common — palmar nodule → cord → progressive MCP and PIP flexion contracture. Digital ischaemia from Raynaud's. Nail fold pathology (paronychia, melanoma — see nail disorders algorithm). Enchondroma (benign bone tumour of phalanx — lytic lesion on X-ray, risk of pathological fracture).
Dupuytren's contracture management briefly included collagenase clostridium histolyticum (Xiapex), but it was withdrawn from the UK and EU market in March 2020 and NICE TA459 was withdrawn — current options are needle fasciotomy and fasciectomy. GPs should know the referral criteria for this condition (more common in men, older age, Northern European descent, diabetes, alcohol, epilepsy/phenobarbitone). The tabletop test is a useful functional assessment, but a positive test is not automatically a reason for referral within 1–2 weeks. Refer routinely to hand surgery/hand therapy if contracture affects function, MCP contracture is approximately 30° or more, there is progressive PIP contracture, the patient cannot place the hand flat, or there is diagnostic uncertainty or concern about another mass; increase urgency with rapid progression, severe functional limitation or neurovascular compromise. The MCP joint contracture at ≥30° is the standard threshold for intervention (percutaneous needle fasciotomy or surgical fasciectomy; collagenase (Xiapex) is no longer available in the UK). Needle fasciotomy: cord divided percutaneously under local anaesthetic — quicker recovery but higher recurrence; lower efficacy for PIP contractures. Surgical fasciotomy/fasciectomy: higher correction rate, more invasive. GPs who observe progressive Dupuytren's contracture without referral when the patient has functional impairment are missing an intervention window.

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Which joint(s) affected (DIP = OA, mucous cyst; PIP = RA, OA; MCP = RA, gout; all = psoriatic, RA, gout flare). Onset: acute (gout, septic arthritis, trauma, trigger finger lock) vs insidious (OA, RA, Dupuytren's). Swelling: bony (OA — Heberden's/Bouchard's nodes) vs soft/warm (inflammatory). Morning stiffness: >1h (RA, PsA, any inflammatory) vs <30min (OA). Occupation and activities (repetitive grip — trigger finger; vibration tools — Raynaud's, de Quervain's). Skin and nails: psoriasis (PsA), nail pitting. Systemic: weight loss, fever (septic, malignancy), rash (RA — neutrophilic dermatosis, psoriasis). Family history: gout, RA, Dupuytren's, Heberden's nodes.
Examination
All fingers systematically. Bony enlargement at DIP (Heberden's) or PIP (Bouchard's): OA. Soft warm synovitis at MCP/PIP (feel for boggy swelling vs bony): RA vs OA. Erythema + hot + maximally tender + fever: septic / acute gout. Dactylitis (entire digit swollen — sausage digit): psoriatic arthritis. Trigger finger: ask patient to flex and extend — palpate A1 pulley for nodule. Kanavel signs (see Step 1). Tabletop test (Dupuytren's). Finkelstein's test (supports de Quervain's — not diagnostic alone). Nail examination (psoriatic, LS, melanoma).
Also document
Exact digit and joint; active versus passive restriction; swelling — bony, synovial, tendon-sheath or diffuse; warmth, erythema and fever; skin/nail psoriasis; trauma, bite, puncture or water exposure; neurovascular status; triggering, locking or extensor lag; contracture and tabletop test; diabetes, immunosuppression and anticoagulants; occupational vibration or repetitive gripping.
Investigations
Only order tests that alter management; do not delay urgent referral for blood tests or imaging. Persistent synovitis / suspected RA RF, anti-CCP if RF negative, ESR/CRP — do not delay referral · Acute hot swollen joint, diagnosis uncertain same-day hospital assessment; aspiration for crystals and culture · Suspected gout serum urate; repeat at least 2 weeks after the flare settles if below 360 µmol/L during the flare; aspiration, or ultrasound/DECT if uncertain · Trauma or suspected fracture plain X-ray per local pathway · Persistent focal bone pain/swelling or lytic lesion X-ray and urgent specialist/sarcoma pathway if suspicious · Typical OA usually no imaging (X-ray only if atypical, uncertain, trauma, suspected inflammatory arthritis, focal bone pain/swelling, deformity or possible tumour/fracture) · Digital ischaemia immediate vascular/hand assessment — do not delay for blood tests. Not routine: ultrasound for every trigger finger or tendon complaint; HbA1c solely because of trigger finger or Dupuytren's; ANA/anti-Scl-70/anticentromere in every Raynaud's presentation; serum ACE.
The joint aspiration and synovial fluid analysis is the single most important investigation in distinguishing septic arthritis from crystal arthropathy (gout or pseudogout) in an acutely swollen hot joint — and GPs cannot usually perform this in primary care, making prompt same-day hospital assessment essential. The synovial fluid findings: septic arthritis: turbid fluid, raised white cell count (predominantly polymorphs), organisms on Gram stain or culture — a negative Gram stain does not exclude infection. Gout: needle-shaped negatively birefringent sodium urate crystals under polarised light microscopy. CPPD (pseudogout): positively birefringent rhomboid calcium pyrophosphate crystals. The critical overlap: septic arthritis and crystal arthropathy can coexist in the same joint — a crystal-positive fluid does not exclude concurrent infection. Do not delay transfer for community investigations: the receiving service arranges aspiration where feasible, and antibiotics are given promptly after cultures unless the patient is septic or clinically unstable.

Rheumatoid Arthritis Early Diagnosis & Gout Criteria

RA — early recognition (NICE NG100)
Refer urgently to rheumatology for persistent synovitis of undetermined cause, particularly if a small hand/foot joint or more than one joint is involved, or there has been a delay of 3 months or more before presentation. Do not wait for blood-test results — normal inflammatory markers, negative RF or negative anti-CCP do not exclude inflammatory arthritis. Request RF, and consider anti-CCP if RF is negative; ESR/CRP may support assessment. X-ray the hands and feet if persistent synovitis is suspected, but do not delay referral. ACR/EULAR classification criteria are a specialist classification tool, not a primary-care diagnostic test. After diagnosis, rheumatology starts first-line conventional DMARD treatment as soon as possible, ideally within 3 months of onset of persistent symptoms.
Gout — diagnosis (NICE NG219)
Definite gout: urate crystals on joint aspiration (gold standard) OR dual-energy CT (DECT) urate deposition OR X-ray tophus. Clinical diagnosis (no aspiration): EULAR clinical gout score — male sex +2 · previous similar attack +2 · onset within 24h +2 · erythema +1 · hypertension/CVD +1 · serum urate >5.88 +3.5. Higher scores make gout more likely. NICE NG219: measure serum urate; if below 360 µmol/L during a flare and suspicion remains high, repeat at least 2 weeks after the flare has settled. If the diagnosis remains uncertain, arrange aspiration and microscopy; consider ultrasound or DECT if aspiration is not possible.
Trigger finger — management
Advise activity modification and analgesia if required. Consider corticosteroid injection where clinically appropriate, the clinician is trained and the local protocol permits — never if infection is suspected. Discuss recurrence, skin depigmentation/subcutaneous atrophy, transient hyperglycaemia, infection and tendon injury. The injection preparation, dose and any repeat interval come from the local formulary/procedure protocol. Refer to hand surgery or the local MSK service if the finger is locked, there is significant functional impairment, symptoms persist or injection is unsuitable.
Anti-CCP is a useful test for rheumatoid arthritis, and positivity predicts a more erosive disease course — but referral is driven by the clinical finding of persistent synovitis, not by serology. NICE NG100 recommends urgent specialist referral for persistent synovitis of undetermined cause, especially with small hand/foot joint or multiple joint involvement, or a delay of 3 months or more before presentation, even if RF, anti-CCP and inflammatory markers are normal. Rheumatology aims to start conventional DMARD treatment as soon as possible, ideally within 3 months of onset of persistent symptoms, because early treatment can prevent joint destruction, deformity and disability. The practical primary care standard: request RF (anti-CCP if RF negative) and ESR/CRP at the same consultation, but make the urgent rheumatology referral at the same time rather than waiting for the results.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day ED / hand surgery
Suspected flexor tenosynovitis (Kanavel signs) · suspected septic arthritis of a finger joint · threatened digital ischaemia (999 if digit/hand threatened, systemically unwell or major trauma) · severe or spreading hand infection · major tendon injury · mallet injury that is open, with volar subluxation, large bony avulsion or inability to maintain extension
Rheumatology (urgent — NICE NG100)
Persistent synovitis of undetermined cause, particularly small hand/foot joints, more than one joint, or a delay of 3 months or more before presentation; do not wait for blood results. Suspected RA, psoriatic arthritis, or inflammatory arthropathy. Tophaceous gout with joint damage. Polymyalgia / GCA (if finger involvement in giant cell arteritis).
Hand surgery (routine)
Trigger finger: locked, significant functional impairment, persistent symptoms or injection unsuitable · Dupuytren's: contracture affecting function, MCP contracture approximately 30° or more, progressive PIP contracture, cannot place hand flat, or diagnostic uncertainty/another mass (more urgent if rapid progression, severe functional limitation or neurovascular compromise) · Mallet finger with failed splinting · Ganglion cyst causing symptoms · De Quervain's: diagnosis uncertain, neurological deficit, trauma or failed conservative treatment · Urgent imaging/sarcoma pathway: persistent focal bone pain/swelling, suspicious lytic lesion or pathological fracture
GP management
Typical OA fingers: topical NSAID (locally formulary-approved preparation) (NICE NG226); oral NSAID if topical ineffective or unsuitable after assessing GI, renal, hepatic and cardiovascular risk; paracetamol and weak opioids not routine. Gout acute: NSAID, colchicine or a short course of oral corticosteroid (NICE NG219), selected by renal function, age/frailty, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, ulcer history, interacting medicines, pregnancy/breastfeeding and local formulary (doses per BNF). Trigger finger, de Quervain's: activity modification, analgesia, splinting/hand therapy; corticosteroid injection by a trained clinician per local protocol. Mild primary Raynaud's. Uncomplicated stable tendon symptoms. Paronychia: warm soaks + antibiotic per local guidance if spreading.
Corticosteroid injection for trigger finger can be offered in primary care where the clinician is trained and the local protocol permits, and never when infection is suspected. The injection is given at the A1 pulley (proximal flexor crease, over the MCP joint) into the tendon sheath, not the tendon. Preparation, dose and any repeat interval should come from the local formulary/procedure protocol rather than a generic algorithm. Discuss recurrence, skin depigmentation/subcutaneous atrophy, transient hyperglycaemia (warn people with diabetes), infection and tendon injury. Review according to symptoms, complications, diabetes control and the local procedure protocol.

Treat

Gout Management & OA of Fingers

Acute gout — treatment
NICE NG219 first line: an NSAID (with PPI), colchicine or a short course of oral corticosteroid, chosen according to comorbidity and co-prescribing. Before prescribing, check renal function, age/frailty, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, ulcer history, interacting medicines, pregnancy/breastfeeding and the local formulary; doses per BNF. Colchicine cautions: renal and hepatic impairment, elderly patients, macrolides, ciclosporin and other strong CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein inhibitors, statins (myopathy risk), gastrointestinal toxicity and overdose risk. Ice + elevation.
Urate-lowering therapy (ULT) — indications and initiation
Discuss urate-lowering therapy with everyone after a first or subsequent flare — it is not restricted to tophi, recurrent attacks or CKD. Usually start 2–4 weeks after a flare has settled; can start during a flare if flares are frequent. Allopurinol or febuxostat first line (allopurinol preferred in major cardiovascular disease). Start low (check eGFR) and increase according to monthly serum urate. Target: serum urate <360 µmol/L; consider <300 µmol/L if tophi, chronic gouty arthritis or continued frequent flares (NICE NG219, 2022). Continue long term, usually lifelong. Offer flare prophylaxis with colchicine while titrating, if suitable. Counsel all patients to stop allopurinol and seek urgent advice if rash, mucosal involvement, fever or systemic illness develops; HLA-B*58:01 testing only according to local policy.
Finger OA management
Topical NSAID first line (use the locally formulary-approved preparation; check product information) (NICE NG226). Oral: NICE NG226 (2022) advises against routine paracetamol or weak opioids for OA — only short-term, infrequent use if other options are unsuitable. Oral NSAID (with PPI) if topical treatment is ineffective or unsuitable, after assessing GI, renal, hepatic and cardiovascular risk. Intra-articular corticosteroid injection may be considered for a selected painful interphalangeal joint — evidence is limited and benefit is short term; not routine. Splinting: mallet finger splint (DIP straight x 6-8 weeks), ring splint for PIP joint in hypermobility. Occupational therapy: joint protection, assistive devices.
The colchicine prophylaxis strategy when initiating allopurinol is essential for preventing the well-known phenomenon of mobilisation gout — when urate-lowering therapy begins, the rapid fall in serum urate mobilises monosodium urate crystals from existing tissue deposits, triggering inflammatory attacks. Flares are common in the first months of urate-lowering therapy and many patients stop allopurinol, incorrectly attributing the flares to the drug itself. NICE NG219 recommends offering flare prophylaxis with colchicine while titrating, if suitable (dose per BNF, with attention to renal/hepatic function, age and interacting medicines). The critical prescribing communication: 'Allopurinol is working correctly even if you get a gout attack in the first few months — do not stop the allopurinol. The colchicine is to protect you during this initial period.'

RA Shared Care, Injections & Hand Therapy

RA — initial DMARD therapy (shared care with rheumatology)
DMARD choice and initiation (e.g. methotrexate, leflunomide, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine) are rheumatology-led decisions. Primary care does not initiate DMARDs; continued prescribing and monitoring follow a formal shared-care arrangement using the current GMMMG shared-care protocol for the specific drug. Hydroxychloroquine: target dose generally ≤5 mg/kg/day using actual body weight; retinal monitoring (SD-OCT and fundus autofluorescence, visual fields where indicated) annually after 5 years in standard-risk patients, or after 1 year if higher risk (eGFR <60, tamoxifen or dose >5 mg/kg/day) (current RCOphth guidance). The prescribing specialist must ensure referral and the commissioned service performs the monitoring, per the GMMMG hydroxychloroquine shared-care protocol.
Joint and tendon injections in primary care
Small-joint and tendon-sheath corticosteroid injections only by a clinician trained in the procedure and where the local protocol permits; never if infection is suspected. The preparation, dose, volume and interval come from the local formulary/procedure protocol. Discuss post-injection flare, skin depigmentation/subcutaneous atrophy, transient hyperglycaemia, infection and tendon injury. Hand OA: may be considered for a selected painful interphalangeal joint (short-term benefit).
Physiotherapy and occupational therapy for hand conditions
OT hand assessment: joint protection advice (avoid lever movements — push doors with forearm not finger joints), assistive devices (built-up handles, key turners, jar openers), workplace adaptations. Splinting: resting splint (overnight for RA to reduce morning stiffness), working splint (functional — for De Quervain's, carpal tunnel). Physiotherapy: hand exercises (range of motion, strengthening), wax bath hydrotherapy for OA/RA.
DMARD monitoring in primary care is a shared-care responsibility that requires systematic organisation, but there is no single schedule for all DMARDs: monitoring varies with the drug, dose and duration, renal/hepatic function, abnormal results or dose changes, and specialist advice. Use the current GMMMG shared-care protocol for the particular DMARD, including its action thresholds, and keep a clear monitoring template in the clinical record. The MHRA Yellow Card scheme should be used to report serious adverse effects.

Lifestyle

Hand Therapy, Joint Protection & Gout Lifestyle

Gout lifestyle (NICE NG219) Explain that there is not enough evidence that avoiding specific foods prevents flares or lowers urate long term. Advise a healthy, balanced diet, maintaining a healthy weight (avoid crash dieting), limiting alcohol, keeping hydrated and staying active. Treat-to-target urate-lowering therapy, not diet alone, is the main long-term treatment.
Joint protection for OA and RA of fingers Leverage principle: use body weight, not finger joints, to open jars (use palm not fingertips). Large joint substitution: carry bags on forearm not in fingers. Avoid sustained gripping (power grip is high load on MCP joints). Pace repetitive hand tasks with regular rest breaks. Local heat (e.g. warm water or a paraffin wax bath) may ease stiffness in OA and RA.
Splinting and assistive technology Mallet finger: continuous DIP extension splint (duration and night splinting per the local hand-therapy protocol, typically 6–8 weeks) — maintain DIP extension while allowing PIP movement; accidental DIP flexion usually means restarting the period of continuous splinting. Urgent hand assessment if open injury, volar subluxation, large bony avulsion/articular fracture or inability to maintain extension; do not use a mallet splint for a suspected boutonnière injury. Stack splint (ring splint): for lateral instability of PIP or DIP in hypermobility syndrome. Thumb spica splint: for de Quervain's or first CMC joint OA. Ring splint (silver ring or thermoplastic): for boutonnière or swan-neck deformity in RA (stabilises joint in functional position). NHSGGC splinting guide + OT referral for custom splints.
Trigger finger self-management Activity modification — avoid repetitive gripping (aggravates A1 pulley thickening). Analgesia if required. Gentle stretching of the finger into extension as far as comfortable. If the finger locks, function is significantly impaired or symptoms persist → hand surgery/local MSK service.
Raynaud's phenomenon in fingers Lifestyle: hand warming (gloves, heated mittens, hand warmers), body warmth (layering — extremity vasospasm triggered by core cooling not just cold hands), avoid vibrating tools, stop smoking. Chemical hand warmers in pockets: allow warming of hands without dampening tactile feedback. Consider a dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker for troublesome primary Raynaud's after checking blood pressure, interactions and adverse effects. Suspect secondary Raynaud's if onset is later, symptoms are severe/asymmetric, there is digital ulceration or ischaemia, abnormal nailfolds, systemic symptoms or features of connective-tissue disease — investigate and refer per the local rheumatology pathway rather than a routine autoimmune panel for everyone.
Occupational assessment for hand conditions Where work is affected (not required for every case), consider occupational health or NHS OT, e.g. workers with Dupuytren's contracture affecting manual dexterity, keyboard workers with RA, construction workers with vibration white finger (hand-arm vibration syndrome — HAVS). Industrial vibration tool exposure → HAVS: early referral to occupational physician. HAVS is an industrial disease under the Prescribed Diseases regulations (PD A11) — patients may be entitled to Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit.
Urate-lowering monitoring and adherence Allopurinol adherence is often poor. Reasons: lack of education on why it must be continued indefinitely, gout flares in first months (attributed to allopurinol), asymptomatic between attacks. Strategies: explain the "target" (serum urate <360 µmol/L, ideally <300), check serum urate monthly during titration, show the patient the reducing urate trend on the graph to motivate adherence. Consider annual serum urate once at target — if above target, review adherence and titrate dose.
The HAVS (Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome) is an occupationally prescribed disease that GPs can diagnose and facilitate compensation claims for — it affects workers using vibrating hand-held tools (pneumatic drills, grinders, chainsaws, road breakers) and causes: Raynaud's phenomenon of the fingers (vibration white finger — a specific occupational form of Raynaud's), sensorineural hand-arm vibration (SNHAV — peripheral neuropathy causing tingling, numbness, reduced grip), and musculoskeletal effects (carpal tunnel syndrome, reduced dexterity). Exposure is regulated by the Control of Vibration at Work Regulations 2005 (employers report diagnosed HAVS under RIDDOR), and it is a Prescribed Disease under the Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit scheme (PD A11 for the vascular and sensorineural components; PD A12 for carpal tunnel syndrome from vibrating tools). GPs who see construction or manufacturing workers with Raynaud's or neuropathic hand symptoms should take a detailed occupational history (years of vibration tool use, daily exposure hours) and refer to the occupational health physician for formal HAVS assessment (the employer, not the GP, makes the RIDDOR report).

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Gout monitoring
Check serum urate monthly during titration (target <360 µmol/L), then consider annual monitoring once target is achieved (NICE NG219). Monitor renal function according to comorbidity, treatment and local protocol. Prophylactic colchicine while titrating, if suitable. If gout flare on allopurinol: do NOT stop allopurinol — treat the acute attack, maintain dose.
RA DMARD monitoring
Drug-specific monitoring per the current GMMMG shared-care protocol (no generic schedule). Disease activity review with rheumatology. Blood pressure and cardiovascular risk. Vaccination per the current Green Book and local immunosuppression guidance — requirements vary with age, comorbidity and concomitant DMARD treatment. Hydroxychloroquine retinal monitoring (annual after 5 years; after 1 year if dose >5 mg/kg/day, eGFR <60 or tamoxifen; current RCOphth guidance) — the prescribing specialist ensures referral and the commissioned service performs monitoring under the shared-care protocol.
Post-injection follow-up
Review according to symptoms, complications, diabetes control and the local procedure protocol — routine review after every injection is not required. Persistent triggering or locking → hand surgery/MSK referral. After a gout flare: discuss urate-lowering therapy (usually started 2–4 weeks after the flare settles).
Same-day ED / hand surgery
Flexor tenosynovitis (Kanavel signs) · Suspected septic finger joint · Ascending lymphangitis from finger · Threatened digital ischaemia (999 if digit threatened or unwell) · Major tendon injury
Urgent / routine referral
Persistent synovitis → urgent rheumatology (NICE NG100). Persistent focal bone pain/swelling or lytic lesion → urgent imaging/sarcoma pathway. Trigger finger locked or not responding to conservative measures → routine hand/MSK service. Dupuytren's meeting referral criteria → routine hand surgery (more urgent if rapid progression or neurovascular compromise).
Safety-net advice
Seek immediate assessment for rapidly increasing pain or swelling; fever or systemic illness; inability to actively or passively move the digit; spreading redness or red streaks; new numbness, weakness, coldness, pallor or blue colour; worsening night pain or persistent bone swelling; any wound, bite or puncture followed by increasing pain/swelling. Stop allopurinol and seek urgent advice if rash, mouth ulcers or fever develop.
The 'safe splinting' principle for mallet finger must be communicated precisely to patients — failure to maintain the DIP joint in continuous extension is a common reason for treatment failure. The splint keeps the DIP extended while allowing PIP movement, and the patient should understand that accidental DIP flexion usually means restarting the period of continuous splinting; follow the local hand-therapy protocol on duration and night splinting. The correct patient instruction: 'When you need to clean under the splint, hold the finger completely straight with your other hand before removing the splint, then slide the splint off — keeping the finger extended throughout. Clean underneath it while keeping the finger straight, then slide the splint back on.' A patient who removes a mallet splint without understanding this principle risks treatment failure.
