1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Serious Underlying Pathology

Fatigue is one of the most common GP presentations. Most causes are benign — but screen for sinister pathology first.
Unexplained weight loss + fatigue is not a direct referral trigger in NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): 40+ → urgent direct-access chest X-ray (2 or more unexplained symptoms such as fatigue and weight loss, or 1 or more if ever smoked); site-specific tests including FIT; 60+ with >5% loss within 6 months → assess for clarifying features, then urgent investigation, suspected cancer pathway or non-specific symptoms pathway referral (rec 1.13.2)
Unexplained lymphadenopathy (± splenomegaly) NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for lymphoma, taking into account B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), itch, breathlessness and alcohol-induced node pain; persistent fatigue, pallor or bruising → very urgent FBC within 48 hours (leukaemia)
Dyspnoea at rest / exertion Fatigue + breathlessness → heart failure, severe anaemia, PE → same-day assessment
Chest pain + fatigue ACS, myocarditis (post-viral) → ECG + troponin → same-day or 999
Jaundice Hepatitis, liver failure, haemolysis → LFTs + urgent hepatology referral
Severe headache + fatigue Temporal arteritis (>50 yrs), raised ICP, meningitis → ESR/CRP + same-day review
Fever >2 weeks Infective endocarditis, TB, HIV, occult sepsis → blood cultures, HIV test, CXR
Suicidal ideation Fatigue + low mood + passive death wish → mental health emergency assessment
Fatigue is one of the commonest presenting complaints in general practice and carries a broad differential. In a primary-care cohort, clear somatic pathology was diagnosed within a year in about 8% of people presenting with fatigue and malignancy was uncommon (Nijrolder et al, CMAJ 2009), but the consequences of missing it are severe. B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) are not referral triggers on their own in NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — they are weighed when deciding on a suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly. Post-viral myocarditis is increasingly recognised, particularly post-COVID — dyspnoea on minimal exertion and exercise intolerance should prompt cardiac investigation. Suicidal ideation in the context of persistent fatigue and low mood must always be actively screened for.
2
Diagnose

History — Characterise the Fatigue

Structured history is the most important diagnostic tool. Fatigue has over 100 potential causes — targeted questioning narrows this efficiently.
Onset & duration
Acute (<1 month) vs chronic (>3 months). Gradual onset suggests systemic disease; acute onset with fever suggests infection
Character
Physical (exertional, muscular) vs mental (cognitive, concentration) vs both. Post-exertional malaise (PEM; NICE NG206 calls it post-exertional symptom exacerbation) — symptoms worsened by activity → ME/CFS pattern
Sleep quality
Hours, quality, unrefreshing sleep, snoring + apnoeas (partner history) → obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA); early morning waking → depression
Mood screen
PHQ-2: "Over the past 2 weeks, have you felt down, depressed, hopeless?" + "Little interest or pleasure?" Positive → PHQ-9. Fatigue is one of the most frequent symptoms of depression.
Associated symptoms
Weight change, fever, night sweats, polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes), cold intolerance (hypothyroidism), dyspnoea, palpitations, bleeding
Lifestyle factors
Work hours, shift work, caring responsibilities, screen time, caffeine/alcohol, exercise habits, social support
Medications
Beta-blockers, antihistamines, opioids, antidepressants, statins (myopathy), antihypertensives — all cause fatigue
Post-exertional malaise (PEM) — defined as worsening of all symptoms after even minor physical or mental effort — is one of the four core features of ME/CFS in NICE NG206 (2021) and fundamentally changes management (NG206: do not offer graded exercise therapy). Two verbally asked screening questions had 97% sensitivity (67% specificity) for depression in general practice (Arroll et al, BMJ 2003); depression is among the commonest identifiable causes of fatigue in primary care. OSA is significantly underdiagnosed — the Wisconsin cohort estimated OSA syndrome in about 4% of men and 2% of women aged 30–60 (Young et al, NEJM 1993, US data) — and causes daytime sleepiness and fatigue and is linked with hypertension and metabolic disease.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis — Diagnostic Framework

Use a systematic framework: TIPES — Toxic, Infective, Psychiatric/Psychological, Endocrine/metabolic, Structural/organic.
Psychiatric (most common)
Depression, anxiety, somatisation, burnout — among the commonest identifiable contributors to fatigue in primary care. Treat the underlying condition.
Endocrine/metabolic
Hypothyroidism, diabetes, Addison's disease, hypoparathyroidism, anaemia, iron deficiency without anaemia, B12/folate deficiency
Sleep disorders
OSA (snoring, daytime somnolence, witnessed apnoeas), insomnia, circadian rhythm disorder, restless legs
Infective / post-infective
Post-viral fatigue (COVID, EBV/glandular fever, CMV), chronic active infection (TB, hepatitis, HIV, Lyme disease)
ME/CFS
NICE NG206 (2021): suspect ME/CFS when all four persist for at least 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children and young people) with reduced activity levels — debilitating fatigue, post-exertional symptom exacerbation, unrefreshing sleep or sleep disturbance, and cognitive difficulties; diagnose if they persist for 3 months and are not explained by another condition. Orthostatic intolerance is a common associated symptom, not a core criterion.
Cardiorespiratory
Heart failure (NYHA class I/II may present as fatigue only), COPD, anaemia, AF, post-myocarditis
Malignancy / haematological
Lymphoma, leukaemia, solid tumour — weight loss, lymphadenopathy, night sweats are clues. Myeloma: NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — offer FBC, calcium and plasma viscosity or ESR at 60+ with persistent bone pain (particularly back pain) or unexplained fracture
Lifestyle / environmental
Poor sleep hygiene, excessive work hours, alcohol dependence (check AUDIT-C), deconditioning, nutritional deficiency
NICE NG206 (2021) fundamentally revised ME/CFS management — graded exercise therapy (GET) is no longer recommended as it worsens PEM. The new approach focuses on energy management and pacing. NG206 lets you suspect ME/CFS at 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children and young people) so that early advice can start, but the diagnosis requires the four core symptoms for 3 months — premature labelling prevents investigation of treatable causes. Iron deficiency without anaemia is a frequently missed and easily treatable cause of fatigue, particularly in women of reproductive age — in a primary-care RCT of non-anaemic menstruating women, oral iron for 12 weeks reduced fatigue more than placebo, with most benefit when ferritin was below 50 µg/L (Vaucher et al, CMAJ 2012), so a trial of iron is reasonable with low ferritin even when haemoglobin is normal.
4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

General appearance
Pallor (anaemia), jaundice, weight loss, cachexia, oedema, lymphadenopathy — systematic inspection
Vital signs
BP (postural hypotension → Addison's, autonomic dysfunction), HR, temperature, oxygen saturation at rest and on exertion
Thyroid
Goitre, lid lag, tremor (hyperthyroidism), dry skin, slow reflexes, myxoedematous facies (hypothyroidism)
Cardiovascular
S3/S4, pulmonary crackles, JVP elevation, pitting oedema → heart failure. Irregular pulse → AF.
Abdomen
Hepatosplenomegaly (haematological malignancy, hepatitis, EBV), adrenal tenderness (Addison's rare)
Lymph nodes
Cervical, axillary, inguinal — size, consistency, tenderness. Rubbery non-tender → lymphoma. Tender → reactive/infective.
Neurological
Proximal weakness (myopathy from statin, hypothyroid, Addison's), cerebellar signs (alcohol), cognitive assessment if indicated
BMI / waist
Obesity → OSA, metabolic syndrome, hypoventilation. BMI >30 increases all-cause fatigue risk significantly.
Postural hypotension (systolic fall ≥20 mmHg or diastolic fall ≥10 mmHg after standing for at least 1 minute — NICE NG136) is a key sign of Addison's disease, which can present insidiously with fatigue, weight loss, and salt craving. AF is a common underdiagnosed cause of fatigue — pulse palpation identifies this immediately. Proximal muscle weakness suggests an endocrine myopathy (hypothyroidism, Cushing's, statin-induced) rather than fatigue alone. Lymph node examination is quick and high-yield — unexplained lymphadenopathy in a fatigued patient should prompt consideration of a suspected cancer pathway referral for lymphoma, weighing night sweats and other B symptoms (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)).

Diagnose

Investigations — Tiered Approach

First-line (all patients)
FBC U&E LFTs TFTs CRP/ESR Glucose / HbA1c Ferritin B12 / folate Calcium
Second-line (directed)
EBV / CMV serology (post-viral suspect) · HIV (offer per NICE NG60 — routinely when taking bloods in high-prevalence areas, and whenever risk factors or indicator conditions are present) · Coeliac screen (IgA tTG + total IgA — NICE NG20) · Cortisol 8–9am (adrenal insufficiency screen if postural hypotension/hyperpigmentation — NICE NG243)
Urine
Urine dip + MSU — chronic UTI/pyelonephritis. Urine protein:creatinine ratio if CKD suspected
Cardiac
ECG — AF, conduction disease, LVH. BNP/NT-proBNP if heart failure suspected (dyspnoea + fatigue + oedema)
Sleep
Epworth Sleepiness Scale for preliminary assessment, and consider STOP-Bang (NICE NG202, 2021) — do not use Epworth alone to decide referral, as not everyone with OSAHS is sleepy. Suspected OSAHS → sleep service (home respiratory polygraphy). No blood test for OSA.
NOT routinely needed
MRI brain, autoimmune screen (ANA, ANCA) — only if specific clinical features suggest systemic inflammatory disease. Avoid over-investigation in low-risk patients.
A structured first-line panel detects the majority of organic causes of fatigue. A low ferritin (iron deficiency without anaemia) is common in premenopausal women and can cause fatigue that responds to oral iron. Coeliac disease affects about 1 in 100 people and can present with fatigue, often without GI symptoms — IgA tTG with total IgA is the first-choice test (NICE NG20). An 8–9am cortisol below 150 nmol/L means adrenal insufficiency is likely: start management and refer to endocrinology; 151–300 nmol/L is a grey zone (refer, short Synacthen test or repeat); above 300 nmol/L makes it very unlikely (NICE NG243, 2024; modern immunoassays). The Epworth score assesses sleepiness but is not used alone to decide OSA referral (NICE NG202).
6
Refer

Referral Criteria

NG12 · lymphoma / leukaemia
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained lymphadenopathy (Hodgkin) or unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly (non-Hodgkin), taking into account B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), itch, breathlessness and alcohol-induced node pain. Persistent fatigue, pallor, unexplained fever, bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC within 48 hours (leukaemia). Raised LDH or unexplained lymphocytosis → discuss with haematology (clinical practice).
NG12 · weight loss
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): unexplained weight loss is not a direct referral trigger — 40 and over with 2 or more unexplained symptoms such as fatigue and weight loss (1 or more if ever smoked) → urgent direct-access chest X-ray; site-specific tests (for example FIT for colorectal) depending on associated symptoms; 60 and over with >5% loss within 6 months → assess for clarifying features, then urgent investigation, suspected cancer pathway referral or non-specific symptoms pathway referral (rec 1.13.2)
Same-day
Suspected Addison's crisis (postural hypotension + confusion + vomiting), severe anaemia (Hb <70), heart failure decompensation
Endocrinology
Confirmed hypothyroidism not responding to levothyroxine, suspected adrenal insufficiency (8–9am cortisol <150 nmol/L: start management and refer; 151–300 nmol/L: refer or short Synacthen test — NICE NG243, 2024). Not for "adrenal fatigue", which is not a recognised diagnosis.
Sleep clinic
Snoring/witnessed apnoeas ± sleepiness (Epworth, consider STOP-Bang) → refer suspected OSAHS to a sleep service, which offers home respiratory polygraphy (NICE NG202, 2021). Do not use Epworth alone to decide referral.
ME/CFS service
ME/CFS criteria met (all four core symptoms — debilitating fatigue, post-exertional symptom exacerbation, unrefreshing sleep or sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulties — for ≥3 months) → NICE NG206 (2021) referral to a specialist ME/CFS team to confirm the diagnosis and plan care. Do not refer to a graded exercise therapy programme.
In NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) the lymphoma trigger is unexplained lymphadenopathy (± splenomegaly for non-Hodgkin); B symptoms are weighed in that decision rather than triggering a referral alone, and persistent fatigue triggers a very urgent FBC rather than a referral. Early CPAP initiation for OSA reduces daytime fatigue, cardiovascular risk, and accident rates — driving ability is impaired in untreated OSA (DVLA regulations apply). ME/CFS services provide specialist assessment, energy management coaching, and support — NICE NG206 (2021) says not to offer graded exercise therapy, and CBT may be offered only to support symptom management, not as a treatment or cure, given harm reported from post-exertional symptom exacerbation.
7
Treat

Condition-Specific Treatment

Hypothyroidism
Levothyroxine
NICE NG145 (2019): under 65 without CVD — consider 1.6 micrograms/kg/day (rounded to the nearest 25 micrograms); 65 and over or with CVD — 25–50 micrograms/day, titrated (dose steps per BNF). Aim to keep TSH within the reference range. Measure TSH every 3 months after a dose change until stable (2 similar in-range results 3 months apart), then yearly.
Iron deficiency (with/without anaemia)
Ferrous sulfate 200 mg once daily
Take on empty stomach. BSG 2021: one tablet daily first-line; one tablet on alternate days if not tolerated — alternate-day single doses also optimise fractional absorption (Stoffel et al, Lancet Haematol 2017). Continue for about 3 months after Hb normalises (BSG 2021). Investigate cause in men / post-menopausal women (FIT per NICE HTG690; BSG 2021 bidirectional endoscopy).
Depression-related fatigue
SSRI + psychological Rx
An SSRI such as sertraline (starting dose 50 mg once daily per BNF), chosen with the person per NICE NG222 (2022, updated December 2025). Refer to NHS Talking Therapies for CBT. Fatigue typically improves as depression responds — warn patient of 2–4 week delay to benefit.
ME/CFS
Energy management / pacing
Stay within energy envelope — avoid boom-and-bust cycles. Symptom management: LDN (low-dose naltrexone) being studied. No proven pharmacological treatment. Refer to specialist service.
B12 deficiencyHydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM — neurological symptoms: alternate days until no further improvement, then every 2 months; no neurological symptoms: 3 times a week for 2 weeks, then every 2–3 months (BNF)
DiabetesOptimise glycaemic control — fatigue improves with HbA1c reduction. If HbA1c rises to 58 mmol/mol or higher on treatment, reinforce advice and intensify towards 53 mmol/mol (NICE NG28); involve the DSN / diabetes team if control stays poor.
OSACPAP for moderate–severe OSAHS, and for mild OSAHS with symptoms affecting daily activities (NICE NG202, 2021). Adherence is usually defined as 4+ hours/night; review compliance at 3 months. Customised or semi-customised mandibular advancement splint if CPAP is declined or not tolerated (NG202).
Alternate-day single doses of iron increase fractional absorption compared with daily divided doses (Stoffel et al, Lancet Haematol 2017) and BSG 2021 suggests alternate days if daily iron is not tolerated — this can improve adherence. BSG 2021 recommends a ferrous salt (sulfate, fumarate or gluconate) first-line. CPAP improves daytime sleepiness and quality of life and modestly lowers blood pressure, but the SAVE trial did not show fewer cardiovascular events with CPAP in moderate–severe OSA (McEvoy et al, NEJM 2016). Treating iron deficiency without anaemia reduced fatigue more than placebo over 12 weeks in non-anaemic menstruating women, most clearly when ferritin was below 50 µg/L (Vaucher et al, CMAJ 2012).

Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Sleep hygiene Fixed wake time (anchor sleep), no screens 1 hour before bed, bedroom cool and dark, avoid caffeine after 2pm, no napping >20 minutes. Improves fatigue in insomnia-related cases.
Graded activity (non-ME/CFS) For deconditioning and lifestyle fatigue: build up gradually towards 150 min/week of moderate activity such as brisk walking (UK CMO physical activity guidelines 2019). Exercise reduces fatigue in randomised trials, including in cancer-related fatigue. NOT for ME/CFS (post-exertional symptom exacerbation risk).
Alcohol reduction AUDIT-C screen. Alcohol disrupts sleep architecture — even moderate drinking impairs sleep quality. Advise not regularly drinking more than 14 units/week, spread over 3 or more days (UK CMOs 2016). Brief motivational intervention effective.
Nutritional review Regular meals, adequate protein, iron-rich foods (red meat, legumes, dark leafy vegetables + vitamin C to enhance absorption), avoid extreme diets causing deficiency.
Stress and workload Occupational fatigue — explore work hours, shift patterns, caring responsibilities. Fit note if appropriate. Self-referral to NHS Talking Therapies for stress/burnout.
Caffeine management High caffeine perpetuates fatigue cycle (crash after stimulant). Gradual reduction. Switch to decaf after 2pm.
Social support Social isolation worsens fatigue — identify and address. Carer fatigue is a separate entity requiring carer's assessment and respite services.
Daylight exposure 30 minutes outdoor morning light — regulates circadian rhythm, improves mood and energy. Particularly effective in seasonal affective disorder (SAD).
A fixed morning wake time is a cornerstone of sleep advice — it anchors the circadian rhythm regardless of sleep onset. Exercise helps fatigue in many chronic conditions in randomised trials, but not in ME/CFS, where NICE NG206 (2021) advises against graded exercise therapy. The critical distinction is ME/CFS where exercise worsens outcomes through PEM — clinicians must assess for this before recommending activity. Alcohol suppresses and fragments REM sleep, especially in the second half of the night, even at moderate doses — this causes unrefreshing sleep and perpetuates daytime fatigue.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

4–6 weeks
Review investigation results. Is identified cause responding to treatment? PHQ-9 if depression screen positive. Epworth score if OSA suspected.
3 months
Reassess — if no cause found and fatigue persists, repeat bloods, extend investigation panel (HIV, coeliac, cortisol). Consider ME/CFS referral if criteria met.
6 months
Persistent unexplained fatigue → full reassessment. New symptoms may have emerged. Reconsider malignancy, systemic inflammatory disease, occult infection.
Levothyroxine
Measure TSH every 3 months after a dose change until stable (2 similar in-range results 3 months apart), then yearly (NICE NG145). Aim for TSH within the reference range; avoid over-replacement — a suppressed TSH on levothyroxine is associated with more dysrhythmias and fractures (Flynn et al, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2010).
Iron therapy
Recheck FBC + ferritin at 3 months. Find and treat cause in men and postmenopausal women: FIT-guided colorectal assessment — suspected cancer pathway referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE HTG690, 2023, formerly DG56; NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)) — and BSG 2021 bidirectional endoscopy (gastroscopy + colonoscopy) as first-line GI investigation, with coeliac serology. Pre-menopausal women — menstrual cause likely.
999 safety-net
Collapse, severe chest pain, acute breathlessness at rest, suicidal crisis
Same-day GP
New weight loss, new lymphadenopathy, worsening dyspnoea, new neurological symptoms, jaundice
Persistent unexplained fatigue at 6 months warrants a fresh review — new symptoms, weight changes, or examination findings may have emerged that point towards a diagnosis. "Unexplained" fatigue is rarely truly unexplained — it is often multi-factorial (mild anaemia + poor sleep + depression) and requires addressing all contributors. Over-replacement with levothyroxine (suppressed TSH) was associated with more dysrhythmias (adjusted HR 1.6) and fractures (HR 2.0) in a Tayside cohort (Flynn et al, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2010) — NICE NG145 advises aiming for TSH within the reference range. Men and postmenopausal women with iron deficiency anaemia need GI investigation: FIT per NICE HTG690 (2023), with a suspected cancer pathway referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g, and BSG 2021 bidirectional endoscopy — GI blood loss and malignancy must be excluded.
