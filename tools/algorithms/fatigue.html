1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Serious Underlying Pathology

Fatigue is one of the most common GP presentations. Most causes are benign — but screen for sinister pathology first.
Unexplained weight loss + fatigue is not a direct referral trigger in NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): 40+ → urgent direct-access chest X-ray (2 or more unexplained symptoms such as fatigue and weight loss, or 1 or more if ever smoked); site-specific tests including FIT; 60+ with >5% loss within 6 months → assess for clarifying features, then urgent investigation, suspected cancer pathway or non-specific symptoms pathway referral (rec 1.13.2)
Unexplained lymphadenopathy (± splenomegaly) NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for lymphoma, taking into account B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), itch, breathlessness and alcohol-induced node pain; persistent fatigue, pallor or bruising → very urgent FBC within 48 hours (leukaemia)
Dyspnoea at rest / exertion Fatigue + breathlessness → heart failure, severe anaemia, PE → same-day assessment
Chest pain + fatigue ACS, myocarditis (post-viral) → ECG + troponin → same-day or 999
Jaundice Hepatitis, liver failure, haemolysis → LFTs + urgent hepatology referral
Possible giant cell arteritis Age 50+ with new temporal or other headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication, visual symptoms, temporal artery abnormality, polymyalgia or constitutional symptoms → start treatment urgently per the local GCA pathway; don't delay for blood tests. Fatigue with headache alone is not sufficient. Meningism, raised ICP features or focal neurology → same-day/999
Persistent or recurrent fever Reassess history, examination and medication exposure; investigate per the differential (FBC, ESR/CRP, renal/liver tests, urinalysis, chest imaging, HIV test, or blood cultures if bacteraemia/endocarditis suspected). Refer/admit if unwell, septic, immunocompromised, or endocarditis, TB or other serious infection suspected
Other same-day / emergency Collapse, severe chest pain or acute breathlessness (999); symptoms of severe anaemia, suspected Addisonian crisis, decompensated heart failure, suspected sepsis/endocarditis, new focal neurological symptoms, significant unexplained weight loss with systemic deterioration → same-day assessment
Suicidal ideation Fatigue + low mood + passive death wish → mental health emergency assessment
Fatigue is one of the commonest presenting complaints in general practice and carries a broad differential. In a primary-care cohort, clear somatic pathology was diagnosed within a year in about 8% of people presenting with fatigue and malignancy was uncommon (Nijrolder et al, CMAJ 2009), but the consequences of missing it are severe. B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) are not referral triggers on their own in NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — they are weighed when deciding on a suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly. Post-viral myocarditis is increasingly recognised, particularly post-COVID — dyspnoea on minimal exertion and exercise intolerance should prompt cardiac investigation. Suicidal ideation in the context of persistent fatigue and low mood must always be actively screened for.
2
Diagnose

History — Characterise the Fatigue

Structured history is the most important diagnostic tool. Fatigue has a broad differential — targeted questioning narrows it efficiently.
Onset & duration
Acute (<1 month) vs chronic (>3 months). Gradual onset suggests systemic disease; acute onset with fever suggests infection
Character
Physical (exertional, muscular) vs mental (cognitive, concentration) vs both. Post-exertional malaise (PEM; NICE NG206: post-exertional symptom exacerbation) — worsening disproportionate to the activity, often delayed by hours or days, with prolonged recovery → ME/CFS pattern. Fatigue alone does not suggest ME/CFS
Sleep quality
Hours, quality, unrefreshing sleep, snoring + apnoeas (partner history) → obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA); early morning waking → depression; restless legs symptoms
Mood screen
PHQ-2: "Over the past 2 weeks, have you felt down, depressed, hopeless?" + "Little interest or pleasure?" Positive → fuller assessment (PHQ-9 or clinical), including suicidal ideation and self-harm risk, anxiety, bipolarity, substance use, psychosocial factors, severity and functional impact. Fatigue is one of the most frequent symptoms of depression.
Associated symptoms
Weight change, fever, night sweats, polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes), cold intolerance (hypothyroidism), dyspnoea, palpitations, bleeding
Lifestyle factors
Work hours, shift work, occupational exposures, caring responsibilities, screen time, caffeine, alcohol and recreational drug use, diet and eating-disorder screen, exercise habits, social support; domestic abuse and social stress where clinically relevant
Medications
Beta-blockers, antihistamines, opioids, antidepressants, statins (myopathy), antihypertensives — all can cause fatigue; check adherence and recent changes
Menstrual / pregnancy
Menstrual history (heavy bleeding), possible pregnancy, postpartum, perimenopause — where relevant
Post-exertional malaise (PEM) — defined as worsening of all symptoms after even minor physical or mental effort — is one of the four core features of ME/CFS in NICE NG206 (2021) and fundamentally changes management (NG206: do not offer graded exercise therapy). Two verbally asked screening questions had 97% sensitivity (67% specificity) for depression in general practice (Arroll et al, BMJ 2003); depression is among the commonest identifiable causes of fatigue in primary care. OSA is significantly underdiagnosed — the Wisconsin cohort estimated OSA syndrome in about 4% of men and 2% of women aged 30–60 (Young et al, NEJM 1993, US data) — and causes daytime sleepiness and fatigue and is linked with hypertension and metabolic disease.
3
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis — Diagnostic Framework

Use a systematic framework: TIPES — Toxic, Infective, Psychiatric/Psychological, Endocrine/metabolic, Structural/organic.
Psychiatric (most common)
Depression, anxiety, somatisation, burnout — among the commonest identifiable contributors to fatigue in primary care. Treat the underlying condition.
Endocrine/metabolic
Hypothyroidism, diabetes, Addison's disease, hypoparathyroidism, anaemia, iron deficiency without anaemia (treat according to ferritin, clinical context and cause), B12/folate deficiency
Sleep disorders
OSA (snoring, daytime somnolence, witnessed apnoeas), insomnia, circadian rhythm disorder, restless legs
Infective / post-infective
Post-viral fatigue (COVID, EBV/glandular fever, CMV — do not routinely test EBV/CMV for fatigue alone), chronic active infection (TB, hepatitis, HIV, Lyme disease)
ME/CFS
NICE NG206 (2021): suspect ME/CFS when all four persist for at least 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children and young people) with reduced activity levels — debilitating fatigue, post-exertional symptom exacerbation, unrefreshing sleep or sleep disturbance, and cognitive difficulties; diagnose if they persist for 3 months and are not explained by another condition. Suspect only when all four core symptoms are present, activity is substantially reduced and another condition does not better explain the presentation; consider alternative diagnoses first. Orthostatic intolerance is a common associated symptom, not a core criterion.
Cardiorespiratory
Heart failure (NYHA class I/II may present as fatigue only), COPD, anaemia, AF, post-myocarditis
Malignancy / haematological
Lymphoma, leukaemia, solid tumour — weight loss, lymphadenopathy, night sweats are clues. Myeloma: NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — offer FBC, calcium and plasma viscosity or ESR at 60+ with persistent bone pain (particularly back pain) or unexplained fracture
Lifestyle / environmental
Poor sleep hygiene, excessive work hours, alcohol dependence (check AUDIT-C), deconditioning, nutritional deficiency
NICE NG206 (2021) fundamentally revised ME/CFS management — graded exercise therapy (GET) is no longer recommended as it worsens PEM. The new approach focuses on energy management and pacing. NG206 lets you suspect ME/CFS at 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children and young people) so that early advice can start, but the diagnosis requires the four core symptoms for 3 months — premature labelling prevents investigation of treatable causes. Iron deficiency without anaemia is a frequently missed and easily treatable cause of fatigue, particularly in women of reproductive age — in a primary-care RCT of non-anaemic menstruating women, oral iron for 12 weeks reduced fatigue more than placebo, with most benefit when ferritin was below 50 µg/L (Vaucher et al, CMAJ 2012), so a trial of iron is reasonable with low ferritin even when haemoglobin is normal — interpreting ferritin in the light of inflammation and investigating the cause.
4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

General appearance
Pallor (anaemia), jaundice, weight loss, cachexia, oedema, lymphadenopathy — systematic inspection
Vital signs
BP (postural hypotension → Addison's, autonomic dysfunction), HR, temperature, oxygen saturation at rest and on exertion
Thyroid
Goitre, lid lag, tremor (hyperthyroidism), dry skin, slow reflexes, myxoedematous facies (hypothyroidism)
Cardiovascular
Pulse rhythm (irregular → AF). If breathlessness, orthopnoea or oedema: JVP, heart sounds, crackles, pitting oedema → heart failure.
Abdomen
Hepatosplenomegaly (haematological malignancy, liver disease, infection)
Lymph nodes
Cervical, axillary, inguinal — size, consistency, tenderness. Assess size, site, persistence, fixation and systemic symptoms — consistency or tenderness alone does not distinguish lymphoma from reactive nodes.
Neurological
Proximal weakness (myopathy from statin, hypothyroid, Addison's), cerebellar signs (alcohol), cognitive assessment if indicated
Weight / BMI
Weight (and growth where relevant). Obesity → OSA, metabolic syndrome; dyspnoea, hypoxia or suspected obesity hypoventilation → appropriate respiratory assessment.
Mood, cognition & function
Mental state, cognition and functional impact on work, caring and daily activities
Postural hypotension (systolic fall ≥20 mmHg or diastolic fall ≥10 mmHg after standing for at least 1 minute — NICE NG136) is a key sign of Addison's disease, which can present insidiously with fatigue, weight loss, and salt craving. AF is a common underdiagnosed cause of fatigue — pulse palpation identifies this immediately. Proximal muscle weakness suggests an endocrine myopathy (hypothyroidism, Cushing's, statin-induced) rather than fatigue alone. Lymph node examination is quick and high-yield — unexplained lymphadenopathy in a fatigued patient should prompt consideration of a suspected cancer pathway referral for lymphoma, weighing night sweats and other B symptoms (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)).

Diagnose

Investigations — Tiered Approach

First-line (targeted)
Guided by history and examination — commonly FBC U&E / eGFR LFTs TFTs Glucose / HbA1c Ferritin CRP/ESR (when inflammatory or infective disease is plausible). Not a universal panel — broad testing generates incidental findings (NICE NG206).
Second-line (directed)
Only where the history, examination or initial results support them: B12 / folate · Calcium · CK · HIV (offer per NICE NG60 — routinely when taking bloods in high-prevalence areas, and whenever risk factors or indicator conditions are present) · Coeliac screen (IgA tTG + total IgA — NICE NG20) · Cortisol 8–9am (adrenal insufficiency screen if postural hypotension/hyperpigmentation — NICE NG243)
Urine
Urinalysis if clinically indicated (urinary symptoms, fever, suspected diabetes or CKD). Urine protein:creatinine ratio if CKD suspected
Cardiac
ECG if palpitations, breathlessness, chest pain or an abnormal pulse — AF, conduction disease, LVH. BNP/NT-proBNP if heart failure suspected (dyspnoea + fatigue + oedema)
Sleep
Suspect OSAHS with 2 or more of: snoring, witnessed apnoeas, unrefreshing sleep, waking headache, excessive sleepiness/tiredness/fatigue, nocturia, choking during sleep, sleep fragmentation or insomnia, cognitive dysfunction or memory impairment (NICE NG202, 2021). Epworth (and STOP-Bang, an adult tool) may support assessment, but do not use Epworth alone, as not everyone with OSAHS is sleepy. Refer to a sleep service for home respiratory polygraphy; home oximetry only if polygraphy is unavailable — a negative oximetry does not exclude OSA. No blood test for OSA.
NOT routinely needed
EBV/CMV serology, ANA/ANCA, cortisol, MRI brain, blood cultures or CT imaging — only if the clinical presentation indicates them. Avoid over-investigation in low-risk patients.
Targeted first-line tests, chosen from the history and examination, detect common treatable organic causes while limiting incidental findings. A low ferritin (iron deficiency without anaemia) is common in premenopausal women and can cause fatigue that responds to oral iron. Coeliac disease affects about 1 in 100 people and can present with fatigue, often without GI symptoms — IgA tTG with total IgA is the first-choice test (NICE NG20). When adrenal insufficiency is clinically suspected, an 8–9am cortisol below 150 nmol/L means adrenal insufficiency is likely: start management and refer to endocrinology; 151–300 nmol/L is a grey zone (refer, short Synacthen test or repeat); above 300 nmol/L makes it very unlikely (NICE NG243, 2024; modern immunoassays). The Epworth score assesses sleepiness but is not used alone to decide OSA referral (NICE NG202).
6
Refer

Referral Criteria

NG12 · lymphoma / leukaemia
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained lymphadenopathy (Hodgkin) or unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly (non-Hodgkin), taking into account B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), itch, breathlessness and alcohol-induced node pain. Persistent fatigue, pallor, unexplained fever, bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC within 48 hours (leukaemia). Raised LDH or unexplained lymphocytosis → discuss with haematology (clinical practice). Pallor, bruising, bleeding, fever or persistent infection → follow the relevant haematological pathway rather than a generic fatigue panel.
NG12 · weight loss
Fatigue alone is not usually a suspected-cancer referral trigger — apply the site-specific NG12 criteria, taking account of age, smoking history and associated symptoms. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): unexplained weight loss is not a direct referral trigger — 40 and over with 2 or more unexplained symptoms such as fatigue and weight loss (1 or more if ever smoked) → urgent direct-access chest X-ray; site-specific tests (for example FIT for colorectal) depending on associated symptoms; 60 and over with >5% loss within 6 months → assess for clarifying features, then urgent investigation, suspected cancer pathway referral or non-specific symptoms pathway referral (rec 1.13.2)
Same-day
Suspected Addisonian crisis (postural hypotension, confusion, vomiting), symptoms of severe anaemia, decompensated heart failure, suspected sepsis/endocarditis, new focal neurological symptoms, significant unexplained weight loss with systemic deterioration, suicidal crisis. 999 for collapse, severe chest pain or acute breathlessness.
Endocrinology
Confirmed hypothyroidism not responding to levothyroxine, clinically suspected adrenal insufficiency (interpret cortisol with the clinical picture; 8–9am cortisol <150 nmol/L: start management and refer; 151–300 nmol/L: refer or short Synacthen test — NICE NG243, 2024). Not for "adrenal fatigue", which is not a recognised diagnosis.
Sleep clinic
Two or more OSAHS features (NICE NG202) → refer suspected OSAHS to a sleep service, which offers home respiratory polygraphy and starts any CPAP (NICE NG202, 2021). Do not use Epworth alone to decide referral; STOP-Bang is an adult tool. Dyspnoea, hypoxia or suspected obesity hypoventilation → appropriate respiratory assessment.
ME/CFS service
ME/CFS criteria met (all four core symptoms — debilitating fatigue, post-exertional symptom exacerbation, unrefreshing sleep or sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulties — for ≥3 months) and alternative diagnoses appropriately considered → NICE NG206 (2021) referral to a specialist ME/CFS team, where available (access and local arrangements vary), to confirm the diagnosis and plan care. Do not refer to a graded exercise therapy programme.
In NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) the lymphoma trigger is unexplained lymphadenopathy (± splenomegaly for non-Hodgkin); B symptoms are weighed in that decision rather than triggering a referral alone, and persistent fatigue triggers a very urgent FBC rather than a referral. CPAP is started by the sleep service after diagnosis and improves daytime sleepiness; driving ability is impaired in untreated OSA (DVLA regulations apply). ME/CFS services provide specialist assessment, energy management coaching, and support — NICE NG206 (2021) says not to offer graded exercise therapy, and CBT may be offered only to support symptom management, not as a treatment or cure, given harm reported from post-exertional symptom exacerbation.

Treat

Condition-Specific Treatment

Hypothyroidism
Levothyroxine
Treat confirmed hypothyroidism according to NICE NG145 (2019) and local endocrinology/formulary guidance — dosing depends on age, cardiovascular disease, frailty, pregnancy and severity. Do not attribute fatigue to hypothyroidism without biochemical confirmation. Monitor TSH after dose changes per NICE/local guidance.
Iron deficiency (with/without anaemia)
Oral iron per local formulary + find the cause
Confirm deficiency (ferritin is affected by inflammation) and investigate the cause, not just replace iron. Oral ferrous salt per BSG 2021 and the current local formulary (alternate-day dosing if daily is not tolerated); continue for about 3 months after Hb normalises. Men and postmenopausal women: follow the local suspected GI cancer / iron-deficiency pathway — FIT (NICE HTG690) does not replace clinical assessment.
Depression-related fatigue
Full assessment, then NICE-guided treatment
A positive PHQ-2 needs fuller assessment (PHQ-9 or clinical), including suicidal ideation and self-harm risk, anxiety, bipolarity, substance use, psychosocial factors, severity and function. Choose treatment per NICE NG222 (2022, updated December 2025) with shared decision-making — psychological therapy (NHS Talking Therapies) and/or medication.
ME/CFS
Energy management / pacing
Energy management within the person's current limits — avoid boom-and-bust cycles. Do not offer graded exercise therapy or fixed incremental exercise programmes (NICE NG206). No proven pharmacological treatment; manage symptoms. Refer to a specialist ME/CFS service where available.
B12 deficiencyHydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM — neurological symptoms: alternate days until no further improvement, then every 2 months; no neurological symptoms: 3 times a week for 2 weeks, then every 2–3 months (check current BNF/local formulary)
DiabetesOptimise diabetes according to the individualised HbA1c target and current NICE diabetes guidance; do not use a single universal HbA1c threshold in a fatigue pathway. Fatigue may reflect hyperglycaemia, hypoglycaemia, complications or another cause; involve the DSN / diabetes team if control stays poor.
OSADiagnosis and treatment are sleep-service-led: CPAP for moderate–severe OSAHS, or mild OSAHS with symptoms affecting daily activities, or a customised/semi-customised mandibular advancement splint if CPAP is declined or not tolerated (NICE NG202, 2021). Adherence is interpreted with symptoms and sleep-service (and DVLA) requirements rather than a single hours rule.
Alternate-day single doses of iron increase fractional absorption compared with daily divided doses (Stoffel et al, Lancet Haematol 2017) and BSG 2021 suggests alternate days if daily iron is not tolerated — this can improve adherence. BSG 2021 recommends a ferrous salt (sulfate, fumarate or gluconate) first-line. CPAP improves daytime sleepiness and quality of life and modestly lowers blood pressure, but the SAVE trial did not show fewer cardiovascular events with CPAP in moderate–severe OSA (McEvoy et al, NEJM 2016). Treating iron deficiency without anaemia reduced fatigue more than placebo over 12 weeks in non-anaemic menstruating women, most clearly when ferritin was below 50 µg/L (Vaucher et al, CMAJ 2012).

Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Sleep hygiene Fixed wake time (anchor sleep), limit screens before bed, bedroom cool and dark, limit caffeine later in the day, keep any naps short. Improves fatigue in insomnia-related cases.
Graded activity (non-ME/CFS) For deconditioning and lifestyle fatigue: build up gradually towards 150 min/week of moderate activity such as brisk walking (UK CMO physical activity guidelines 2019). NOT for ME/CFS (post-exertional symptom exacerbation risk).
Alcohol reduction AUDIT-C screen. Alcohol disrupts sleep architecture — even moderate drinking impairs sleep quality. Advise not regularly drinking more than 14 units/week, spread over 3 or more days (UK CMOs 2016). Brief motivational intervention effective.
Nutritional review Regular meals, adequate protein, iron-rich foods (red meat, legumes, dark leafy vegetables + vitamin C to enhance absorption), avoid extreme diets causing deficiency.
Stress and workload Occupational fatigue — explore work hours, shift patterns, caring responsibilities. Fit note if appropriate. Self-referral to NHS Talking Therapies for stress/burnout.
Caffeine management High intake can disrupt sleep — consider gradual reduction, particularly later in the day.
Social support Social isolation worsens fatigue — identify and address. Carer fatigue is a separate entity requiring carer's assessment and respite services.
Daylight exposure Regular outdoor daylight, ideally in the morning, may help circadian rhythm and mood — particularly relevant in seasonal affective disorder (SAD).
A fixed morning wake time is a cornerstone of sleep advice — it anchors the circadian rhythm regardless of sleep onset. Graded activity can help deconditioning-related fatigue, but not ME/CFS, where NICE NG206 (2021) advises against graded exercise therapy. The critical distinction is ME/CFS where exercise worsens outcomes through PEM — clinicians must assess for this before recommending activity. Alcohol suppresses and fragments REM sleep, especially in the second half of the night, even at moderate doses — this causes unrefreshing sleep and perpetuates daytime fatigue.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

4–6 weeks
Review investigation results. Is identified cause responding to treatment? PHQ-9 if depression screen positive. Epworth score if OSA suspected.
3 months
If fatigue persists, review the original history, examination and results. Reassess for new symptoms, medication effects, sleep disorders, mood disorder, ME/CFS and evolving systemic disease. Repeat or extend investigations only where clinically indicated. Consider ME/CFS referral if criteria are met.
6 months
Persistent unexplained fatigue → full reassessment. New symptoms may have emerged. Reconsider malignancy, systemic inflammatory disease, occult infection.
Levothyroxine
Monitor TSH after dose changes per NICE NG145/local guidance. Aim for TSH within the reference range; avoid over-replacement — a suppressed TSH on levothyroxine is associated with more dysrhythmias and fractures (Flynn et al, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2010).
Iron therapy
Recheck FBC + ferritin at 3 months. Find and treat cause in men and postmenopausal women: FIT-guided colorectal assessment — suspected cancer pathway referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE HTG690, 2023, formerly DG56; NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)) — and the local suspected GI cancer / iron-deficiency pathway (BSG 2021 bidirectional endoscopy where indicated), with coeliac serology. FIT does not replace clinical assessment. Pre-menopausal women — menstrual cause likely.
999 safety-net
Collapse, severe chest pain, acute breathlessness at rest, suicidal crisis
Same-day GP
New weight loss, new lymphadenopathy, worsening dyspnoea, new neurological symptoms, jaundice, fever, night sweats, bleeding
Return / review if
Fatigue worsens or becomes functionally limiting; weight loss, fever, night sweats, bleeding, lymphadenopathy or breathlessness develop; new neurological symptoms; sleepiness creates driving or occupational risk; symptoms persist despite the agreed plan
Persistent unexplained fatigue at 6 months warrants a fresh review — new symptoms, weight changes, or examination findings may have emerged that point towards a diagnosis. "Unexplained" fatigue is rarely truly unexplained — it is often multi-factorial (mild anaemia + poor sleep + depression) and requires addressing all contributors. Over-replacement with levothyroxine (suppressed TSH) was associated with more dysrhythmias (adjusted HR 1.6) and fractures (HR 2.0) in a Tayside cohort (Flynn et al, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2010) — NICE NG145 advises aiming for TSH within the reference range. Men and postmenopausal women with iron deficiency anaemia need GI investigation: FIT per NICE HTG690 (2023), with a suspected cancer pathway referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g, and BSG 2021 bidirectional endoscopy via the local suspected GI cancer / iron-deficiency pathway — GI blood loss and malignancy must be excluded.
Educational use only. Based on NICE NG206 (ME/CFS, 2021), NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) (suspected cancer), NICE HTG690 (FIT, 2023, formerly DG56), BSG iron deficiency anaemia guideline (Snook et al, Gut 2021), NICE NG145 (thyroid disease, 2019), NICE NG202 (OSAHS, 2021), NICE NG243 (adrenal insufficiency, 2024), NICE NG20 (coeliac disease, 2015), NICE NG136 (hypertension, 2019), NICE NG28 (type 2 diabetes), NICE NG222 (depression, 2022, updated December 2025), NICE NG60 (HIV testing) and the BNF. Always adapt to individual patient context.
