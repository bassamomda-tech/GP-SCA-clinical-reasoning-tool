1
Safety

Red Flags — the soft-tissue and bone sarcoma pathway

Soft-tissue sarcoma — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Adult with an unexplained lump that is increasing in size → consider an urgent direct-access ultrasound (within 2 weeks). If the scan suggests sarcoma, or is uncertain and clinical concern persists → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral.
Soft-tissue sarcoma — children and young people (NG12) Unexplained lump increasing in size → consider a very urgent direct-access ultrasound (within 48 hours); suggestive or uncertain scan with ongoing concern → very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours), discussed/referred through paediatric services. Do not treat "urgent ultrasound" and "suspected cancer referral" as the same action.
Suspicious features (UK sarcoma guideline) The UK soft tissue sarcoma guideline (Hayes et al, British Journal of Cancer 2024) lists size over 5 cm, deep to the deep fascia, increasing size and pain as features that raise the chance of malignancy. A lump that recurs after previous excision is treated with the same suspicion in practice. Fixation, rapid or progressive enlargement or an indeterminate ultrasound also increase concern. Any one concerning feature should prompt imaging or specialist discussion — do not wait for a lump to reach 5 cm. Do not reassure simply because a lump is painless, smaller than 5 cm, apparently mobile or previously labelled a "lipoma" — a sarcoma may be painless and relatively small at presentation.
Bone sarcoma — NICE NG12 Child or young person with unexplained bone swelling or pain → consider a very urgent X-ray (within 48 hours). Adult whose X-ray suggests possible bone sarcoma → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral.
Suspicious lymph node — NICE NG12 Not every node needs a cancer referral — assess site, size, duration, progression, consistency, fixation/matting, tenderness, local infection, generalised nodes, fever, night sweats, weight loss, itch, hepatosplenomegaly, immunosuppression and HIV risk. Adult with persistent, enlarging, hard, fixed, supraclavicular or generalised nodes, or nodes with systemic features → urgent investigation and consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Children have different thresholds — cervical lymphadenopathy is common and often reactive.
Breast or axillary lump — NICE NG12 Aged 30 or over with an unexplained breast lump (with or without pain) → suspected cancer pathway referral; aged 30 or over with an unexplained axillary lump → consider suspected cancer referral for breast cancer. Associated lymphadenopathy may need the lymphoma pathway instead, depending on the clinical picture. Do not manage a breast or axillary lump as a generic subcutaneous lump.
Neck, thyroid and testis — NICE NG12 Adult aged 45 or over with an unexplained neck lump that is persistent or changing → suspected cancer pathway referral via the relevant head-and-neck pathway (consider infection, thyroid disease, salivary pathology and lymphoma as appropriate; not every neck lump at every age follows this route). Unexplained thyroid lump → assessment through the thyroid pathway (not a generic sarcoma presentation). Non-painful enlargement or change in shape/texture of a testis → suspected cancer referral; unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms → urgent direct-access ultrasound. A clearly extra-testicular, transilluminating scrotal lump with a secure diagnosis may follow a different pathway, but uncertainty should prompt urgent ultrasound.
Do NOT excise Never "shell out" or locally excise a possible sarcoma — unplanned excision can compromise definitive surgery and contaminate tissue planes. A suspected sarcoma should be imaged and referred to the appropriate diagnostic/sarcoma service before biopsy or excision; biopsy planning is undertaken by the specialist team so the biopsy tract can be removed at definitive surgery. No incisional biopsy or "diagnostic excision" in primary care when sarcoma is possible.
Abscess / infection Hot, tender, fluctuant, spreading erythema ± systemic upset → incision and drainage for an appropriate fluctuant abscess; same-day assessment if spreading cellulitis, signs of sepsis, immunocompromise or anatomically high-risk site. Do not attempt office drainage where there is concern about a deep abscess, vascular lesion, hand/facial involvement, joint involvement or systemic infection.
Pulsatile / vascular A pulsatile or expansile mass may represent an aneurysm, pseudoaneurysm or vascular malformation — particularly groin lumps after arterial access, injection drug use, trauma or surgery. Do not aspirate or biopsy. Same-day assessment if painful, expanding, bleeding, distal ischaemia, neurological compromise or haemodynamic instability; otherwise seek urgent vascular/radiology advice and arrange appropriate Doppler imaging.
The whole point of the lumps pathway is to separate the rare soft-tissue sarcoma from the very common benign lump, because the cost of missing it — or of excising it unplanned — is high. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) keys its adult recommendation on one feature: an unexplained lump that is increasing in size earns an urgent direct-access ultrasound, and a suggestive or uncertain scan with persisting concern earns a suspected cancer pathway referral. Children and young people get the same logic on a 48-hour clock. The size, depth and pain criteria come from the UK sarcoma specialists' guideline (Hayes et al, 2024) rather than from NG12, and they are best used as extra reasons to image and to lower your threshold, not as a checklist that must be complete. The cardinal error is calling such a lump a lipoma and shelling it out, which contaminates tissue planes and compromises the definitive operation. A tumour may be smaller than 5 cm and still be malignant, so do not wait for a threshold. Lumps in the breast, axilla, neck and testis have their own NG12 routes, thyroid lumps follow the thyroid pathway, and persistent, enlarging or otherwise suspicious nodes follow the lymphoma recommendations in clinical context.
2
Diagnose

History — growth, pain and the timeline

Duration and growth
How long present and changing how fast? A lump unchanged for years is reassuring; recent or steady growth is the key red flag. Ask for old photographs or a previous measurement in the notes.
Pain
Painless does not equal benign (many sarcomas are painless), but new pain in a soft-tissue mass is a red flag. Tenderness that fluctuates with inflammation suggests a cyst or abscess.
Previous lump or excision
Recurrence at a site of prior "lipoma" excision raises sarcoma — was the original ever sent for histology, and what did it show?
Systemic features
Weight loss, night sweats, fever, itch (lymphoma); known cancer (metastasis, e.g. breast, lung, renal, melanoma); immunosuppression.
Trauma / infection
Recent trauma (haematoma, fat necrosis), bites or scratches (regional node), injecting sites (abscess, pseudoaneurysm), or signs of local infection.
Function
Tingling or weakness distal to the lump (nerve sheath tumour, ganglion compressing a nerve); restricted joint movement; interference with clothing or daily activities.
Family / syndromic
Neurofibromatosis type 1 (multiple neurofibromas; risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour), familial lipomatosis, Li–Fraumeni, previous radiotherapy to the area (radiation-associated sarcoma years later).
Patient's agenda
What are they worried it is? Cancer fear, cosmetic concern and discomfort need different conversations and different outcomes.
History triages the lump along a single axis: how it is behaving over time. A soft, mobile lump that has been unchanged for years is almost always benign, whereas any lump that is new, growing, or has recurred after a previous "lipoma" excision earns imaging — growth is also the exact trigger NG12 uses. Pain is a useful but asymmetric clue: its presence is a red flag, but its absence is not reassuring. Systemic symptoms point towards lymphoma or metastasis, and a relevant background (neurofibromatosis type 1, prior radiotherapy to the field) raises the pre-test probability of malignancy. In a population study from north-west England, the lifetime risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour in NF1 was estimated at 8–13% (Evans et al, J Med Genet 2002), which is why a neurofibroma that changes behaviour deserves urgent attention.
3
Diagnose

Examination — the five S characteristics

Site
Limb (the thigh is a common sarcoma site), trunk, head and neck, breast, axilla, groin, testis. Note relation to joints, tendons and neurovascular bundles.
Size
Measure with a ruler or tape and document exact dimensions in cm, in three planes where possible — 5 cm is a warning size in the UK sarcoma guideline (not a threshold to wait for) and your measurement becomes the growth baseline.
Shape and surface
Well-defined and lobulated (lipoma) vs irregular, ill-defined or fixed (concerning).
Skin
Punctum and central dimple (epidermoid cyst); skin tethering, ulceration, colour change or a nodule within the skin (skin cancer, including nodular melanoma).
Consistency and depth
Soft, fluctuant, slip sign (suggests lipoma but not specific enough to exclude sarcoma); firm or hard, fixed, deep to fascia (concerning). Mobility relative to skin and to deep tissues; relation to fascia and muscle — check whether a presumed "lipoma" is actually subfascial or fixed. Tense contraction of the underlying muscle: a superficial lump stays mobile, a deep one becomes fixed or disappears. Transillumination is not reliable enough to diagnose a cyst or ganglion (a solid lesion may transmit light and a cyst may not).
Other
Pulsatility (vascular), compressibility or cough impulse (hernia), Tinel's sign over the lump (may support a nerve-related lesion but is not diagnostic of a nerve-sheath tumour), regional lymph nodes, distal neurovascular status, and café-au-lait macules or axillary freckling (NF1).
A structured examination of the lump — site, size, shape, surface and consistency, plus depth — captures the features that drive the decision. Benign lumps tend to be superficial, soft or fluctuant, mobile and well-defined: a lipoma slips under the examining finger and a cyst may have a punctum — but slip sign and transillumination are not reliable enough to exclude sarcoma or confirm a cyst. The features that move a lump onto the imaging pathway are growth, size over 5 cm, a deep (sub-fascial) position, fixation and a hard irregular consistency. Asking the patient to tense the underlying muscle is a quick bedside test of depth. Documenting size in centimetres creates the baseline against which growth — the NG12 trigger — is judged at review. Always examine the regional nodes: a node draining the lump changes the differential.

Diagnose

Differential diagnosis — and the feature that separates each

Adipose
Lipoma — a small, superficial, stable, soft, mobile lesion is often a lipoma; rapid growth, pain, deep location, fixation, recurrence, size around or above 5 cm or diagnostic uncertainty should prompt imaging rather than routine excision. Angiolipoma — small, often multiple and tender, forearms and trunk.
Cystic
Epidermoid cyst (punctum, cheesy contents, may become inflamed); pilar cyst (scalp, smooth, no punctum, often familial); ganglion (near joints and tendon sheaths, firm, dorsal wrist commonest); Baker's cyst (popliteal fossa, may rupture and mimic DVT — a new swollen painful calf should be assessed through the DVT pathway, not simply labelled a ruptured cyst).
Lymph node
Reactive (tender, mobile, recent infection, shrinks over weeks) vs lymphoma or metastasis (hard or rubbery, fixed or matted, enlarging, systemic symptoms).
Vascular / neural
Haemangioma or vascular malformation (compressible, bluish); neurofibroma or schwannoma (along a nerve, mobile side-to-side not along the nerve; Tinel's may be positive but is not diagnostic); aneurysm (pulsatile, expansile).
Inflammatory
Abscess (hot, fluctuant), rheumatoid nodule (extensor surfaces), gouty tophus (ears, joints, chalky), fat necrosis or haematoma after trauma, injection-site granuloma.
Hernia
Groin, umbilical and incisional lumps with a cough impulse — reducible or not; an irreducible tender hernia is a surgical emergency.
Malignant
Do not miss Soft-tissue sarcoma (often a painless, enlarging deep mass), metastasis, lymphoma, breast cancer, or a skin cancer with a subcutaneous component (nodular melanoma, squamous cell carcinoma, Merkel cell carcinoma).
The differential is dominated by a few benign diagnoses — lipoma, epidermoid cyst, ganglion and reactive lymph node — each with a recognisable bedside signature. Holding the malignant possibilities alongside them (sarcoma, lymphoma, metastasis, skin cancer) is what keeps the pathway safe, because a deep, growing "lipoma" is exactly how a sarcoma presents. Two NG12 skin recommendations matter here: consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for a skin lesion that raises the suspicion of squamous cell carcinoma, and for a pigmented or non-pigmented lesion that suggests nodular melanoma. Trauma-related lumps (haematoma, fat necrosis) and infective ones should declare themselves on history and resolve with time; persistence or atypical behaviour still prompts imaging.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — image before you excise

Ultrasound
First-line when the diagnosis is clinically uncertain, or the lump is enlarging, deep, fixed or painful, of uncertain cystic/solid nature, recurrent after excision, or its vascularity or relation to a tendon/nerve needs assessment. A classic, small, superficial, stable epidermoid cyst or lipoma with a secure clinical diagnosis may not need urgent imaging — if in doubt, imaging is safer than excision. NG12: urgent direct access (within 2 weeks) in adults with an unexplained lump increasing in size; within 48 hours in children and young people. The request should include exact site, duration and growth, size, depth and mobility, pain, history of previous excision, and "?sarcoma" where clinically appropriate.
MRI
Indeterminate, deep, large or suspicious lesions: specialist imaging pathway, often MRI — usually arranged by the sarcoma diagnostic service, before any planned biopsy. Ultrasound suspicious or indeterminate with persistent clinical concern → suspected cancer referral.
X-ray
For a bony mass or persistent focal bone pain/swelling (NG12 bone sarcoma route), and to look for calcification or bone involvement beneath a soft-tissue mass. Adult X-ray suggesting bone sarcoma → suspected cancer pathway; child/young person with unexplained bone swelling or pain → very urgent direct-access X-ray and specialist assessment. A normal X-ray does not exclude a bone tumour when pain or swelling persists.
Bloods
Target to the presentation — do not routinely order LDH, urate, HIV or blood film for every subcutaneous lump. Lymphadenopathy/systemic symptoms: FBC, blood film, CRP/ESR, renal and liver function; additional tests guided by clinical context. Possible lymphoma: a normal FBC or LDH does not exclude it. Recurrent abscesses: HbA1c; consider immunodeficiency assessment if infections are severe, unusual or recurrent. Gouty tophus: serum urate, interpreted in context. HIV test where clinically indicated, particularly unexplained generalised lymphadenopathy, recurrent infections or relevant sexual/occupational risk.
Biopsy
Image-guided core needle biopsy is planned by the sarcoma service so the track can be removed at definitive surgery — not an incisional biopsy in primary care.
Do NOT
Excise, shell out or incisionally biopsy a possible sarcoma locally; do not aspirate or biopsy a pulsatile mass; do not aspirate a lesion that is not confidently a ganglion; do not attribute a growing lump to a "fatty lump" without imaging.
Imaging, not surgery, is the first investigation for any lump that is not clearly a simple benign lesion. Ultrasound is the accessible first-line test that distinguishes cystic from solid, gauges size and depth, and flags lesions that need the sarcoma service — and NG12 gives GPs direct access to it for exactly this purpose. The UK sarcoma guideline (Hayes et al, 2024) advises that a tissue diagnosis should, where possible, be made before treatment by percutaneous core biopsy reviewed by a specialist sarcoma pathologist. An unplanned local procedure contaminates the tissue planes the definitive operation must clear, so biopsy and excision of a possible sarcoma belong to the specialist team. A clear request (size, depth, growth, "?sarcoma") helps the radiologist triage and apply the right protocol.

Refer

Referral criteria

Sarcoma — NG12
Adult: ultrasound suggestive of soft-tissue sarcoma, or uncertain with persisting clinical concern → consider suspected cancer pathway referral to the sarcoma diagnostic service. Children and young people: very urgent referral (within 48 hours).
Bone sarcoma — NG12
Adult: X-ray suggests possible bone sarcoma → consider suspected cancer pathway referral. Children and young people: very urgent referral (within 48 hours). A normal X-ray with persisting unexplained bone pain still needs review and a low threshold for specialist advice.
Lymphoma — NG12
Adult with persistent, enlarging, hard, fixed, supraclavicular or generalised lymphadenopathy, or nodes with systemic features (or splenomegaly for non-Hodgkin) → bloods and urgent investigation/consider suspected cancer pathway referral, weighing associated symptoms — not every unexplained node without clinical context. Children: different thresholds; cervical nodes are common and often reactive.
Site-specific — NG12
Unexplained breast lump aged 30+ (refer); unexplained axillary lump aged 30+ (consider breast pathway; lymphoma pathway if nodal picture); persistent or changing unexplained neck lump aged 45+ (head-and-neck pathway); thyroid lump → thyroid pathway; non-painful testicular enlargement or change in shape/texture (refer) — unexplained or persistent testicular symptoms → urgent direct-access ultrasound; skin lesion suspicious of squamous cell carcinoma or nodular melanoma. Follow current local (e.g. Bolton/NHS Greater Manchester) lump, breast, lymph-node and sarcoma pathways.
Routine surgical / dermatology
Symptomatic but clearly benign lumps for removal — subject to local commissioning policy (Evidence-Based Interventions programme).
Same-day
Sepsis or rapidly spreading cellulitis; expanding painful vascular mass; active bleeding; neurovascular compromise; irreducible tender hernia; suspected deep infection or joint involvement; clinically unstable patient. A stable pulsatile mass → urgent vascular/radiology advice and Doppler imaging rather than automatic same-day vascular surgery.
Referral follows the red-flag assessment directly. The headline route is the NG12 soft-tissue sarcoma pathway — triggered by a growing unexplained lump, usually after an urgent ultrasound — with parallel routes for bone sarcoma (X-ray first), for unexplained lymphadenopathy (lymphoma), and for lumps in organ-specific sites (breast, axilla, neck, thyroid, testis, skin). In England, suspected cancer pathway referrals are now measured against the Faster Diagnosis Standard (cancer diagnosed or ruled out within 28 days), which NG12 reflected in its October 2023 change of terminology. Everything that is confidently benign and merely symptomatic goes via routine routes, and many areas restrict removal of benign lipomas and ganglia to set criteria. The skill is matching the lump to the correct pathway rather than defaulting to local excision.

Treat

Management of the common benign lumps

Lipoma
Reassure ± routine excision
Reassure only when the diagnosis is clinically secure and the lesion is stable, superficial and asymptomatic. Image or refer if large, deep, painful, enlarging, recurrent or atypical — a large or deep lesion should be imaged first, not simply referred for removal. Routine removal of symptomatic benign lipomas is subject to the local Evidence-Based Interventions policy. Send all excised tissue for histology.
Epidermoid / pilar cyst
Leave or excise when quiet
A cyst with a punctum and typical clinical appearance can often be managed conservatively. Inflamed cysts may be sterile inflammation rather than infection — use antibiotics (per local antimicrobial guideline, e.g. Greater Manchester) only when there is cellulitis, systemic illness or another clear indication. Incision and drainage may be required for a fluctuant abscess. Delay definitive excision until inflammation has settled and include the cyst wall to reduce recurrence. Image or refer a cyst that is atypical, recurrent, firm, fixed or rapidly enlarging.
Ganglion
Reassure / aspirate / excise
Many ganglia can be observed if symptoms are minimal. If painful, functionally limiting or causing nerve symptoms, discuss hand/orthopaedic options. Aspiration may provide temporary relief but recurrence is common; do not aspirate a lesion that is not confidently a ganglion. Surgical excision is a specialist decision and is not automatically indicated after one failed aspiration (see local Evidence-Based Interventions policy).
Abscess
Confirm it is not vascular or deep. Incision and drainage is often definitive for a fluctuant abscess, depending on size and site, proximity to major vessels, nerves or joints, systemic illness, diabetes or immunosuppression, recurrent disease and possible hidradenitis suppurativa. Do not attempt office drainage for a suspected deep abscess, vascular lesion, hand/facial or joint involvement or systemic infection. Antibiotics only where clinically indicated (surrounding cellulitis, systemic upset, immunocompromise) — choice and dose per the current Greater Manchester/local antimicrobial guideline. Same-day referral if systemically unwell, spreading infection, immunocompromised or anatomically high risk. Recurrent abscesses: assess for hidradenitis suppurativa, diabetes (HbA1c), chronic staphylococcal carriage (including PVL-associated Staphylococcus aureus) or immunodeficiency, depending on the clinical pattern.
Reactive node
Treat an identified local infection if appropriate and re-examine; persistent, enlarging, hard/fixed, supraclavicular or generalised nodes, or nodes with systemic features → bloods and urgent referral (lymphoma route).
Always histology
Send every excised lump for histology — this is the safety net that catches an unexpected malignancy. Ensure the specimen is correctly labelled, the clinical differential and site are recorded, and the result is reviewed and communicated per local pathology arrangements. Do not assume a lesion was benign because it looked benign clinically.
Do NOT
Locally excise any lump with sarcoma features; reassure-and-discharge a growing deep lump without imaging.
For the genuinely benign lumps, management is mostly reassurance with selective removal for symptoms, function or diagnostic doubt — a confirmed small lipoma or a quiet cyst needs nothing more than safety-netting. The NHS England and Academy of Medical Royal Colleges Evidence-Based Interventions programme restricts routine removal of benign skin lesions and wrist ganglia to defined criteria, so explaining the natural history (many ganglia settle) avoids frustration later. Two rules keep this safe: every excised lump goes for histology, and no lump with sarcoma features is excised locally. Abscesses are drained, with antibiotics reserved for surrounding cellulitis or systemic involvement; recurrent abscesses deserve assessment for hidradenitis suppurativa, diabetes, chronic staphylococcal carriage (including PVL-producing staphylococci) or immunodeficiency.

Lifestyle

Self-care, reassurance and safety information

Clear reassurance Most lumps are benign — naming the diagnosis and its natural history reduces anxiety and requests for unnecessary excision.
What to monitor Any growth, new pain, fixation, skin change or a new lump nearby — do not wait for it to reach about 5 cm (roughly a golf ball) before coming back.
Measure it Suggest the patient photographs the lump beside a ruler or coin every few weeks — an objective record of growth is more useful than memory.
Wound care After any excision: keep clean and dry, watch for infection, and make sure the histology result is followed up and communicated.
Cyst care Avoid squeezing cysts (provokes inflammation and infection); seek review if hot, painful or discharging.
Skin awareness Sun protection and checking for new or changing skin lesions, especially nodules that bleed or grow.
Syndrome surveillance In neurofibromatosis type 1, report any neurofibroma that becomes painful, hard or rapidly enlarging, or new neurological symptoms.
Self-examination Breast and testicular self-awareness — know what is normal and report change promptly.
Because the great majority of lumps are benign, the most valuable intervention is clear, specific reassurance paired with equally specific safety-netting. Patients remember concrete triggers — rapid growth, new pain, size beyond a golf ball, fixation or skin change — far better than a vague instruction to return if worried, and dated photographs against a ruler turn "I think it's bigger" into evidence. After any excision, closing the loop on histology is part of safe care, and patients with predisposing conditions such as neurofibromatosis type 1 need to know which changes in a known lump should prompt urgent review.

Safety

Follow-up, safety-netting and the SCA consultation

Safety-net the benign label
Review if the lump grows, becomes painful, fixes to deep tissue, recurs, or the skin changes (do not wait for it to reach 5 cm) → ultrasound and, if suggestive or indeterminate with persistent concern, the sarcoma pathway.
Planned re-measure
If you are unsure whether a superficial lump is growing, book a review (for example in 4–6 weeks) and re-measure with the same method; growth at review is the NG12 trigger for ultrasound.
Histology follow-up
Ensure every excised specimen is correctly labelled with site and clinical differential, and its histology is reviewed, communicated and acted on; recall the patient if unexpected malignancy.
Recurrence
A lump recurring at a previous excision site is treated as suspicious → image and discuss with the appropriate specialist, even if the original was called benign — particularly if the original pathology is unavailable, incomplete or uncertain.
Lymph node
Persistent, enlarging, hard/fixed, supraclavicular or generalised lymphadenopathy, or systemic features, with no clear cause at review → targeted blood tests and urgent referral (NG12 lymphoma route).
Infection
Same-day Spreading erythema, fever, or a rapidly enlarging tender mass.
Document
Site; dimensions in cm; duration and growth; superficial/deep; soft/firm/hard; mobile/fixed; painful/painless; cystic/solid/vascular; skin changes; regional nodes; neurovascular findings; working diagnosis, imaging or referral, and the safety-net advice given.
SCA phrasing
"From what I can feel, this is most likely a harmless fatty lump. Because it has grown, I'd like a scan within the next two weeks to be sure — it's a quick ultrasound. If the scan shows anything unusual I'll refer you to a specialist team who deal with lumps like this. In the meantime, if it gets bigger, becomes painful or you notice other lumps, please come back sooner."
Safety-netting is what makes a "benign lump" diagnosis safe over time, because the single most important sarcoma sign — growth — only declares itself later. Documenting size in centimetres creates the baseline, and a planned re-measure converts uncertainty into a decision. The patient should leave knowing the specific changes that mandate re-imaging and referral. Two system-level nets matter just as much: ensuring every excised lump's histology is reviewed and acted on, and treating recurrence at an excision site as suspicious regardless of the first label. In the SCA, examiners reward a clear explanation of why a scan is needed despite a probably benign lump, an honest mention that the aim is to rule out something serious, and a concrete follow-up plan.
