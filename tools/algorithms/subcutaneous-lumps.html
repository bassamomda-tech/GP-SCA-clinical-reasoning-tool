1
Safety

Red Flags — the soft-tissue and bone sarcoma pathway

Soft-tissue sarcoma — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Adult with an unexplained lump that is increasing in size → consider an urgent direct-access ultrasound (within 2 weeks). If the scan suggests sarcoma, or is uncertain and clinical concern persists → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral.
Soft-tissue sarcoma — children and young people (NG12) Unexplained lump increasing in size → consider a very urgent direct-access ultrasound (within 48 hours); suggestive or uncertain scan with ongoing concern → very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours).
Suspicious features (UK sarcoma guideline) The UK soft tissue sarcoma guideline (Hayes et al, British Journal of Cancer 2024) lists size over 5 cm, deep to the deep fascia, increasing size and pain as features that raise the chance of malignancy. A lump that recurs after previous excision is treated with the same suspicion in practice.
Bone sarcoma — NICE NG12 Child or young person with unexplained bone swelling or pain → consider a very urgent X-ray (within 48 hours). Adult whose X-ray suggests possible bone sarcoma → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral.
Suspicious lymph node — NICE NG12 Adult with unexplained lymphadenopathy → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma, weighing fever, night sweats, breathlessness, itch, weight loss and (Hodgkin) alcohol-induced node pain.
Breast or axillary lump — NICE NG12 Aged 30 or over with an unexplained breast lump (with or without pain) → suspected cancer pathway referral; aged 30 or over with an unexplained axillary lump → consider the same route.
Neck, thyroid and testis — NICE NG12 Aged 45 or over with an unexplained neck lump (laryngeal), any unexplained thyroid lump, or a non-painful enlargement or change in shape or texture of a testis → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral.
Do NOT excise Never "shell out" or locally excise a lump with any sarcoma feature — an unplanned excision makes definitive surgery harder. Image and refer instead.
Abscess / infection Hot, tender, fluctuant, spreading erythema ± systemic upset → incision and drainage; same-day assessment if spreading cellulitis or signs of sepsis.
Pulsatile / vascular An expansile, pulsatile mass (?aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm, for example in the groin after arterial puncture) → do not aspirate; urgent imaging and vascular opinion.
The whole point of the lumps pathway is to separate the rare soft-tissue sarcoma from the very common benign lump, because the cost of missing it — or of excising it unplanned — is high. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) keys its adult recommendation on one feature: an unexplained lump that is increasing in size earns an urgent direct-access ultrasound, and a suggestive or uncertain scan with persisting concern earns a suspected cancer pathway referral. Children and young people get the same logic on a 48-hour clock. The size, depth and pain criteria come from the UK sarcoma specialists' guideline (Hayes et al, 2024) rather than from NG12, and they are best used as extra reasons to image and to lower your threshold, not as a checklist that must be complete. The cardinal error is calling such a lump a lipoma and shelling it out, which contaminates tissue planes and compromises the definitive operation. Lumps in the breast, axilla, neck, thyroid and testis have their own NG12 routes, and suspicious nodes follow the lymphoma recommendations.
2
Diagnose

History — growth, pain and the timeline

Duration and growth
How long present and changing how fast? A lump unchanged for years is reassuring; recent or steady growth is the key red flag. Ask for old photographs or a previous measurement in the notes.
Pain
Painless does not equal benign (many sarcomas are painless), but new pain in a soft-tissue mass is a red flag. Tenderness that fluctuates with inflammation suggests a cyst or abscess.
Previous lump or excision
Recurrence at a site of prior "lipoma" excision raises sarcoma — was the original ever sent for histology, and what did it show?
Systemic features
Weight loss, night sweats, fever, itch (lymphoma); known cancer (metastasis, e.g. breast, lung, renal, melanoma); immunosuppression.
Trauma / infection
Recent trauma (haematoma, fat necrosis), bites or scratches (regional node), injecting sites (abscess, pseudoaneurysm), or signs of local infection.
Function
Tingling or weakness distal to the lump (nerve sheath tumour, ganglion compressing a nerve); restricted joint movement; interference with clothing or daily activities.
Family / syndromic
Neurofibromatosis type 1 (multiple neurofibromas; risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour), familial lipomatosis, Li–Fraumeni, previous radiotherapy to the area (radiation-associated sarcoma years later).
Patient's agenda
What are they worried it is? Cancer fear, cosmetic concern and discomfort need different conversations and different outcomes.
History triages the lump along a single axis: how it is behaving over time. A soft, mobile lump that has been unchanged for years is almost always benign, whereas any lump that is new, growing, or has recurred after a previous "lipoma" excision earns imaging — growth is also the exact trigger NG12 uses. Pain is a useful but asymmetric clue: its presence is a red flag, but its absence is not reassuring. Systemic symptoms point towards lymphoma or metastasis, and a relevant background (neurofibromatosis type 1, prior radiotherapy to the field) raises the pre-test probability of malignancy. In a population study from north-west England, the lifetime risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour in NF1 was estimated at 8–13% (Evans et al, J Med Genet 2002), which is why a neurofibroma that changes behaviour deserves urgent attention.

Diagnose

Examination — the five S characteristics

Site
Limb (the thigh is a common sarcoma site), trunk, head and neck, breast, axilla, groin, testis. Note relation to joints, tendons and neurovascular bundles.
Size
Measure with a ruler or tape and document in cm — 5 cm is a warning size in the UK sarcoma guideline and your measurement becomes the growth baseline.
Shape and surface
Well-defined and lobulated (lipoma) vs irregular, ill-defined or fixed (concerning).
Skin
Punctum and central dimple (epidermoid cyst); skin tethering, ulceration, colour change or a nodule within the skin (skin cancer, including nodular melanoma).
Consistency and depth
Soft, fluctuant, slip sign (lipoma); firm or hard, fixed, deep to fascia (concerning). Tense contraction of the underlying muscle: a superficial lump stays mobile, a deep one becomes fixed or disappears. Transilluminates (cyst, ganglion).
Other
Pulsatility (vascular), compressibility or cough impulse (hernia), Tinel's sign over the lump (nerve sheath tumour), regional lymph nodes, distal neurovascular status, and café-au-lait macules or axillary freckling (NF1).
A structured examination of the lump — site, size, shape, surface and consistency, plus depth — captures the features that drive the decision. Benign lumps tend to be superficial, soft or fluctuant, mobile and well-defined: a lipoma slips under the examining finger, a cyst may transilluminate and have a punctum. The features that move a lump onto the imaging pathway are growth, size over 5 cm, a deep (sub-fascial) position, fixation and a hard irregular consistency. Asking the patient to tense the underlying muscle is a quick bedside test of depth. Documenting size in centimetres creates the baseline against which growth — the NG12 trigger — is judged at review. Always examine the regional nodes: a node draining the lump changes the differential.

Differential diagnosis — and the feature that separates each

Adipose
Lipoma — soft, mobile, lobulated, slip sign; the commonest soft-tissue lump. Angiolipoma — small, often multiple and tender, forearms and trunk.
Cystic
Epidermoid cyst (punctum, cheesy contents, may become inflamed); pilar cyst (scalp, smooth, no punctum, often familial); ganglion (near joints and tendon sheaths, firm, transilluminates, dorsal wrist commonest); Baker's cyst (popliteal fossa, may rupture and mimic DVT).
Lymph node
Reactive (tender, mobile, recent infection, shrinks over weeks) vs lymphoma or metastasis (hard or rubbery, fixed or matted, enlarging, systemic symptoms).
Vascular / neural
Haemangioma or vascular malformation (compressible, bluish); neurofibroma or schwannoma (along a nerve, mobile side-to-side not along the nerve, Tinel's positive); aneurysm (pulsatile, expansile).
Inflammatory
Abscess (hot, fluctuant), rheumatoid nodule (extensor surfaces), gouty tophus (ears, joints, chalky), fat necrosis or haematoma after trauma, injection-site granuloma.
Hernia
Groin, umbilical and incisional lumps with a cough impulse — reducible or not; an irreducible tender hernia is a surgical emergency.
Malignant
Do not miss Soft-tissue sarcoma (often a painless, enlarging deep mass), metastasis, lymphoma, breast cancer, or a skin cancer with a subcutaneous component (nodular melanoma, squamous cell carcinoma, Merkel cell carcinoma).
The differential is dominated by a few benign diagnoses — lipoma, epidermoid cyst, ganglion and reactive lymph node — each with a recognisable bedside signature. Holding the malignant possibilities alongside them (sarcoma, lymphoma, metastasis, skin cancer) is what keeps the pathway safe, because a deep, growing "lipoma" is exactly how a sarcoma presents. Two NG12 skin recommendations matter here: consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for a skin lesion that raises the suspicion of squamous cell carcinoma, and for a pigmented or non-pigmented lesion that suggests nodular melanoma. Trauma-related lumps (haematoma, fat necrosis) and infective ones should declare themselves on history and resolve with time; persistence or atypical behaviour still prompts imaging.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — image before you excise

Ultrasound
First-line for any indeterminate soft-tissue lump — separates cystic from solid, measures size and depth, shows vascularity. NG12: urgent direct access (within 2 weeks) in adults with an unexplained lump increasing in size; within 48 hours in children and young people. Write the clinical question and "?sarcoma" on the request.
MRI
The definitive imaging for deep or large soft-tissue masses — usually arranged by the sarcoma diagnostic service, not before a planned biopsy.
X-ray
For a bony mass or a suspected bone lesion (NG12 bone sarcoma route), and to look for calcification or bone involvement beneath a soft-tissue mass.
Bloods
If lymphoma or systemic disease suspected: FBC and film, ESR or CRP, LDH, liver and renal function; urate if a tophus is suspected. Consider HIV testing where nodes are unexplained.
Biopsy
Image-guided core needle biopsy is planned by the sarcoma service so the track can be removed at definitive surgery — not an incisional biopsy in primary care.
Do NOT
Excise, shell out or incisionally biopsy a possible sarcoma locally; do not aspirate a pulsatile mass; do not attribute a growing lump to a "fatty lump" without imaging.
Imaging, not surgery, is the first investigation for any lump that is not clearly a simple benign lesion. Ultrasound is the accessible first-line test that distinguishes cystic from solid, gauges size and depth, and flags lesions that need the sarcoma service — and NG12 gives GPs direct access to it for exactly this purpose. The UK sarcoma guideline (Hayes et al, 2024) advises that a tissue diagnosis should, where possible, be made before treatment by percutaneous core biopsy reviewed by a specialist sarcoma pathologist. An unplanned local procedure contaminates the tissue planes the definitive operation must clear, so biopsy and excision of a possible sarcoma belong to the specialist team. A clear request (size, depth, growth, "?sarcoma") helps the radiologist triage and apply the right protocol.

Refer

Referral criteria

Sarcoma — NG12
Adult: ultrasound suggestive of soft-tissue sarcoma, or uncertain with persisting clinical concern → consider suspected cancer pathway referral to the sarcoma diagnostic service. Children and young people: very urgent referral (within 48 hours).
Bone sarcoma — NG12
Adult: X-ray suggests possible bone sarcoma → consider suspected cancer pathway referral. Children and young people: very urgent referral (within 48 hours). A normal X-ray with persisting unexplained bone pain still needs review and a low threshold for specialist advice.
Lymphoma — NG12
Adult with unexplained lymphadenopathy (or splenomegaly for non-Hodgkin) → consider suspected cancer pathway referral, weighing associated symptoms.
Site-specific — NG12
Breast lump aged 30+ (refer); axillary lump aged 30+; neck lump aged 45+; thyroid lump at any age; testicular enlargement or change in texture; skin lesion suspicious of squamous cell carcinoma or nodular melanoma.
Routine surgical / dermatology
Symptomatic but clearly benign lumps for removal — subject to local commissioning policy (Evidence-Based Interventions programme).
Same-day
Abscess with spreading cellulitis or sepsis; irreducible tender hernia; pulsatile or vascular mass (vascular surgery).
Referral follows the red-flag assessment directly. The headline route is the NG12 soft-tissue sarcoma pathway — triggered by a growing unexplained lump, usually after an urgent ultrasound — with parallel routes for bone sarcoma (X-ray first), for unexplained lymphadenopathy (lymphoma), and for lumps in organ-specific sites (breast, axilla, neck, thyroid, testis, skin). In England, suspected cancer pathway referrals are now measured against the Faster Diagnosis Standard (cancer diagnosed or ruled out within 28 days), which NG12 reflected in its October 2023 change of terminology. Everything that is confidently benign and merely symptomatic goes via routine routes, and many areas restrict removal of benign lipomas and ganglia to set criteria. The skill is matching the lump to the correct pathway rather than defaulting to local excision.

Treat

Management of the common benign lumps

Lipoma
Reassure ± routine excision
A small, superficial, soft, static lipoma needs no treatment. Removal is usually reserved for functional problems or diagnostic doubt; local policies often list size over 5 cm, sub-fascial position, rapid growth or pain as reasons to refer — which are also reasons to image first. Send all excised tissue for histology.
Epidermoid / pilar cyst
Leave or excise when quiet
Asymptomatic cysts can be left. If recurrently inflamed, excise the whole wall once quiescent. An inflamed cyst is often sterile — warm compresses; incision and drainage if fluctuant; antibiotics (dose per BNF) only if spreading cellulitis.
Ganglion
Reassure / aspirate / excise
Many wrist ganglia resolve without treatment. Evidence-Based Interventions guidance: no treatment unless pain, tingling or numbness, or concern; aspiration first if needed; surgery only if aspiration fails and hand function is restricted.
Abscess
Incision and drainage is the definitive treatment; antibiotics if surrounding cellulitis, systemic upset or immunocompromise. Recurrent abscesses: check glucose/HbA1c, consider hidradenitis suppurativa or PVL-associated Staphylococcus aureus.
Reactive node
Treat the source, re-measure at review; persisting or enlarging nodes move to the lymphoma route.
Always histology
Send every excised lump for histology — this is the safety net that catches an unexpected malignancy.
Do NOT
Locally excise any lump with sarcoma features; reassure-and-discharge a growing deep lump without imaging.
For the genuinely benign lumps, management is mostly reassurance with selective removal for symptoms, function or diagnostic doubt — a confirmed small lipoma or a quiet cyst needs nothing more than safety-netting. The NHS England and Academy of Medical Royal Colleges Evidence-Based Interventions programme restricts routine removal of benign skin lesions and wrist ganglia to defined criteria, so explaining the natural history (many ganglia settle) avoids frustration later. Two rules keep this safe: every excised lump goes for histology, and no lump with sarcoma features is excised locally. Abscesses are drained, with antibiotics reserved for surrounding cellulitis or systemic involvement; recurrent abscesses deserve a search for diabetes and for PVL-producing staphylococci.

Lifestyle

Self-care, reassurance and safety information

Clear reassurance Most lumps are benign — naming the diagnosis and its natural history reduces anxiety and requests for unnecessary excision.
What to monitor Rapid growth, new pain, size beyond about 5 cm (roughly a golf ball), fixation, skin change or a new lump nearby.
Measure it Suggest the patient photographs the lump beside a ruler or coin every few weeks — an objective record of growth is more useful than memory.
Wound care After any excision: keep clean and dry, watch for infection, and make sure the histology result is followed up and communicated.
Cyst care Avoid squeezing cysts (provokes inflammation and infection); seek review if hot, painful or discharging.
Skin awareness Sun protection and checking for new or changing skin lesions, especially nodules that bleed or grow.
Syndrome surveillance In neurofibromatosis type 1, report any neurofibroma that becomes painful, hard or rapidly enlarging, or new neurological symptoms.
Self-examination Breast and testicular self-awareness — know what is normal and report change promptly.
Because the great majority of lumps are benign, the most valuable intervention is clear, specific reassurance paired with equally specific safety-netting. Patients remember concrete triggers — rapid growth, new pain, size beyond a golf ball, fixation or skin change — far better than a vague instruction to return if worried, and dated photographs against a ruler turn "I think it's bigger" into evidence. After any excision, closing the loop on histology is part of safe care, and patients with predisposing conditions such as neurofibromatosis type 1 need to know which changes in a known lump should prompt urgent review.

Safety

Follow-up, safety-netting and the SCA consultation

Safety-net the benign label
Review if the lump grows, becomes painful, exceeds about 5 cm, fixes to deep tissue, or the skin changes → ultrasound and the sarcoma pathway.
Planned re-measure
If you are unsure whether a superficial lump is growing, book a review (for example in 4–6 weeks) and re-measure with the same method; growth at review is the NG12 trigger for ultrasound.
Histology follow-up
Ensure every excised specimen's histology is reviewed and acted on; recall the patient if unexpected malignancy.
Recurrence
A lump recurring at a previous excision site is treated as suspicious → image and refer, even if the original was called benign.
Lymph node
Persisting or enlarging lymphadenopathy with no clear cause at review → blood tests and the NG12 lymphoma route.
Infection
Same-day Spreading erythema, fever, or a rapidly enlarging tender mass.
Document
Size in cm, depth, characteristics, working diagnosis, imaging or referral, and the safety-net advice given.
SCA phrasing
"From what I can feel, this is most likely a harmless fatty lump. Because it has grown, I'd like a scan within the next two weeks to be sure — it's a quick ultrasound. If the scan shows anything unusual I'll refer you to a specialist team who deal with lumps like this. In the meantime, if it gets bigger, becomes painful or you notice other lumps, please come back sooner."
Safety-netting is what makes a "benign lump" diagnosis safe over time, because the single most important sarcoma sign — growth — only declares itself later. Documenting size in centimetres creates the baseline, and a planned re-measure converts uncertainty into a decision. The patient should leave knowing the specific changes that mandate re-imaging and referral. Two system-level nets matter just as much: ensuring every excised lump's histology is reviewed and acted on, and treating recurrence at an excision site as suspicious regardless of the first label. In the SCA, examiners reward a clear explanation of why a scan is needed despite a probably benign lump, an honest mention that the aim is to rule out something serious, and a concrete follow-up plan.
