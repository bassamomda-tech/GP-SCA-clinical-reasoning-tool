Safety

Red Flags — Severe Hypernatraemia, Cerebral Oedema Risk & Central DI Emergency

Sodium >155 mmol/L + acute onset + neurological symptoms (confusion, seizures, reduced consciousness) Severe acute hypernatraemia → osmotic brain cell shrinkage → cerebral haemorrhage risk. → 999. Clearly acute, symptomatic hypernatraemia may need a faster initial reduction (about 5 mmol/L in the first hour) under senior critical-care/endocrine supervision, but the fall should not then exceed the agreed 24-hour limit (10 mmol/L in 24 hours). HDU/ICU monitoring. A focal neurological deficit is a separate emergency differential (stroke, haemorrhage, venous sinus thrombosis) until proven otherwise.
Sodium ≥160 mmol/L Severe hypernatraemia — high mortality risk. → Urgent hospital assessment and monitored treatment. Use 999 if confused, drowsy, shocked, fitting, unable to drink, rapidly deteriorating or cannot be transferred safely. Any sodium concentration with shock, seizures, reduced consciousness or severe dehydration: 999/emergency transfer.
Polyuria (>50 mL/kg/day, about >3 L/day in many adults) + polydipsia + inappropriately dilute urine + hypernatraemia or rising sodium Possible diabetes insipidus (DI) — central or nephrogenic. → Same-day acute medical assessment and urgent endocrinology discussion. Check glucose, U&Es/renal function, calcium, potassium, serum and urine osmolality and urine volume; review medications; consider primary polydipsia and osmotic diuresis. Do not perform or arrange water deprivation testing directly from primary care in a patient with hypernatraemia or suspected complete DI — water-deprivation or copeptin-based testing must be performed in a specialist setting with close monitoring and a clear rescue plan.
Hypernatraemia + polyuria + recent head injury / neurosurgery / pituitary surgery / meningitis / encephalitis Central diabetes insipidus — ADH deficiency from hypothalamic or pituitary damage. → 999/same-day. Involve endocrinology/acute medicine urgently (high-dependency-level care is recommended for cranial DI with hypernatraemia). Restore intravascular volume first if depleted (often 0.9% sodium chloride). Then replace the free-water deficit with oral/NG water where safe, or IV dextrose-containing fluid under hospital supervision. Desmopressin according to the specialist/inpatient protocol, with close sodium and urine-output monitoring — no fixed community dose for a newly suspected case.
Hypernatraemia + fever + reduced consciousness + nursing home or hospital inpatient (particularly elderly, intubated, or with swallowing difficulty) Hospital-acquired hypernatraemia from inadequate free water intake. → Oral/NG free water where safe, or IV 5% glucose under hospital monitoring (resuscitate first if shocked). Fluid balance reassessment. Medication review (excess sodium-containing IV fluids, enteral feeds). Rate of fall no more than about 0.5 mmol/L/h and 10–12 mmol/L in 24 h.
Infant + irritability + high-pitched cry + bulging fontanelle + hypernatraemia following diarrhoea/vomiting or heat exposure Hypernatraemic dehydration in infant — high risk of cerebral venous thrombosis and permanent neurological damage. → 999/urgent paediatric admission and paediatric specialist management. Replace the deficit slowly, usually over 48 hours, aiming for a sodium fall not exceeding 0.5 mmol/L/hour or 12 mmol/L in 24 hours (NICE NG29), with electrolytes every 4–6 hours initially, or more frequently if clinically indicated.
The rate of sodium correction in hypernatraemia is the most clinically critical parameter and the source of the most serious iatrogenic complications — the brain adapts to chronic hypernatraemia by generating idiogenic osmoles (taurine, glutamine, inositol, myoinositol) within neurons to prevent cell shrinkage. If hypernatraemia is corrected too rapidly, the intracellular osmoles cannot be cleared quickly enough, causing net influx of water into neurons — cerebral oedema, resulting in seizures, herniation, and death. The correction rate rule: for chronic hypernatraemia (duration >48 hours or unknown): maximum fall in serum sodium of about 0.5 mmol/L per hour and maximum 10-12 mmol/L per 24 hours, usually replacing the deficit over about 48 hours with adjustment for ongoing losses and comorbidity. For clearly acute hypernatraemia with neurological symptoms: a faster initial reduction (about 5 mmol/L in the first hour) may be needed under senior critical-care/endocrine supervision, but the fall should not then exceed the agreed 24-hour limit (Society for Endocrinology: 10 mmol/L in 24 hours). In practice, most community-presenting hypernatraemia has been developing over days — treat as chronic. Serial 2-4 hourly sodium monitoring during correction is mandatory.

Diagnose

Causes of Hypernatraemia — Classification

Water deficit (most common mechanism)
Pure water loss without proportional sodium loss → hypernatraemia. Insensible losses (most common community cause): fever, sweating, tachypnoea, hot environment. Renal water loss (Diabetes Insipidus): Central DI (ADH deficiency — head trauma, pituitary/hypothalamic tumour, neurosurgery, infiltrative disease — sarcoidosis, histiocytosis) or Nephrogenic DI (ADH resistance — chronic lithium use, most common drug cause; hypercalcaemia; hypokalaemia; CKD; inherited). GI losses (osmotic diarrhoea — lactulose, sorbitol, rotavirus — produces hypotonic stool). Inadequate intake: distinguish reduced access to water (dependency, nursing home residents, intubated patients), impaired cognition or swallowing, and impaired thirst in older people — true adipsia (adipsic DI) is a specialist condition — from central and nephrogenic DI.
Sodium excess (less common)
Iatrogenic: excess normal saline (0.9% NaCl) or hypertonic saline administration, sodium bicarbonate infusions, hypertonic enteral feeds, salt-containing enemas, sodium-containing effervescent or soluble medicines. Deliberate salt ingestion: rare — child abuse (salt poisoning), intentional harm: follow child or adult safeguarding procedures the same day. Drowning in sea water: hypertonic ingestion. (Primary hyperaldosteronism and Cushing syndrome cause sodium retention, but clinically significant hypernatraemia is uncommon — consider only with a specific endocrine presentation.)
Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)
HHS (previously HONK — hyperosmolar non-ketotic) is defined by hyperosmolality and severe hyperglycaemia, usually with marked dehydration and little or no significant ketoacidosis — not by hypernatraemia. Measured sodium may appear normal or even low initially due to glucose-driven water movement, and may rise as glucose falls. Calculated osmolality = 2 × sodium + glucose + urea (all mmol/L; used by JBDS to monitor HHS). Effective osmolality/tonicity = 2 × sodium + glucose (urea is an ineffective osmole). Use the local JBDS calculation and pathway rather than an isolated corrected-sodium coefficient. HHS target: gradual osmolality reduction over 48h.
Sodium interpretation in hyperglycaemia needs care when reviewing a patient with both elevated glucose and elevated or apparently normal sodium — hyperglycaemia causes water to move from the intracellular to extracellular compartment (by osmotic effect), diluting the plasma sodium. As a result, the measured sodium appears lower than the true sodium that would exist if glucose were normal. Corrected-sodium coefficients vary according to the formula and glucose range, so no single coefficient is universally correct. In HHS, management is guided primarily by clinical volume status, measured sodium, the calculated/measured osmolality trend, the glucose fall, urine output and renal and cardiac status. JBDS emphasises that sodium may initially rise as glucose falls with treatment (water moves back intracellularly), and that this alone does not indicate a need to switch to hypotonic fluid. Do not switch fluids solely because sodium rises while glucose is falling — monitor calculated osmolality and fluid balance and use the local JBDS pathway.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Symptoms: thirst (intense — normally the primary defence against hypernatraemia; absent in adipsia or altered consciousness), polyuria + nocturia (DI — urine output >50 mL/kg/day, or about >3 L/day in many adults; measured 24-hour volume preferable where feasible; dilute, pale), confusion + restlessness + irritability (early cerebral effects), weakness, lethargy. Severe: seizures, reduced consciousness. Duration of symptoms (acute vs chronic — determines correction rate). Fluid intake: adequate access to water? Swallowing problems? Care setting (nursing home, hospital, intubated)? Fluid losses: diarrhoea, vomiting, fever duration, environmental heat, burns. Medications: lithium (nephrogenic DI — most common drug cause), loop diuretics (can worsen dehydration), lactulose (osmotic diarrhoea → hypotonic fluid loss, i.e. water lost in excess of sodium), demeclocycline (nephrogenic DI), amphotericin, foscarnet, sodium-containing effervescent or soluble medicines (sodium load). PMH: diabetes (HHS), renal disease, pituitary/hypothalamic history, neurosurgery, head trauma, sarcoidosis.
Examination
Hydration status: mucous membranes (dry), skin turgor (reduced in dehydration — less reliable in elderly), sunken eyes, capillary refill time. Blood pressure + HR (postural hypotension in significant volume depletion — tachycardia). Neurological: GCS/consciousness level (hypernatraemia causes CNS depression — confusion, irritability, coma if severe), focal neurology (treat as a separate emergency differential until proven otherwise; cerebral haemorrhage and venous thrombosis are recognised complications, especially in severe infant hypernatraemic dehydration), neuromuscular irritability (hyperreflexia or spasticity occur only with severe neurological involvement — not a reliable routine finding). Temperature. Urine output: oliguric (volume depletion) vs polyuric (DI). Body weight (compare with recent documented weight if available — acute weight loss reflects volume loss).
Investigations
Serum U&Es (sodium, potassium, urea, creatinine) (urgently + repeat frequency during correction) · Serum osmolality (calculated: 2×Na + glucose + urea; measured if DI suspected) · Urine osmolality + urine sodium + urine specific gravity (concentrated, e.g. >600 mOsm/kg, suggests an appropriate response; inappropriately dilute urine suggests impaired renal water conservation — suggestive, not diagnostic alone: interpret with serum osmolality, volume status, urine volume, recent intake, renal function, diuretics and hyperglycaemia) · Serum glucose + HbA1c (HHS; check capillary glucose and ketones at the assessment in every dehydrated patient) · Calcium (hypercalcaemia causes nephrogenic DI) · 12-hour serum lithium level (if on lithium — toxicity can occur within the nominal therapeutic range) · Copeptin-based or water-deprivation testing (specialist endocrine setting only — not arranged from primary care; urinary ADH is not a routine test) · MRI pituitary/hypothalamus (central DI suspicion)
The urine osmolality in hypernatraemia is the key discriminating investigation between appropriate physiological response, diabetes insipidus, and primary polydipsia — in normal hypernatraemia from water depletion, the hypothalamus detects high plasma osmolality and releases ADH, which causes the kidney to retain water and concentrate the urine maximally. Expected urine osmolality in appropriate response to hypernatraemia: >600-800 mOsm/kg (very concentrated urine). In diabetes insipidus: the kidney cannot concentrate urine because either ADH is absent (central DI) or the kidney cannot respond to ADH (nephrogenic DI). Urine osmolality in DI is inappropriately dilute for the degree of hypernatraemia — but urine osmolality alone does not distinguish central DI, nephrogenic DI and primary polydipsia; interpret it with serum osmolality, urine volume, renal function and medication history. The diagnostic test is specialist-supervised water deprivation (or copeptin-based) testing in an endocrine setting, never arranged directly from primary care in a hypernatraemic patient — urine osmolality is measured as fluid is withheld, then desmopressin is given. If urine concentrates after desmopressin: central DI. If urine does not concentrate: nephrogenic DI. If urine concentrates before desmopressin: not DI (appropriate response, or primary polydipsia).

Diabetes Insipidus — Central vs Nephrogenic & Lithium

Central diabetes insipidus
ADH deficiency from hypothalamic-pituitary axis damage. Causes: trauma (head injury — most common acquired cause), post-neurosurgery (pituitary surgery — a recognised complication of trans-sphenoidal surgery), primary or metastatic pituitary/hypothalamic tumours (craniopharyngioma, germinoma, metastases), infiltrative disease (sarcoidosis, histiocytosis X, haemochromatosis), infection (meningitis, encephalitis), autoimmune (anti-ADH antibodies), Wolfram syndrome (DIDMOAD — DI + DM + Optic Atrophy + Deafness). Clinical: polyuria + polydipsia + nocturia + dilute urine. In acute post-surgical: triphasic response (DI → SIADH → DI again). Diagnosis: specialist-supervised water deprivation test + desmopressin challenge, or copeptin-based testing. Treatment: desmopressin (DDAVP) — individualised dose and route per the endocrinology plan (see Step 6).
Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
ADH produced normally but kidneys cannot respond. Causes: lithium (most common drug cause — lithium enters principal cells via ENaC channels, accumulates, reduces aquaporin-2 expression — reversible early, irreversible with prolonged high-level exposure), hypercalcaemia (>2.8 mmol/L — calcium activates CaR receptor → inhibits AQP2), hypokalaemia (severe, chronic), CKD (tubular damage reduces concentrating ability), amyloidosis, sickle cell disease (medullary sickling). Inherited (rare): X-linked AVP-R2 mutation (boys) or AQP2 mutation. Treatment: correct reversible cause. Amiloride (for lithium-induced — blocks ENaC, reduces lithium entry into principal cells) or a thiazide (paradoxically reduces urine volume, but increases lithium concentrations and toxicity risk) only with specialist involvement.
Lithium-induced nephrogenic DI management
Same-day assessment if hypernatraemic, dehydrated, confused or unable to maintain oral intake. Check 12-hour lithium concentration (target depends on indication, treatment phase, age and specialist plan — toxicity can occur within the nominal therapeutic range), U&Es/eGFR, calcium, glucose, serum osmolality and urine osmolality. Ensure adequate hydration (dehydration increases lithium toxicity risk). Do not stop lithium abruptly without urgent psychiatric/specialist advice unless there is toxicity or an acute medical indication. Discuss amiloride, thiazide treatment and lithium dose reduction/switching with psychiatry, endocrinology or nephrology — thiazides increase lithium concentrations and toxicity risk. If amiloride is initiated, monitor potassium and renal function closely.
Lithium-induced nephrogenic DI is one of the most common and practically important causes of polyuria encountered in primary care — a substantial proportion of patients on long-term lithium develop some degree of nephrogenic DI, ranging from mild polyuria to severe diabetes insipidus with very large urine volumes. The mechanism: lithium enters renal collecting duct principal cells via the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) and inhibits the adenylate cyclase pathway, reducing aquaporin-2 (AQP2) water channel expression and impairing ADH-mediated water reabsorption. In early stages, this is reversible on stopping lithium; in patients with prolonged lithium treatment and high trough levels, some tubular damage may be permanent. In a patient who needs to continue lithium, amiloride (blocks ENaC, reducing lithium entry into principal cells), lithium dose reduction or switching are specialist decisions with psychiatry, endocrinology or nephrology; adequate hydration matters because dehydration increases lithium serum levels and toxicity risk.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / emergency transfer
Any sodium with shock, seizures, reduced consciousness, confusion or severe dehydration · Sodium ≥160 if drowsy, unable to drink, rapidly deteriorating or cannot be transferred safely (otherwise urgent hospital assessment and monitored correction) · Infant with hypernatraemic dehydration · Acute central DI post-head injury or neurosurgery
Same-day/acute medical admission
Sodium ≥160 — urgent hospital admission and monitored correction · Sodium 150–159 — same-day acute medical assessment in most patients; immediate if symptomatic, dehydrated, unable to drink, polyuric or suspected DI/HHS · Sodium 146–149 if elderly/frail, symptomatic, confused, polyuric, unable to drink or cause unclear · Dehydration with acute kidney injury (rising creatinine) · Suspected HHS — emergency admission
Endocrinology
Suspected or confirmed diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic) — urgent discussion; specialist water-deprivation/copeptin testing and desmopressin initiation · Pituitary/hypothalamic cause of central DI · Any recurrent or chronic DI
Nephrology
Nephrogenic DI from CKD or structural renal causes · Lithium-induced DI with significant renal impairment · Lithium-induced DI: amiloride, thiazide or lithium changes with psychiatry, endocrinology or nephrology
Safeguarding
Hypernatraemia from inadequate intake in a dependent adult (care-home resident or someone who relies on carers for drinks), especially if recurrent or with pressure ulcers, weight loss or poor care, may be neglect → raise an adult safeguarding concern with the local authority (Care Act 2014) at the same time as treating, not after the sodium improves, and request a care-needs reassessment. Suspected salt poisoning in a child or dependent adult → safeguarding referral the same day alongside emergency care.
GP management
A mild, isolated hypernatraemia result (146–149 mmol/L) in a clinically well, cognitively intact adult may sometimes be managed initially with oral/enteral fluids and urgent repeat assessment, but only after reviewing volume status, renal function, glucose, medications and the cause (dehydration, fever, excess diuretics). Seek same-day advice if the result is new, persistent, rising, unexplained, associated with polyuria, or if the patient is elderly, frail or unable to ensure adequate intake. Home management also needs a named person who will provide measured drinks (patient, relative, carer or care-home fluid chart); if nobody can, arrange same-day assessment. Check capillary glucose. Stop sodium-containing effervescent or soluble medicines where possible. Never fluid-restrict a hypernatraemic patient. Suspected lithium NDI: 12-hour lithium level, U&Es/eGFR, calcium, glucose and osmolalities, then specialist advice — do not start amiloride or a thiazide without it. Acute diarrhoeal illness: oral rehydration solution, ensure adequate free water replacement.
Oral rehydration may be appropriate for a mild, isolated hypernatraemia (sodium 146–149 mmol/L) in a clinically well, cognitively intact adult who can drink, once volume status, renal function, glucose, medications and the cause have been reviewed — intravenous fluids introduce the risk of overcorrection and are not necessary in mild cases with adequate oral access. Free water (plain water, not sports drinks or normal saline) is the appropriate correction fluid for water-deficit hypernatraemia. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) may be used for combined isotonic + water losses (gastroenteritis). The important contraindication to oral correction: any patient with reduced consciousness, swallowing difficulty, or who cannot reliably drink adequate volumes needs IV correction in a monitored setting. Arrange an urgent repeat at an interval set by the local laboratory and clinical context; the fall should not exceed about 0.5 mmol/L per hour or 10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours.

Treat

IV Fluid Correction & Desmopressin

IV fluid choice for hypernatraemia correction (hospital — individualised specialist prescription)
Hospital/secondary-care management: IV fluids by competent staff with an individualised fluid-management plan, seeking expert help for hypernatraemia (NICE CG174). Choice depends on the clinical situation. Shock or marked intravascular depletion — resuscitate first with appropriate isotonic crystalloid, usually 0.9% sodium chloride, with senior input. Stable water-deficit hypernatraemia — oral/NG water where safe; otherwise IV 5% glucose or an appropriate hypotonic fluid (e.g. 0.45% sodium chloride) under hospital monitoring. Ongoing GI loss — replace the specific loss and free-water deficit according to the sodium trend. HHS — JBDS pathway: initially 0.9% sodium chloride; change to 0.45% only if osmolality is not falling adequately. Cardiac/renal failure — specialist fluid prescription; risk of pulmonary oedema. DI with ongoing dilute polyuria — specialist endocrine management, balancing desmopressin and free-water replacement. Do not give hypotonic fluid as the initial resuscitation fluid in shock; once circulation is restored, replace the free-water deficit carefully with oral/enteral water or an appropriate hypotonic IV fluid under monitoring. Rate calculation: estimate water deficit: Water Deficit (L) = Total Body Water × [(Serum Na / 140) − 1]. TBW = 0.6 × body weight in kg (men), 0.5 × body weight in kg (women). Example: 70 kg man, Na 158: TBW = 42L; Deficit = 42 × [(158/140) − 1] = 42 × 0.129 = 5.4L. Distribute over 48h (max 10-12 mmol/L/day fall).
Desmopressin (DDAVP) for central DI
Established central DI: individualised plan from endocrinology — not started in the community for a newly suspected case. Intranasal: desmopressin 10-20 mcg (1-2 puffs) once or twice daily — most common route for long-term central DI. Oral: 100-200 mcg TDS (oral bioavailability well below 1% — higher doses needed). Sublingual (lyophilisate): 60-120 mcg three times daily — increasing use for both DI and nocturia. IV/IM/SC: inpatient protocol only (Society for Endocrinology: 1–2 micrograms IV/IM in known cranial DI with excessive, inappropriately dilute urine output; further doses only when dilute polyuria returns, to prevent over-correction). Monitoring: serum sodium + urine osmolality at 1-2 weeks after dose change. Risk of hyponatraemia with over-treatment. Patients should know how to recognise breakthrough polyuria and hyponatraemia, but any dose omission or delay should follow the written specialist plan. Fluid intake guidance: do not over-drink while on desmopressin — hyponatraemia risk.
Amiloride for nephrogenic DI (lithium-induced) — specialist-initiated
Decision with psychiatry, endocrinology or nephrology; specialist initiation in significant renal disease. Amiloride 5-10 mg OD (specialist dose): potassium-sparing diuretic that blocks ENaC in the collecting duct and reduces urine output in lithium NDI. Before starting, check eGFR and potassium; warn about ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs, trimethoprim and potassium supplements (hyperkalaemia). Repeat U&Es after initiation and after each dose change. Consider lithium dose reduction with the specialist. Do not combine amiloride and a thiazide routinely without specialist oversight — thiazides reduce urine volume but increase lithium concentrations and toxicity risk.
The desmopressin hyponatraemia risk is a critical prescribing safety point that must be communicated to every patient on desmopressin for central DI — desmopressin is a synthetic analogue of ADH that maximally stimulates V2 receptors in the renal collecting duct, causing maximal urinary concentration and water retention. If the patient drinks ad libitum with unrestricted fluid intake while on desmopressin, they will over-retain water and develop hyponatraemia. The safety instruction: patients on desmopressin must: (1) take the lowest effective dose; (2) not over-drink; (3) follow their individualised written plan from endocrinology, including how to recognise breakthrough polyuria and whether any dose delay is permitted; and (4) have serum sodium checked after any dose change, illness, or change in fluid intake. Hyponatraemia from desmopressin overdose in central DI patients presents with headache, nausea, seizures, and confusion — identical to SIADH — and is treated by stopping desmopressin and fluid restriction, with specialist advice. Conversely, vomiting, nil-by-mouth status, impaired consciousness and inability to access water are emergencies — omitted desmopressin or impaired water access can cause life-threatening hypernatraemia.

Specific Causes — HHS, Hypercalcaemia DI & Infant Hypernatraemia

Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)
Hospital management — shown for context, not a GP treatment algorithm. HHS is a medical emergency requiring hospital admission; follow the current JBDS HHS guideline. If significant ketonaemia/acidosis is present (mixed DKA/HHS), use the DKA pathway. Features: marked hyperglycaemia (>30 mmol/L), severe dehydration (serum osmolality >320 mOsm/kg), sodium often raised (not a defining feature), little or no significant ketoacidosis. Management (JBDS guideline): IV 0.9% NaCl initially (despite hypernatraemia — the volume depletion priority outweighs the hypernatraemia initially); switch to 0.45% NaCl only if the osmolality stops falling despite adequate 0.9% NaCl replacement; insulin (0.05 units/kg/h) only once the glucose stops falling with fluids alone, unless there is significant ketonaemia (JBDS); prophylactic LMWH unless contraindicated, per the inpatient protocol (VTE risk from hyperviscosity). Target: osmolality fall of 3-8 mOsm/kg/h; glucose fall no faster than about 5 mmol/L/h; sodium fall <10 mmol/L/day. Do not switch fluids solely because sodium rises while glucose is falling — monitor calculated osmolality and fluid balance.
Hypercalcaemia-induced nephrogenic DI
Mechanism: hypercalcaemia activates calcium-sensing receptors (CaSR) in the thick ascending limb of Henle and in principal cells of the collecting duct, reducing AQP2 expression and blocking the medullary concentrating gradient. Result: nephrogenic DI + polyuria. Correct hypercalcaemia (see hypercalcaemia algorithm): monitored IV 0.9% NaCl and, where indicated, IV bisphosphonate — hospital decisions; a loop diuretic is not calcium-lowering treatment and is reserved for fluid overload (Society for Endocrinology emergency guidance, 2016). DI usually resolves as calcium normalises.
Infant hypernatraemia — special considerations
Breast milk-fed neonates: hypernatraemic dehydration from inadequate breastfeeding (poor latching, insufficient milk supply) — blood sodium up to 165-180 mmol/L possible before diagnosis. Weight loss >10% of birth weight + jaundice + irritability. Paediatric hypernatraemic dehydration requires admission and paediatric specialist management. Replace the deficit slowly, usually over 48 hours, aiming for a sodium fall not exceeding 0.5 mmol/L/hour or 12 mmol/L in 24 hours (NICE NG29); isotonic fluid initially when IV treatment is required; electrolytes every 4–6 hours initially, or more frequently if clinically indicated. Fluid requirements depend on weight, deficit, maintenance, ongoing losses, renal function, glucose and the sodium trajectory — no universal fixed-rate regimen. Risk of: cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, subdural haemorrhage, permanent neurological damage from rapid correction (cerebral oedema). Urgent expert paediatric advice and admission.
The JBDS HHS management protocol represents current best practice for a condition with historically high mortality — the key departure from classical teaching is that insulin infusion should be DELAYED in HHS until adequate fluid resuscitation is underway and the glucose is no longer falling with fluids alone (unless there is significant ketonaemia). The reason: in HHS, the high glucose is maintaining intravascular osmolality and volume — rapid insulin administration before adequate fluid replacement causes glucose (and therefore osmolality) to fall precipitously, drawing water back intracellularly and causing circulatory collapse. Equally important: the initial fluid of choice for HHS is 0.9% NaCl despite the hypernatraemia — the priority is restoring circulating volume and avoiding further osmotic instability. The switch to 0.45% NaCl is made only if the osmolality is no longer falling despite adequate 0.9% NaCl replacement. This is counterintuitive for trainees who naturally want to give hypotonic fluid for hypernatraemia — understanding the two-phase HHS protocol prevents this management error.

Lifestyle

Hydration, Monitoring & Long-Term DI Management

Hydration in vulnerable populations Elderly (especially care home residents): institutionalised elderly have the highest risk of hypernatraemia from inadequate fluid intake — impaired thirst mechanism (reduced hypothalamic osmoreceptor sensitivity with ageing), cognitive impairment (forgetting to drink), swallowing difficulty, physical inability to access fluids, diuretic use. Each resident should have a documented daily fluid target, with intake monitored. Meeting hydration needs is a legal standard for registered care providers (Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, regulation 14). Dehydration warning signs: concentrated urine, confusion, constipation, dry mouth, weight loss.
Fluid management for DI patients Patients with central or nephrogenic DI should always carry clear written instructions: daily fluid requirements (may be 3-10L in severe DI), an individualised desmopressin plan from endocrinology (including what to do during illness), sodium monitoring frequency, and when to seek emergency care. MedicAlert bracelet: recommended for central DI (particularly post-pituitary surgery) — if patient is unconscious and cannot communicate DI diagnosis, inappropriate isotonic fluid resuscitation will cause hypernatraemia worsening. Emergency card: available via Pituitary Foundation (pituitary.org.uk).
Desmopressin travel and sick-day rules Travel: carry desmopressin in hand luggage (not checked luggage — risk of loss). Keep at room temperature (most formulations stable for 4-6 weeks at room temperature). Hot climates increase insensible losses → may need increased desmopressin dose or extra fluid intake. Sick day rules: vomiting, nil-by-mouth status, impaired consciousness and inability to access water are emergencies — seek urgent hospital assessment; parenteral desmopressin, IV fluids and sodium monitoring follow the written specialist plan/inpatient protocol. Contact endocrinology if confusion develops.
Lithium monitoring for nephrogenic DI prevention Follow NICE CG185 and the local GMMMG lithium shared-care protocol: 12-hour lithium level every 3 months during the first year, then every 6 months (every 3 months in higher-risk patients); U&Es/eGFR, calcium and thyroid function at least every 6 months, with additional monitoring during illness, dehydration, interacting-drug changes or renal decline. The target level depends on indication, treatment phase, age and specialist plan — toxicity can occur within the nominal therapeutic range. Urine specific gravity + 24h urine volume if polyuria reported. If urine specific gravity <1.005 consistently: formal assessment for nephrogenic DI (urine osmolality during polyuric period). Lowest effective lithium level reduces NDI risk; amiloride is a specialist decision.
Preventing hospital-acquired hypernatraemia Hospital-acquired hypernatraemia (sodium rising from normal to >145 during admission) is associated with increased mortality. Causes: inadequate prescription of free water (excessive use of 0.9% NaCl without free water maintenance), nil-by-mouth orders without IV water replacement, hyperglycaemia, fever, inadequate monitoring. Prevention: prescribe daily fluid balance including free water maintenance alongside IV crystalloids; monitor sodium every 24-48h in high-risk patients (elderly, ITU, patients on diuretics, patients with altered consciousness, patients on NG feeding).
Hypercalcaemia and DI — patient education Patients with known hypercalcaemia (primary hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, sarcoidosis) are at risk of developing polyuria from nephrogenic DI as calcium levels rise. Educate: if experiencing increased thirst and urine frequency, check calcium + renal function urgently. Adequate hydration is important in hypercalcaemia to prevent nephrocalcinosis. Adjusted calcium above 3.5 mmol/L, or any level with confusion, drowsiness, persistent vomiting or severe dehydration → emergency admission (999); 3.0–3.5 mmol/L → same-day assessment (see hypercalcaemia algorithm).
Breastfeeding and neonatal hypernatraemia prevention Suspected neonatal hypernatraemic dehydration requires urgent paediatric/neonatal assessment. Warning features: poor milk transfer, inadequate intake, excessive weight loss, jaundice, irritability and low urine or stool output. Assess feeding, latch, milk transfer, weight trajectory, jaundice, urine and stool output, following local infant-feeding and weighing schedules. Provide skilled breastfeeding support (including lactation consultant referral) and use expressed breast milk or supplementary formula where clinically necessary; supplementation is a treatment to prevent harm, not a failure of breastfeeding.
Diet and sodium balance High-sodium diet worsens water losses from the kidney (osmotic load requires more free water to excrete) — relevant in patients with DI where sodium restriction reduces urine volume. Low-sodium diet in nephrogenic DI: reduces the obligatory urine volume by decreasing the renal solute load. Practical: no added salt, avoid processed food (most dietary sodium in the UK comes from processed food). Any sodium-restriction target or thiazide use in nephrogenic DI should be set by the specialist team (thiazides raise lithium levels).
The dehydration assessment in elderly care home residents is one of the most common and practically impactful primary care interventions — water-loss dehydration is common in care home residents (about 1 in 5 in the UK DRIE study) and common clinical signs do not reliably detect it. The consequences: impaired cognition, increased falls risk, constipation, urinary tract infection susceptibility, pressure sore development, and acute kidney injury vulnerability. CQC regulation 14 (nutrition and hydration) provide structured approaches for care homes. GPs conducting care home visits or reviewing care home residents should check: whether the resident has a documented daily fluid intake target, whether fluid intake is monitored, whether thickened fluids are offered where needed for dysphagia, and whether medications that increase water loss (diuretics, laxatives) are being managed in the context of adequate fluid replacement.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Sodium correction monitoring
During IV correction: sodium every 2-4 hours (hospital). Calculate correction rate: target fall no more than about 0.5 mmol/L/h and 10–12 mmol/L/day (chronic or unknown duration). If correcting too fast: slow infusion rate, consider small IV 0.9% NaCl bolus to slow the rate of sodium fall. If neurological deterioration during correction (paradoxical worsening = cerebral oedema from overcorrection): stop hypotonic fluids, consider hypertonic saline 3% (specialist/ICU decision).
Diabetes insipidus outpatient monitoring
After desmopressin initiation: serum sodium + urine specific gravity at 2 weeks. Stable DI: sodium + urine osmolality every 3-6 months. Watch for: hyponatraemia (over-treatment), breakthrough polyuria (under-dosing or dose timing). Annual renal function (nephrogenic DI + CKD assessment).
Lithium DI monitoring
12-hour lithium level every 3 months in year 1, then every 6 months (3-monthly if higher risk); U&Es/eGFR, calcium and thyroid function at least every 6 months (NICE CG185, GMMMG shared care). If polyuria confirmed: 24h urine volume + urine osmolality and specialist advice. If a specialist starts amiloride: U&Es and potassium after initiation and after each dose change.
Community hypernatraemia safety-net
Mild hypernatraemia (146–149) managed initially at home only if clinically well, cognitively intact, the cause is clear and a named person will provide drinks: urgent repeat at an interval set by the local laboratory and clinical context. Same-day medical review if the result is new, persistent, rising, unexplained or associated with polyuria, or the patient is elderly, frail or unable to ensure adequate intake. If the patient deteriorates (new confusion, reduced consciousness, seizure, inability to drink) before review: 999. Remote or out of hours: hydration, cognition and access to drinks cannot be judged by phone — a raised sodium with confusion or drowsiness, vomiting, reduced drinking, or a patient who lives alone or depends on carers needs face-to-face assessment the same day. Tell the patient and carer: call 999 for new confusion, drowsiness, a fit, collapse or being unable to drink; contact the practice or NHS 111 the same day for vomiting, drinking less, passing much less urine, or passing large volumes of urine with thirst.

Same-day medical
Sodium 150–159 (most patients) · Sodium 146–149 if elderly/frail, symptomatic, polyuric, unable to drink or cause unclear · Dehydration with acute kidney injury · Possible neglect in a dependent adult: adult safeguarding concern the same day, alongside treatment · Suspected central DI (polyuria + dilute urine + rising sodium) — discuss with endocrinology; no unsupervised water deprivation testing
The central DI triphasic response after pituitary or hypothalamic surgery is a specific clinical pattern that GPs whose patients have had trans-sphenoidal pituitary surgery must know — Phase 1 (days 1-3): acute DI from surgical trauma and oedema disrupting ADH release from the neurohypophysis — polyuria + hypernatraemia requires desmopressin. Phase 2 (days 4-7): SIADH — damaged neurons release stored ADH uncontrolled — water retention + hyponatraemia. Stop desmopressin during this phase. Phase 3 (permanent): if the ADH-producing neurons are permanently damaged, permanent DI — restart desmopressin. If neurons recover, DI resolves. The risk of unrecognised Phase 2 SIADH while continuing desmopressin is severe hyponatraemia. Post-operative endocrine monitoring is specialist-led but GPs should be aware of this pattern when patients present post-operatively with sodium abnormalities.
