1
Safety

Red Flag Exclusion — Rule Out Life-Threatening Causes First

Gynaecomastia is usually benign but must exclude male breast cancer, testicular malignancy, systemic disease and endocrine emergencies. Act before reassurance.

Hard, irregular, unilateral breast mass Fixed, non-tender, skin tethering / nipple retraction → breast suspected cancer pathway referral — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): aged 30 and over with an unexplained breast lump, with or without pain; applies to men. Do not delay referral for blood tests or breast imaging
Nipple discharge / change Unilateral nipple discharge, retraction or other concerning nipple change in a man aged 50 and over → suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12). Spontaneous unilateral bloody discharge or clinically suspicious nipple/skin changes → urgent specialist discussion irrespective of age; exclude Paget's disease / carcinoma
Testicular mass / pain Acute severe testicular pain (especially with nausea, high-riding testis or abnormal lie) → immediate emergency assessment for torsion. Painless testicular mass or change in size/shape/texture → urgent testicular USS + urology referral via the local suspected cancer pathway; do not wait for tumour markers. A painful mass may be epididymo-orchitis, torsion or trauma
Rapid onset + systemic symptoms Weight loss, drenching sweats, lymphadenopathy → Urgent bloods + CT; exclude lymphoma, lung cancer (paraneoplastic)
Signs of liver failure Jaundice, spider naevi, caput medusae, asterixis → Same-day medical review; hepatic failure reduces oestrogen clearance
Severe hypogonadism features Kallmann-like picture: absent puberty, anosmia, eunuchoid proportions → Urgent endocrinology; may mask serious pituitary pathology
Galactorrhoea True milky nipple discharge + gynaecomastia → Check prolactin (repeat if mildly raised; consider stress, venepuncture, medication, hypothyroidism, macroprolactin); urgent endocrine discussion if markedly raised or with headache / visual field symptoms; exclude prolactinoma, antipsychotics, pituitary macro-adenoma
New gynaecomastia + essential drugs Spironolactone, digoxin, amiodarone or anti-androgen therapy → Do NOT stop without discussing the indication and alternatives with the prescribing specialist

Male breast cancer is rare but gynaecomastia is a recognised presentation — delay is common because GPs and patients dismiss male breast symptoms. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) recommends a suspected cancer pathway referral for anyone aged 30 and over with an unexplained breast lump — men included — and for unilateral nipple discharge, retraction or other concerning nipple change at 50 or over. Investigation before referral is not required when breast cancer is suspected.

Testicular tumours (hCG-secreting germ cell tumours, and oestrogen-secreting Leydig or Sertoli cell tumours) and lung carcinomas secrete hCG or oestrogens, causing gynaecomastia before the primary tumour is detected. Missing this means missing a curable cancer.

Hepatic failure, renal failure, hyperthyroidism and pituitary pathology all disrupt sex-hormone binding globulin and peripheral aromatisation — gynaecomastia may be the presenting feature of these serious systemic conditions.

2
Diagnose

Confirm Diagnosis — True Gynaecomastia vs Pseudogynaecomastia

First, establish whether this is true glandular gynaecomastia or adipose tissue (pseudogynaecomastia). Distinguish with the pinch test and focused history.

Pinch test
Grasp breast between thumb and forefinger and slide fingers toward nipple. True gynaecomastia: a firm/rubbery, mobile, subareolar glandular disc, usually concentric with the nipple (no size threshold is diagnostic; early or pubertal discs may be small). Pseudogynaecomastia: predominantly soft adipose tissue without a discrete glandular disc; fingers meet without resistance
Duration
Ask onset: <6 months = active proliferative phase (potentially reversible) · >12 months = likely fibrosis / hyalinisation (less likely to regress)
Tenderness
Tender gynaecomastia = active inflammation (pubertal, drug-induced, hypogonadism) · Non-tender, hard = suspect malignancy or long-standing fibrosis
Laterality
Bilateral = usually physiological, drug-induced or systemic · Unilateral gynaecomastia can occur, especially early, and is not automatically malignant · Suspicion is driven by an eccentric, hard, fixed or irregular lesion, nipple/skin changes or lymphadenopathy
Age pattern
3 physiological peaks: neonatal (maternal oestrogens), pubertal (age 13–14, resolves in 18 months), older adult (50s–70s, rising SHBG + falling testosterone)
Presentation type
Pubertal: typical, no red flags → reassure and observe, no tests · Drug-induced: clear link to a causative medicine, no atypical features → causality review, no routine tests · Adult (cause unclear): recent, marked, progressive or atypical → selective tests (Step 5)
Drug history
Detailed medication review including prescribed, OTC, anabolic steroids, recreational drugs, herbal remedies. See Step 3 for full drug list

Many men with apparent gynaecomastia actually have pseudogynaecomastia (lipomastia) — particularly relevant in obesity. The management differs completely: pseudogynaecomastia is addressed through weight management; true gynaecomastia requires cause-finding and targeted treatment.

Duration is critical for prognosis: active-phase gynaecomastia (<6 months) can regress spontaneously or with drug withdrawal. After 12 months, stromal fibrosis means medical treatment is unlikely to work and surgical referral may be the only option if distressing.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Identify Underlying Cause / Aetiology

Classify by aetiology — drives all subsequent management decisions. Common causes include physiological pubertal or age-related gynaecomastia, medication or anabolic-steroid exposure, obesity/pseudogynaecomastia, hypogonadism, liver or renal disease, and hyperthyroidism. Tumours are uncommon but must be considered when there are red flags or an atypical presentation.

Physiological
Neonatal: maternal oestrogen exposure, resolves in weeks · Pubertal: age 10–17, transient oestrogen:androgen imbalance, resolves 18–24 months · Senescent: age >50, falling testosterone, rising SHBG, increased adipose aromatisation
Drug-induced
Well-recognised: Spironolactone, Anti-androgens (bicalutamide, flutamide), Oestrogens/DES, Anabolic steroids (post-cycle), Finasteride/Dutasteride, Cimetidine, Ketoconazole, Digoxin
Less certain association: Omeprazole, Amlodipine, Metronidazole, Verapamil, Risperidone, Haloperidol, Tricyclics, Metoclopramide — not equivalent to the list above
Recreational: Alcohol (via liver disease) · Cannabis, heroin, amphetamines — associations reported but uncertain
Causality review: temporal relationship · dose and duration · pharmacological plausibility · alternative causes · whether the medicine can safely be stopped or changed
Hypogonadism
Primary (hypergonadotrophic): Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), orchitis, trauma, chemotherapy · Secondary (hypogonadotrophic): hypopituitarism, Kallmann syndrome, haemochromatosis, obesity
Hyperthyroidism
Increased SHBG reduces free testosterone · Free oestrogen relatively elevated · Treat hyperthyroidism → gynaecomastia often resolves
Liver disease
Cirrhosis: impaired oestrogen metabolism + SHBG elevation · Portal hypertension → adrenal dysfunction
Renal failure
Chronic kidney disease: elevated LH/FSH, reduced testosterone production, hyperprolactinaemia from reduced clearance
Tumour-related Exclude first
Leydig/Sertoli cell tumours: secrete oestrogens directly · Adrenal tumours: excess oestrogen/DHEA · Lung, gastric, renal carcinoma: ectopic hCG production · Lymphoma: rare
Idiopathic
No cause found after appropriate, selective assessment · Diagnosis of exclusion · Often represents subtle subclinical endocrine shifts or unrecognised drug exposure

The cause determines management: drug-induced gynaecomastia → stop/switch the drug; hypogonadism → endocrinology/andrology assessment; hyperthyroidism → treatment via the endocrine pathway (NICE NG145); tumour → urgent specialist referral. Getting the aetiology right prevents unnecessary drug treatment with aromatase inhibitors or SERMs.

Klinefelter syndrome (1:600 males) is frequently undiagnosed into adulthood. Gynaecomastia is the most common presenting feature. These patients also have 20–50× increased risk of male breast cancer — important cascade of discoveries from one GP consultation.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Systematic Physical Assessment

Full examination is essential to identify systemic causes and exclude malignancy. Always examine genitalia in males with gynaecomastia (with chaperone).

Breast examination
Palpate bilaterally · Note: size, symmetry, texture (soft/firm/hard), tenderness, skin changes · Subareolar glandular disc = true gynaecomastia · Hard, irregular, fixed, skin tethering, nipple retraction → Suspected cancer referral
Nipple inspection
Discharge (express gently) · Paget's changes · Retraction or inversion · Ulceration → unilateral discharge, retraction or other concerning nipple change aged 50 and over = suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12); spontaneous unilateral bloody discharge, ulceration or suspicious nipple/skin change = urgent specialist discussion at any age
Axillary lymph nodes
Palpate axillae bilaterally · Hard, matted, fixed nodes + breast mass = strongly suspicious for malignancy · Soft, mobile = reactive (common in obesity, skin conditions)
Testicular examination
Size, symmetry, consistency · Normal adult testis: 15–25 mL · Small, firm testes in young male → Klinefelter syndrome · Painless mass or change in shape/texture → urgent USS + urology (suspected cancer pathway) · Acute severe pain → emergency torsion assessment
Body habitus / virilisation
Assess muscle mass, fat distribution, body hair (axillary, pubic, facial), penile development · Eunuchoid proportions = hypogonadism · Excess central adiposity = peripheral aromatisation source
Signs of liver disease
Jaundice, spider naevi, palmar erythema, Dupuytren's, hepatosplenomegaly, testicular atrophy, loss of axillary/pubic hair = cirrhosis
Thyroid
Palpate for goitre · Check for tremor, lid lag, tachycardia, warm moist skin → hyperthyroidism screen
Blood pressure + weight
BMI + waist circumference · Obesity: elevated aromatase in adipose → peripheral oestrogen excess · Hypertension may indicate drugs causing gynaecomastia (spironolactone, CCBs)

Testicular examination changes management in a significant minority: small testes in a young man with gynaecomastia and infertility strongly suggests Klinefelter syndrome (requires karyotype + endocrinology referral). A painless testicular nodule with gynaecomastia needs urgent USS and urology referral; a painful mass may instead reflect epididymo-orchitis, torsion or trauma.

Signs of liver disease in a man with gynaecomastia should prompt LFTs, clotting, and USS liver — cirrhosis-related gynaecomastia requires hepatology input, not breast treatment. Similarly, thyroid signs should trigger TFTs before any hormonal investigation.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — Staged Approach to Blood Tests & Imaging

Blood tests are directed by history and examination — there is no universal panel. Many cases need NO tests. Never delay a suspected cancer referral to do tests.

When NOT to investigate
Typical pubertal gynaecomastia without red flags → reassurance only · Typical neonatal gynaecomastia → observe and reassure · Clinically obvious pseudogynaecomastia · Clearly causative medicine with no atypical features → causality review, no tests · Typical age-related gynaecomastia without concerning symptoms
Initial tests Selective
Consider when the cause is unclear, recent, marked, progressive or atypical: morning total testosterone (preferably fasting, not when acutely unwell; repeat a low result before diagnosing hypogonadism) · LH and FSH · Renal function · LFTs · TSH · β-hCG if rapid onset, testicular abnormality or tumour concern · Prolactin if galactorrhoea, pituitary symptoms, hypogonadism or relevant medication · Oestradiol if marked gynaecomastia, rapid progression or suspected oestrogen-secreting tumour
Second-line bloods
If LH/FSH elevated: Karyotype (exclude Klinefelter) · Consider SHBG and calculated free testosterone where appropriate · Raised β-hCG: AFP and LDH are specialist tumour markers after urology assessment — not used to exclude a testicular tumour · Clearly raised oestradiol or virilisation/feminisation: discuss with endocrinology — adrenal steroid testing and imaging are specialist-directed · Raised prolactin: repeat if mildly raised, consider stress, venepuncture, medication, hypothyroidism and macroprolactin; urgent endocrine discussion if markedly raised or with headache / visual field symptoms — pituitary MRI via endocrinology or the local pituitary pathway, not by a numeric threshold alone
Interpretation: LH/FSH
High LH/FSH + low testosterone = primary hypogonadism (Klinefelter, orchitis) · Low LH/FSH + low testosterone = secondary hypogonadism (pituitary, haemochromatosis) · Low LH/FSH + high oestradiol = oestrogen-secreting tumour
Interpretation: hCG
A raised or unexpected β-hCG requires confirmation and urgent specialist discussion, particularly with a testicular mass, rapid gynaecomastia or other suspicious features · Do not interpret a borderline result in isolation — low-level results can reflect assay interference, heterophile antibodies, pituitary hCG (older men), laboratory variation or exogenous hCG; discuss repeat testing with the laboratory or urology/endocrine team · Causes include testicular germ cell tumour and ectopic hCG from lung, gastric or pancreatic carcinoma
Scrotal USS Targeted
Indicated if: testicular abnormality on examination · β-hCG raised or unexplained · Rapid progression or highly atypical presentation · Strong clinical concern despite a normal examination · Do NOT order routinely in all gynaecomastia
Breast imaging
Mammogram: indicated if hard/irregular lump, skin changes, nipple discharge (as part of the suspected cancer pathway referral — secondary care will arrange) · NOT routine for typical bilateral soft gynaecomastia · Do not delay a suspected cancer referral to arrange imaging
Bone density (DXA)
Only if hypogonadism confirmed (for fracture risk assessment before testosterone replacement) · Arrange via endocrinology or metabolic bone clinic

Over-investigation of pubertal gynaecomastia is common and counterproductive — it medicalises a normal physiological process, causes unnecessary anxiety, and rarely changes management. Restrict bloods to adults with unexplained gynaecomastia or when systemic disease is suspected.

The testosterone:LH ratio is the key discriminant: primary hypogonadism (testicular failure) gives high LH + low testosterone; secondary (pituitary/hypothalamic) gives low LH + low testosterone. This distinction determines whether to refer to urology vs endocrinology and whether testosterone replacement is appropriate.

Check β-hCG when onset is rapid, there is a testicular abnormality or a tumour is suspected. A raised or unexpected result needs confirmation and urgent specialist discussion rather than interpretation in isolation. Testicular germ cell tumours are among the commonest cancers in young men and are highly curable if caught early.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to Escalate & To Whom

Most gynaecomastia is managed in primary care. Refer when investigation reveals systemic cause, malignancy suspected, or treatment is required beyond GP scope.

999 / A&E
Acute liver failure or severe systemic illness · Acute adrenal crisis · Severe hypercalcaemia (e.g. with confusion) · Pituitary emergency · Acute severe testicular pain → immediate emergency torsion assessment
Same-day
Jaundice or signs of liver decompensation without acute liver failure → urgent medical assessment
Suspected cancer · breast
Aged 30 and over with an unexplained breast lump, with or without pain (NICE NG12, updated April 2026 — applies to men) · Aged 50 and over with unilateral nipple discharge, retraction or other concerning nipple change · Any age: hard, irregular, fixed or eccentric mass, skin tethering, ulceration, Paget's or spontaneous unilateral bloody discharge → urgent specialist discussion · No investigation needed before referral
Suspected cancer · urology
Non-painful testicular enlargement or change in shape/texture (NICE NG12) → urgent USS + urology · Do not wait for tumour markers · Raised or unexplained β-hCG → urgent specialist investigation (not a 999 response)
Urgent Endocrinology 2–4 weeks
Confirmed hypogonadism (primary or secondary) · Klinefelter karyotype · Markedly raised prolactin, or raised prolactin with headache / visual field symptoms · Low LH + low testosterone (pituitary pathology suspected) · Clearly raised oestradiol or virilisation/feminisation · Adrenal tumour on imaging
Routine Endocrinology
Confirmed hypogonadism requiring testosterone replacement assessment · Young male with senescent pattern gynaecomastia (exclude secondary cause) · Recent, painful, persistent gynaecomastia for consideration of specialist tamoxifen (aromatase inhibitors not routinely recommended)
Routine Surgery / Breast
Longstanding fibrotic gynaecomastia · Significant pain · Major functional or psychological distress · Failure or unsuitability of medical treatment · Persistent deformity after the underlying cause has been addressed · Follow the local ICB surgical-funding policy · Must be confirmed true gynaecomastia, not obesity
Primary care management
Physiological pubertal gynaecomastia (observe 18–24 months) · Drug-induced (stop/switch offending drug) · Obesity-related pseudogynaecomastia (lifestyle intervention) · Idiopathic mild / asymptomatic with normal investigations
Specialist-initiated only
Tamoxifen · Testosterone replacement · Definitive hyperthyroidism plan (antithyroid drugs may be started while awaiting specialist assessment, NICE NG145) · Adrenal/pituitary investigation · Surgery · GP receives a written monitoring and re-referral plan if shared care is agreed

NICE NG12 (Suspected cancer: recognition and referral, updated April 2026) applies its breast criteria to men as well as women: an unexplained breast lump at 30 or over is a suspected cancer pathway referral, whatever its other features, as is unilateral nipple discharge, retraction or other concerning change at 50 or over. The barrier to a suspected cancer pathway referral in males must be lower than in females given lower baseline suspicion — this is where diagnostic delay occurs.

NHS England data shows median time from symptom to diagnosis for male breast cancer is significantly longer than female breast cancer. GP referral behaviour is the key modifiable factor. When in doubt, refer on the suspected cancer pathway — breast clinics are equipped to discharge rapidly if benign.

Surgical referral for cosmetic subcutaneous mastectomy has a profound impact on quality of life in men with significant gynaecomastia. Document the impact on quality of life, body image, social functioning and expectations, and follow the local ICB surgical-funding policy; a formal psychiatric or psychological assessment is not mandatory in every case.

7
Treat

Treatment Pathway — Stepwise Management by Cause

Treatment is cause-specific. First-line is always cause removal / drug withdrawal. Medical therapy is only appropriate in the active proliferative phase (<6–12 months). GP: cause removal, reassurance, observation and lifestyle. Specialist-initiated only: tamoxifen, testosterone, definitive thyroid treatment and surgery.

Drug-induced (confirmed)
Stop or switch causative drug First-line
Allow 3–6 months for regression. Do NOT stop spironolactone, digoxin, amiodarone or anti-androgen therapy without discussing the indication and alternatives with the prescribing specialist
Pubertal (physiological)
Watchful waiting First-line
Reassure: most resolve within 18–24 months. No investigation unless persistent >2 years or red flags. 6-monthly review
Obesity / pseudogynaecomastia
Weight management programme First-line
Refer to NHS Tier 2 weight management. Weight loss may improve pseudogynaecomastia and metabolic health, but established glandular tissue may persist. Semaglutide is not a routine primary-care treatment: NICE TA875 restricts it to eligible adults within a specialist multidisciplinary weight-management service (generally BMI ≥35 with a weight-related comorbidity, or BMI 30–34.9 meeting specialist-service criteria)
Hypogonadism (confirmed)
Testosterone replacement Specialist-initiated
Endocrinology/andrology-led, per current SPC and local protocol. Diagnose only in a symptomatic patient with consistently low morning testosterone (repeat before treatment); LH/FSH classify primary vs secondary; consider SHBG and calculated free testosterone. Assess fertility intentions (testosterone suppresses spermatogenesis) and contraindications. Monitoring (haematocrit, PSA/prostate risk, symptoms, testosterone) via the endocrine/urology protocol. Testosterone can worsen gynaecomastia via aromatisation — discuss with the specialist
Hyperthyroidism
Treat underlying hyperthyroidism Specialist-led
Refer to endocrinology (NICE NG145), with a beta-blocker for symptoms while waiting. Antithyroid drugs may be considered while awaiting specialist assessment (NICE NG145), based on biochemical severity; dose per local endocrine protocol and SPC. Before carbimazole: check FBC and LFTs; counsel about agranulocytosis and hepatotoxicity; advise immediate review for fever, sore throat, mouth ulcers, jaundice or dark urine; do not restart after agranulocytosis; consider pregnancy potential and MHRA contraception advice. The specialist plans definitive treatment. Gynaecomastia usually resolves with euthyroid state

Medical therapy for persistent idiopathic/active-phase gynaecomastia — Secondary care initiation only

Step 1Tamoxifen 10–20 mg OD — SERM, specialist-initiated, off-label (document consent) · For persistent, painful, recent-onset gynaecomastia; best evidence in anti-androgen-associated cases · Evidence limited; benefit less likely once fibrosis is established; not for asymptomatic or longstanding fibrotic gynaecomastia · Cautions: VTE, visual symptoms, drug interactions, hepatic disease · Long-term bicalutamide monotherapy: prophylactic breast radiotherapy, then consider tamoxifen if unsuccessful (NICE NG131)
Step 2Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole) — not routinely recommended · Less consistent evidence; not standard treatment for idiopathic gynaecomastia · Specialist decision only, off-label
Step 3Surgical: Subcutaneous mastectomy — for longstanding fibrotic gynaecomastia, significant pain, major functional or psychological distress, failed or unsuitable medical treatment, or persistent deformity after the cause is addressed · Liposuction-assisted or open · Document impact on quality of life, body image, social functioning and expectations; follow local ICB surgical-funding policy
What NOT to do
Do NOT start tamoxifen in primary care without specialist assessment · Do NOT use aromatase inhibitors routinely · Do NOT prescribe testosterone without confirming hypogonadism and specialist review · Do NOT stop spironolactone, digoxin, amiodarone or anti-androgen therapy without discussing with the prescribing specialist · Do NOT make a formal psychological assessment mandatory before every surgical referral

Tamoxifen is an off-label specialist option (licensed in breast cancer treatment), with the best — though limited — evidence in relatively recent, painful gynaecomastia, particularly anti-androgen-associated cases. Aromatase inhibitors have less consistent evidence and are not standard treatment for ordinary idiopathic gynaecomastia.

The critical timing concept: medical treatment only works during active proliferation (ductal epithelial hyperplasia). After fibrosis and hyalinisation (>12 months), no drug reduces breast tissue volume — surgery is the only effective option. This is why accurate assessment of duration is essential at Step 2.

Testosterone replacement in hypogonadal men can paradoxically worsen gynaecomastia initially through peripheral aromatisation of testosterone to oestradiol. Any response to this is specialist-led — adding an aromatase inhibitor is not a routine GP response.

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions — Lifestyle as Active Treatment

Address obesity, alcohol excess, anabolic-steroid use and recreational-drug use where relevant. Explain that weight loss may improve pseudogynaecomastia and metabolic health, but established glandular tissue may persist.

Weight reduction Appropriate for obesity and metabolic risk; may improve pseudogynaecomastia, but may not reverse established glandular gynaecomastia. Refer to NHS Tier 2 weight management service. Semaglutide is not a routine primary-care treatment — NICE TA875 limits it to eligible adults within a specialist multidisciplinary weight-management service (generally BMI ≥35 with a weight-related comorbidity, or BMI 30–34.9 meeting specialist-service criteria).
Alcohol reduction Excess alcohol causes liver disease (reducing oestrogen clearance) and promotes central adiposity. AUDIT screen. Target <14 units/week with 2 alcohol-free days. Offer brief intervention for hazardous drinking. Refer to community alcohol services if dependent.
Stop anabolic steroids / SARMs Anabolic steroids are a common cause of gynaecomastia in men aged 18–40. Suppression of LH/FSH leads to rebound oestrogen excess post-cycle. Provide non-judgmental harm reduction advice. Refer to specialist AAS clinic if available locally.
Recreational drugs Cannabis and other recreational drugs have been linked to gynaecomastia, but a confirmed endocrine mechanism is lacking — do not present cessation as an established treatment. Offer drug-service support as part of general health advice.
Resistance exercise Increases endogenous testosterone and reduces peripheral fat. Prescribe 150 min/week moderate activity + 2× resistance training sessions. Exercise referral scheme via social prescribing link worker if available.
Review herbal supplements Ask about supplements and products with possible oestrogen-like activity (e.g. dong quai, soy isoflavones in excess) as part of the causality review. Evidence that lavender or tea tree oil causes gynaecomastia is limited — not an established general cause.
Psychological support Gynaecomastia causes significant body dysmorphia, social withdrawal, and avoidance of intimacy / sport in adolescents. Offer NHS Talking Therapies referral or CBT for body image concerns if psychological distress is evident. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 at presentation. School referral for adolescents.
Dietary review Healthy balanced diet to support weight management and lean mass. Vitamin D supplementation if deficient. No specific diet or supplement treats glandular gynaecomastia.

In obese men, adipose tissue is the primary source of peripheral oestrogen via aromatase activity — a vicious cycle, as oestrogen also promotes further fat deposition. Weight loss may improve pseudogynaecomastia and metabolic health, but established glandular tissue may persist.

Anabolic steroid use is underreported, so ask directly and non-judgementally. Gynaecomastia from AAS is particularly distressing as it occurs in men who are exercising specifically for physique reasons. Post-cycle gynaecomastia is often severe as LH/FSH suppression leads to rebound oestrogen excess.

Psychological impact is often underappreciated by clinicians. Ask about the impact on mood, body image and social functioning, particularly in adolescents.

9
Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Gynaecomastia needs structured review: to confirm resolution, detect progression, and maintain vigilance for malignancy. Tailor interval to cause and severity.

Pubertal (watchful wait)
Review at 6 months — confirm no change in character (no hardening, no lateralisation) · If resolving: discharge at 18–24 months · If NOT resolving after 24 months: investigate as per adult pathway and consider endocrine referral
Drug-induced (drug stopped)
Review at 3 months after drug cessation — measure breast disc size (document in cm) · Significant regression expected by 3–6 months · If no regression by 6 months: investigate fully, consider if drug truly causative
Idiopathic / investigation negative
Review at around 3 months with repeat examination · Repeat bloods only if clinically indicated (e.g. borderline or changing results), not at fixed intervals · Persistent, painful, recent-onset → specialist discussion · Longstanding symptomatic → surgical assessment
On medical treatment (SERM / AI)
Specialist-led, drug- and protocol-specific monitoring · GP should receive a written monitoring and re-referral plan if shared care is agreed · Safety-net for VTE and visual symptoms on tamoxifen
On testosterone replacement
Monitoring (haematocrit, PSA/prostate risk, symptoms, testosterone level) per the endocrine/urology protocol and written shared-care plan · Worsening gynaecomastia → discuss with the specialist
Post-surgical
GP wound check at 1 week · Review at 6 weeks: healing, patient satisfaction · Long-term: document recurrence (recurrence possible if cause not treated, e.g. ongoing AAS use)
Safety-net emergency
Chest pain or sudden breathlessness → emergency assessment irrespective of gynaecomastia (consider PE if on tamoxifen or VTE risk factors) · Acute severe testicular pain → emergency torsion assessment · Jaundice → urgent medical assessment (999 only with acute liver failure or severe systemic illness, e.g. confusion)
Safety-net same-day GP
Breast mass becomes hard, irregular, or fixed at any review · New nipple discharge · Rapid increase in size · New systemic symptoms (weight loss, sweats) · New painless testicular lump · Raised β-hCG → urgent specialist investigation (not 999)
Klinefelter monitoring
Per specialist / shared-care plan: testosterone level · Bone density (DXA) · Metabolic screen (HbA1c, lipids) · Haematology review if polycythaemia on TRT · Breast cancer surveillance per local protocol (higher lifetime risk)

Safety-netting is critical because gynaecomastia is a recurring presentation — the GP may be the only clinician reviewing a man whose gynaecomastia is gradually changing character over years. Documenting size in the notes (in cm, not just "improved" or "same") allows objective comparison at follow-up and supports timely escalation.

Men with Klinefelter syndrome have 20–50× the risk of breast cancer compared to 46,XY males. While absolute risk remains low (~3% lifetime), this group needs clear long-term monitoring. Many local trusts now have Klinefelter clinics — if available, refer for shared care.

Tamoxifen use carries a small but real VTE risk. If a man is on tamoxifen for gynaecomastia and develops leg swelling or breathlessness, VTE must be excluded. Ensure this is documented in safety-netting advice given to the patient at initiation.
