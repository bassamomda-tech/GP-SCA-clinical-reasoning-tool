1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Organic Causes and Immediate Danger

Screen for immediate risk and for organic causes before assuming a primary psychiatric illness. Keep yourself safe: sit near the door, have a colleague aware, and leave if the situation escalates.
Command hallucinations to harm Voices instructing violence to self or others, with intent or inability to resist → same-day crisis team or duty psychiatrist; 999 if imminent danger
Suicidal intent with a plan Especially with persecutory beliefs, severe distress, recent loss or substance use → do not leave alone; crisis team same day or 999
Risk to others Specific threats, a named person in the delusional system, access to weapons → crisis team and police as needed; consider children in the household (safeguarding)
Fever, headache or confusion Encephalitis (viral or autoimmune) or meningitis — neck stiffness, photophobia, seizures, reduced consciousness → 999
Fluctuating consciousness / disorientation Suggests delirium rather than primary psychosis → find the medical cause (Delirium algorithm)
New seizures or focal signs Weakness, dysphasia, diplopia, abnormal movements → 999 or same-day ED (stroke, lesion, autoimmune encephalitis)
Severe self-neglect Not eating or drinking, unsafe living conditions, catatonic features (mutism, posturing, refusal to eat) → same-day assessment
Intoxication or overdose Stimulants, synthetic cannabinoids, hallucinogens, or a deliberate overdose → same-day ED; toxicology in hospital
Puerperal onset New psychosis in the weeks after childbirth → same-day perinatal mental health or crisis assessment; this is a psychiatric emergency
NG12 Progressive neurological loss Late-onset psychosis with progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function (cognitive or personality change with focal features) → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), rec 1.9.1)
Risk to self and others is the first judgement because it decides where the rest of the assessment happens. A minority of apparent first-episode psychoses have a medical cause — autoimmune encephalitis, CNS infection, metabolic disturbance, a brain lesion or substance intoxication — and features such as fluctuating consciousness, disorientation, seizures, focal signs or fever should make you think organic first. Postpartum psychosis can escalate within hours and carries risk to mother and baby, so it needs same-day specialist assessment. Command hallucinations are not an automatic predictor of action, but combined with intent, distress or previous violence they signal high risk that should never be managed alone in primary care.
2
Diagnose

Confirm Psychotic Features — Core Symptom Screen

Identify positive, negative and disorganised symptoms, the time course, and the effect on function. Ask open questions first ("Has anything strange or puzzling been happening?") before specific ones.
Positive symptoms
Hallucinations (usually auditory — commenting, arguing or commanding voices); delusions (persecutory, reference, grandiose, control); thought insertion, withdrawal or broadcasting; passivity experiences
Negative symptoms
Blunted affect, poverty of speech, loss of motivation, anhedonia, social withdrawal — often precede florid symptoms by months (prodrome) and are easily mistaken for depression
Disorganisation
Formal thought disorder (loosening of associations, tangential speech), odd or disorganised behaviour, incongruous affect
Duration (DSM-5)
Under 1 month → brief psychotic disorder; 1–6 months → schizophreniform disorder; 6 months or more with functional decline → schizophrenia. Referral does not wait for the category
Collateral history
Family or friends, with consent where possible: previous baseline, when the change began, drugs and alcohol, sleep, stressors, any threats or unusual behaviour
Mental state exam
Appearance, behaviour, speech, mood, thought form and content, perception, cognition (orientation, attention), insight, and risk to self, others and from others
Attenuated symptoms
Brief or low-intensity psychotic experiences with distress or decline in function may indicate increased risk of psychosis → refer without delay for specialist assessment (NICE CG178, 2014)
Accurate description of symptoms and time course determines urgency and route, but the diagnostic label is for the specialist team. Brief psychotic episodes can resolve, yet they still need urgent assessment because they can be the start of a longer illness. Negative symptoms and the prodrome (social withdrawal, falling performance at work or college) are frequently missed and often drive long-term disability. When insight is impaired, the collateral account is usually the most reliable source, and it also reveals risk information the patient may not share. Recording the patient's own words for delusions and voices helps the receiving team and allows change to be tracked.
3
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis — Classification

Separate primary psychotic disorders from affective, substance-related and organic causes. More than one can coexist.
Primary psychosis
Schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder → Early Intervention in Psychosis if first episode
Mania with psychosis
Elevated or irritable mood, grandiosity, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, overactivity; delusions usually grandiose → urgent psychiatric assessment (NICE CG185, bipolar disorder, 2014)
Psychotic depression
Severe low mood with mood-congruent delusions (guilt, poverty, illness, nihilism) or derogatory voices; high suicide risk → urgent psychiatric assessment
Substance-induced
Cannabis (especially high-potency), synthetic cannabinoids, stimulants, hallucinogens, ketamine; alcohol withdrawal hallucinosis. Symptoms closely tied to use; may persist beyond intoxication
Medication-induced
Corticosteroids, levodopa and dopamine agonists, anticholinergics, mefloquine, some antiepileptics, isotretinoin (mood and behaviour change)
Organic / secondary
Delirium, dementia (especially Lewy body), epilepsy, head injury, stroke, brain tumour, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency, HIV, syphilis, autoimmune encephalitis, Huntington's disease, Wilson's disease (young people)
Not psychosis
Culturally shared beliefs, overvalued ideas, obsessional thoughts recognised as one's own, PTSD re-experiencing, dissociative experiences, autism-related differences in communication
Treatment diverges by cause: bipolar disorder needs mood stabilisation and has its own guideline (CG185), psychotic depression needs combined treatment and careful suicide-risk management, and organic psychosis needs the medical cause treated. Substance-induced symptoms are common and often overlap with a primary disorder, so a positive drug history does not end the assessment. Early Intervention in Psychosis services should be accessible to anyone with a first episode or first presentation of psychosis, irrespective of age or the duration of untreated psychosis (NICE CG178), so uncertainty about the category is a reason to refer, not to wait. Late-onset psychosis (over about 60) should prompt a particularly careful search for delirium, dementia, sensory loss and medication effects.
4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Physical Causes

A physical examination is part of every new presentation of psychosis and gives the baseline needed before antipsychotic treatment.
Observations
Temperature, pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, capillary glucose — fever or tachycardia with psychosis suggests an organic cause, intoxication or withdrawal
Neurological
Cranial nerves, focal deficits, gait, cerebellar signs, abnormal movements, meningism — any new abnormality → same-day neurology or ED
Cognition
Orientation and attention (4AT if any suspicion of delirium: ≥4 = possible delirium). Disorientation points away from primary psychosis
Eyes
Pupil size (stimulants dilate, opioids constrict), nystagmus (intoxication, Wernicke's), Kayser-Fleischer rings (rare — Wilson's disease)
Thyroid
Goitre, tremor, tachycardia, eye signs — thyrotoxicosis can mimic mania or agitation
Substance signs
Injection marks, nasal damage, smell of alcohol, withdrawal tremor and sweating
Baseline for treatment
Weight (plotted), waist circumference, pulse, BP, and any existing movement disorder — CG178 lists these as baseline measures before an antipsychotic
People with severe mental illness have far more physical illness and die considerably earlier than the general population, mostly from cardiovascular and respiratory disease, and much of this is preventable. A first examination establishes the baseline for antipsychotic monitoring and excludes treatable medical causes. NICE CG178 lists weight, waist circumference, pulse and blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, movement disorders and diet and activity as baseline measures before an antipsychotic is started. A neurological examination matters because encephalitis, CNS tumours and neurodegenerative disease can all present with psychiatric symptoms before obvious neurological signs.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — Exclude Organic Cause and Set a Baseline

Send what you can at the first contact, but do not let tests delay an urgent referral.
Bloods — first-line
FBC U&E LFT TFT CRP Calcium B12 / folate HbA1c or fasting glucose Lipids Prolactin (the last three are CG178 antipsychotic baseline tests)
Infection screen
HIV and syphilis serology where there are risk factors or atypical features; autoimmune antibodies (for example anti-NMDA receptor) are a specialist test if onset is rapid with seizures, movement disorder or reduced consciousness
Urine drug screen
With consent, in first episodes. A positive result does not exclude a primary psychosis; a negative one does not exclude synthetic cannabinoids
Pregnancy test
In anyone who could be pregnant, before an antipsychotic is started and because pregnancy changes the referral route
ECG
Before an antipsychotic if the SPC specifies it, if examination shows cardiovascular risk, or there is a personal history of cardiovascular disease (NICE CG178). A prolonged QTc → discuss with the prescribing psychiatrist
Neuroimaging
Not routine. Indicated for focal signs, seizures, late onset, rapid cognitive decline or atypical features — via the specialist team, or NG12 direct-access MRI for progressive neurological loss
Blood tests exclude common metabolic, endocrine and nutritional causes and form the physical-health baseline that the specialist team needs; sending them from primary care saves time. Prolactin, glucose and lipids are part of the CG178 baseline because antipsychotics differ widely in their effects on each. A drug screen informs the discussion about substance use but does not settle the diagnosis, since substance use and primary psychosis often coexist. The ECG criteria come from CG178: it is not mandatory for everyone, but many antipsychotics prolong the QT interval, and electrolyte disturbance or other QT-prolonging medicines add to the risk.
6
Refer

Referral Pathway — EIP and Urgent Psychiatry

Almost every first presentation of psychosis needs specialist referral. The GP's role is recognition, risk assessment, an organic screen and a fast, well-informed referral.
999 / Emergency
Imminent risk to self or others, organic emergency (encephalitis, seizure, overdose), or unable to assess safely
Same-day
First episode with significant risk, severe distress, poor insight, self-neglect or postpartum onset → crisis resolution and home treatment team or duty psychiatrist (NICE CG178)
EIP — urgent
All first-episode or first-presentation psychosis, any age, regardless of duration (NICE CG178, 2014). Treatment should start in EIP within 2 weeks of referral (NICE QS80, 2015; NHS England access and waiting time standard, 2016). Refer directly to the local EIP team where possible
Specialist assessment
Attenuated or transient psychotic symptoms with distress or decline → refer without delay to a specialist mental health or EIP service (NICE CG178)
Children and young people
Sustained psychotic symptoms for 4 weeks or more → urgent referral to a specialist mental health service; transient or attenuated symptoms → CAMHS or EIP (age 14 and over) without delay (NICE CG155, 2013, updated 2016)
Mental Health Act 1983
If the person refuses assessment and is at risk: request an MHA assessment via the Approved Mental Health Professional (AMHP) service — an application needs two doctors, one section 12 approved, ideally one who knows the patient. Police can use section 136 anywhere except a private dwelling; section 135 needs a warrant to enter premises
Referral content
Symptoms in the patient's words, duration, risk assessment, substance use, physical findings, blood results, medication, safeguarding and carer details
A shorter duration of untreated psychosis is associated with better outcomes, which is why QS80 sets a 2-week standard for starting treatment in EIP and why GPs should refer on suspicion rather than waiting for diagnostic certainty. EIP services provide a full package of medication, psychological therapy, family work, vocational and educational support. CG178 asks that people presenting to EIP are assessed without delay and that those in crisis are referred to crisis resolution and home treatment teams. Knowing the Mental Health Act roles (AMHP, section 12 doctor, and the limits of police powers under sections 135 and 136) lets the GP act quickly and lawfully when a person at risk declines help — a common SCA scenario.

Treat

Pharmacological Management — Antipsychotic Initiation

GP role: do not start an antipsychotic for a first presentation of sustained psychotic symptoms in primary care unless it is done in consultation with a consultant psychiatrist (NICE CG178). Treatment is normally started and supervised by EIP or the CMHT; the GP continues it under shared care.
Choosing an antipsychotic
Shared decision specialist-led
CG178 names no single first-line drug: the choice is made with the person (and carer if they agree), weighing metabolic effects (weight gain, diabetes), extrapyramidal effects (including akathisia), cardiovascular effects (QT), hormonal effects (prolactin) and other unpleasant effects. Doses per BNF, starting low.
Trial and response
Optimum dose for 4–6 weeks
Carry out a trial at optimum dosage for 4–6 weeks, recording indications, expected benefits, side effects and adherence (NICE CG178). Avoid combining regular antipsychotics except briefly when switching.
Acute agitation (bridge)
Oral benzodiazepine advice only
Only on the advice of the crisis team or psychiatrist, short term, dose per BNF. Avoid in respiratory compromise and intoxication with other sedatives.
Treatment-resistant
Clozapine specialist only
Offered when illness has not responded to adequate doses of at least 2 different antipsychotics, at least one a non-clozapine second-generation drug (NICE CG178). Requires registration with a clozapine monitoring service and regular blood counts — never initiated in primary care. Record it on the GP system so interactions, smoking changes and constipation are recognised.
BaselineWeight, waist, pulse, BP, HbA1c or fasting glucose, lipids, prolactin, movement disorders, diet and activity; ECG when indicated (NICE CG178)
Weeks 1–6Weight weekly; watch for akathisia, dystonia, parkinsonism, sedation; ask about adherence
12 weeksWeight, pulse, BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids (NICE CG178)
1 year, then annuallyRepeat the physical health check; primary care monitors at least annually once responsibility transfers (NICE CG178)
Antipsychotics differ more in side effects than in efficacy, which is why CG178 frames the choice as a shared decision rather than naming a preferred drug. Some cause marked weight gain and diabetes risk; others cause more akathisia, prolactin rise or QT prolongation. Starting low reduces extrapyramidal effects, and akathisia is easily mistaken for worsening agitation. A 4–6 week trial at an adequate dose is needed before judging response. Clozapine is the evidence-based option for treatment-resistant schizophrenia; neutropenia monitoring is mandatory and GPs must know the patient is taking it, because constipation, infection and changes in smoking (which alter clozapine levels) can be dangerous.
8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Support — Recovery and Wellbeing

NICE CG178 recommends offering psychological interventions — CBT for psychosis and family intervention — alongside medication for a first episode.
CBT for psychosis One-to-one, at least 16 planned sessions, following a treatment manual (NICE CG178). Accessed via EIP or CMHT psychology
Family intervention At least 10 planned sessions over 3 months to 1 year, with or without other families (NICE CG178; NICE QS80 statement 3)
Cannabis Daily high-potency cannabis use is associated with nearly five-fold odds of psychotic disorder (Di Forti et al., Lancet Psychiatry, 2019). Brief intervention at each contact; drug service referral
Physical health Stop-smoking support, physical activity, diet advice and weight management — antipsychotics add to cardiovascular risk
Carer support Carer's assessment, information about the illness and the care plan, and signposting to Rethink Mental Illness, Mind and local carers' groups
Work and education Supported employment (Individual Placement and Support) via EIP helps people return to work or study
Alcohol and other drugs Non-judgemental approach; dual-diagnosis pathway if substance use is significant
Sleep and routine Regular sleep and daily structure; disrupted sleep is a common early sign of relapse
CG178 places CBT for psychosis and family intervention alongside antipsychotic medication, and QS80 makes family intervention a quality statement. Family intervention helps families understand the illness, reduce conflict and recognise early warning signs. Cannabis is the most important modifiable risk factor for psychosis in young people: the EU-GEI study (Di Forti et al., 2019) found an adjusted odds ratio of about 4.8 for daily high-potency use, and continued use after a first episode is linked with relapse. Smoking is common among people with psychosis and is a major contributor to their excess cardiovascular and respiratory death; offering cessation support is one of the most effective things primary care can do.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring and Safety-Netting

The GP keeps a shared-care role with EIP or the CMHT; physical health monitoring is a core primary-care responsibility.
Within days
Confirm EIP or crisis team contact has happened; if not, chase. Reassess risk at each contact
1–2 weeks
Tolerance of medication, akathisia (inner restlessness, pacing — often mistaken for worsening psychosis), dystonia, sedation, adherence
12 weeks
Weight, pulse, BP, glucose or HbA1c, lipids; mood and suicide risk (post-psychotic depression is common)
Annual
Physical health check at least once a year (NICE CG178): weight/BMI, waist, BP, pulse, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, smoking, alcohol and drugs, CVD risk; also dental, cervical and other screening, and medication review
Relapse plan
Record personal early warning signs with the patient and carer (sleep change, withdrawal, suspiciousness, stopping medication) and who to contact
999 safety-net
Voices telling them to harm themselves or others and they feel they may act, intent to harm, severe agitation, high fever with rigidity or confusion (possible neuroleptic malignant syndrome)
Same-day
Clear deterioration in mental state, carer concerns, stopped medication, acute dystonia, marked restlessness, or no contact yet from the specialist team
Wording
"If things get worse, or you feel you might act on the voices, call 999 or go to A&E. You can call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option at any time, and here is the crisis team's number. I will check on Friday that the early intervention team has been in touch."
Referrals get lost, and people with new psychosis often do not chase them, so a GP check within days closes the loop. Metabolic changes appear early on some antipsychotics, which is why CG178 builds in weekly weights and a 12-week check. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is rare but life-threatening: fever, rigidity, altered consciousness and autonomic instability after starting or increasing an antipsychotic need emergency admission. Written relapse plans allow earlier intervention and fewer admissions. For the SCA, examiners look for a risk assessment that covers self, others and vulnerability, involvement of family with consent, specific safety-netting routes and a named follow-up.
