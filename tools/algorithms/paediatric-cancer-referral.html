1
Safety

The NG12 Action Levels — Know the Exact Timeframes

NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) attaches a specific timeframe to each action. Learn these levels — every feature below maps to one of them. NG12 uses "children" for under-16s and "young people" for 16–24s; young people may be referred through children's or adult services depending on age and local arrangements.

Immediate
An acute admission or referral within a few hours, or sooner if necessary — for the child who may have acute leukaemia, or the child with a new squint and loss of the red reflex.
Very urgent
Appointment or test within 48 hours — the workhorse level in children: very urgent FBC, very urgent specialist appointment, or very urgent direct-access X-ray or ultrasound.
Suspected cancer pathway
The former "2-week-wait" route, now aligned to the Faster Diagnosis Standard (cancer diagnosed or ruled out within 28 days) — e.g. absent fundal ("red") reflex → ophthalmology.
Other action
A specific test, a non-urgent referral, or "consider referral" (such as persistent parental concern), with safety-netting and review.
Unexplained petechiae → Immediate Immediate specialist assessment for leukaemia — do not wait for an FBC.
Unexplained hepatosplenomegaly → Immediate Immediate specialist assessment for leukaemia.
New squint with loss of red reflex → Immediate Immediate referral to ophthalmology (NICE NG127, suspected neurological conditions).
Systemically unwell child Toxic, septic-looking, reduced consciousness, respiratory distress (mediastinal mass), severe pain → emergency admission (999 / A&E), not an outpatient pathway. Petechiae with fever may be meningococcal sepsis as well as leukaemia.
The whole value of NG12 in children is the timeframe attached to each action — "refer" without "within 48 hours" is too slow for diseases that can progress in days. Petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly carry a high enough leukaemia risk that NG12 escalates them past the 48-hour FBC straight to immediate assessment. Notice how much tighter the children's recommendations are than the adult ones: most childhood actions are very urgent (48 hours), where most adult actions are suspected cancer pathway referrals. In October 2023 the NG12 update aligned the suspected cancer pathway with NHS England's Faster Diagnosis Standard, so the old "2-week-wait" label is now shorthand rather than the formal definition.
2
Assess

Focused History and Examination of the Worrying Child

History
Duration and progression; persistent / unexplained features (the NG12 keywords) — morning vomiting or headache, bone pain waking at night, limp, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue, recurrent infection, easy bruising or bleeding; parental concern and the number of prior presentations for the same problem.
Examination — always
Plot growth and (under-2s) head circumference; temperature; full skin (petechiae, bruising, pallor); lymph nodes (site, size, generalised?); abdomen (mass, hepatosplenomegaly — palpate gently and not repeatedly); eyes (red reflex, squint, proptosis, visual behaviour); neuro (gait, coordination, cerebellar signs, cranial nerves, fundi); bones and joints (swelling, focal tenderness).
"Unexplained" = act
NG12 hinges on unexplained and persistent — a feature with no clear benign cause triggers the relevant action level, not reassurance.
Background risk
Down syndrome (higher leukaemia risk), neurofibromatosis type 1, Li–Fraumeni, previous cancer treatment, family history of retinoblastoma, immunodeficiency.
Teenagers and young adults
Young people often present late, alone or after several consultations. Lymphomas, bone sarcomas, testicular germ cell tumours, brain tumours and melanoma all feature in this age group, and for 16–24-year-olds the adult NG12 recommendations (for example testicular change, or an unexplained lump increasing in size) may also apply — refer through the teenage and young adult service your area uses. Ask directly about weight loss, night sweats, lumps and bone pain, and offer time without a parent present.
Safeguarding lens
Unexplained bruising or petechiae raise both leukaemia and non-accidental injury — an FBC and clotting are part of either pathway; do not let one explanation close off the other.
Systemically unwell
A toxic, septic-looking child or one with reduced consciousness needs emergency admission, not an outpatient pathway.
Childhood cancers hide behind common, non-specific symptoms, so the protective step is a complete, structured examination of every worrying child — skin, nodes, abdomen, eyes, bones and neurology — because the discriminating sign (petechiae, an abdominal mass, an absent red reflex, cerebellar signs) is often found only when specifically sought. The words "unexplained" and "persistent" are the NG12 triggers: a feature without a clear benign explanation is what converts a routine review into a timed referral. Background conditions such as Down syndrome or NF1 raise the prior probability, and bruising always needs a dual lens — haematological and safeguarding — because both can be missed if the first explanation is accepted too quickly.

Leukaemia

Leukaemia — Immediate vs Very Urgent FBC (48h)

Leukaemia is the commonest childhood cancer — around 3 in 10 cases in UK children (Cancer Research UK). NG12 splits features into immediate referral vs a very urgent FBC within 48 hours.

Immediate referral
Unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment (don't wait for the FBC).
FBC ≤48h Pallor
→ very urgent FBC within 48 hours.
FBC ≤48h Persistent fatigue
→ FBC within 48h.
FBC ≤48h Unexplained fever
→ FBC within 48h.
FBC ≤48h Unexplained persistent infections
→ FBC within 48h.
FBC ≤48h Generalised lymphadenopathy
→ FBC within 48h.
FBC ≤48h Unexplained bruising or bleeding
→ FBC within 48h.
FBC ≤48h Persistent or unexplained bone pain
→ FBC within 48h (± X-ray, Step 6).
Request a film
Ask for a blood film with the FBC and write "?leukaemia" so the laboratory reviews it the same day.
Acting on the result
Cytopenias, blasts on the film, or a very high or very low white count → phone paediatrics the same day. A normal FBC does not fully exclude leukaemia if concern persists — discuss anyway.
The FBC with film is fast, accessible and high-yield, so NG12 uses it as the 48-hour gate for most leukaemia features. Many of these symptoms overlap with viral illness — the trigger is being unexplained or persistent. Petechiae and hepatosplenomegaly skip the test because their predictive value is high enough to warrant immediate assessment. Bone pain is a classic early leukaemia symptom in young children and is often mistaken for "growing pains" or a limp after minor trauma; night pain, refusal to walk and pallor together should prompt a same-week FBC at the latest. Early leukaemia can have a near-normal count, which is why persisting concern overrides a reassuring result.

Brain / CNS

Brain and CNS Tumours — Very Urgent (48h) · HeadSmart

Brain, spinal and other CNS tumours account for around 2 in 10 childhood cancers (Cancer Research UK) and are the commonest solid tumours. NG12: consider a very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours) for newly abnormal cerebellar or other central neurological function.

Refer ≤48h Newly abnormal neuro exam
New cerebellar signs (ataxia, nystagmus, intention tremor) or other new central neurological abnormality → very urgent referral within 48 hours.
HeadSmart headache features
Persistent headache that wakes the child, or is present on waking; persistent headache in a child under 4; persistent headache with any other brain tumour symptom → CNS imaging (HeadSmart guideline, endorsed by RCPCH).
HeadSmart other features
Persistent nausea or vomiting (more than 2 weeks counts as persistent), especially on waking; visual symptoms (reduced acuity, new squint, abnormal eye movements, papilloedema); motor symptoms (abnormal gait, balance or coordination); increasing head circumference; growth or endocrine problems; behavioural change.
By age
Infants: increasing head circumference, bulging fontanelle, developmental regression, vomiting. School-age: headache, visual problems, clumsiness, falling school performance.
Action
Low threshold — discuss or refer urgently. HeadSmart advises urgent imaging for high-risk presentations and imaging within 4 weeks for lower-risk persistent symptoms.
Childhood brain tumours are notoriously slow to diagnose because early signs are subtle and intermittent. The HeadSmart guideline — developed by the Children's Brain Tumour Research Centre in Nottingham and endorsed by the RCPCH — lists the symptom patterns that should prompt imaging, and its national awareness campaign was associated with faster diagnosis in UK children (Neuro-Oncology 2016). NG12's 48-hour referral for newly abnormal cerebellar or central neurological function exists to compress delay further: a new neurological sign is never "wait and see". Plotting head circumference and growth, and checking eye movements, fundi and gait, are the examination steps most often missed.

Abdominal

Neuroblastoma, Wilms' and Retinoblastoma

Refer ≤48h Abdominal mass / enlarged organ
Palpable abdominal mass or unexplained enlarged abdominal organ → consider a very urgent referral within 48 hours for neuroblastoma or Wilms' tumour. Palpate gently; do not repeatedly palpate.
Refer ≤48h Visible haematuria
Unexplained visible haematuria → very urgent referral within 48h (Wilms' tumour).
Other neuroblastoma clues
Clinical practice, not NG12 criteria: periorbital bruising or proptosis ("panda eyes" without trauma), bone pain, limp, pallor, irritability, unexplained fever or weight loss in a young child, a hard mass in the neck, or opsoclonus-myoclonus → discuss urgently with paediatrics.
Suspected cancer pathway Absent fundal ("red") reflex
Absent fundal reflex → suspected cancer pathway referral to ophthalmology for retinoblastoma (NG12). The NG12 wording changed in 2024 because the reflex can appear a different colour in children with darker skin.
Immediate New squint + loss of red reflex
New-onset squint with loss of the red reflex in one or both eyes → immediate ophthalmology referral (NICE NG127).
White pupil in photographs
A parent reporting a white or "cat's eye" reflection in flash photos is a leucocoria history — examine the red reflex yourself and act on it.
An abdominal mass in a young child is a Wilms' tumour or neuroblastoma until proven otherwise; examination is gentle because a Wilms' tumour is fragile. Retinoblastoma is the classic absent-red-reflex diagnosis — sight- and life-threatening but highly curable when caught early. NG12 routes an absent fundal reflex through the suspected cancer pathway, while NG127 escalates a new squint with loss of the red reflex to immediate referral because of the risk of rapid progression. Parents often notice the white reflex in photographs before any professional does, which is why a history of an odd eye appearance in pictures deserves a proper look with the ophthalmoscope.

Lymphoma / Sarcoma

Lymphoma and Bone / Soft-Tissue Sarcoma

Refer ≤48h Hodgkin lymphoma
Unexplained lymphadenopathy → consider a very urgent referral within 48 hours. When deciding, take into account fever, night sweats, shortness of breath, pruritus or weight loss.
Refer ≤48h Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
Unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly → consider a very urgent referral within 48 hours, taking the same associated symptoms into account.
Reactive nodes
Clinical practice: small, soft, mobile nodes with a clear recent infection are common — re-examine and measure in a few weeks. Nodes that are hard, fixed, matted, supraclavicular, enlarging, or accompanied by systemic symptoms are not "reactive" until proven.
X-ray ≤48h Bone sarcoma
Unexplained bone swelling or bone pain → consider a very urgent direct-access X-ray within 48 hours (osteosarcoma, Ewing's sarcoma).
Refer ≤48h If X-ray abnormal
X-ray suggests possible bone sarcoma → consider a very urgent referral within 48 hours.
USS ≤48h Soft-tissue sarcoma
Unexplained lump increasing in size → consider a very urgent direct-access ultrasound within 48 hours; very urgent referral (≤48h) if the scan suggests sarcoma, or is uncertain and clinical concern persists.
NG12 does not require systemic symptoms before a child with unexplained lymphadenopathy is referred — fever, night sweats, breathlessness, itch and weight loss are things to weigh, not a threshold to meet. Breathlessness matters especially because a mediastinal mass can compromise the airway, so a child with nodes and new respiratory symptoms needs same-day assessment. "Growing pains" and minor trauma are the traps for bone sarcoma: unexplained or persistent bone pain or swelling earns a 48-hour X-ray, not reassurance, and a normal X-ray does not end the story if pain persists. An enlarging unexplained soft-tissue lump warrants ultrasound within 48 hours; persistent clinical concern overrides an uncertain scan.
7
Investigate

Direct-Access Investigations and Acting on Results

FBC ≤48h
Pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, unexplained persistent infections, generalised lymphadenopathy, persistent or unexplained bone pain, unexplained bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC (with film) within 48 hours for leukaemia.
X-ray ≤48h
Unexplained bone pain or swelling → very urgent direct-access X-ray within 48 hours.
USS ≤48h
Unexplained lump increasing in size → very urgent direct-access ultrasound within 48 hours.
Useful additions
Clinical practice: blood film, reticulocytes, clotting screen if bruising, LDH and urate if leukaemia or lymphoma suspected, urine dipstick for blood; request results are phoned through.
Acting on results
Abnormal FBC (cytopenias, blasts, very high or low white count) → discuss with paediatrics the same day. X-ray or ultrasound suggesting sarcoma → very urgent referral ≤48h. A normal test does not exclude cancer if concern persists — refer anyway.
Don't delay referral
For immediate features (petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly) refer without waiting for any test.
Who follows up?
Name the clinician who will check the result within the 48-hour window, and tell the family how and when they will hear.
NG12 gives primary care direct, fast access to the few tests that matter in children — the FBC with film, and direct-access X-ray and ultrasound — precisely so a child can be triaged within 48 hours without first waiting for a clinic appointment. A very urgent test is only as fast as the person who reads the result, so the safe system names who will act on it and when. The governing safety principle is that a normal result never overrides ongoing clinical or parental concern: the test informs urgency, it does not licence reassurance, and the immediate features bypass testing altogether.

Refer / Safety-net

Parental Concern, Communication and Safety-Netting

Persistent parental concern
NG12: take into account the insight and knowledge of parents and carers, and consider referral if a parent or carer has persistent concern or anxiety about the child's symptoms, even when the symptoms are most likely to have a benign cause.
Repeat presentations
Clinical practice: repeated consultations for the same problem are a signal to step back, re-examine fully and lower the referral threshold — ideally with continuity or a second opinion.
Safety-net actively
Continue to investigate until symptoms are explained or resolved; give specific verbal and written advice on which symptoms warrant urgent re-attendance and the expected timescale.
What "very urgent" means
Explain a 48-hour referral or test to the family, what will happen, and that it is precautionary; document red flags, the action taken, the timeframe and the review plan.
Make the timeframe explicit on the referral
State "immediate", "very urgent (within 48 hours)" or "suspected cancer pathway" on the referral so it is triaged correctly — and phone the on-call paediatrician for immediate and 48-hour referrals.
Talking to the child
Use age-appropriate language; young people aged 16–24 may want to be seen alone for part of the consultation.
SCA phrasing
"I can see you know your child well, and that matters to me. Most children with these symptoms turn out to have something simple, but some of the signs I've found mean I want a blood test within the next two days to be sure. I'll check the result myself and ring you. If new spots or bruises appear, or she becomes more unwell, come straight back or call 111 out of hours."
Parents often detect serious illness early, and NG12 explicitly makes persistent parental concern a reason to consider referral even when the clinician's own assessment is reassuring. Repeated attendance is a recognised feature of delayed diagnosis in children's cancer, so each re-attendance should be treated as a fresh opportunity for a full examination rather than a continuation of the previous impression. Stating the exact NG12 timeframe on the referral (and phoning for the urgent ones) is what ensures the child is actually seen inside the window. In the SCA, validating the parent's knowledge, explaining the plan and its timeframe, and committing to personal follow-up of the result are the behaviours examiners look for.

Almost every NG12 childhood-cancer feature funnels into one of two fast routes — immediate (petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly) or very urgent within 48 hours (almost everything else), with the suspected cancer pathway reserved for an absent fundal reflex. Holding the whole summary in view shows how tight the children's timeframes are compared with adults, and that "very urgent FBC within 48 hours" is by far the commonest single action. The table is a summary for revision; always check the full NG12 wording, and your local children's cancer pathway, before acting on a borderline case.
