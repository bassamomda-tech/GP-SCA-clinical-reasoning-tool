Safety

Red Flags — Severe Immunodeficiency, HIV & Malignancy

Lymphocytes <0.5 × 10⁹/L + recurrent opportunistic infections (PCP, CMV, candida, toxoplasma) Severe lymphopenia with functional immunodeficiency. → Same-day hospital assessment if opportunistic infection is suspected; discuss with immunology/haematology/HIV service. Offer and recommend an HIV test. Lymphocyte subsets (CD4) with specialist advice. In confirmed HIV, CD4 <200 cells/mm³ represents severe immunosuppression and may meet an AIDS surveillance definition, but it is not a diagnosis of HIV or AIDS when HIV status is unknown; a low CD4 without HIV needs investigation for other causes of T-cell deficiency.
Lymphopenia + constitutional symptoms (fever + night sweats + weight loss) + lymphadenopathy or mediastinal mass Haematological malignancy (lymphoma — particularly Hodgkin with lymphopenia as paraneoplastic feature, T-cell lymphoma destroying T-cells). → urgent referral via the local haematology / suspected-cancer pathway (NICE NG12: unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly). Do not delay referral while arranging LDH or CT — imaging is normally arranged by the pathway.
Lymphopenia in a patient started on methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, or biological DMARD Iatrogenic lymphopenia from immunosuppression. Follow the drug-specific Greater Manchester shared-care protocol and review trends in FBC, neutrophils, platelets, renal and liver function. Do not stop treatment solely because of isolated mild lymphopenia; withhold and contact the specialist if there is significant or progressive cytopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia, serious infection, abnormal blood film or a drug-specific threshold is reached. Reactivation of TB, PCP, CMV, and other opportunistic infections is the danger.
New confirmed HIV diagnosis with CD4 <200 cells/mm³ Late HIV presentation with severe immunosuppression. → Same-day hospital assessment if suspected opportunistic infection, severe systemic illness or neurological/respiratory symptoms; otherwise urgent HIV-service review (not necessarily admission solely because of the CD4 count). ART, PCP and other opportunistic-infection prophylaxis are initiated by the HIV team.
Lymphopenia + severe COVID-19 or severe sepsis in hospital Lymphopenia is a reliable marker of severe acute infection (cortisol and cytokine storm deplete lymphocytes) and a poor prognostic indicator. Monitor closely. Secondary infections risk (fungal, bacterial) in prolonged lymphopenia.
Severely unwell + fever + cytopenias + organomegaly, hepatitis, coagulopathy, neurological features or very high ferritin Suspect haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) / macrophage activation syndrome (MAS). Ferritin >10,000 micrograms/L is a major warning sign but is not diagnostic (also seen in severe infection, liver failure, malignancy and other inflammatory states). → Immediate hospital assessment; discuss with haematology/critical care. Do not initiate dexamethasone or etoposide from primary care.
Lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count below the laboratory reference range — thresholds vary with age, laboratory and context) is most commonly a transient, reactive, benign finding — it occurs routinely in: acute viral illness (almost universal in COVID-19, influenza, EBV), bacterial sepsis, trauma and surgery (cortisol release suppresses lymphocytes), prolonged glucocorticoid use, and in response to stress and catecholamines. Clinically significant lymphopenia is usually persistent or progressive on repeat testing, marked, or accompanied by clinical features — duration and severity cut-offs are pragmatic descriptors, not universal referral thresholds. The most important cause to exclude urgently is HIV — HIV specifically infects and destroys CD4+ T-helper lymphocytes, causing progressive lymphopenia and severe immunosuppression (in confirmed HIV, CD4 below 200 cells/mm³ is a commonly used marker of advanced disease). The UK HIV prevalence of approximately 1 in 600 adults means that HIV should be considered in any patient with unexplained persistent lymphopenia, particularly in: men who have sex with men, people from high-prevalence countries (sub-Saharan Africa), people who use intravenous drugs, and sexual contacts of known HIV-positive individuals.

Causes of Lymphopenia — Classification

Infections (most common — usually transient)
Acute viral illness: COVID-19 (60-80% have lymphopenia — correlates with severity), influenza, EBV, CMV, HIV (early seroconversion illness), hepatitis. Bacterial sepsis: cortisol surge + cytokines deplete lymphocytes. Mycobacterial (TB — particularly miliary TB). Fungal (severe systemic fungal infections). These are usually self-resolving within 4-8 weeks — confirm resolution with repeat FBC.
Medications (very common)
Corticosteroids: most common iatrogenic cause — redistribute lymphocytes and lower the measured count, without necessarily causing clinically important T-cell immunodeficiency. Cytotoxic agents (methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine — dose-dependent). Biologicals: rituximab (depletes B cells specifically), anti-TNF agents (modest lymphopenia), mycophenolate. Natalizumab (MS treatment). Radiation therapy (lymph nodes in field).
Autoimmune / inflammatory
SLE (particularly: anti-lymphocyte antibodies directly destroy lymphocytes — correlates with disease activity). Rheumatoid arthritis (Felty syndrome). Sarcoidosis. Coeliac disease. Primary Sjogren syndrome.
Primary immunodeficiency
Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID — primarily an antibody deficiency with low immunoglobulins + recurrent infections; does not necessarily cause lymphopenia). Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID — very low T and B cells — presents in infancy). DiGeorge syndrome (thymic aplasia — T-cell deficiency). Recurrent or severe sinopulmonary infection with low IgG, IgA and/or IgM → clinical immunology referral. CVID diagnosis requires specialist assessment, exclusion of secondary causes and assessment of vaccine responses/B-cell phenotype where appropriate.
Other causes
Lymphoma (T-cell lymphoma destroying T-cells — lymphopenia as part of the disease). Protein-calorie malnutrition (lymphocytes require adequate protein for synthesis). Renal failure (uraemia). Zinc deficiency (consider only in a relevant context — malnutrition, malabsorption, bariatric surgery, chronic diarrhoea or restricted diet). Burns. Thoracic duct disruption (trauma, lymphangiectasia).
Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is the most prevalent primary immunodeficiency in adults, affecting approximately 1 in 25,000, and is frequently not diagnosed until the patient has experienced 5-7 years of recurrent infections. The characteristic presentation: recurrent bacterial sinopulmonary infections (sinusitis, bronchitis, pneumonia) since childhood or young adulthood, often with the same organisms (Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae), combined with hypogammaglobulinaemia (low IgG, IgA, and/or IgM) and poor vaccine responses. Lymphopenia may be present (lymphocytes fail to mature into immunoglobulin-secreting plasma cells). The immunological workup for suspected CVID: serum immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM — all low or markedly reduced), lymphocyte subset analysis (CD19+ B-cells: present but functionally impaired, or reduced; T-cells: variable), and vaccine response (failure to respond to protein or polysaccharide vaccines confirms functional immunodeficiency). GPs should suspect CVID in any patient with more than three episodes of documented bacterial respiratory infection per year despite standard treatment — referral to clinical immunology is appropriate and immunoglobulin replacement therapy is transformative for these patients.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigation Strategy

History
Duration and trend: new, persistent or progressive (review previous FBCs). Associated symptoms: fever, night sweats, weight loss (malignancy/HIV/TB). Infections: recurrent sinopulmonary, opportunistic (PCP, CMV, candida — suggests severe T-cell deficiency). Drug history: steroids (duration, dose), DMARDs, biologicals, antiretrovirals (paradoxically, HIV treatment can initially cause lymphopenia before reconstitution). Sexual history, IVDU (HIV risk). Travel history (TB, leishmaniasis). Autoimmune history.
Examination
Lymphadenopathy (generalised = HIV, lymphoma, EBV; regional = reactive, TB, metastatic). Splenomegaly (EBV, HIV, lymphoma, leishmaniasis). Oral candidiasis (T-cell deficiency). Skin (Kaposi sarcoma in HIV, skin lymphoma, SLE rash). Assess for opportunistic infection signs.
Investigations
Repeat FBC + differential (usually in 4–8 weeks, or sooner according to severity; compare with previous FBCs — new, persistent or progressive?) · Blood film (if persistent, marked, progressive or with other cytopenias) · HIV 4th generation test (offer and recommend where HIV is clinically possible — indicator conditions, epidemiological risk, recurrent/unusual infections, lymphadenopathy, weight loss, mononucleosis-like illness; low threshold in Greater Manchester; pre-test information and consent per local policy) · Immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM) (recurrent bacterial infections, bronchiectasis, chronic sinus disease or suspected antibody deficiency) · Lymphocyte subsets (CD4/CD8/CD19/NK) (only if HIV confirmed or strongly suspected, opportunistic/unusual infections, persistent severe lymphopenia, suspected primary or secondary T-cell immunodeficiency, or on specialist advice) · Directed by history and examination, not routine for every persistent case: EBV + CMV serology · CRP + ESR · ANA (SLE — anti-dsDNA, complement C3/C4 if ANA positive) · LDH · Chest X-ray (TB, lymphoma, sarcoid) · microbiology
Lymphocyte subset analysis (flow cytometry measuring CD4+ T-helper, CD8+ T-suppressor, CD19+ B-cells, and CD56+ NK cells) is the key investigation for characterising the specific type of lymphopenia — the differential diagnosis and management differs fundamentally depending on which lymphocyte subtype is depleted. T-cell deficiency (low CD4): HIV, CVID, DiGeorge, post-BMT, corticosteroid excess — risk of intracellular pathogens (PCP, CMV, toxoplasma, Mycobacteria, fungi). B-cell deficiency (low CD19): rituximab therapy, CVID, XLA (X-linked agammaglobulinaemia) — risk of encapsulated bacteria (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae). NK cell deficiency: associated with specific primary immunodeficiencies — recurrent herpesvirus infections. CD4:CD8 ratio inversion (below 1.0): HIV (CD4 depleted while CD8 rises in response); lymphoma.

Diagnose

Confirmation & Descriptive Severity Bands

Confirm first
Confirm an unexpectedly low absolute lymphocyte count with a repeat FBC and differential, unless the patient is acutely unwell or there are features of significant immunodeficiency, malignancy or marrow disease. Interpret the result alongside the neutrophil count, haemoglobin, platelets, blood film and clinical context. The bands below are practical descriptors, not universal disease classifications — thresholds vary with age, laboratory range, clinical context and persistence.
Mild: 1.0-1.5 × 10⁹/L
Most commonly reactive (viral illness, recent stress, corticosteroids). Repeat FBC after resolution of the precipitant; isolated mild lymphopenia does not usually need an extensive immediate immunology work-up. If persists: offer an HIV test where clinically possible; immunoglobulins if recurrent infections.
Moderate: 0.5-1.0 × 10⁹/L
More likely significant if persistent. Offer an HIV test. Drug review. Blood film. EBV/CMV, immunoglobulins and ANA (SLE) guided by history and examination. Repeat — if persistent or progressive, or with other cytopenias: haematology/immunology referral.
Severe: <0.5 × 10⁹/L
Higher risk of opportunistic infection, particularly with cellular immunodeficiency. Offer an HIV test promptly. Blood film. Lymphocyte subsets with specialist advice. Same-day hospital assessment if fever/sepsis or suspected opportunistic infection; otherwise prompt immunology/haematology discussion. Do not start empirical PCP prophylaxis on the total lymphocyte count alone — a CD4 count cannot be inferred from it.
Transient vs persistent
Transient (<4 weeks, after viral illness or steroid): repeat FBC at 4-8 weeks — expect normalisation. Persistent (without clear cause): targeted work-up guided by history and examination. Duration cut-offs are pragmatic, not universal referral thresholds. Progressive lymphopenia on serial FBCs: urgent haematology.
The PCP prophylaxis threshold decision is an important primary care immunology principle — Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) is an opportunistic infection that occurs when CD4+ T-helper cell counts fall below 200 cells/mm³ (in HIV-positive patients) or when T-cell function is severely impaired by any cause. PCP prophylaxis should be initiated only when the patient meets HIV, haematology, transplant or immunosuppressive-treatment criteria, usually following specialist or local protocol advice. Co-trimoxazole is commonly first line, but dose, duration and alternatives depend on the indication, renal function, blood counts, allergy and concomitant medicines. In HIV, CD4 <200 cells/mm³ is a commonly used threshold for primary PCP prophylaxis, but initiation and discontinuation should be managed with the HIV service. Do not infer a CD4 count from the total lymphocyte count. In non-HIV patients the decision depends on the underlying immunosuppressive regimen, haematological malignancy or transplant status, corticosteroid dose and duration, and additional immunosuppressive drugs. GPs who identify lymphopenia in a patient receiving combination immunosuppression should contact rheumatology or the managing specialist about PCP prophylaxis.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day hospital assessment
Fever or sepsis with severe lymphopenia · Suspected PCP, CMV, disseminated fungal infection, toxoplasmosis or TB · Severe respiratory, neurological or systemic symptoms · Suspected HLH/MAS · Pancytopenia, blasts or major blood-film abnormality · Severe lymphopenia with significant clinical immunodeficiency
HIV service (ideally within 48 hours; certainly within 2 weeks)
Confirmed positive HIV test · Suspected acute HIV · Low CD4 · Opportunistic infection (same day if unwell). ART initiation, baseline resistance testing, opportunistic-infection assessment and prophylaxis are coordinated by the HIV team.
Haematology (suspected cancer pathway)
Progressive/persistent unexplained lymphopenia with other cytopenias, abnormal blood film, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly or constitutional symptoms · Suspected lymphoma (NICE NG12 — do not delay for LDH or CT)
Clinical immunology
Recurrent/unusual infections · Low immunoglobulins (suspected CVID) · Severe persistent lymphopenia · Recurrent opportunistic infections without HIV · Suspected primary immunodeficiency
Rheumatology
Active SLE or inflammatory disease requiring immunosuppression adjustment · Significant or progressive cytopenia on a DMARD (per the drug-specific shared-care protocol)
GP management
Transient reactive lymphopenia post-viral illness: repeat FBC at 4-8 weeks, confirm resolution. Steroid-associated: review indication and dose with the prescriber — do not stop abruptly. Mild persistent without cause: repeat FBC + film, review the trend, offer an HIV test where clinically possible; immunoglobulins, ANA and CXR only if guided by history and examination.
A new positive HIV test requires prompt referral to an HIV service, ideally within 48 hours and certainly within two weeks — GPs who diagnose HIV (or receive a positive result from a patient's home test) should arrange referral for: CD4 count and viral load measurement, HIV drug resistance testing (genotypic resistance testing before ART initiation), vaccination status review (pneumococcal, Hep A/B if not immune, HPV), STI co-testing (syphilis, GC, Chlamydia), and ART initiation by the HIV team (BHIVA guidelines recommend ART for all people diagnosed with HIV regardless of CD4 count). GPs should not simply send a suspected cancer referral letter — the HIV clinic referral should be direct and urgent, with the patient contacted personally to explain the positive result and the importance of early specialist assessment. The 'TasP' principle (treatment as prevention) means that ART initiation reduces plasma viral load to undetectable, making the patient non-infectious to sexual partners — every week of delay in ART initiation is a week of potential ongoing transmission.

Treat

Management by Cause

HIV-associated lymphopenia
Antiretroviral therapy (ART) — HIV-service led
BHIVA: ART is recommended for all people diagnosed with HIV, regardless of CD4. Refer promptly to the HIV service; ART initiation, baseline resistance testing, opportunistic-infection assessment and PCP/toxoplasma/MAC prophylaxis are coordinated by the HIV team. Do not start ART in primary care unless there is a specific local shared pathway and appropriate expertise. CD4 recovery on ART is variable.
Steroid-induced lymphopenia
Review indication and dose with the prescriber
Steroids can redistribute lymphocytes and lower the measured count without necessarily causing clinically important T-cell immunodeficiency. Do not reduce or stop corticosteroids abruptly. Recovery depends on dose, duration, underlying disease and other immunosuppressants. Assess the overall immunosuppressive regimen and infection risk (including any need for PCP prophylaxis, per specialist/local protocol) rather than using the lymphocyte count in isolation.
CVID
Immunoglobulin replacement therapy (specialist-initiated)
IVIg (IV) or SCIg (subcutaneous), dosed by clinical immunology according to infection burden, organ damage and clinical response. Do not prescribe or initiate in primary care unless part of an established specialist shared-care arrangement. GP role: vaccination per the Green Book and immunology advice (live-vaccine decisions depend on the specific immune defect), infection surveillance.
SLE-associated lymphopenia
Hydroxychloroquine + immunosuppression
Hydroxychloroquine 200-400 mg OD (first-line for all SLE). For severe lymphopenia with active disease: mycophenolate or azathioprine (rheumatology). Assess for APS co-existing. Annual FBC to track lymphocyte trends.
Suspected zinc deficiency (selected patients)
Not a generic treatment for lymphopenia
Consider zinc deficiency only in a relevant clinical context (malnutrition, malabsorption, bariatric surgery, chronic diarrhoea, restricted diet). Routine zinc testing and supplementation are not justified for lymphopenia in most adults. Prolonged or high-dose supplementation can cause zinc toxicity and copper deficiency.
IVIg (intravenous immunoglobulin) replacement therapy for CVID is one of the most transformative treatments in clinical immunology — patients with CVID who have had multiple hospitalisations per year for recurrent pneumonia or sinusitis experience dramatic quality-of-life improvement after starting IgG replacement therapy. The mechanism: IVIg provides pooled IgG antibodies from thousands of donors, covering essentially all common pathogens. Dosing is specialist-led and guided by infection burden, organ damage, clinical response and immunology advice rather than a universal trough IgG target. Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIg) is increasingly used as an alternative — weekly or biweekly self-administration at home improves independence and quality of life. GPs should be aware of the distinction: the goal of IgG replacement is not to normalise the serum IgG level (which may remain below normal despite replacement) but to achieve a level that prevents recurrent serious infections.

Infection Prophylaxis & Vaccination — Specialist-Led

PCP prophylaxis
Do not recommend prophylaxis for lymphopenia alone — a total lymphocyte count below 0.5 × 10⁹/L does not automatically justify it. PCP prophylaxis should be initiated only when the patient meets HIV, haematology, transplant or immunosuppressive-treatment criteria, usually following specialist or local protocol advice. Co-trimoxazole is commonly first line, but dose, duration and alternatives depend on the indication, renal function, blood counts, allergy and concomitant medicines. In HIV, CD4 <200 cells/mm³ is a commonly used threshold, but initiation and discontinuation are managed with the HIV service.
Toxoplasma, MAC & other
Toxoplasma, MAC and other opportunistic-infection prophylaxis require HIV-specialist or relevant immunology/haematology advice. Toxoplasma prophylaxis depends on CD4 count and Toxoplasma IgG status (co-trimoxazole may provide dual protection); MAC prophylaxis is situation-specific with effective ART.
Antifungal prophylaxis (severe T-cell deficiency)
Not for uncomplicated isolated lymphopenia and not started in primary care. Fluconazole or posaconazole prophylaxis is used only in selected haematology, transplant and specialist immunodeficiency settings. Treat symptomatic candidiasis (see oral health).
Herpesvirus prophylaxis
Aciclovir prophylaxis is not indicated on CD4 <200 alone — used only in selected haematology, transplant and specialist immunodeficiency settings on specialist advice. VZV-naive + immunosuppressed: varicella ZIG (zoster immunoglobulin) within 96h of exposure. Shingles: use the non-live recombinant vaccine (Shingrix, two doses) where indicated; Zostavax is not a current routine option.
Vaccination in lymphopenia
Do not make live-vaccine decisions from the lymphocyte count alone. Check the Green Book Chapter 6 and consult immunology, HIV or the relevant specialist when there is cellular/combined immunodeficiency, HIV with low CD4, haematological malignancy, transplant status or significant immunosuppressive therapy; some antibody deficiencies do not themselves contraindicate live vaccines. Live influenza vaccine (LAIV), oral typhoid, yellow fever, varicella, MMR and BCG require vaccine-specific assessment. Inactivated vaccines (annual inactivated flu, COVID-19, pneumococcal per Green Book chapter 25, HBV, HAV) are generally safe but may produce a weaker immune response. Timing around rituximab and other B-cell-depleting therapies should be specialist-led. Re-vaccination after ART initiation in HIV (immune reconstitution may allow better vaccine response at CD4 >200).
The recombinant herpes zoster vaccine (Shingrix — GSK) is a significant advance for immunocompromised patients — unlike the live-attenuated zoster vaccine (Zostavax), Shingrix uses recombinant VZV glycoprotein E antigen with an AS01B adjuvant, making it safe in immunocompromised patients who cannot receive live vaccines. Efficacy in immunocompromised populations (HIV positive with CD4 >200, post-BMT, haematological malignancy) is approximately 68-90%, significantly higher than Zostavax's approximately 51% in immunocompetent elderly patients. The NHS has committed to expanded Shingrix access. GPs managing immunocompromised patients (HIV on ART, post-transplant, on long-term immunosuppressive DMARDs, haematological malignancy in remission) should ensure these patients have received two doses of Shingrix 2 months apart. This is one of the most important vaccine quality improvements for immunocompromised patients in recent years.

Lifestyle

Infection Prevention, Support & Patient Education

Infection risk reduction in immunocompromised Avoid crowded indoor spaces during respiratory viral seasons. Handwashing (20 seconds with soap) before meals and after any potential exposure. Avoid raw meat, unpasteurised dairy, raw eggs (listeria, salmonella risk). Avoid contact with people with known infectious illness. Safe food handling. Cat litter avoidance (toxoplasma — particularly relevant if CD4 <100).
HIV positive — living well ART adherence support (once-daily single-tablet regimens are available — prescribed by the HIV service). U=U: a person with HIV who is on effective ART and has a confirmed sustained undetectable viral load does not transmit HIV sexually; this applies when viral suppression is maintained and monitored. Annual sexual health review. Mental health (depression affects 30-40% of HIV-positive patients — PHQ-9). Exercise (maintains CD4 count and quality of life). HIV support organisations: Terrence Higgins Trust (THT), NAM/aidsmap.
CVID patient education CVID is a lifelong condition requiring indefinite IgG replacement. Understand what to look out for: fever, increasing cough, purulent sputum = infection requiring prompt antibiotic treatment (do not wait 7 days as might be advised for a healthy patient). Carry a letter explaining immunodeficiency for emergency presentation. UK Primary Immunodeficiency Network (UKPIN) — primary immunodeficiency patient support. IPOPI (international organisation).
Steroid-related lymphopenia — patient safety Patients on long-term corticosteroids should carry a steroid emergency card. Stress dosing during illness (double prednisolone for minor illness; seek urgent assessment if vomiting preventing oral dosing). Sick day rules for steroid dose adjustment. Annual review of steroid dose necessity.
Oral health in lymphopenia Oral candidiasis is common in T-cell deficiency. Treat according to the current local antimicrobial guideline: topical nystatin or miconazole may be suitable for mild disease; systemic fluconazole may be required for extensive, recurrent or refractory disease, prescribed at the appropriate dose and duration after checking interactions and liver function (a single 150 mg dose is a vaginal candidiasis regimen, not oral). Chlorhexidine mouthwash is not a routine treatment for candidiasis. Recurrent oral candidiasis should prompt assessment for HIV, diabetes, inhaled corticosteroid use, antibiotics, dentures and other immunosuppression; refer to oral medicine if recurrent.
Mental health and chronic illness Chronic immunodeficiency (particularly HIV, CVID, SLE) has significant psychological burden. PHQ-9 + GAD-7 at each annual review. NHS Talking Therapies referral for mild-moderate depression/anxiety. Peer support: condition-specific charities provide community and advocacy. Address stigma explicitly (HIV stigma remains significant).
Pregnancy in immunodeficiency HIV: maintain ART throughout pregnancy (prevent vertical transmission — risk reduced to <0.5% with viral load suppression at delivery). CVID: IgG replacement continues throughout pregnancy (dose may need increase in third trimester). Corticosteroid dose management in pregnancy: endocrinology + obstetric support for Addisonian patients. Immunosuppressive DMARDs: check safety in pregnancy with prescriber before conception.
Travel precautions in lymphopenia Live travel vaccines (yellow fever, oral typhoid, BCG, MMR) require vaccine-specific assessment — check the Green Book and seek specialist advice in cellular/combined immunodeficiency or significant immunosuppression. Travel to malaria-endemic areas requires chemoprophylaxis + insect avoidance. Food safety particularly important (compromised hosts at higher risk from food-borne pathogens). Carry supply of antibiotics for early self-treatment of infection while travelling. Travel health clinic consultation before any international travel.
The U=U (Undetectable = Untransmittable) principle is the most important public health communication in HIV medicine in recent years — multiple high-quality studies (PARTNER, PARTNER2, Opposites Attract) demonstrated that HIV-positive individuals who are on effective ART with a confirmed sustained undetectable viral load (below 200 copies/ml), maintained and monitored, have a zero risk of transmitting HIV to HIV-negative sexual partners through condomless sex. This is not just a statistical claim — the studies included thousands of HIV-negative partners who had unprotected sex with HIV-positive individuals with undetectable viral loads over multiple years, and not a single transmission occurred. This finding has profound implications for: the mental health of HIV-positive individuals (reducing fear of transmission), decisions about disclosure to sexual partners, and the broader public health narrative around HIV. GPs caring for HIV-positive patients should ensure they know about U=U and its implications — this information significantly reduces HIV-related psychological distress and reduces stigma.

Follow-Up & Monitoring

Transient reactive lymphopenia
FBC + differential at 4-8 weeks after acute illness/steroid. Confirm normalisation. If not normalised: blood film, offer an HIV test where clinically possible, and targeted further investigation (immunoglobulins if recurrent infections) guided by history and examination.
HIV on ART
CD4 count and HIV viral load monitoring per the HIV service protocol — frequency depends on ART status, viral suppression, CD4 count, age and comorbidity. ART adherence check. Annual: FBC, LFTs, renal function, lipids, HbA1c (metabolic side effects of some ART), syphilis + sexual health screen. Shared care between HIV clinic and GP.
CVID on IgG replacement
Trough IgG and dosing reviewed by clinical immunology (no universal target level). FBC 6-monthly. Annual: spirometry (pulmonary complications), hepatitis serology, lymph node surveillance (MALT lymphoma risk). Lung function annually (bronchiectasis from recurrent infections).
SLE-associated lymphopenia
Monthly FBC while disease active. CD4 count if lymphocytes <0.5 persistently. ANA + dsDNA + complement at each flare. Annual urinalysis (lupus nephritis). Annual BP.
Same-day / urgent
New opportunistic infection features in lymphopenic patient → hospital urgently · Suspected opportunistic infection, severe systemic illness or neurological/respiratory symptoms → same-day hospital assessment; a stable patient with a new HIV diagnosis and low CD4 → urgent HIV-service review (not necessarily emergency admission solely because of the CD4 count) · CVID patient with suspected pneumonia → antibiotics immediately + hospital if severe
Within 1 week
Persistent unexplained severe lymphopenia → haematology/immunology · Significant or progressive cytopenia on a DMARD (or drug-specific threshold reached) → withhold per shared-care protocol + contact specialist; isolated lymphopenia below the reference range → usually continue, look for other causes, repeat, and discuss with rheumatology if a downward trend · HIV positive + CD4 falling below 200 → HIV service for prophylaxis + ART review
Avoid
Empirical PCP prophylaxis based solely on total lymphocyte count · Routine antifungal or HSV prophylaxis in primary care · Routine CD4 testing for mild isolated lymphopenia · Automatic cessation of DMARDs solely because lymphocytes are below the laboratory reference range · Routine fluconazole 150 mg single-dose treatment for oral candidiasis · Labelling CD4 <200 as an automatic AIDS diagnosis without confirmed HIV infection
The shared care model for HIV between HIV clinic and GP is increasingly important as HIV-positive patients on stable ART live long, healthy lives and their primary care needs (cardiovascular risk, diabetes, cancer screening, mental health) become as important as their HIV management. The GP's specific roles in shared care: annual NHS Health Check (QRISK3 — HIV-positive patients have increased cardiovascular risk from both the virus itself and from some ART medications), cancer screening (HIV-positive patients have higher risk of anal, cervical, and certain other cancers — ensure screening uptake is documented), medication review (drug interactions between ART and common medications are common and clinically significant), mental health review (depression affects 30-40% — PHQ-9 annually), and substance use screening. The GP who knows their HIV-positive patients by name and provides proactive, non-stigmatising primary care significantly improves long-term outcomes for this group.
