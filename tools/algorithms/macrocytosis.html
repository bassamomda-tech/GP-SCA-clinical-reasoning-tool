Safety

Red Flags — Megaloblastic Crisis, Leukaemia & Liver Failure

Macrocytosis + severe symptomatic anaemia ± pancytopenia + hypersegmented neutrophils on film + cardiac failure, syncope, chest pain, hypoxia, confusion or neurological deterioration Severe megaloblastic anaemia (B12 or folate). → Urgent hospital assessment. IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg (hydroxocobalamin is given IM — BNF), with oral folic acid once B12 cover is in place. Red-cell transfusion is an individual clinical decision (symptoms, haemodynamic status, active bleeding, cardiac or respiratory disease, rate of onset, cause, response to treatment); do not withhold it solely because the anaemia is due to B12 or folate deficiency. Hypokalaemia can occur during a brisk erythropoietic response — monitor potassium.
Macrocytosis + blast cells on blood film + bone marrow failure features (pancytopenia) Acute leukaemia (AML — common in elderly) or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). → Immediate discussion with haematology or acute medical services — also for unexplained pancytopenia, rapidly falling counts, severe neutropenia with fever or active bleeding. Bone marrow examination, cytogenetics and molecular testing are specialist investigations.
Macrocytosis + jaundice + hepatomegaly + AST/ALT elevated + heavy alcohol history Alcoholic liver disease or megaloblastic process combined with liver disease. → Urgent LFTs + coagulation screen. INR elevated in liver failure. Hospital if decompensated liver disease (ascites, encephalopathy, variceal bleeding).
Macrocytosis + rapidly progressive neurological symptoms (weakness, sensory loss, unsteady gait) + B12 low or borderline Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord. → Take diagnostic bloods if this does not delay treatment, then start IM hydroxocobalamin urgently — do not delay replacement while awaiting results (NICE NG239). Assess falls, gait, weakness and bladder/bowel symptoms. Urgent medical/neurological assessment if severe, progressive or diagnostically uncertain. MRI spine only if cord compression, demyelination or another neurological diagnosis is in the differential. (Also see B12 deficiency algorithm.)
Macrocytosis in a patient on methotrexate + oral ulcers + marrow suppression Methotrexate toxicity — folate antagonist causing megaloblastic bone marrow suppression. → Withhold methotrexate, arrange urgent FBC, renal and liver tests, assess for sepsis and mucositis, and contact the prescribing specialist or acute medical/haematology team immediately. Folinic-acid rescue should follow the specialist or local toxicology protocol.
Macrocytosis + normocytic red cells + thrombocytopenia + cold agglutinins (blood clumping in sample tube at room temperature) Cold agglutinin disease — autoimmune haemolytic anaemia causing falsely elevated MCV from red cell clumping. → Haematology. Warm sample before analysis. FBC in warm tube.
Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is a diagnosis that requires explicit consideration whenever macrocytosis with unexplained cytopenia (anaemia, neutropenia, or thrombocytopenia) is present in a patient over 60 — it is a clonal bone marrow disorder in which haematopoietic stem cells produce morphologically abnormal cells that fail to mature normally, leading to ineffective erythropoiesis. In MDS the film may show dysplastic neutrophils or abnormal platelets; macro-ovalocytes with hypersegmented neutrophils instead point to B12/folate deficiency. MDS affects approximately 5 per 100,000 adults per year, with median diagnosis age approximately 70. The clinical presentation: fatigue from anaemia (the most common symptom), recurrent infections (neutropenia), and bruising/bleeding (thrombocytopenia). The WHO MDS classification (2022) has multiple subtypes determined by cytogenetics and bone marrow morphology. GPs should refer to haematology any patient with unexplained macrocytic anaemia (particularly with cytopenia, dysplastic features on film, or age over 60) when B12, folate, thyroid, alcohol, and drug causes have been excluded.

Causes of Macrocytosis — Classification

Megaloblastic macrocytosis (impaired DNA synthesis)
Vitamin B12 deficiency (most important — pernicious anaemia, vegan diet, metformin, gastric surgery, ileal disease). Folate deficiency (poor diet, malabsorption, pregnancy, methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin, alcohol — folate stores last only 3-4 months). Combined B12 + folate deficiency (coeliac, bariatric surgery). Drug-induced: methotrexate (DHFR inhibitor), trimethoprim (weak DHFR inhibitor — usually subclinical), phenytoin, azathioprine (6-MP interference with DNA synthesis), hydroxyurea, cytarabine.
Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis
Alcohol (most common cause of macrocytosis in UK adults — direct toxic effect on red cell membrane lipids + folate interference). Liver disease (increased membrane cholesterol deposition on red cells — target cells on film). Hypothyroidism (reduced erythropoiesis rate — large but normal cells). Reticulocytosis (young red cells are large — haemolysis, post-haemorrhage, after iron/B12 treatment). Aplastic anaemia / MDS. Pregnancy (physiological mild macrocytosis). Smoking (mild macrocytosis from CO effects).
MCV classification
MCV 100-110 fl — mild: often alcohol, hypothyroidism or drug effect. MCV 110-120 fl — moderate. MCV >120 fl — marked. These are descriptive bands only. Interpret alongside Hb, WBC, platelets, film, reticulocytes, symptoms, rate of change, liver disease, alcohol and medication history, and B12/folate status. Marked or progressive macrocytosis, particularly with anaemia, other cytopenias, abnormal blood-film findings or no apparent cause, warrants urgent clinical review and possible haematology referral. There is no single MCV threshold that independently diagnoses MDS or mandates same-day referral.
Alcohol is the most common cause of isolated macrocytosis in UK primary care — approximately 60-70% of heavy drinkers develop macrocytosis, and in a patient presenting with incidental MCV elevation without anaemia, alcohol should be the first consideration. The mechanism of alcohol-induced macrocytosis is multifactorial: direct toxic effect of acetaldehyde on developing red cell precursors (acetaldehyde is incorporated into red cell membrane lipids, altering membrane fluidity and shape), secondary folate deficiency (poor diet in heavy drinkers + alcohol-induced folate malabsorption), and direct toxic effect on the bone marrow. The key clinical point: alcohol macrocytosis causes large round macrocytes (without hypersegmented neutrophils — unlike megaloblastic macrocytosis), and the MCV may take several months to fall after abstinence. Persistent macrocytosis despite abstinence should prompt reassessment for other causes. The AUDIT-C at every macrocytosis consultation is essential — many patients under-declare alcohol use.

Investigation Strategy

First-line investigations (all macrocytosis)
Repeat FBC if unexpected (review previous FBCs and trends; confirm MCV is genuinely raised) · FBC + blood film (macro-ovalocytes + hypersegmented neutrophils = megaloblastic; round macrocytes ± target cells = alcohol/liver; dysplastic neutrophils/abnormal platelets = ?MDS) · Serum B12 or active B12 · Serum folate · TSH (hypothyroidism) · LFTs + GGT (alcohol, liver disease) · Reticulocyte count (elevated = haemolysis/post-treatment; reduced = bone marrow failure) · Renal function · Full drug and alcohol history (methotrexate, hydroxyurea, trimethoprim, azathioprine, antiretrovirals; nutrition; bleeding, haemolysis and neurological symptoms). If reticulocytosis or haemolysis suspected: bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, DAT where clinically appropriate. B12 deficiency is not excluded by a normal haemoglobin or normal MCV — neurological B12 deficiency may occur without macrocytosis or anaemia (NICE NG239).
Second-line (if first-line normal)
Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies (if autoimmune gastritis/pernicious anaemia suspected; do not routinely request anti-parietal cell antibodies; a negative result does not fully exclude autoimmune gastritis — specialist assessment may be needed) · Anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (coeliac) · MMA (selected patients with an indeterminate B12 result; affected by renal impairment) · Homocysteine (less specific; affected by folate status and renal function) · AUDIT-C / formal alcohol history (if GGT elevated or clinical suspicion) · Hepatitis B/C serology (if liver disease suspected) · Haematology referral + bone marrow (unexplained macrocytosis + cytopenia after all above excluded)
Blood film findings by cause
Megaloblastic (B12/folate, some antifolate/antimetabolite drugs): macro-ovalocytes, anisopoikilocytosis, hypersegmented neutrophils (5+ lobes in >5% of neutrophils), sometimes thrombocytopenia or neutropenia — prompts assessment for B12/folate deficiency, not alcohol. Alcohol/liver: round macrocytes, target cells, acanthocytes (spur cells in severe liver disease), stomatocytes. MDS: dysplastic neutrophils (e.g. hyposegmented Pelger-Huët-like cells), abnormal platelets, dysplastic forms. Reticulocytosis: polychromasia (large bluish cells — young red cells).
The blood film is the most information-rich investigation in the macrocytosis workup — the morphological findings narrow the differential diagnosis dramatically and guide subsequent targeted investigations. The hypersegmented neutrophil is the single most specific finding for megaloblastic haematopoiesis: any neutrophil with 6 or more lobes, or more than 5% of neutrophils with 5 lobes, constitutes hypersegmentation and strongly suggests B12 or folate deficiency (or antifolate drugs such as methotrexate). No single laboratory test is definitive for B12 deficiency, so interpret the film in clinical context (BSH). The oval macrocyte (elliptocyte with one blunt and one pointed end) is the characteristic red cell shape in megaloblastic anaemia — it differs from the round macrocytes of alcohol and liver disease. GPs who request a blood film alongside FBC are getting information that automated cell analysers cannot provide — and the film result should prompt specific targeted investigation rather than a generic 'macrocytosis workup' approach.

Drug-Induced Macrocytosis — Key Drug Interactions

Methotrexate
Mechanism: irreversible DHFR (dihydrofolate reductase) inhibition → impaired folinic acid synthesis → reduced DNA synthesis → megaloblastic marrow. Mild macrocytosis may occur during treatment; a rising MCV with falling counts, mucositis, infection or clinical deterioration is concerning. SPS: if MCV >105 fL, check B12, folate and TSH, hold treatment and contact the specialist. Management: folic acid 5 mg weekly (on a different day to methotrexate) — standard co-prescription. If macrocytosis worsening + oral ulcers + cytopenia: methotrexate toxicity — hold dose + check FBC + LFTs + creatinine + contact the prescribing specialist urgently. Folinic-acid rescue follows the specialist or local toxicology protocol.
Trimethoprim (chronic use)
Weak DHFR inhibitor. Clinical macrocytosis uncommon at standard prophylactic doses (100 mg at night for UTI prevention — BNF) but possible in: combined use with methotrexate (avoid co-prescribing trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole with methotrexate where possible because of potentially life-threatening marrow toxicity; if unavoidable, this requires specialist oversight and a clear monitoring plan), elderly patients on chronic low-dose trimethoprim, or patients with pre-existing marginal folate status. In anyone on chronic trimethoprim with macrocytosis, review renal function, FBC, B12, folate, interacting medicines and the indication for continued prophylaxis.
Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide)
Ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor — blocks DNA synthesis at S-phase. Causes significant macrocytosis as a pharmacological effect (not toxicity). Used for: CML, sickle cell disease (reduces sickling crises), essential thrombocythaemia. Interpret the MCV alongside the FBC, clinical response and specialist monitoring plan. Do not alter the dose solely because the MCV is high. Folic-acid supplementation should follow the haematology protocol.
Antiretroviral therapy (ART)
Zidovudine and some older antiretroviral regimens commonly cause macrocytosis. Discuss macrocytosis in a patient receiving ART with the HIV team before changing treatment. Exclude B12/folate deficiency, liver disease and other causes. Do not review or change ART in primary care without HIV specialist involvement.
The trimethoprim + methotrexate interaction is a potentially fatal combination that every GP must know — trimethoprim (even at standard doses) inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, the same enzyme that methotrexate targets. Co-prescribing trimethoprim to a patient on methotrexate (for rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, or cancer) can cause acute severe methotrexate toxicity with: sudden severe pancytopenia, mucositis, severe diarrhoea, and sepsis. Case series show deaths from this interaction. The mechanism: trimethoprim blocks the folate pathway that allows cells to recover from methotrexate toxicity. The safety rule: avoid co-prescribing trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole with methotrexate where possible — choose an alternative antibiotic (e.g. nitrofurantoin for UTI). If the combination is unavoidable, it requires specialist oversight and a clear monitoring plan. This interaction is listed in the BNF under 'interactions' for both trimethoprim and methotrexate, and should be checked at every prescription using the clinical system's drug interaction alert.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Urgent / same-day (haematology or acute medical)
Blast cells on film · Suspected acute leukaemia · Unexplained pancytopenia, rapidly falling counts or pancytopenia with clinical compromise · Severe neutropenia with fever (neutropenic sepsis) · Active bleeding · Progressive neurological B12 symptoms · Severe methotrexate toxicity · Decompensated liver disease · Severe symptomatic megaloblastic anaemia. Urgency depends on symptoms, bleeding, infection, rate of change and the local haematology pathway.
Haematology (routine/urgent per local pathway)
Persistent unexplained macrocytosis with anaemia · Macrocytosis with any unexplained cytopenia · Dysplastic blood-film features (?MDS) · Progressive MCV rise without an explanation · Persistent macrocytosis after correction of B12/folate deficiency and abstinence from alcohol where relevant. Isolated, stable macrocytosis with no anaemia or cytopenias does not automatically require urgent haematology referral.
Gastroenterology
Confirmed pernicious anaemia (single gastroscopy) · Coeliac disease causing macrocytosis · IBD on azathioprine with macrocytosis
Neurology
B12 deficiency + neurological features (SACD): start B12 urgently; urgent medical/neurological assessment if severe, progressive or diagnostically uncertain — see B12 algorithm
GP management
Alcohol-related macrocytosis: address alcohol use, assess nutritional status, check folate and B12; prescribe folic acid only if deficiency or a clear ongoing risk is present; recheck FBC. B12 deficiency: treat according to cause and neurological status, using the local B12 protocol — IM hydroxocobalamin for autoimmune gastritis (pernicious anaemia), malabsorption or neurological features; oral cyanocobalamin 50–150 micrograms daily for diet-related deficiency only (BNF; NICE NG239 — see B12 algorithm). Folate deficiency: folic acid 5 mg OD x 4 months (after B12 excluded/treated). Hypothyroidism: levothyroxine (see TFT algorithm). Drug-induced: dose review with prescriber + folate supplementation if appropriate.
The pernicious anaemia gastroscopy referral for macrocytic anaemia is an important quality standard — whenever a patient is diagnosed with pernicious anaemia as the cause of their macrocytic anaemia (positive anti-intrinsic factor antibody + B12 deficiency + appropriate clinical context), a single gastroscopy should be arranged. This is because PA causes atrophic gastritis, which increases the risk of both gastric cancer (approximately 1-3% lifetime risk — 2-3x higher than general population) and type I gastric carcinoid tumours (neuroendocrine tumours — relatively indolent but require monitoring). GPs who diagnose PA and initiate B12 injections without arranging gastroscopy are missing an important quality step. The referral should be to gastroenterology for routine OGD; the macrocytic anaemia itself has been treated, but the structural gastric pathology needs assessment.

Treat

Treating the Cause of Macrocytosis

B12 deficiency (pernicious anaemia)
IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg x 6 doses over 2 weeks, then 1 mg every 2–3 months lifelong (BNF; NICE NG239)
Treat clinically significant B12 deficiency according to the cause and neurological status. Do not delay treatment in suspected megaloblastic anaemia with neurological symptoms while awaiting test results. Lifelong IM replacement when autoimmune gastritis is the cause or suspected cause, or after total gastrectomy or complete terminal ileal resection; oral or IM may be appropriate for some medicine-induced deficiency (NICE NG239). Use the local B12 treatment protocol and current product information for loading and maintenance schedules. See B12 algorithm for full detail. Haematological response: reticulocyte peak day 5-7. MCV normalises over 2-3 months. Neurological recovery: weeks to months (partial if severe SACD). Annual TFTs + coeliac screen at PA diagnosis.
Folate deficiency
Folic acid 5 mg OD x 4 months (then maintenance if ongoing risk)
After confirming B12 is adequate (to avoid masking B12 deficiency). Identifies and treats the cause: poor diet, coeliac, alcohol. In pregnancy: 400 mcg/day pre-conception and first trimester (neural tube defect prevention); 5 mg/day if higher risk (previous or family history of neural-tube defect, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, certain haematological conditions, medicines affecting folate absorption or metabolism such as some antiepileptics); BMI alone is not an indication for more than 400 micrograms (NICE NG247). MCV normalises within 4-8 weeks.
Alcohol macrocytosis
Address alcohol use + assess nutrition; folic acid only if deficiency or clear ongoing risk
CAGE/AUDIT-C + alcohol history. Check folate and B12; do not assume isolated alcohol-related macrocytosis requires 5 mg folic acid (give it if deficiency is demonstrated or strongly suspected, or per local alcohol-dependence protocol). MCV may take several months to normalise after abstinence. Brief intervention for alcohol reduction (FRAMES model). NHS Drink Less app. Alcohol liaison service referral if dependent. GGT monitoring (normalises before MCV). Persistent macrocytosis despite abstinence: reassess for other causes.
Hypothyroid macrocytosis
Levothyroxine (see TFT algorithm)
MCV normalises within 3-6 months of achieving euthyroidism. Often combined with mild anaemia of hypothyroidism (normocytic + macrocytic mix). Coexisting B12 deficiency in PA + hypothyroidism (polyglandular autoimmune) — check both.
Drug-induced macrocytosis
Review drug and dose with prescriber; folate per indication and specialist protocol
Methotrexate: ensure folic acid 5 mg weekly prescribed. Hydroxycarbamide: macrocytosis expected; do not alter the dose solely because MCV is high; folic acid per haematology protocol. Trimethoprim/co-trimoxazole + methotrexate: avoid where possible (potentially life-threatening marrow toxicity); if unavoidable, specialist oversight and a clear monitoring plan. Antiretroviral: discuss with the HIV team before changing treatment. MCV should improve within 2-3 months of drug cessation/dose reduction.
Folic acid dose selection is important — the standard prescribed dose for treating folate deficiency or B12 deficiency haematological complications is 5 mg OD (high-dose) for 4 months. This is different from: (1) the pregnancy prevention dose (400 mcg/day — low-dose for neural tube defect prevention in normal-risk pregnancy); (2) the high-risk pregnancy dose (5 mg/day — for previous or family history of NTD, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, certain haematological conditions, or medicines affecting folate absorption or metabolism; not for BMI alone — NICE NG247); and (3) the co-prescription with methotrexate (5 mg weekly, on a different day to methotrexate — not daily). Prescribing the wrong dose is a common error: prescribing 400 mcg for treatment of established deficiency (insufficient); prescribing 5 mg daily indefinitely for a woman taking methotrexate (exceeds the weekly requirement and may reduce methotrexate efficacy). GPs should also recall that folic acid must never be prescribed alone in a B12-deficient patient without simultaneously treating the B12 deficiency.

MDS — Recognition, Referral & GP Supportive Role

MDS in primary care
Unexplained macrocytosis with persistent anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, dysplastic blood-film features or worsening counts warrants haematology referral. Bone marrow examination, cytogenetics and molecular testing are specialist investigations.
Specialist-led treatment
MDS treatment, transfusion thresholds and iron-chelation decisions are managed by haematology. Transfusion is individualised (it may be one unit at a time depending on symptoms, frailty, cardiac status and local policy). Iron-chelation decisions depend on age, transfusion burden, expected survival, organ function and local protocol; ferritin is an imperfect marker of tissue iron.
GP responsibilities
Monitor symptoms, infection and bleeding; coordinate vaccinations and comorbidity care; support advance-care planning in conjunction with the haematology team.
Quality of life and ACP in MDS
Advance care planning and goals-of-care discussions should be supported in conjunction with the haematology team. Palliative care integration when appropriate.
The GP's role in MDS is recognition, referral and supportive care. Disease-modifying treatment, transfusion and iron chelation are specialist haematology decisions and change quickly, so follow the haematology team's plan. Patients on haematology treatment who develop fever, infection or bleeding need urgent assessment as advised by their specialist team.

Lifestyle

Supporting Recovery & Long-Term B12/Folate Health

B12-rich food sources Animal products exclusively. Richest: clams, liver, kidney, beef, oily fish (mackerel, sardines), eggs, dairy. Fortified nutritional yeast (vegans). Fortified plant milks and cereals. Daily B12 requirement: 1.5-2.0 mcg. Pernicious anaemia: diet irrelevant (absorption mechanism absent) — injections are the only treatment.
Folate-rich food sources Dark green leafy vegetables (spinach, asparagus, broccoli, Brussels sprouts — 100-200 mcg/100g). Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, black beans — 150-200 mcg/100g cooked). Fortified cereals (up to 200 mcg/serving). Orange juice. Liver (very high but limit in pregnancy). Advise a balanced diet containing folate-rich foods; food preparation and bioavailability vary. Supplement in pregnancy regardless of diet.
Alcohol cessation support NHS alcohol services: One You app, Drink Free Days app. Local alcohol liaison nurse referral. AA (Alcoholics Anonymous). SMART Recovery (evidence-based group therapy). Pharmacotherapy for alcohol dependence: acamprosate 666 mg TDS (reduces craving) or naltrexone 50 mg OD (reduces reward). Supervised disulfiram (with specialist oversight). GGT falls sooner than MCV, which may take several months — use as objective motivational feedback.
Monitoring after treatment B12 deficiency: reticulocyte count at day 5-7 (confirms treatment response). FBC at 4-6 weeks (Hb rising). Follow-up around 3 months after starting B12 (earlier if clinically indicated — NICE NG239), then clinical follow-up based on route, symptoms, adherence, cause and local protocol; serum B12 is not routinely useful to assess response to regular IM replacement. Folate deficiency: FBC at 4-6 weeks. Annual folate check in ongoing risk groups (malabsorption, coeliac, methotrexate). Hypothyroid macrocytosis: TFT + FBC at 6 weeks of levothyroxine.
Folic acid in women of childbearing age NICE NG247 (2025) recommends 400 mcg folic acid daily for all women planning pregnancy and continuing throughout the first trimester. High-dose folic acid 5 mg daily for specified higher-risk groups: previous or family history of neural-tube defect, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, certain haematological conditions, and medicines affecting folate absorption or metabolism (e.g. some antiepileptics). BMI ≥25 alone is not an indication for more than 400 micrograms. GPs should advise folic acid supplementation at every pre-conception consultation and opportunistically to all women of childbearing age not using contraception.
MDS patient support MDS UK Patient Support Group (mdsuk.org) — patient information, buddy support programme, clinical trial information. Blood Cancer UK (formerly Bloodwise) — broader haematological cancer support. Macmillan Cancer Support — financial and practical support. ACP (advance care planning): ReSPECT process for patients with high-risk MDS. Hospice at home for transfusion-dependent patients unable to attend hospital.
Exercise in megaloblastic anaemia recovery Patients with severe megaloblastic anaemia (Hb <80 g/L) should rest until Hb improves (typically 4-8 weeks of B12/folate treatment). Once Hb above 100 g/L: gentle aerobic exercise (walking 15-30 min/day). Do not rush return to full activity — the myelin repair in SACD takes months. Physiotherapy for balance and proprioceptive rehabilitation if neurological B12 deficiency.
Avoiding macrocytosis drug interactions Methotrexate patients: always prescribe folic acid 5 mg weekly simultaneously. Avoid trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole where possible; if unavoidable, specialist oversight and a clear monitoring plan. Review the complete medication list for DHFR inhibitors or DNA synthesis inhibitors at each medication review. Regular FBC on methotrexate per SPS/shared-care monitoring schedule (intervals vary by specialty and risk) — watch the MCV trend. Any acute deterioration in macrocytosis + oral ulcers + cytopenias = methotrexate toxicity protocol.
The folic acid pre-conception advice is one of the most important primary prevention interventions in UK primary care — neural tube defects (NTDs: spina bifida, anencephaly) affect approximately 1,000 pregnancies per year in the UK, and folic acid supplementation (400 mcg/day) started at least 1 month before conception and continued through the first trimester reduces NTD risk by approximately 70%. Despite decades of public health messaging, only approximately 30% of women take folic acid pre-conception because many pregnancies are unplanned. Mandatory fortification of non-wholemeal wheat flour with folic acid was legislated in 2024 and applies from 13 December 2026 (0.25 mg per 100 g flour) — the Government estimates it will prevent around 200 NTD-affected pregnancies a year (about 20% of UK cases). GPs should still advise folic acid supplementation to all women planning pregnancy, noting that food fortification does not substitute for supplementation in the pre-conception period.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Macrocytosis workup completion
Confirm all first-line investigations reviewed and documented. If cause identified: treat and recheck FBC at 4-8 weeks (confirm response). If no cause after first-line workup: second-line investigations. If still unexplained: haematology referral.
After B12/folate treatment
Initial follow-up around 3 months after starting B12 replacement, or earlier if clinically indicated (NICE NG239). On regular IM replacement: monitor symptoms and functional response; FBC if clinically indicated or per local protocol; do not routinely use serum B12 to assess response; investigate persistent macrocytosis or cytopenias rather than simply repeating vitamin levels. On oral treatment: review response and adherence; further testing may be appropriate. B12 and folate at each coeliac annual review.
Monitoring methotrexate-treated patients
Intervals vary by specialty and risk — follow SPS and the local shared-care protocol. SPS (rheumatology): FBC, U&E/creatinine, LFTs and albumin every 2 weeks initially for at least 6 weeks, then monthly for 3–12 months, then at least every 12 weeks once stable; more frequent monitoring in higher-risk patients. If MCV >105 fL: check B12, folate and TSH, hold and contact the specialist. Macrocytosis trend: if MCV rising acutely + oral ulcers: hold methotrexate + urgent FBC + contact rheumatology. Confirm folic acid 5 mg weekly is prescribed at every MTX prescription.
MDS shared care
FBC, transfusion and ferritin monitoring per the haematology plan. Monitor symptoms, infection and bleeding. Review goals of care with the haematology team. ACP documents up to date. Palliative care team referral when appropriate.
Same-day
Blast cells on film or suspected acute leukaemia · Unexplained pancytopenia, rapidly falling counts, severe neutropenia with fever or active bleeding → immediate haematology/acute medical discussion · Methotrexate toxicity (mucositis + pancytopenia) → hold MTX + contact specialist/acute team · Severe symptomatic anaemia or progressive neurological B12 symptoms → urgent hospital assessment
Prompt review
An MCV above 110–120 fL, particularly if progressive or associated with anaemia, other cytopenias, abnormal film findings or neurological symptoms, warrants prompt review; urgency is determined by the overall clinical picture rather than the MCV alone · Unexplained macrocytosis after initial workup → haematology referral per local pathway · Macrocytosis + worsening cytopenia on serial FBCs → haematology · No FBC response after 6 weeks of B12 treatment → review diagnosis
The absence of a reticulocyte response 7-10 days after starting B12 (or folate) treatment is an important clinical signal — if the reticulocyte count does not rise above normal within 5-10 days of IM hydroxocobalamin injection in a patient with megaloblastic anaemia, the diagnosis must be reconsidered. Possible explanations: concurrent iron deficiency (iron is required for new red cell production — supplement simultaneously), concurrent infection or inflammation suppressing the reticulocyte response, incorrect diagnosis (MDS rather than megaloblastic anaemia — MDS may present with macrocytosis and fail to respond to B12/folate), co-existing coeliac disease with malabsorption, or very severe bone marrow failure requiring longer recovery. An absent or minimal reticulocyte response at day 7-10 should prompt blood film review and haematology referral.
