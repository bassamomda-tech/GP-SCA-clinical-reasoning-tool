Safety

Red Flags & NICE NG12 Breast Criteria — Act First

Most breast symptoms are benign, but the referral decision is made on age and examination findings — not on how benign the lump feels. If an NG12 criterion is met, refer first; do not arrange imaging first.

Unexplained breast lump, aged 30 and over With or without pain, women and men → Suspected cancer pathway breast referral (NICE NG12)
Nipple change in one nipple only, aged 50 and over Discharge, retraction or other change of concern → Suspected cancer pathway (NICE NG12). Applies to men too
Skin change suggestive of breast cancer Peau d'orange, dimpling, tethering, ulceration, a red, swollen breast that does not behave like infection → Consider suspected cancer pathway (NICE NG12) at any age
Unexplained axillary lump, aged 30 and over → Consider suspected cancer pathway breast referral (NICE NG12); generalised nodes or splenomegaly → see the lymphadenopathy pathway
Hot, tender breast with systemic illness Fever, rigors, tachycardia, spreading cellulitis or a fluctuant mass → Same-day assessment (abscess / sepsis); 999 if signs of septic shock
Known breast cancer + new back pain with leg weakness, sensory level or sphincter change → 999 / immediate MSCC pathway (NICE NG234). New persistent bone pain, confusion or thirst (hypercalcaemia) → Same-day acute oncology
Male breast: eccentric, hard or fixed lump Or nipple retraction, bloodstained discharge or ulceration → Suspected cancer pathway; do not label as gynaecomastia without examining
"Mastitis" in a woman who is not breastfeeding, not settling No improvement after one course of antibiotics, or an underlying mass → refer to the breast clinic to exclude inflammatory breast cancer (Suspected cancer pathway if a lump or suspicious skin change)

NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) sets the breast criteria by age: an unexplained lump at 30 and over, or a change of concern in one nipple at 50 and over, warrants a suspected cancer pathway referral; skin changes and an unexplained axillary lump at 30 and over warrant consideration of one. The criteria apply to men as well as women.

Inflammatory breast cancer is easily mistaken for mastitis — a red, swollen, peau d'orange breast in a woman who is not lactating, or one that fails to settle with antibiotics, needs specialist assessment. Breast cancer is the commonest source of metastatic spinal cord compression; new back pain in a patient with a breast cancer history needs a low threshold for urgent assessment.

2
Diagnose

Focused History — What Is the Symptom?

Lump
When noticed, how found, change in size, relation to the menstrual cycle, pain, single or multiple, previous lumps or cysts, trauma (fat necrosis)
Pain
Cyclical (bilateral, premenstrual, diffuse) or non-cyclical (focal, one-sided, persistent); chest wall pain. Pain alone with a normal examination → see the Mastalgia algorithm
Nipple
Discharge: spontaneous or only on squeezing, one or both sides, single or multiple ducts, colour (bloodstained, clear, milky, green). Retraction: new or longstanding, one-sided. Eczema, scaling or erosion of the nipple itself → see the Nipple problems algorithm
Skin
Dimpling, puckering, peau d'orange, redness, swelling, ulceration; how quickly it appeared
Axilla
Lump, recent infection, vaccination in that arm, shaving folliculitis, hidradenitis, systemic symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss)
Lactation and pregnancy
Breastfeeding (mastitis, galactocoele, abscess), possible pregnancy, recent weaning
Risk factors
Age; previous breast cancer, atypical hyperplasia or DCIS; family history (first-degree relatives, young age, bilateral, male breast or ovarian cancer, known BRCA — NICE CG164); chest radiotherapy (e.g. for Hodgkin lymphoma); HRT; alcohol; smoking (periductal mastitis)
Men
Drugs (spironolactone, anti-androgens, finasteride, digoxin, anabolic steroids, cannabis, opioids), alcohol and liver disease, testicular symptoms, weight change, puberty; family history of male breast or BRCA-related cancer
Screening
Last mammogram (NHS Breast Screening Programme invites women aged 50 to their 71st birthday every 3 years); a recent normal mammogram does not exclude cancer in a new symptom
Ideas, concerns, expectations
Most attend worried about cancer — ask directly; explain what the referral pathway means

Spontaneous, single-duct, one-sided or bloodstained discharge is pathological; bilateral, multi-duct discharge produced only on squeezing is usually physiological. Galactorrhoea (milky, bilateral) needs a pregnancy test, prolactin and drug review rather than a breast referral. Screening mammography is for women without symptoms — a woman with a new symptom needs a symptomatic referral even if her last screen was recent.

3
Diagnose

Examination — Both Breasts, Axillae and Nodes

Chaperone
Offer a chaperone and document the offer and the response (GMC). Examine even when the patient is sure it is "only a cyst"
Inspection
Sitting, arms by the side, then hands pressed on hips and arms raised: asymmetry, skin dimpling or tethering, peau d'orange, redness, nipple retraction or deviation, eczema or ulceration
Palpation
Lying with the arm behind the head: all four quadrants, the axillary tail and behind the nipple, both breasts. Describe any lump: site (clock face and distance from the nipple), size in cm, shape, consistency, mobility, tethering to skin or muscle, tenderness
Nodes
Axillary, supraclavicular and cervical nodes on both sides
Nipple discharge
Note colour and whether single or multiple ducts if it is seen; do not repeatedly squeeze the nipple to produce discharge
Men
Gynaecomastia: rubbery or firm disc of tissue centred under the areola, often bilateral and sometimes tender. Pseudogynaecomastia: soft fat without a disc. Cancer: hard, eccentric, painless, may be fixed, with nipple or skin change. Examine the testes and abdomen (liver) in gynaecomastia
Systemic
Spine and bone tenderness, liver, chest, if a history of breast cancer or advanced disease is suspected

Benign and malignant lumps cannot be reliably told apart clinically — a smooth, mobile lump in a woman over 30 still needs triple assessment. A careful description lets the breast team and any later GP compare. Testicular examination in a man with gynaecomastia screens for a hormone-secreting testicular tumour.

4
Diagnose

Investigations — Refer, Do Not Image First

Breast lump or suspicious change
No imaging from primary care: the breast clinic does triple assessment (clinical examination, imaging — ultrasound under 40, mammography plus ultrasound from 40 — and core biopsy). Arranging a scan first only delays the pathway
Possible pregnancy
Urine hCG before attributing breast change to pregnancy, and before any drug
Milky bilateral discharge (galactorrhoea)
Pregnancy test, prolactin, TSH, renal function; drug review (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, domperidone, SSRIs) — see the Nipple problems algorithm
Gynaecomastia (men)
Usually: LFTs, U&Es, TSH, morning testosterone, LH, FSH, oestradiol, prolactin, hCG and AFP — see the Gynaecomastia algorithm. Raised hCG or AFP, or a testicular lump → urgent urology referral (testicular tumour)
Lactational mastitis
No routine tests. Milk culture if not responding at 48 hours, recurrent, hospital-acquired or atypical
Do not
Do not request mammography for a symptomatic patient, do fine-needle aspiration of a lump in primary care, or use a normal ultrasound or mammogram to avoid a referral that meets NG12

One-stop breast clinics are designed to complete triple assessment at the first visit. Ultrasound is preferred under 40 because dense breast tissue limits mammography. In men, gynaecomastia bloods screen for liver, thyroid, gonadal and tumour causes; a raised hCG suggests a germ-cell tumour.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis — Common Patterns

Fibroadenoma
Commonest lump under 30: smooth, firm, rubbery, very mobile, painless. Under 30 → consider non-urgent breast clinic referral
Breast cyst
Commonest from 35 to the menopause (or on HRT): smooth, well defined, may appear suddenly and be tender. Over 30 → still a suspected cancer pathway referral; the clinic aspirates
Fibrocystic change / nodularity
Diffuse, bilateral, lumpy, often tender before periods; no discrete lump. A discrete lump is not "nodularity"
Breast cancer
Hard, irregular, painless, fixed or tethered; skin or nipple change; axillary nodes. Can be smooth and mobile — examination alone cannot exclude it
Mastitis / abscess
Lactational: painful, hot, red wedge, flu-like symptoms. Periductal (non-lactational): smokers, subareolar, recurrent, may form a fistula. Abscess: fluctuant, tender mass
Fat necrosis
After trauma or surgery: firm, may tether skin — mimics cancer; refer by the NG12 age criteria
Duct ectasia / intraductal papilloma
Discharge: ectasia (green-brown, multiple ducts, often bilateral, perimenopausal); papilloma (single duct, often bloodstained) — refer, as cancer cannot be excluded clinically
Skin and axillary lumps
Sebaceous cyst, lipoma, hidradenitis suppurativa, reactive axillary node (infection, recent vaccination), accessory breast tissue; lymphoma
Men
Gynaecomastia (puberty, older age, drugs, liver disease, hypogonadism, hyperthyroidism, testicular tumour), pseudogynaecomastia, male breast cancer (rare, usually over 60, BRCA2 carriers)
Chest wall
Costochondritis, rib injury, Mondor's disease (thrombophlebitis of a superficial vein — tender cord)

Age is the biggest single discriminator: under 30, breast cancer is rare and fibroadenoma is common, which is why NICE uses age 30 as the lump threshold. Even so, a young woman with a lump that is hard, fixed, enlarging, or with a strong family history needs prompt specialist assessment on clinical judgement.

6
Refer

Referral — Which Route and How Fast

999
Septic shock from breast infection; suspected spinal cord compression in known cancer (immediate MSCC pathway)
Same-day
Breast abscess or mastitis with systemic illness or spreading cellulitis (surgical / breast on-call); suspected hypercalcaemia in known cancer (acute oncology)
Suspected cancer pathway · NICE NG12
Refer: aged 30 and over with an unexplained breast lump, with or without pain; aged 50 and over with discharge, retraction or other change of concern in one nipple only. Consider: skin changes suggestive of breast cancer; aged 30 and over with an unexplained axillary lump. Male breast: an unexplained lump at 30 and over, or a suspicious mass, also qualifies
Non-urgent breast clinic
Consider for: under 30 with an unexplained breast lump with or without pain (sooner on clinical judgement if hard, fixed, enlarging or with a strong family history); nipple discharge under 50 per the local pathway; persistent focal pain with a normal examination; recurrent periductal mastitis
Family history
Moderate or high risk on NICE CG164 criteria → secondary care breast family history service or genetics per the local pathway; no imaging from primary care for family history alone
Men
Gynaecomastia with abnormal bloods → endocrinology; raised hCG/AFP or testicular lump → urgent urology; persistent troublesome gynaecomastia → routine breast or endocrine clinic per the local pathway
Primary care manage
Cyclical mastalgia with a normal examination; lactational mastitis responding to treatment; pubertal gynaecomastia; physiological bilateral discharge on squeezing only

The NHS Faster Diagnosis Standard expects cancer to be diagnosed or ruled out within 28 days of a suspected cancer referral. Keep NICE's wording: "refer" for a lump at 30 and over and nipple change at 50 and over, "consider" for skin changes and axillary lumps. Many breast services also accept non-urgent symptomatic referrals for under-30s — follow your local pathway.

Treat

Management at This Consultation

NG12 criterion met
Refer today Suspected cancer
Explain why, that most referrals are not cancer, and that the clinic may do a scan and biopsy the same day. Check contact details and availability over the next 2 weeks; give written information. Simple analgesia if painful
Lactational mastitis
Keep milk flowing ± flucloxacillin
Continue breastfeeding or expressing from the affected side, paracetamol or ibuprofen, cold compresses. Antibiotics if systemically unwell, an infected nipple fissure, or no improvement after 12–24 hours of effective milk removal. Usual adult dose: flucloxacillin 500 mg four times daily for 10–14 days (NICE CKS; check the SPC and local formulary). Penicillin allergy: a macrolide per local guidance, checking interactions. Review at 48 hours
Non-lactational / periductal mastitis
Antibiotic per local pathway
Consider co-amoxiclav where appropriate (mixed and anaerobic organisms), per the local antimicrobial guideline. Smoking cessation. Not settling after one course, or any mass → breast clinic (exclude abscess and inflammatory cancer)
Breast pain, normal examination
Reassure, supportive bra, analgesia
Well-fitting supportive bra, paracetamol or a topical NSAID; pain diary for two cycles. Full pathway: Mastalgia algorithm
Gynaecomastia
Find the cause
Pubertal: reassure, most resolves within 2 years. Drug-related: review the culprit with the prescriber. Bloods and testicular examination as above. Tamoxifen or surgery only via specialists
Under 30, discrete lump
Non-urgent breast clinic
Consider non-urgent referral (NICE NG12). Re-examining after the next period is reasonable only for vague nodularity — not for a discrete lump

Next steps: see Benign breast disorders · Breast cancer · Mastitis and breast abscess

Effective milk removal is the core treatment of lactational mastitis — stopping feeding on the affected side increases the risk of abscess. Flucloxacillin is safe in breastfeeding. Repeated antibiotic courses for a breast that will not settle delay the diagnosis of abscess and inflammatory breast cancer.

8
Lifestyle

Breast Awareness, Screening & Risk Reduction

Breast awareness Know what is normal: look and feel regularly, including the armpit; report any change promptly. Men can get breast cancer too.
NHS breast screening Women are invited from 50 to their 71st birthday every 3 years; older women can self-refer. Screening is not for symptoms.
Alcohol Breast cancer risk rises with alcohol; advise no more than 14 units a week, spread over 3 or more days (UK CMO).
Weight and activity Healthy weight after the menopause and regular physical activity lower breast cancer risk.
Smoking Strongly linked with periductal mastitis and its recurrence — offer stop-smoking support.
HRT Discuss the small increase in breast cancer risk with combined HRT as part of shared decisions; do not stop HRT just because of a referral without discussion.
Breastfeeding Good attachment, frequent feeding and avoiding sudden weaning reduce mastitis; infant feeding support if recurrent.
Family history Record the family history; a moderate or high risk under NICE CG164 opens access to enhanced surveillance and genetic assessment.

Breast awareness is preferred over formal monthly self-examination — the aim is prompt reporting of change. Alcohol, post-menopausal obesity and inactivity are the main modifiable risk factors for breast cancer.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Suspected cancer referral
Practice process to confirm the appointment was booked and attended; ask the patient to contact the practice if they have not heard within a week. Diagnosis or cancer ruled out within 28 days (Faster Diagnosis Standard)
Mastitis
Review at 48 hours if on antibiotics or not improving; ultrasound and breast assessment if a lump persists or abscess is suspected
Non-urgent referral or watchful waiting
Review if the lump grows, hardens or a new symptom appears; re-refer or upgrade the referral if criteria are later met
After a benign clinic result
Back to breast awareness; return with any new or different symptom — a previous benign result does not cover a new change
Safety-net 999
Hot, painful breast with high fever, shivering, confusion or feeling faint; new leg weakness or loss of bladder or bowel control with back pain in someone with breast cancer
Safety-net same-day
Breast redness spreading quickly, a hot painful swelling, or feeling unwell with a breast infection; on antibiotics and worse after 48 hours
Return soon
Any new lump, skin dimpling, nipple turning in, bloodstained or one-sided discharge, or a lump that does not go after a period

Failure to follow up referrals is a recurrent theme in delayed cancer diagnosis. Document examination findings, the chaperone offer, the referral route and the safety-net advice.
