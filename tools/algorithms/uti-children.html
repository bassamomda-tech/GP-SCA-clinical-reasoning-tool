1
Safety

Red flags — the age rule and the child who is more than infected

Assess every febrile child with the NICE NG143 (Fever in under 5s, 2019) traffic-light features and the NICE NG51 (Sepsis, 2016) risk criteria before thinking about the urine. Age changes the rules more than any other factor.

Infant under 3 months with suspected UTI NICE NG224 (2022): refer immediately to paediatric specialist care, with a urine sample sent for urgent microscopy and culture. Parenteral antibiotics are given there (NG143) — do not start oral antibiotics and wait → same-day paediatrics
Sepsis features at any age Mottled or ashen skin, reduced responsiveness, weak or high-pitched cry, grunting, tachypnoea, cold peripheries with prolonged capillary refill, reduced urine output, non-blanching rash → 999 / emergency admission. Do not delay for a urine sample.
Cannot keep fluids or antibiotics down Persistent vomiting or clinical dehydration — oral treatment will fail → admit
Seriously ill, or a feature of atypical UTI Poor urine flow, abdominal or bladder mass, raised creatinine, septicaemia, no response to a suitable antibiotic within 48 hours, or a non-E. coli organism (NG224 definition) → same-day paediatric discussion and ultrasound during the acute infection
Palpable abdominal mass Atypical UTI, and also an NG12 red flag: NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — consider a very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours) for specialist assessment for Wilms' tumour (and neuroblastoma) in a child with a palpable abdominal mass or unexplained enlarged abdominal organ; unexplained visible haematuria is also a Wilms' criterion → NG12 48-hour referral
Known renal tract abnormality Antenatal hydronephrosis, known reflux, a single or duplex kidney, or a neuropathic bladder with a febrile UTI → same-day paediatric discussion
Hypertension, faltering growth or proteinuria Suggests established renal scarring or an underlying renal problem → paediatric nephrology, not just another course
Safeguarding cues Genital injury, an STI, a disclosure, or recurrent UTI with neglect or poor attendance → follow local safeguarding procedures. UTI alone is not evidence of abuse.
Urinary infection is a common cause of fever without a focus in young children, and in infants it may be bacteraemic with non-specific signs (poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, jaundice, irritability, or hypothermia rather than fever). That is why NG224 takes the under-3-months group out of primary care altogether: the decision is referral, not dipstick interpretation. Above that age, the sepsis and traffic-light assessment (NG51, NG143) decides where the child is managed; the urine decides what they have. An abdominal mass does double duty: it defines atypical UTI in NG224, and it is an NG12 criterion for very urgent referral for Wilms' tumour or neuroblastoma, so it must never be attributed to a full bladder without checking again after voiding.
2
Diagnose

Clinical probability — who needs a urine test

Test the urine of any infant or child with symptoms or signs suggesting UTI, and of any child with unexplained fever of 38°C or higher (NICE NG224). Presentation depends on age.

Under 3 months
Fever, vomiting, lethargy, irritability, poor feeding, faltering growth; less often abdominal pain, jaundice, haematuria, offensive urine. Localising urinary symptoms are rarely reported.
3 months to under 3 years (preverbal)
Fever is the commonest sign; also abdominal pain, loin tenderness, vomiting, poor feeding; less often lethargy, offensive urine, haematuria, new wetting.
3 years and over (verbal)
Frequency, dysuria, urgency, suprapubic or abdominal pain, new daytime wetting or enuresis, haematuria; loin pain or fever points to the upper tract.
Risk factors to ask about and record
Poor urine flow · previous suspected UTI · recurrent fever of uncertain origin · antenatally diagnosed renal abnormality · family history of vesicoureteric reflux or renal disease · constipation · dysfunctional voiding · enlarged bladder or abdominal mass · evidence of a spinal lesion · poor growth · raised blood pressure (NG224).
Getting a sample
Clean-catch urine is the recommended method. If that is not possible, use a urine collection pad (not cotton wool balls, gauze or sanitary towels). Catheter or suprapubic aspiration only if non-invasive methods are not possible, usually in hospital.
Handling the sample
Test or refrigerate promptly; if it cannot reach the laboratory within 4 hours, refrigerate or use a boric acid container filled to the line.

Differentials to hold in mind

Other source of fever
Otitis media, pneumonia, viral illness, meningitis — a positive dipstick does not end the search in an unwell child; leucocytes rise in many febrile illnesses (including Kawasaki disease and appendicitis).
Vulvovaginitis / balanitis
Dysuria from external soreness with a clean sample that grows nothing; look at the genitalia.
Threadworm
Nocturnal perianal or vulval itch with dysuria and a negative culture.
Constipation and voiding dysfunction
Frequency, urgency and wetting without infection; a loaded rectum on examination.
Diabetes
Polyuria, thirst, new bed-wetting and weight loss — check capillary glucose.
Adolescents
Sexually transmitted infection and pregnancy in a sexually active young person with dysuria.
Children under about 3 cannot describe dysuria or frequency, so fever may be the only clue — which is why NG224 asks for urine testing in unexplained fever of 38°C or higher rather than waiting for urinary symptoms. The risk-factor list matters because each item either raises the chance that this is a UTI or points to an underlying abnormality that changes imaging. The sample method is the other decision that determines everything downstream: a contaminated pad sample can label a child with a UTI they do not have, commit them to imaging, and leave the real cause of the fever untreated. Collecting a clean catch takes patience, and it is worth building that time into the consultation.
3
Diagnose

Classify the episode — upper or lower, typical, atypical or recurrent

NICE NG224 classification decides antibiotic duration and the imaging schedule. Classify at the moment of diagnosis and write it in the notes.

Upper UTI (acute pyelonephritis)
Bacteriuria and fever of 38°C or higher; or bacteriuria with fever below 38°C plus loin pain or tenderness.
Lower UTI (cystitis)
Bacteriuria without fever or systemic features: frequency, dysuria, suprapubic pain.
Atypical UTI
Any of: seriously ill · poor urine flow · abdominal or bladder mass · raised creatinine · septicaemia · failure to respond to a suitable antibiotic within 48 hours · infection with a non-E. coli organism.
Recurrent UTI
Two or more episodes of upper UTI; or one episode of upper UTI plus one or more of lower UTI; or three or more episodes of lower UTI.
First typical UTI
Responds to treatment within 48 hours, E. coli, no atypical features. Over 6 months of age: no routine imaging.
Asymptomatic bacteriuria
A positive culture in a well child without symptoms is not a UTI: no antibiotics, no prophylaxis, no follow-up (NG224).
The classification is not academic: it drives two separate decisions. Upper versus lower sets the antibiotic course (7 to 10 days versus 3 days in the protocol). Typical, atypical or recurrent, combined with age, sets the imaging. The earlier version of this step described recurrent UTI as two lower UTIs with one upper, which is not the NG224 definition; the correct middle limb is one upper UTI plus at least one lower UTI. Previous episodes only count if they were genuinely UTIs, so check whether a culture confirmed them before adding them up. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is deliberately excluded: treating it does not prevent scarring and drives resistance.
4
Diagnose

Examination — severity, source and underlying cause

Traffic-light observations
Temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, capillary refill, colour, activity and hydration, interpreted by age using NG143. Any red feature → emergency pathway (Step 1).
Hydration
Dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes or fontanelle, reduced skin turgor, reduced wet nappies.
Abdomen and loins
Suprapubic tenderness (lower tract), loin or renal-angle tenderness (upper tract). A palpable bladder after voiding, or a renal or abdominal mass, is atypical UTI and an NG12 flag (Step 1).
External genitalia
Vulvovaginitis, labial adhesions, phimosis or balanitis — can mimic or predispose to UTI; look for injury or discharge.
Spine and lower limbs
Sacral dimple, hair tuft or naevus, abnormal gait or leg neurology — a spinal lesion causing a neuropathic bladder.
Blood pressure
Measure with an appropriately sized cuff in recurrent or atypical UTI and where scarring is known; compare with age, sex and height centiles.
Growth
Plot height and weight: faltering growth with recurrent UTI suggests an underlying renal problem.
Other focus
Ears, throat, chest, skin and meninges — a urinary finding does not exclude a second source.
Examination answers three questions: how unwell is the child (where they are managed), is this upper or lower tract (how long to treat), and is there a reason for infection (whether to image or refer). The spine and genitalia are easily forgotten but reveal the predisposing causes that NG224 lists as risk factors. Blood pressure is the examination finding most often omitted, and it is the one that detects the long-term consequence of scarring. Examining for another source prevents the common error of a positive dipstick closing the diagnostic search in a child with, for example, early meningitis.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — reading the urine by age, and the imaging schedule

Under 3 months
Urgent microscopy and culture, arranged as part of the immediate paediatric referral (NG224). Dipstick is not used to decide management.
3 months to under 3 years
Dipstick (NG224, 2022): leucocyte esterase or nitrite positive → start antibiotics and send urine for culture. Both negative → UTI unlikely; do not start antibiotics and do not send for culture unless another culture indication applies (below).
3 years and over
Nitrite positive (with or without leucocytes) → start antibiotics and send culture if a culture indication applies. Leucocytes positive, nitrite negative → send culture and start antibiotics only if there is good clinical evidence of UTI. Both negative → not UTI; look for another cause.
Send for culture when
Suspected upper UTI · high or intermediate risk of serious illness · a single positive dipstick result (leucocytes or nitrite alone) · recurrent UTI · no response to treatment within 24–48 hours · symptoms and dipstick do not match.
Bloods
Only if unwell or atypical: FBC, CRP, U&E and creatinine, blood culture — usually in the paediatric assessment unit.

Imaging schedule (NICE NG224)

Under 6 months
Typical UTI responding within 48 hours: ultrasound within 6 weeks. Atypical or recurrent: ultrasound during the acute infection, DMSA at 4–6 months, and MCUG.
6 months to under 3 years
Typical: no routine imaging. Atypical: ultrasound during the acute infection and DMSA at 4–6 months. Recurrent: ultrasound within 6 weeks and DMSA at 4–6 months. MCUG not routine; consider if dilatation on ultrasound, poor urine flow, non-E. coli infection or a family history of reflux.
3 years and over
Typical: no routine imaging. Atypical: ultrasound during the acute infection. Recurrent: ultrasound within 6 weeks and DMSA at 4–6 months.
Do not
Routinely retest the urine of a child who is well after a UTI; use CT or plain films to investigate UTI.
The 2022 update of NG224 changed the approach in children aged 3 months to under 3 years: a dipstick is now used, antibiotics are started only if leucocyte esterase or nitrite is positive, and a double-negative dipstick makes UTI unlikely enough that culture is not needed unless another indication applies. Nitrite is the more specific marker because it needs urine to sit in the bladder for some hours, which is why a negative nitrite is less reassuring in a child who voids frequently. The imaging tables are the most-tested and most-missed part of the guideline: ultrasound looks for structural problems and obstruction, DMSA at 4–6 months looks for established scarring (after acute inflammation settles), and MCUG looks for reflux in infants. A child who has a further UTI while waiting for DMSA may need the scan brought forward.
6
Refer

Referral — who, how fast

Same day / 999
Under 3 months with suspected UTI · sepsis features at any age · cannot tolerate oral fluids or antibiotics · dehydration · seriously ill.
Same-day paediatric discussion
Atypical UTI (poor flow, mass, raised creatinine, no response at 48 hours, non-E. coli) · febrile UTI with a known renal tract abnormality.
NG12 48-hour
Palpable abdominal mass, unexplained enlarged abdominal organ or unexplained visible haematuria → very urgent referral for specialist assessment for Wilms' tumour / neuroblastoma (NICE NG12, updated April 2026).
Consider paediatric referral
Upper UTI in a child aged 3 months or over (NG224 advises considering referral); recurrent UTI where imaging needs coordinating.
Paediatric nephrology
Bilateral renal abnormalities, impaired kidney function, raised blood pressure or proteinuria (NG224) · scarring on DMSA · reflux needing management.
Bladder and bowel service
Dysfunctional voiding, daytime wetting or constipation that is not responding to first-line treatment.
Primary care
Well child aged 3 months or over with lower UTI or a mild upper UTI tolerating oral antibiotics, with a clear 48-hour review plan and any imaging requested by you.

Referral letters, imaging request wording and follow-up: see the UTI in children protocol.

Referral decisions depend on age, how unwell the child is and whether the episode is atypical. NG224 moves every infant under 3 months straight to paediatric care. For older children with upper UTI it advises considering referral rather than mandating it, so a well, drinking 2-year-old with pyelonephritis can often be treated at home with close review. Imaging requests for typical and recurrent UTI can usually be made directly from primary care; the risk is that nobody makes them. An abdominal mass is the one finding where NG12's 48-hour cancer pathway sits alongside the UTI pathway, and both should run in parallel.
7
Treat

Treatment — summary (detail in the protocol)

Antibiotic choice, doses by weight and age, and local-resistance adjustments are on the UTI in children protocol. The decisions this pathway hands on are:

Under 3 months
No oral antibiotic in primary care — immediate paediatric referral for parenteral treatment (NG224, NG143).
Upper UTI, 3 months and over
Oral antibiotic for 7–10 days with low resistance (a cephalosporin such as cefalexin, or co-amoxiclav) if well enough; IV via paediatrics if unwell or vomiting (NG224; NICE NG111, 2018). Do not use nitrofurantoin — it does not reach the kidney.
Lower UTI, 3 months and over
3 days of an antibiotic guided by local resistance — trimethoprim or nitrofurantoin first choice if resistance risk is low (NICE NG109, 2018). Doses per BNFC.
Adjust on culture
Review sensitivities at 48 hours; switch if resistant. No improvement at 48 hours = atypical UTI (Step 3).
Symptoms
Paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain and fever, adequate fluids.
Prophylaxis
Not routinely after a first UTI; may be considered in recurrent UTI, usually as a specialist-guided decision (NG224; NICE NG112, 2018).
Doses have been removed from this step because they depend on weight and age bands and are kept up to date on the protocol and in the BNFC; duplicating them here created a risk of drift. The durations and drug classes come from NG224 and the antimicrobial prescribing guidelines NICE NG109 (lower UTI) and NICE NG111 (pyelonephritis). The most important safety point is that nitrofurantoin is for bladder infection only. Failure to improve within 48 hours is not a cue to prescribe another course blind: it defines atypical UTI and brings forward ultrasound and a paediatric discussion.

Lifestyle

Prevention and carer advice

Treat constipation A loaded rectum impairs bladder emptying and is one of the commonest reversible contributors to recurrence; manage per NICE CG99 (Constipation in children, 2010).
Regular, complete voiding Encourage regular toilet trips through the day and at school, unhurried voiding, and not deferring; address dysfunctional voiding patterns.
Fluids Adequate drinks spread through the day, including at school.
Hygiene Wipe front to back; avoid bubble baths, perfumed soaps and wipes if they cause vulval soreness.
Early recognition Teach carers the symptoms for their child's age, and to bring a clean-catch sample with them when they seek help.
Asymptomatic bacteriuria Explain that a positive test in a well child does not need treatment, and that routine repeat testing after recovery is not recommended.
Prevention in children is mostly about bladder and bowel habits rather than antibiotics. Constipation and dysfunctional voiding are the two most treatable causes of recurrence, and a bladder and bowel service can help where first-line advice fails. Explaining to parents what will and will not be done (no routine retesting, no prophylaxis after a first typical UTI) reduces anxious cycles of repeat sampling and prevents the treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria, which NG224 advises against.

Safety

Follow-up, imaging review and safety-netting

48 hours
Review the culture and the child: organism, sensitivities, response. Not improving → reassess in person and treat as atypical (Steps 3 and 6).
Imaging
Request at diagnosis per the NG224 table (Step 5); tell parents what is booked and when; diarise to check the ultrasound and DMSA results were done and reviewed.
Renal parenchymal defects
Children with scarring need blood pressure checks and assessment for proteinuria; bilateral abnormality, impaired function, raised BP or proteinuria → paediatric nephrology (NG224).
No routine retest
Do not retest the urine of a child who is well after treatment (NG224).
Call 999
Child floppy, very drowsy or hard to wake, mottled or blue, breathing fast or grunting, a rash that does not fade, or a fit.
Same-day review
Not improving after 24–48 hours of antibiotics, vomiting doses, drinking much less, far fewer wet nappies, new loin pain or new high fever.
Recurrence
Bring a clean-catch sample to any future feverish illness and mention the previous UTI — it changes how the next episode is classified.

Parent wording and follow-up schedule: UTI in children protocol.

The most common silent failure in this pathway is imaging that was indicated but never requested, or requested and never checked. Scarring causes no symptoms in childhood and presents years later as hypertension or chronic kidney disease, so the imaging decision should be made and documented at the time of diagnosis. Safety-netting should use the NG143 amber and red features in plain language, with a specific time frame (24–48 hours), because parents otherwise wait for the course to finish. Telling parents that previous UTIs matter at the next fever helps the next clinician classify recurrence correctly.
