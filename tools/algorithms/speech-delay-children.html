Safety

Red Flags — Regression, Hearing Loss & Neurological Causes

Loss of previously acquired speech or language skills at any age Developmental regression — never normal. Consider: Landau-Kleffner syndrome (acquired epileptic aphasia), autism spectrum disorder (regression typically 18-24 months), Rett syndrome (girls — stereotyped hand movements, deceleration of head growth), metabolic disorders. → Prompt referral; urgency depends on type and tempo (NICE CG128). Language/social regression under 3 years → autism team; language regression over 3 years → paediatrician/paediatric neurologist first; motor regression at any age → paediatrician/paediatric neurologist. Same-day/urgent assessment if seizures, altered consciousness, rapid neurological deterioration, new focal neurological signs, loss of motor skills or features of epileptic encephalopathy. EEG is specialist-led (e.g. suspected Landau-Kleffner).
Speech delay + bilateral profound or severe hearing loss not yet identified Sensorineural hearing loss — an important treatable cause (severe bilateral loss is not the only hearing-related cause; conductive loss from OME is common). Cochlear implant suitability and timing are specialist decisions. Newborn hearing screening does not exclude later-onset, mild, unilateral or fluctuating hearing loss. → Urgent paediatric audiology if severe/profound hearing loss suspected (same-day/urgent specialist assessment if urgent audiology cannot be accessed). Arrange hearing assessment for every child with suspected speech, language or communication difficulty, but do not delay speech-and-language therapy, developmental assessment or autism referral while awaiting audiology — referrals should proceed in parallel.
Speech delay + autistic features (no pointing by 12 months, no babble by 12 months, no single words by 16 months, no two-word phrases by 24 months, poor eye contact, repetitive behaviours) Autism spectrum disorder (ASD). → Refer to the local multidisciplinary autism diagnostic pathway (community paediatrics/neurodevelopmental services; CAMHS is not the default service for every child). Do not delay autism referral awaiting speech and language therapy, and do not delay speech and language support awaiting autism assessment.
Speech delay + global developmental delay + dysmorphic features or microcephaly Genetic syndrome (Down syndrome, fragile X, 22q11 deletion, CHARGE, Williams). → Prompt community paediatrics referral (± genetics). Chromosomal microarray and other genetic testing are specialist-led.
Child with speech delay + parental concern about safeguarding / neglect Severe social deprivation and neglect can cause profound speech delay through absence of language exposure. → children's social care (safeguarding) referral if abuse or neglect suspected. Safeguarding concerns should be based on the child's welfare and observed circumstances, not on speech delay alone — follow local safeguarding procedures.
Speech delay + new-onset seizures or neurological signs (hypotonia, hyperreflexia, abnormal movements) Neurological cause for speech delay. → Same-day/urgent paediatric or paediatric neurology assessment. MRI brain, EEG and metabolic investigations are specialist-led.
Developmental regression — the loss of previously acquired skills — always requires prompt referral; urgency depends on the type and tempo of regression. A child who was saying 10 words at 18 months and has lost them by 22 months is not 'just a bit slow' — this pattern needs prompt assessment. The most important diagnoses not to miss: (1) Landau-Kleffner syndrome (acquired epileptic aphasia) — children typically develop normally until 3-8 years, then lose language due to continuous epileptiform discharges in the temporal language areas during sleep. The EEG during sleep is diagnostic; waking EEG may be normal. Treatment with corticosteroids or antiepileptics can restore language if treated early. (2) Autism spectrum disorder — language regression in ASD typically occurs between 15-24 months (after a period of apparently normal development), often accompanied by loss of social engagement and the emergence of repetitive behaviours. The RCGP curriculum requires GPs to identify developmental regression as a red flag requiring prompt referral. NICE CG128: language/social regression under 3 years → autism team; language regression over 3 years → paediatrician/paediatric neurologist first; motor regression at any age → paediatrician/paediatric neurologist. Seizures, altered consciousness, rapid deterioration or focal signs need same-day/urgent assessment.

Speech & Language Milestones — Approximate Expectations (Not Diagnostic Cut-offs)

Prelinguistic (birth to 12 months)
Birth–2 months: startles to sound, calms to familiar voice. 3–4 months: cooing, turns to voice, laughs. 6 months: babbling (consonant-vowel combinations — "bababa", "mamama"). 9 months: varied babble with intonation, responds to name. 10–12 months: first words emerging, waves bye-bye, plays peek-a-boo, points (proto-imperative pointing by 12 months).
12–24 months (approximate)
12 months: 1–3 meaningful words (mama/dada used specifically). 15 months: 5–10 words, proto-declarative pointing (to share interest, not just request). 18 months: 10–50 words, follows simple 1-step commands, identifies body parts. 24 months: 50+ words, 2-word combinations ("more milk", "daddy go"), 50% speech intelligible to strangers. Concern if: no meaningful single words by 16 months, no spontaneous two-word combinations by 24 months.
2–5 years (approximate)
2 years: 2-word sentences, 50% intelligible to strangers. 3 years: 3-word sentences, 75% intelligible, uses pronouns (I, me, you), 200+ words. 4 years: 4+ word sentences, 100% intelligible, tells stories, uses past tense, 1,000+ words. 5 years: complex sentences, can retell stories, rhyming, pre-literacy phonological awareness. Concern if: not using sentences by 3 years, not fully intelligible to strangers by 4 years.
Expressive vs receptive delay
Expressive delay (output only): child understands well, follows commands appropriately, but speaks fewer/less clear words than expected. Prognosis varies — review progress with SLT. Receptive delay (understanding impaired): more concerning — needs SLT and broader developmental assessment. Mixed expressive-receptive delay: highest concern — usually has an underlying cause (hearing loss, ASD, intellectual disability, neurological).
Red flags (more robust than single milestones)
No babbling or social communication gestures by around 12 months · No meaningful single words by 16 months · No spontaneous two-word combinations by 24 months · Loss of previously acquired language or social skills at any age · Poor comprehension, reduced joint attention or absent pointing · Speech that is difficult to understand beyond what would be expected for age. A single delayed milestone should prompt a broader developmental assessment rather than an automatic diagnosis.
The distinction between expressive-only delay and receptive-expressive delay is the single most important clinical discriminator in speech delay assessment — a child with pure expressive delay who has age-appropriate comprehension (follows two-step commands, understands complex instructions, responds appropriately to questions) has a much more favourable prognosis than a child with impaired comprehension. Pure expressive delay in children aged 18-30 months with good comprehension and good non-verbal communication (pointing, gesturing, making eye contact) is often described as 'late talking' — which often improves, but the outcome varies and cannot be predicted reliably. However, even 'late talkers' with expressive-only delay benefit from early speech and language therapy referral because: (1) the GP cannot reliably distinguish pure expressive delay from early receptive delay or ASD in a 10-minute consultation; (2) early SLT input is appropriate regardless of cause; and (3) some late talkers have persistent language difficulties.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History (structured)
Prenatal/perinatal: maternal illness in pregnancy (TORCH infections — CMV, rubella, toxoplasma, herpes), prematurity (speech delay is common in ex-preterm infants), birth hypoxia (HIE), NICU admission. Past medical history: recurrent otitis media with effusion (OME/glue ear — most common cause of fluctuating conductive hearing loss), meningitis, seizures, head injury. Developmental history: age of first words, comprehension, play, gesture/pointing, joint attention, motor and adaptive skills, any regression. Nursery/school information where available. Family history: speech delay (heritable), ASD, deafness. Social history: language spoken at home (bilingual exposure does not cause language disorder — consider total vocabulary and functional communication across all languages, using an interpreter if needed; do not assume delayed English is explained by bilingualism), screen use and background TV, opportunities for responsive interaction.
Examination
General: dysmorphic features (Down, fragile X, Williams, 22q11). Head circumference (microcephaly — Rett, Angelman, congenital infection; macrocephaly — fragile X, Sotos). Tympanic membranes (OME — dull, retracted, fluid level, no cone of light). Hearing (informal — not a substitute for audiology). Orofacial: cleft palate (including submucous cleft — examine with a torch, bifid uvula suggests submucosal cleft), tongue tie (significant ankyloglossia impairing articulation — but rarely causes language delay). Neurological: tone, reflexes, coordination. Observation of child: eye contact, pointing, joint attention, response to name, social smile.
Investigations
Core approach (otherwise well child with isolated speech delay): developmental history and examination; Hearing assessment (every child — method by developmental age and cooperation: visual reinforcement audiometry in younger children, play audiometry in preschool children, pure-tone audiometry when able to cooperate, tympanometry where middle-ear effusion is suspected; GP can refer directly to paediatric audiology; do not delay SLT or autism referral while waiting); SLT referral; assessment for social communication difficulties and autism features; review of comprehension, play, motor skills and adaptive function; nursery/school information. M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers) may be used as a toddler screen, usually around 16–30 months — not diagnostic and does not replace developmental history and clinical observation; a positive screen prompts follow-up interview and/or referral via the local autism pathway; a negative screen does not exclude autism. No routine blood tests for isolated speech delay. FBC + ferritin only if risk factors or clinical features of iron deficiency; TFTs if clinically suspected, newborn screening missing/abnormal, or broader developmental concerns. Specialist-led: genetic testing/chromosomal microarray, MRI, EEG and metabolic tests — based on dysmorphism or congenital anomalies, global developmental delay/intellectual disability, microcephaly or abnormal neurological examination, regression, seizures or focal neurological signs. NICE advises against routine medical investigations as part of autism diagnostic assessment unless clinically indicated.
The M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up) is a validated toddler screening tool for autism — it is not diagnostic — it is a 20-item parent questionnaire designed for children aged 16-30 months that screens for the core features of ASD. The R (revised) version has improved specificity over the original M-CHAT. The F (follow-up) module provides structured follow-up questions for intermediate-risk scores to reduce false positives. The tool is freely available and takes approximately 5 minutes to complete — It may be used locally (for example when there is a language or social concern in this age group) but should not replace developmental history and clinical observation. A positive screen should prompt the follow-up interview and/or referral according to the local autism pathway, without waiting for speech therapy assessment results. A negative screen does not exclude autism. The key scoring: low risk (score 0-2) = rescreen at next routine check; medium risk (3-7) = complete follow-up interview; high risk (8-20) = refer via the local autism pathway.

Causes of Speech Delay — Diagnostic Framework

Hearing impairment (common treatable cause)
Conductive: otitis media with effusion (OME / glue ear) — most common. NICE NG233: formal assessment with otoscopy, hearing testing and tympanometry; reassess hearing after 3 months for bilateral OME with hearing loss (unilateral — consider reassessment after 3 months, or intervene earlier if communication or daily life is affected). Options include hearing aids/bone-conduction devices or grommets when OME-related hearing loss is clinically significant (shared decision-making). Grommets may improve hearing; language progress depends on the child's developmental profile, duration of hearing loss and coexisting conditions. Sensorineural: congenital SNHL (TORCH infection, genetic — connexin-26 GJB2 mutation most common). Universal Newborn Hearing Screening (UNHS) detects most bilateral SNHL at birth — but it does not exclude later-onset, mild/moderate, unilateral or fluctuating hearing loss.
Autism spectrum disorder
Speech delay is the most common presenting concern in ASD. Core features: impaired social communication (poor eye contact, reduced joint attention, no pointing or limited pointing, lack of social smile or reduced), repetitive behaviours (hand flapping, spinning, lining up toys), restricted interests, sensory sensitivities. DSM-5 criteria (NICE CG128). Range: highly verbal with subtle social deficits to non-verbal with intellectual disability. Local multidisciplinary autism diagnostic pathway (community paediatrics, paediatric neurodevelopmental services, child psychiatry, psychology, SLT — CAMHS is not the default service for every child). ADOS-2 and ADI-R may support assessment but are not standalone gold-standard tests — NICE states autism-specific tools should not be used alone to diagnose autism.
Environmental / social causes
Limited opportunities for responsive interaction may contribute, but avoid framing parents as the cause of the child's speech delay; safeguarding concerns should be based on the child's welfare and observed circumstances, not on speech delay alone. Bilingual exposure does not cause language disorder: code-switching is normal; consider total vocabulary and functional communication across all languages (interpreter or bilingual SLT where needed). Do not assume delayed English is explained by bilingualism. Refer to SLT as for any child; investigate only if clinically indicated.
Structural / neurological causes
Cerebral palsy (dysarthria — motor speech disorder, not language). Intellectual disability (global developmental delay). Specific language impairment (SLI/DLD — developmental language disorder): normal hearing, normal non-verbal intelligence, no ASD, but persistent language disorder. Cleft palate (hypernasality). Childhood apraxia of speech (CAS — inconsistent consonant errors, groping oral movements). Selective mutism (speaks at home but not in social settings — anxiety-based, not language disorder).
Developmental language disorder (DLD) — previously called specific language impairment (SLI) — is one of the most common neurodevelopmental conditions in children, affecting approximately 7-10% of school-age children. It is defined as a persistent language disorder in a child with: normal hearing, normal non-verbal intelligence, no ASD, no cerebral palsy, and no known genetic syndrome. Children with DLD have significant difficulty with both understanding and using language — grammar, vocabulary, narrative, and phonological processing are all typically impaired. The prognosis is variable, and difficulties often persist into adolescence. DLD is associated with substantial educational, social, and mental health difficulties in adolescence and adulthood. The RCSLT (Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists) estimates that approximately 1-2 children in every classroom of 30 has DLD. Early identification allows SLT and education support to be put in place. GPs should be aware that DLD is not a diagnosis of exclusion made in infancy — it requires formal SLT assessment, and the diagnosis is typically established around age 4-5 when non-verbal intelligence can be reliably assessed.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day / urgent
Developmental regression with seizures, altered consciousness, rapid deterioration or focal neurological signs → paediatric/paediatric neurology · Motor regression at any age → paediatrician/paediatric neurologist · Suspected severe or profound hearing loss where urgent audiology cannot be accessed · Acute neurological symptoms
Prompt community paediatrics / autism referral
Language or social regression under 3 years → autism team · Language regression over 3 years → paediatrics/paediatric neurology first · Positive M-CHAT-R/F after follow-up interview, or significant autism features across settings → local autism pathway (do not delay for SLT first) · Global developmental delay + speech delay · Dysmorphism, microcephaly or abnormal neurological examination · Suspected genetic syndrome · Persistent receptive or mixed receptive-expressive delay · Ex-preterm child with multiple developmental concerns
Speech and Language Therapy (SLT — all speech delay)
Refer to the local speech-and-language therapy service at the earliest appropriate opportunity — promptly for no meaningful words by 16 months, no spontaneous two-word combinations by 24 months, poor comprehension, unclear speech, or persistent parental concern at any age. Pathways vary: some services accept direct parent, nursery or health-visitor referral rather than GP referral. Do not delay referral while awaiting audiology or autism assessment — refer in parallel.
Audiology
Every child with speech delay — in parallel with SLT/autism referral, not before it. Prompt for unclear speech with possible hearing loss, or suspected OME/fluctuating hearing. GP can refer directly to paediatric audiology. Method depends on developmental age and cooperation: visual reinforcement audiometry (VRA) in younger children, play audiometry in preschool children, pure-tone audiometry (PTA) when able to cooperate, tympanometry where middle-ear effusion is suspected. A passed newborn screen (AOAE) does not exclude later-onset, mild, unilateral or fluctuating loss.
ENT
OME with persistent hearing loss at 3-month reassessment (or earlier if communication or daily life is affected) → ENT/audiology to consider hearing aids or grommets with shared decision-making (NICE NG233). Suspected submucous cleft palate → ENT/cleft team. Adenoid hypertrophy causing conductive loss.
Autism pathway / CAMHS / paediatric psychology
ASD diagnostic assessment via the local multidisciplinary autism pathway (CAMHS only where that is the local route). Selective mutism (child mental-health/psychology pathway + school liaison). Significant behavioural comorbidity with DLD.
Local pathways
Insert and verify the local Bolton SLT, paediatric audiology, community paediatrics and autism referral pathways (including whether direct parent, nursery or health-visitor referral is accepted) before use.
The simultaneous referral principle for speech delay is important — GPs should refer to both audiology and SLT at the same consultation, rather than waiting for audiology results before referring to SLT. The reason: NHS waiting times for both audiology and SLT can be long. If the GP waits for audiology before referring to SLT, the total delay to first SLT contact is doubled. Even if hearing loss is found, SLT input is still needed alongside hearing management. Audiology is important because hearing loss is a common, treatable cause — but it should run in parallel with SLT, developmental and autism referrals, not before them. Early SLT access allows support to start sooner.

Speech & Language Therapy Approaches

SLT interventions for pre-schoolers (1-4 years)
Parent-mediated approaches (responsive interaction, shared book reading, nursery support) — specific commercial programmes such as Hanen "It Takes Two to Talk" may be offered by local services but are not universally required. Makaton signing (signs and symbols for children with limited expressive speech — supports communication while speech develops, but should not be expected to accelerate spoken language in every child). AAC such as PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System) introduced according to the child's communication needs, usually with SLT input. Intensive play-based SLT for late talkers.
SLT for school-age children (5+)
Narrative therapy (story grammar — teaching children to structure narratives). Phonological awareness training (preparation for reading — phoneme segmentation, blending, rhyming — Nuffield Dyspraxia Programme for CAS). Vocabulary enrichment programmes. Social communication groups (e.g. Social Thinking, Lego therapy) — optional, service-led approaches, not standard treatments. Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC — e.g. PECS, communication apps, voice output devices) for non-verbal or minimally verbal children, introduced according to communication needs, usually with SLT input.
Grommets for OME with hearing loss + speech delay
Considered when OME-related hearing loss is clinically significant (NICE NG233), using shared decision-making; hearing aids or bone-conduction devices are alternatives. Inserted under GA (day case); usually improve hearing; extrude naturally. Language progress after grommets depends on the child's baseline developmental profile, duration of hearing loss and coexisting conditions. Swimming: grommets without plugs generally safe for surface swimming; avoid diving. Review with SLT post-grommets to assess language catch-up.
Selective mutism treatment
Selective mutism requires a child mental-health/psychology pathway with graded exposure, anxiety-focused psychological treatment, school support and a coordinated home–school plan. Initially: avoid pressure to speak, offer non-verbal communication options, agree a graded exposure plan, involve school/SENCO, and assess for anxiety and other neurodevelopmental conditions. Medication is specialist-led and may be considered only in severe cases when psychological intervention alone has not been sufficient.
Parent-mediated early intervention for speech delay is a core approach for children under 3 — programmes which train parents to modify their communication behaviours (reducing directives, following the child's lead, expanding utterances, using parallel talk and self-talk) can support language development in pre-schoolers. The reason: children aged 1-3 spend the vast majority of their waking hours with their parents/caregivers, not with SLTs (who see them for limited sessions). Changing the quality of every interaction throughout the day — which is what parent-mediated intervention achieves — produces far more learning opportunities than clinic-based therapy alone. Specific programmes (e.g. Hanen 'It Takes Two to Talk') may be offered by local services but are not universally required. GPs should be aware that when families are referred to SLT, the most effective intervention may be offered to the parents rather than directly to the child — this should be explained proactively to avoid parental disappointment.

Treat

Managing Common Underlying Causes

Otitis media with effusion (OME) management
NICE NG233: formal assessment with otoscopy, hearing testing and tympanometry. Bilateral OME with hearing loss: reassess hearing after 3 months. Unilateral hearing loss: consider reassessment after 3 months, or intervene earlier if communication or daily life is affected. Persistent clinically significant hearing loss: consider hearing aids/bone-conduction devices or grommets, using shared decision-making (ENT/audiology). During watchful waiting: preferential seating in classroom (front, near teacher), face-to-face communication, clear speech at home, reduce background noise. Consider auto-inflation (e.g. Otovent balloon) if the child can engage with it. Do not offer antibiotics, oral or nasal corticosteroids, antihistamines or decongestants for OME.
Iron deficiency (when clinically indicated)
Iron deficiency is not an established routine cause of isolated speech delay — blanket testing is not needed. Investigate iron deficiency when clinically indicated, such as restricted diet, excessive cow's milk intake, pallor, fatigue, pica, poor growth or anaemia. Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant — interpret with the clinical picture and local laboratory reference range. Treat confirmed iron deficiency according to BNFC and local paediatric guidance. Dietary sources: red meat, oily fish, fortified cereals, lentils, green leafy vegetables, vitamin C enhances absorption. Avoid excessive cow's milk intake (>500 mL/day in toddlers — reduces iron absorption and appetite for iron-rich food).
Screen use and interaction
WHO (international, 2019) advises no sedentary screen time under 2 years and no more than 1 hour/day for 2-4 year olds; the RCPCH (2019) does not set time limits but advises family screen-time plans and avoiding screens in the hour before bed. Encourage responsive, face-to-face interaction, shared book reading, singing and play. Minimise background television and use a family plan for screen use. Avoid framing parents as the cause of the child's speech delay.
Bilingual children — reassurance and support
Bilingual exposure does not cause language disorder. A child may have fewer words in each individual language, but total vocabulary and functional communication should be considered across all languages. Parents should not be advised to stop using their home language. Assess the child's understanding and use of all languages, using an interpreter or bilingual speech-and-language professional where necessary. Do not assume delayed English is explained by bilingualism (NICE CG128) — refer to SLT if communication across all languages is below expected. Schools: bilingual children benefit from high-quality English language exposure in pre-school/nursery. Interpreter for SLT appointments if needed.
The screen time and speech delay relationship deserves explicit clinical communication because it is modifiable — observational studies (e.g. Chonchaiya 2008, Zimmerman 2007) suggest that background television exposure and heavy screen use in under-2s are associated with reduced parent-child verbal interaction, reduced vocabulary at 24 months, and slower language acquisition. The mechanism is displacement: time spent watching screens is time not spent in face-to-face serve-and-return vocal interaction with adults. The AAP (American Academy of Pediatrics — international) recommends avoiding screen time under 18 months (excluding video calls with family members), and maximum 1 hour per day of high-quality, co-viewed, interactive content for ages 2-5 (not background TV). GPs can ask about screen use when discussing development and suggest positive alternatives — responsive, face-to-face interaction, shared book reading, singing and play, minimising background television and agreeing a family plan for screen use — without framing parents as the cause of the child's speech delay.

Lifestyle

Language-Rich Environment, Reading & Parent Engagement

Serve and return interaction Responsive serve-and-return verbal interaction supports language development — the adult notices the child's focus, responds with language, and the child responds. "Oh, you're looking at the dog! That's a big brown dog. What sound does the dog make?" Every exchange builds neural connections. Encourage parents: narrate daily activities, describe what you see, ask questions, respond to babble as if it's meaningful.
Shared book reading Shared book reading supports language development — reading aloud to children from birth (Reach Out and Read programme) increases vocabulary, phonological awareness, and school readiness. Encourage regular shared reading that fits family routines (not a fixed target). Book Gifting Scheme: Bookstart (UK) provides free books to families at 9 months and 3 years. Choose wordless picture books for non-verbal children — commenting on pictures requires no reading ability. Libraries: free; Rhyme Time sessions.
Nursery rhymes and songs Nursery rhymes build phonological awareness (awareness of sound patterns in words) which supports later reading. Songs build vocabulary, rhythm, and prosody (speech melody). Singing to and with children from birth. Songs in the home language as well as English. Baby Rhyme Time at local libraries. Singing during daily routines: bath time, getting dressed, car journeys.
Reducing background noise Background TV — even when the child is not watching — can reduce parent-child verbal interaction. Minimise background television and use a family plan for screen use. Turn off the TV and radio as background noise during meal times and play. Create a "language-rich, screen-free" hour each day. Quiet environments allow children to hear speech more clearly, which is especially important for children with mild hearing loss from OME.
Support for parents with postnatal depression Parental mental health difficulties such as postnatal depression (PND) can make responsive interaction harder — offer support without framing the parent as the cause of the child's speech delay. PHQ-9 screen at every baby review. NHS Talking Therapies referral for PND (CBT or brief counselling). Peer support (PANDAS foundation). Home-Start volunteer support.
Makaton and signing support Makaton is a language programme using symbols and signs to support communication — it does not delay spoken speech development and can reduce communication frustration and provide multi-modal language input, but should not be described as consistently accelerating spoken language in every child. Signing/AAC should be introduced according to the child's communication needs, usually with SLT input. NHS provides free Makaton workshops in many areas. Mr Tumble (BBC CBeebies) uses Makaton — a rare example of beneficial screen content for language-delayed children.
School readiness and EHCP Children with significant speech and language delay may need an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP) for additional support in school. GPs can support EHCP applications by providing medical information. EHCP process: local authority; paediatric SLT assessment is a key component. Prognosis varies according to receptive language, social communication, hearing, cognition, autism, motor development and family/environmental factors — review progress with SLT and education colleagues.
Support organisations ICAN (ican.org.uk) — UK charity for children with speech and language difficulties; free helpline, information for parents and teachers. Talking Point (talkingpoint.org.uk) — online resource with milestone information and GP/parent tools. RCSLT (rcslt.org) — professional body for SLTs, patient resources. National Deaf Children's Society (ndcs.org.uk) — hearing loss support. National Autistic Society (autism.org.uk).
The Bookstart programme (funded by Booktrust, supported by NHS in the UK) is a universal early-years book-gifting programme — every family in the UK receives a free Bookstart pack at the 9-12 month health visitor review (containing board books, rhyme cards, and reading guidance) and a second pack at age 3. It encourages families to share books from an early age. Health visitors and GPs have the opportunity at the 9-12 month review to: (1) ensure the Bookstart pack was received; (2) demonstrate how to read interactively with a board book; (3) signpost to library Rhyme Time sessions; and (4) emphasise the importance of talking to the baby from birth. A brief Bookstart discussion at the review is a simple way to encourage shared reading.

Follow-Up, Monitoring & School Readiness

Follow-up schedule
2-year developmental review (usually health visitor): tools such as the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-3) or PEDS (Parents' Evaluation of Developmental Status) may be used locally but are not universal mandatory requirements. Record developmental concerns accurately, discuss with the health visitor or community paediatric team, and refer promptly to local SLT/audiology pathways. Urgency depends on regression, hearing loss, global delay, neurological signs and safeguarding concerns. 3-year review: confirm SLT engaged + assess progress + confirm hearing assessment done. 4-5 years (pre-school): confirm school readiness — can child communicate their needs, follow instructions, engage in group activities?
Response to SLT — prognosis varies
Prognosis varies according to receptive language, social communication, hearing, cognition, autism, motor development and family/environmental factors. Progress should be reviewed with SLT and education colleagues. Children with receptive-expressive delay may need EHCP support in school. Autistic children: language development varies widely; support should be needs-led and neurodiversity-affirming — do not present ABA/EIBI as universally indicated or the default treatment. DLD: improvement with therapy but often persistent difficulty — needs ongoing school support.
Monitoring for ASD if initial screen negative
A negative M-CHAT-R/F at 18 months does not exclude ASD — ASD can present later, or early features may be subtle. Continue monitoring at 2-year and 3-year reviews. Any parental concern about ASD at any age: refer via the local autism pathway. ASD diagnosis is not age-limited. Some high-functioning ASD children are not diagnosed until primary or secondary school. Do not make an autism diagnosis dependent on M-CHAT, ADOS or ADI-R alone — NICE requires multidisciplinary assessment based on developmental history, observation, differential diagnosis and information from home and education.
Safeguarding and speech delay
Document: home circumstances, language spoken at home, parent engagement with services. If child is not attending SLT appointments + speech not improving + safeguarding concerns: escalate to HV + social care. Non-engagement with services for a child with significant delay is a safeguarding concern.
Same-day / urgent
Regression with seizures, altered consciousness, rapid deterioration or focal neurological signs → paediatric/paediatric neurology · Motor regression at any age → paediatrician/paediatric neurologist · Suspected severe or profound hearing loss where urgent audiology cannot be accessed · Acute neurological symptoms
Prompt referral
Language/social regression under 3 years → autism team · Language regression over 3 years → paediatrics/paediatric neurology first · Positive M-CHAT-R/F after follow-up interview, or significant autism features across settings → local autism pathway · No meaningful words by 16 months → SLT + audiology · No spontaneous two-word combinations by 24 months → SLT + audiology (+ community paediatrics if broader concerns) · Poor comprehension, or unclear speech with possible hearing loss → SLT + audiology · Persistent parental concern at any age → SLT
The age 2 developmental review is the most important speech and language screening opportunity in primary care — it is the standardised contact where delayed language development can be identified and early intervention initiated before school entry. In England, the 2-year check is conducted by health visitors using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-3) and the integrated review combining the NHS 2-year check with the Early Years Foundation Stage 2-year progress check from the child's early years setting (nursery/childminder). GPs who see children at the 2-year routine immunisation visit should use this contact proactively to: (1) ask about language development using the 24-month milestones as a guide (50+ words, 2-word combinations); (2) consider the M-CHAT-R/F (a screen, not a diagnostic test) if any language or social concern; (3) check tympanic membranes; and (4) refer to SLT and audiology simultaneously if any delay identified. The average age of specialist ASD diagnosis in the UK is 5-6 years — despite families first raising concerns with GPs at 18-24 months. Reducing this diagnostic delay requires GPs to act on concerns and refer promptly via the local autism pathway.
