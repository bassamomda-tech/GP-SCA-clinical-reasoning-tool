Safety

Red Flags — Malignancy, Hyperparathyroid Crisis & Osteomalacia

Adjusted calcium >3.5 mmol/L however well the patient seems, or ≥3.0 mmol/L with significant symptoms; confusion, reduced consciousness, persistent vomiting, dehydration, AKI, arrhythmia/ECG abnormality or suspected hypercalcaemic crisis Same-day emergency hospital assessment; 999 if clinically unstable. Adjusted calcium 3.0–3.5 mmol/L in a stable patient without symptoms still needs same-day face-to-face assessment or specialist discussion, though not automatically a 999 ambulance — urgency depends on symptoms, rate of rise, comorbidity and local acute pathways (Society for Endocrinology: above 3.5 mmol/L needs urgent correction). In primary care: assess ABCDE, repeat/confirm if appropriate but do not delay transfer of an unwell patient, ECG if immediately available, stop contributing calcium/vitamin D preparations, seek urgent hospital advice. IV saline (with cardiac/renal assessment and monitoring for fluid overload) and IV bisphosphonate are hospital treatments, initiated by the medical, endocrine, oncology or acute team with renal and product-specific precautions.
Elevated ALP + bone pain + pathological fracture + weight loss Bone metastases (most commonly from: breast, prostate, lung, kidney, thyroid). → suspected cancer pathway referral for the likely primary; imaging directed by the clinical presentation (suspected spinal cord compression — back pain with neurological symptoms or signs: contact the acute oncology/MSCC team immediately for whole-spine MRI within 24 hours, NICE NG234). Bone-targeted treatment (bisphosphonate/denosumab) is oncology-led.
Persistent hypercalcaemia + suppressed PTH PTH-independent hypercalcaemia — consider malignancy urgently. Confirm, then review malignancy symptoms and examination; FBC, renal function, LFTs, phosphate, ALP, inflammatory markers; protein electrophoresis + serum free light chains if myeloma possible; chest imaging if respiratory symptoms, lymphoma or granulomatous disease suspected; medication/supplement causes; 25-OH vitamin D. Seek specialist advice; suspected cancer pathway for the likely primary site. PTHrP and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D are not routine primary-care tests — only after specialist discussion.
Low phosphate + low calcium + elevated ALP + bone pain + proximal muscle weakness + waddling gait Osteomalacia (vitamin D deficiency in adults — rickets in children). Looser zones (pseudofractures) on X-ray. Vitamin D + calcium replacement. Often severe in housebound elderly or those with low dietary calcium.
Persistent isolated raised non-hepatic ALP (normal calcium, normal phosphate) in an older adult, after excluding recent fracture and other causes Possible Paget's disease of bone (osteitis deformans) — not diagnosed on ALP level alone; discuss with rheumatology/endocrinology or metabolic bone services. X-ray: characteristic "cotton wool" skull, cortical thickening, enlarged pagetic bone. Bisphosphonate for Paget's bone pain (symptom-directed, not to normalise ALP).
Low calcium + high phosphate + elevated PTH + impaired renal function Secondary hyperparathyroidism from CKD (CKD-MBD). Interpret trends in calcium, phosphate, PTH, ALP and renal function together; address hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia and vitamin D deficiency. Renal referral/advice if PTH progressively rising, persistent hyperphosphataemia, hypercalcaemia or complex CKD-MBD — phosphate binders and active vitamin D are renal-led.
Hypercalcaemic crisis is a medical emergency — at serum calcium above 3.0–3.5 mmol/L, patients develop: nausea, vomiting (further dehydration worsening hypercalcaemia), polyuria (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus from calcium impairing renal concentrating ability), constipation, confusion (calcium impairs neuronal function), QT shortening on ECG (shortened ST segment — 'tombstone T-wave'). The initial hospital treatment is IV 0.9% saline — rehydration promotes urinary calcium excretion — with assessment of cardiac and renal function and monitoring for fluid overload (Society for Endocrinology emergency guidance). Loop diuretics (furosemide) should not be used routinely to lower calcium — they are reserved for fluid overload after specialist assessment — older textbooks recommended furosemide to 'flush' calcium, but this increases dehydration and worsens the hypercalcaemia. After rehydration, an IV bisphosphonate (e.g. zoledronic acid) may be given in hospital, with renal-function and product-specific precautions. Denosumab for refractory malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia is an oncology/specialist treatment. PTHrP is not routinely required in primary care or in every patient with suppressed PTH — it is a specialist test when the cause remains unclear.

Interpreting the Bone Profile — A Systematic Approach

Components of the standard bone profile
Adjusted (corrected) calcium: accounts for albumin binding. Use the laboratory's reported adjusted calcium and check its definition — do not re-apply a local formula (e.g. adjusted Ca = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 – albumin)) if the lab already reports it. Normal: 2.20–2.60 mmol/L. Phosphate: normal 0.8–1.5 mmol/L. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP): produced by liver, bone, gut, and placenta. Bone isoform elevated in: high bone turnover (Paget's, bone metastases, healing fractures, osteomalacia). Albumin: for calcium correction. PTH: parathyroid hormone — measure with a concurrent adjusted calcium once hypercalcaemia is confirmed (NICE NG132) or in hypocalcaemia; use the local reference range (e.g. 1.6–6.9 pmol/L). Biotin supplements can interfere with some PTH assays. Ionised calcium is not used routinely to diagnose PHPT.
Hypercalcaemia with elevated/unsuppressed PTH → Primary hyperparathyroidism
Most common cause of hypercalcaemia in outpatients (vs malignancy = most common in hospitalised). PTH inappropriately normal or elevated in the context of high calcium. 80% single adenoma. Symptoms: "bones, stones, groans, and psychic moans." Parathyroid imaging (USS neck, sestamibi) is specialist pre-operative localisation, not a diagnostic test for PHPT. Surgery (parathyroidectomy) is curative for symptomatic or severe hypercalcaemia.
Hypercalcaemia with suppressed PTH → Malignancy or granulomatous disease
PTH below the lower reference limit = non-PTH-driven hypercalcaemia. Malignancy (PTHrP — solid tumours; osteolytic metastases — breast, myeloma; excess 1,25-OH-D — lymphoma). Granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, TB — macrophages produce 1,25-OH-D autonomously). Vitamin D toxicity. Thyrotoxicosis.
Low calcium (hypocalcaemia)
With high PTH (secondary hyperparathyroidism): vitamin D deficiency (most common in UK), CKD. With low PTH (hypoparathyroidism): post-thyroidectomy/parathyroid surgery, autoimmune, magnesium deficiency (Mg required for PTH release — always check Mg in refractory hypocalcaemia).
Elevated ALP only
Bone origin (elevated with pain/fracture): Paget's disease, osteomalacia, metastases. Liver origin (elevated with deranged LFTs): liver disease, cholestasis — GGT elevated in liver ALP. Physiological: pregnancy (placental ALP), growing children, healing fractures. To differentiate: ALP isoforms or GGT (elevated GGT = liver origin; normal GGT + normal LFTs = bone origin).
The ALP isoform distinction is practically important because ALP can be elevated from liver, bone, or other sources, and the clinical significance and management differ completely. The quickest differentiator in primary care is GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) — GGT is elevated when ALP is of hepatic origin (cholestasis, hepatitis, alcohol, hepatic metastases) and is normal when ALP is of bone origin (Paget's, osteomalacia, bone metastases). A pattern of isolated elevated ALP with normal GGT, normal bilirubin, and normal transaminases points definitively to bone ALP — this should trigger bone investigation (repeat and trend; calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, PTH, renal function; radiography or specialist referral directed by symptoms and level) rather than hepatic investigation. Also consider recent fracture or surgery, pregnancy, renal disease and medications. If GGT is also elevated, hepatic investigation takes priority. Some laboratories offer formal ALP isoenzyme fractionation to distinguish bone vs liver ALP — request this if the GGT vs LFT pattern is inconclusive.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigation Pathway

History
Symptoms of hypercalcaemia: "bones, stones, groans, moans" — bone pain, renal stones, nausea/constipation, depression/cognitive impairment. Symptoms of hypocalcaemia: paraesthesiae (perioral, fingertips), muscle cramps, tetany, seizures. Bone pain (Paget's, metastases, osteomalacia). Muscle weakness (vitamin D deficiency — proximal). Weight loss (malignancy). Drug history: thiazides (hypercalcaemia), lithium (hypercalcaemia via PTH), calcium supplements and calcium-containing antacids (milk-alkali), vitamin D and vitamin A supplements, biotin (PTH assay interference), bisphosphonates.
Examination
Chvostek + Trousseau signs (hypocalcaemia). BP (hypertension in primary hyperPTH). Proximal muscle weakness (stand from chair without arms — vitamin D deficiency/osteomalacia). Bony deformity (Paget's — skull enlargement, bow legs, kyphosis). Lymphadenopathy (lymphoma, sarcoidosis, malignancy). Breast/prostate/lung examination if bone metastases suspected. Abdominal mass (renal cell, lymphoma).
Investigation pathway
Measure adjusted calcium if symptoms of hypercalcaemia, osteoporosis/fragility fracture, renal stones, or incidental Ca ≥2.6. Repeat at least once if ≥2.6 (or ≥2.5 with features of PHPT). PTH only when Ca ≥2.6 on 2 occasions (or ≥2.5 on 2 occasions with suspected PHPT), always with a concurrent adjusted calcium (NICE NG132). PTH above the lab midpoint with suspected PHPT, or below midpoint with Ca ≥2.6 → specialist advice; PTH below the lower limit → investigate other causes, particularly malignancy. 25-OH-D (vitamin D status). Albumin (if not in bone profile). Phosphate + Mg (if calcium abnormal). eGFR + creatinine (renal cause/consequence). Suppressed PTH: FBC, LFTs, inflammatory markers + malignancy history/examination. Serum electrophoresis + serum free light chains (if myeloma possible). CXR (if respiratory symptoms, lymphoma or granulomatous disease suspected). PSA only via the usual symptom-led prostate pathway; ACE is not a screening test for sarcoidosis; PTHrP and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D only after specialist discussion. Elevated ALP: GGT + LFTs (liver vs bone). Repeat and trend; bone-specific ALP if available; calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, PTH, renal function if non-hepatic. X-ray symptomatic bones (Paget's). Further imaging directed by the clinical presentation.
Myeloma is an important but frequently delayed diagnosis that GPs must consider in the context of hypercalcaemia — multiple myeloma causes hypercalcaemia through osteoclast activation (RANKL-mediated bone resorption by myeloma cells → calcium release), and the calcium is driven by osteolysis rather than PTH or PTHrP. The key diagnostic tests: serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) showing a paraprotein band (M-protein), serum free light chain ratio (κ/λ ratio — very sensitive for light-chain myeloma), and bone marrow biopsy. The CRAB criteria for myeloma (requiring treatment): Calcium elevated, Renal impairment (creatinine >177 μmol/L), Anaemia (Hb <10g/dL), Bone lesions (lytic lesions — specialist imaging; a skeletal survey is not the preferred investigation). NICE NG12: offer FBC, calcium, ESR/plasma viscosity and protein electrophoresis + serum free light chains to people aged 60 and over with persistent bone pain (particularly back pain) or unexplained fracture; use a suspected cancer pathway if these tests suggest myeloma.

Key Differential Combinations

High Ca + high PTH
Primary hyperparathyroidism (adenoma 80%, hyperplasia 15%, carcinoma rare). Urine calcium assessment to help exclude FHH (familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia). Before or alongside referral: eGFR/creatinine, DXA (lumbar spine, hip and distal radius), renal tract ultrasound, vitamin D. Parathyroid imaging is for pre-operative localisation only. Refer to a parathyroid surgeon if: symptomatic hypercalcaemia, renal stones, fragility fracture or osteoporosis, or adjusted Ca ≥2.85 — referral can be considered even without these (NICE NG132).
High Ca + low PTH
Confirm, then: malignancy symptoms and examination; FBC, renal function, LFTs, phosphate, ALP, inflammatory markers; SPEP + serum free light chains if myeloma possible; chest imaging if respiratory symptoms, lymphoma or granulomatous disease suspected; medication and supplement causes. Vitamin D toxicity: check 25-OH-D. 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D only after specialist discussion (lymphoma/granulomatous disease). Thyrotoxicosis. Seek specialist advice urgently.
Low Ca + high PTH
Vitamin D deficiency (most common — 25-OH-D <25 nmol/L = deficiency). CKD-MBD (secondary hyperparathyroidism). Malabsorption (coeliac, post-bariatric). Check 25-OH-D first — replace if deficient, then recheck PTH.
High ALP only
Repeat and trend; check GGT/LFTs and bone-specific ALP if available; ask about recent fracture or surgery, bone pain, pregnancy, renal disease, vitamin D deficiency, medications, malignancy symptoms. Paget's disease (isolated non-hepatic ALP with normal Ca/PO₄ and focal bone involvement on X-ray/scan — ALP may be normal in limited disease; ">3× ULN" is not a diagnostic criterion). Osteomalacia (ALP elevated + low phosphate + low 25-OH-D + bone pain + proximal myopathy). Healing fracture (recent fracture raises ALP × 2–3 for weeks). Liver disease (check GGT). Bone metastases (ALP elevated + bone pain + primary malignancy known).
Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH)
Important mimic of primary hyperPTH. CASR gene variants (less often GNA11 or AP2S1). Calcium mildly elevated, PTH normal/slightly elevated, urine calcium low. A calcium:creatinine clearance ratio <0.01 supports FHH but is not diagnostic — there is overlap with PHPT, particularly at 0.01–0.02. Falsely low results: vitamin D deficiency, CKD, low calcium intake, thiazides, lithium, incomplete urine collection. Discuss equivocal or strongly suggestive results with endocrinology/genetics. Confirmed FHH generally does not benefit from parathyroidectomy.
Raised PTH with normal calcium
Do not diagnose normocalcaemic PHPT from one raised PTH result — it is a diagnosis of exclusion requiring repeated measurements and exclusion of vitamin D deficiency, CKD, low calcium intake, malabsorption, hypercalciuria and drug effects.
Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH) is the most important benign mimic of primary hyperparathyroidism — failure to diagnose FHH before parathyroid surgery results in unnecessary operations that do not correct the hypercalcaemia (because the mutation is in the calcium-sensing receptor, not the parathyroid glands). The diagnostic test: 24-hour urine calcium + creatinine to calculate the calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio (CCCR). In primary hyperPTH: CCCR >0.02 (elevated urinary calcium from high filtered load). In FHH: CCCR usually <0.01 (kidneys avidly reabsorb calcium because the calcium-sensing receptor mutation makes the kidney 'think' calcium is normal) — but values overlap, especially 0.01–0.02, and results can be falsely low with vitamin D deficiency, CKD, low calcium intake, thiazides, lithium or incomplete collection; inappropriate surgery is a recognised consequence of overinterpreting urinary calcium. CCCR = (urine Ca × serum Cr) / (serum Ca × urine Cr). The 24-hour urine calcium should be checked before referring any patient with hypercalcaemia + mild/normal PTH for parathyroid surgery — a low or equivocal urine calcium should prompt discussion with endocrinology/genetics (family history, CaSR mutation analysis) before any surgical decision.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day / 999
Corrected Ca >3.5 mmol/L however well, or ≥3.0 mmol/L with significant symptoms → same-day hospital assessment; emergency transfer if clinically unstable · Corrected Ca 3.0–3.5 mmol/L without symptoms → same-day face-to-face assessment or specialist discussion · Confusion, reduced consciousness, persistent vomiting, dehydration, ECG abnormality or AKI with hypercalcaemia → emergency assessment · Adjusted Ca <1.90 mmol/L, or hypocalcaemia with tetany, seizure, laryngospasm, significant QT prolongation or arrhythmia → emergency hospital management
Suspected cancer
Suppressed PTH with persistent hypercalcaemia → investigate PTH-independent causes urgently; consider malignancy and seek specialist advice · Hypercalcaemia plus persistent back/bone pain, unexplained fracture or abnormal myeloma screen → suspected cancer/myeloma pathway (NICE NG12)
Endocrinology / parathyroid surgery
Confirmed PHPT with symptoms, renal stones, osteoporosis/fragility fracture or calcium ≥2.85 → endocrinology/parathyroid surgery pathway (NICE NG132) · PHPT with calcium <2.85 and no complications → discuss with endocrinology/local pathway; surveillance may be appropriate · Hypoparathyroidism (new diagnosis)
Rheumatology / respiratory (sarcoidosis)
Hypercalcaemia + suspected sarcoidosis (e.g. bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on chest imaging) — ACE is not a screening test
Nephrology
CKD with progressively rising PTH, persistent hyperphosphataemia, hypercalcaemia or complex CKD-MBD · Persistent hypercalcaemia post-renal transplant
Rheumatology / metabolic bone
Persistent isolated non-hepatic ALP with bone pain → investigate for fracture, Paget's disease, osteomalacia and malignancy; refer if persistent or marked
GP management
Primary hyperPTH surveillance only as agreed with endocrinology/local pathway (age alone does not justify GP-only surveillance). Vitamin D deficiency: replace + recheck. Paget's disease: specialist-led bisphosphonate for bone pain or clinically significant active disease.
Primary hyperparathyroidism surveillance without surgery is appropriate for asymptomatic patients who do not meet surgical criteria — NICE NG132 (2019) refers for surgery if there are symptoms of hypercalcaemia, end-organ disease (renal stones, fragility fracture or osteoporosis) or albumin-adjusted calcium 2.85 mmol/L or above; the international workshop criteria (international) add: serum calcium >0.25 mmol/L above ULN, age under 50, eGFR below 60, osteoporosis (T-score <-2.5 at any site or fragility fracture), 24h urine calcium >10 mmol/24h (nephrolithiasis risk), any symptomatic patient. Patients who do not meet these criteria can be monitored conservatively with: albumin-adjusted calcium and eGFR at least annually (ensure calcium remains stable, not rising), DXA every 2–3 years, and renal tract ultrasound if symptoms develop or a renal stone is suspected (NICE NG132). The surgeon's decision is based on a combination of risk factors and patient preference — NICE advises that referral can be considered even when surgical criteria are absent, so surveillance should be agreed with endocrinology rather than decided on age alone. However, over 10–15 years, approximately 30% of asymptomatic patients will develop an indication for surgery — the monitoring schedule allows timely identification.

Treat

Vitamin D, Paget's & Hypocalcaemia

Vitamin D deficiency (25-OH-D <25 nmol/L)
Loading regimen
Where rapid correction is clinically required: cumulative loading up to approximately 300,000 IU using a licensed preparation and an explicit schedule (e.g. colecalciferol 50,000 IU weekly × 6), OR 4,000 IU daily × 10 weeks (National Osteoporosis Society vitamin D guideline). Then maintenance: 800–2,000 IU/day. Recheck 25-OH-D at 3 months. Pragmatic clinical target 50–75 nmol/L (not a NICE target). Check calcium when indicated — PHPT, granulomatous disease, renal disease, malabsorption, high-dose treatment or hypercalcaemic symptoms. Do not prescribe calcium routinely — aim for adequate dietary calcium; supplement only if intake is inadequate or there is a specific indication (e.g. osteomalacia). Distinguish treatment from public-health supplementation (10 micrograms/400 IU daily for at-risk adults).
Paget's disease of bone Bone pain or clinically significant active disease (specialist-led)
Zoledronic acid 5 mg IV single infusion
Commonly used for Paget's; diagnosis and treatment via rheumatology/endocrinology or metabolic bone services. Check renal function, calcium and vitamin D first; follow the current SPC and local specialist pathway. Treatment is for bone pain or clinically significant active disease, not to normalise ALP in every asymptomatic patient. Oral alternative: risedronate 30 mg OD × 2 months. Side effects: flu-like reaction 24–48h after IV (paracetamol/ibuprofen prophylaxis). Osteonecrosis of jaw risk (rare — dental review before). Recheck ALP at 3–6 months.
Primary hyperPTH — medical management (not surgical candidate)
Cinacalcet — endocrinology-initiated (usually 30 mg BD, specialist titration)
Calcimimetic — increases calcium-sensing receptor sensitivity → reduces PTH secretion → lowers serum calcium. Does NOT improve bone density (unlike surgery). Only when surgery is unsuccessful, unsuitable or declined, and adjusted Ca ≥2.85 with symptoms or ≥3.0 with or without symptoms (NICE NG132). Not initiated in primary care. Calcium after initiation and dose changes per SPC/specialist plan, then every 2–3 months. Cautions: hypocalcaemia and QT prolongation, nausea/vomiting, hepatic impairment, CYP-mediated drug interactions; pregnancy — specialist advice. Continuation per the local shared-care protocol (the 2017 cinacalcet protocol is past its 2020 review date — confirm with the ICB/formulary).
Hypoparathyroidism (post-surgical or autoimmune)
Alfacalcidol + calcium — specialist-led doses (state elemental calcium, not salt)
Active vitamin D analogue (bypasses PTH-dependent hydroxylation step). Target calcium: lower half of normal range (2.0–2.25 mmol/L) to avoid hypercalciuria. Monitor serum calcium, phosphate, magnesium, renal function and urinary calcium at a frequency set by endocrinology. Parathyroid hormone replacement therapy is specialist-only for refractory cases (recombinant PTH 1-84, Natpara, has been discontinued).
Hypocalcaemia — acute
Emergency — send to hospital (IV calcium gluconate with ECG monitoring)
Adjusted calcium <1.90 mmol/L, or tetany, seizures, laryngospasm, or significant paraesthesia with QT prolongation or arrhythmia. Obtain ECG and check calcium, magnesium, phosphate, renal function and PTH where this does not delay treatment. IV calcium gluconate is a hospital treatment. Correct hypomagnesaemia — it impairs PTH secretion and makes hypocalcaemia refractory. Oral maintenance specialist-led.
Zoledronic acid for Paget's disease achieves the most sustained remission of any bisphosphonate — a single IV infusion of 5 mg zoledronic acid is commonly used, after checking renal function, calcium and vitamin D status. The Paget's Association-endorsed 2019 clinical guideline (Ralston et al., JBMR) recommends bisphosphonates for Paget's bone pain, with zoledronic acid the most likely to give a favourable pain response, and recommends symptom-directed treatment rather than treating to normalise ALP. The acute phase reaction (flu-like symptoms 24–72 hours after the infusion: fever, myalgia, headache, fatigue) occurs in approximately 30% of patients receiving their first zoledronic acid infusion. Prophylactic paracetamol or ibuprofen 500–1000 mg 6-hourly for 24–48 hours significantly reduces this reaction. The reaction is mediated by γδ T-cell release of cytokines (pyrogenic) and is less severe with subsequent infusions. Dental review before zoledronic acid is important — osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ), while much rarer with single-dose Paget's treatment than with monthly high-dose oncological bisphosphonate regimens, remains a risk. Patients should have any necessary dental work completed before starting bisphosphonate therapy.

Bisphosphonates for Osteoporosis & Bone Metastases

Osteoporosis treatment (NICE TA464 · NOGG 2024)
Base treatment on fracture risk assessment (FRAX/QFracture, with DXA where appropriate), not T-score alone. First-line: alendronic acid 70 mg weekly (or 10 mg daily). Take on an empty stomach with a full glass of water, remain upright for 30 minutes after (oesophageal erosion prevention). Check renal function and follow product-specific renal restrictions (SPC: not recommended if creatinine clearance <35 ml/min). Avoid in oesophageal stricture, achalasia or inability to remain upright for 30 min. Review persistent dysphagia, odynophagia or worsening reflux promptly. Side effects: oesophagitis (most common), atypical femoral fracture (rare; may be bilateral, with prodromal thigh/groin pain — patients to report thigh, groin or hip pain; if suspected, image both femurs and seek specialist advice), ONJ (rare — report oral symptoms). Duration individualised (NOGG 2024): generally at least 5 years oral, longer if high risk (age, prior hip/vertebral fracture, glucocorticoids, new fracture, current BMD, overall fracture risk); a routine drug holiday based on T-score alone is not appropriate.
IV zoledronate (osteoporosis)
Check renal function, calcium, vitamin D and hydration; review renal and cardiovascular risk; counsel about acute-phase reactions; dental assessment particularly where there is major dental disease or other ONJ risk factors. Generally reviewed after 3 years (NOGG 2024).
Denosumab 60 mg every 6 months (osteoporosis)
Do not stop or delay denosumab without a documented alternative antiresorptive plan — stopping or substantially delaying it can cause rapid bone turnover and multiple vertebral fractures. Before each dose: confirm the dose is due, check adjusted calcium and correct hypocalcaemia, ensure adequate vitamin D and calcium intake, assess renal function and hypocalcaemia risk; enhanced calcium monitoring after the first dose if at increased risk, particularly severe CKD (MHRA). ONJ precautions.
Bone metastases — bisphosphonate role
Oncology treatments, not GP treatment options: IV zoledronic acid and denosumab 120 mg SC monthly reduce skeletal-related events (pathological fractures, spinal cord compression, radiotherapy for bone pain). Denosumab 120 mg monthly for bone metastases is distinct from denosumab 60 mg every 6 months for osteoporosis. GP awareness: patients on these treatments require dental review (ONJ risk), calcium + vitamin D supplementation, and renal function monitoring.
Renal osteodystrophy (CKD-MBD)
Interpret trends in calcium, phosphate, PTH, ALP and renal function together rather than single values (KDIGO 2017). Address modifiable factors first: hyperphosphataemia, high phosphate intake, hypocalcaemia, vitamin D deficiency, CKD progression. Calcitriol and vitamin D analogues are not used routinely in CKD G3a–G5 not on dialysis — reserved for severe and progressive hyperparathyroidism under renal supervision. Phosphate binders are not started solely because CKD is present — persistent hyperphosphataemia and renal pathways guide treatment. There is no universal PTH target for CKD 3b+; the 2–9× ULN range applies principally to CKD G5D on dialysis. Bisphosphonate decisions in CKD with biochemical CKD-MBD need specialist input. Nephrology-led — GP monitors bloods.

Next steps: see Osteoporosis · Chronic kidney disease · Haematological cancers · Malignant hypercalcaemia

The alendronic acid oesophageal injury prevention instructions are one of the most important prescribing instructions in primary care — alendronic acid (and other oral bisphosphonates) are highly corrosive to oesophageal mucosa if they remain in contact with it. The safety rules are absolute: (1) take with a full glass (200 ml) of plain water (not juice, coffee, or mineral water — these reduce bioavailability + increase oesophageal exposure time); (2) take on an empty stomach, at least 30 minutes before any food or drink; (3) remain completely upright (sitting or standing — not reclining or lying down) for at least 30 minutes after taking the tablet. If these instructions are not followed, the tablet can lodge in the oesophagus, causing severe oesophageal ulceration, stricture, or perforation. GPs should ask about compliance with these instructions at every bisphosphonate prescription renewal. Patients who cannot comply (e.g., severe GORD, inability to stand) should be considered for IV zoledronic acid (once yearly — no oesophageal risk) or denosumab 60 mg SC every 6 months (which must not be stopped or delayed without an alternative antiresorptive plan).

Lifestyle

Bone Health, Dietary Calcium & Fall Prevention

Dietary calcium targets Adult: 700 mg/day (UK RNI). Osteoporosis prevention: 1,000–1,200 mg/day. Calcium-rich foods: dairy (milk 120 mg/100ml, yoghurt 200 mg/100g, cheese 800 mg/100g), canned sardines with bones (500 mg/100g), tofu (set with calcium sulphate — 500 mg/100g), fortified plant milks (120 mg/100ml), dark leafy greens (kale 72 mg/100g). Supplements if dietary inadequate: calcium carbonate 500 mg BD (take with meals — requires gastric acid for absorption) or calcium citrate (better absorbed without food, preferable if on PPI or achlorhydria).
Vitamin D optimisation UK RNI: 10 mcg (400 IU)/day universally for all adults — everyone should consider it in autumn and winter (insufficient sunlight for synthesis October–March). At-risk groups (housebound, care homes, skin covered outdoors, dark skin): 10 mcg daily all year round (SACN 2016 / UK government advice); higher doses such as 800–1000 IU/day are a clinical choice for those at high risk of deficiency. Paget's and osteomalacia treatment: 800–2000 IU/day maintenance after loading. Pragmatic clinical target 25-OH-D: 50–75 nmol/L (not a universal NICE target).
Weight-bearing exercise for bone health Weight-bearing exercise (running, brisk walking, dancing, team sports) stimulates bone formation through mechanical loading (Wolff's law). Target: 150 min/week moderate weight-bearing activity. Resistance training (weights, resistance bands) particularly effective for upper limb and spine bone density. Swimming and cycling: cardiovascular benefit but less bone-specific stimulus. Prescribe exercise at every bone health consultation.
Fall prevention for osteoporosis patients NICE NG249 multifactorial falls intervention: lying-to-standing BP (orthostatic hypotension), medication review (STOPP — sedatives, antihypertensives), strength + balance physiotherapy, vision assessment, home hazard assessment (OT), vitamin D/calcium. Hip protectors for very high-risk patients. Annual review: fracture risk calculated (FRAX or QFracture).
Smoking and bone health Smoking increases bone loss (antioestrogen effect + direct osteoclast stimulation) and is a recognised fracture risk factor (FRAX/NOGG). NHS Stop Smoking Service referral. A concrete motivational message: "Stopping smoking will reduce your fracture risk significantly — it is one of the most effective things you can do for your bones."
Alcohol and bone health Alcohol intake of 3 or more units a day is a recognised fracture risk factor (FRAX/NOGG), with reduced bone density and increased fracture risk — direct toxic effect on osteoblasts + impaired calcium absorption + falls risk. Advise keeping within 14 units/week (UK Chief Medical Officers). Advise AUDIT-C at each bone health consultation.
Paget's disease activity monitoring ALP every 6–12 months after bisphosphonate treatment (confirms remission). Neurological symptoms (hearing loss from temporal bone Paget's — audiology referral; spinal cord compression from vertebral Paget's — same-day assessment and urgent MRI). Sarcomatous transformation is rare — acute or progressive severe pain in known Paget's warrants urgent specialist assessment and imaging.
Primary hyperPTH dietary considerations During surveillance: maintain adequate dietary calcium and vitamin D (calcium restriction paradoxically increases PTH secretion — do NOT restrict calcium). Adequate hydration (reduces renal stone risk). Avoid thiazide diuretics (increase calcium reabsorption → worsen hypercalcaemia). Avoid calcium supplements above dietary intake if calcium already elevated.
The calcium restriction paradox in primary hyperparathyroidism is counter-intuitive but important — GPs might instinctively advise patients with hypercalcaemia from primary hyperPTH to restrict dietary calcium. This is incorrect: calcium restriction in primary hyperPTH increases PTH secretion (the parathyroid adenoma is autonomous but still responds to a perceived low calcium signal by secreting more PTH), which then stimulates: (1) bone resorption (worsening osteoporosis), and (2) increased 1,25-OH-D production (worsening gut calcium absorption in the long run by stimulating vitamin D activation). The correct advice: maintain normal dietary calcium intake (700–1,000 mg/day from food, not supplements), ensure adequate hydration (within cardiac and renal limits, to prevent renal stones), and avoid calcium supplements unless vitamin D deficiency is also present. Adequate hydration is the most important non-surgical intervention for preventing renal stones in primary hyperPTH — the most common complication of untreated primary hyperPTH.

Safety

Follow-Up & Monitoring

Primary hyperPTH — surveillance (non-surgical)
At least annually: albumin-adjusted calcium and eGFR. DXA every 2–3 years. Renal tract ultrasound if symptoms develop or a renal stone is suspected (NICE NG132). Refer for surgery if: adjusted calcium reaches ≥2.85, osteoporosis or fragility fracture develops, renal stones develop, or patient becomes symptomatic (NICE NG132).
Vitamin D deficiency — after loading
25-OH-D at 3 months (confirm repletion). PTH should normalise within 3–6 months if vitamin D was the sole cause of hyperPTH. If PTH remains elevated after vitamin D replete → check adjusted calcium and renal function and consider coexisting primary hyperPTH (normocalcaemic PHPT is a diagnosis of exclusion — see Step 4).
Osteoporosis on bisphosphonate
Review oral bisphosphonate after at least 5 years and IV zoledronate after 3 years (NOGG 2024). Treatment pauses individualised by age, prior hip/vertebral fracture, glucocorticoid use, new fractures, current BMD and overall fracture risk — not T-score alone. Ask about thigh, groin or hip pain and oral symptoms. Denosumab: never stop or delay without an alternative plan.
Paget's disease on bisphosphonate
ALP and symptom response at 3–6 months, then as advised by the specialist. Audiology if temporal Paget's (hearing loss). Neurology/spinal: any new neurological symptoms → same-day assessment and urgent MRI.
Same-day / 999
Adjusted Ca >3.5 however well, or ≥3.0 with significant symptoms, or confusion/vomiting/dehydration/AKI/ECG change → same-day hospital assessment (999 if unstable) · Adjusted Ca <1.90 or suspected acute symptomatic hypocalcaemia (tetany, seizure, laryngospasm, paraesthesia with QT prolongation or arrhythmia) → emergency hospital management · Acute or progressive severe bone pain in Paget's → urgent specialist assessment and imaging
Within 2 weeks
Persistent hypercalcaemia with suppressed PTH → malignancy work-up urgently, in parallel; same-day if unwell or malignancy features · Persistent elevated ALP with bone pain + low 25-OH-D → osteomalacia → loading vitamin D + rheumatology if severe
Safety-net — say it
Call 999 for confusion or drowsiness, a fit or collapse, chest pain or an irregular heartbeat, a tight throat or noisy breathing with tingling or cramps, or back pain with new leg weakness or numbness or loss of bladder or bowel control. Contact us or 111 the same day for vomiting or being unable to keep fluids down, severe thirst or passing much more urine, new tingling around the mouth or fingers or muscle cramps, or new bone pain or a fracture. On a bisphosphonate or denosumab: report thigh, groin or hip pain and mouth or jaw symptoms, and never let a denosumab dose become overdue. If your calcium is high, take no calcium or vitamin D supplements unless agreed. Remote or out of hours: adjusted calcium ≥3.0 mmol/L or any symptom above needs same-day face-to-face assessment; <1.90 mmol/L is an emergency (Step 5).
Current NOGG 2024 guidance generally advises at least 5 years of oral bisphosphonate (longer for higher-risk patients) and review of IV zoledronate after 3 years; any treatment pause is individualised by age, prior hip/vertebral fracture, glucocorticoid use, new fractures, current BMD and overall fracture risk. The treatment review is a National Osteoporosis Guideline Group recommendation that applies to alendronic acid and other oral bisphosphonates for osteoporosis — after 5 years of bisphosphonate use, bisphosphonate molecules accumulate in bone (half-life in bone approximately 10+ years) and the risk of atypical subtrochanteric or diaphyseal femoral fractures increases. The drug holiday (stopping bisphosphonate for 2–3 years) allows clearance of accumulated drug while residual bone benefit is maintained. The decision is not based on T-score alone — it combines DXA reassessment with clinical risk factors and overall fracture risk. During any treatment pause, the patient should maintain adequate calcium and vitamin D intake (supplementing only where needed), weight-bearing exercise, fall prevention measures, and annual FRAX reassessment. Restart bisphosphonate if FRAX indicates high fracture risk during the holiday.
