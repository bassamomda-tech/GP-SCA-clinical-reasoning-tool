1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Haematological Emergency

Screen for acute leukaemia, blast crisis, suspected leucostasis and sepsis. Urgency depends on the clinical state, differential count, blood-film findings, haemoglobin and platelet results, rate of change, organomegaly and symptoms—not the total WCC alone. Follow the laboratory comment and local haematology referral pathway.

Hyperleukocytosis / leucostasis Contact the acute haematology service urgently for WCC ≥100 × 10⁹/L, circulating blasts, rapidly rising WCC or symptoms suggestive of leucostasis. Arrange emergency assessment if there are neurological, respiratory, cardiac or visual symptoms, or clinical instability; transport is determined by the clinical state.
Blasts on film Any reported blasts or abnormal immature-cell population → urgent discussion with haematology, generally the same day (contact the reporting haematologist or local advice line). Emergency assessment if clinical instability, anaemia, thrombocytopenia, bleeding or infection.
Severe symptoms Headache, confusion, visual changes, breathlessness, chest pain or priapism with marked leucocytosis → emergency assessment (suspected leucostasis; diagnosis and management are specialist-led)
Cytopenias Leucocytosis with new anaemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, abnormal red-cell indices, blasts or a leukoerythroblastic film → urgent haematology advice. Emergency assessment for active bleeding, severe anaemia symptoms, febrile neutropenia, sepsis or haemodynamic instability.
B symptoms Unexplained fever, drenching night sweats or weight loss → very urgent FBC (within 48 hours) for leukaemia; consider the lymphoma suspected cancer pathway if there is unexplained generalised lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, especially with fever, night sweats, weight loss, pruritus or breathlessness (NICE NG12)
Marked splenomegaly Marked splenomegaly with abnormal blood counts → same-day haematology discussion (CML, myelofibrosis)
Suspected sepsis Assess using clinical observations, organ dysfunction, perfusion, mental state and the local adult sepsis pathway; a high NEWS2 triggers urgent review and escalation. Escalate immediately for shock, altered consciousness, severe respiratory compromise or rapidly deteriorating physiology.
Unexplained bruising/bleeding Petechiae, gum bleeding, haematuria → same-day haematology (leukaemia with DIC); emergency assessment if active significant bleeding

Hyperleukocytosis (commonly defined as WCC >100 × 10⁹/L) can cause leucostasis, but the risk varies with the underlying disease, blast count, myeloid versus lymphoid lineage, symptoms, platelet and haemoglobin results and biochemical evidence of tumour lysis. A numerical WCC threshold does not automatically mandate a 999 ambulance in a clinically stable patient; diagnosing and managing leucostasis/hyperviscosity is haematology-led.

Blast cells indicate acute leukaemia (AML/ALL). Some laboratory “blast” or “immature cell” flags need confirmation by a manual film, so contact the reporting haematologist/pathologist or local haematology advice line rather than automatically sending every patient by 999.

B symptoms (fever, sweats, weight loss) in the context of leucocytosis suggest haematological malignancy. NICE NG12 sets very urgent FBC and suspected cancer pathway referral routes for suspected haematological cancer.

Septic leucocytosis can be marked (leukaemoid reaction). The leucocytosis is secondary; the priority is identifying and treating the source of infection urgently.

2
Diagnose

Confirm WCC Elevation — Repeat FBC and Differential

Confirm persistent leucocytosis and identify the predominant white cell lineage. A single raised WCC may be transient (infection, stress, exercise); a single isolated mild leucocytosis with a clear explanation in a well patient is often reactive and does not need immediate repeat testing.

Review first
Review the original FBC and absolute differential, haemoglobin, platelets, red-cell indices, previous results and trend. Read the laboratory comment and abnormal-cell flags. Check whether the abnormality is absolute or relative.
Repeat FBC
If clinically well and the differential and film are reassuring, repeat after the suspected reactive cause has resolved — often within 2–6 weeks, depending on the clinical context and laboratory advice. Repeat sooner or discuss with haematology if the WCC is rising, markedly abnormal, or associated with cytopenias, organomegaly, abnormal cells or systemic symptoms.
WCC differential
Request full differential — neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils. Essential for phenotyping.
Blood film
Request blood film if the elevation is persistent or unexplained, there are cytopenias, the differential is abnormal, there are immature cells, nucleated red cells or left shift, or there is splenomegaly or systemic concern. Identifies blasts, atypical lymphocytes, left shift.
Clinical context
Recent infection? Medicines (steroids, G-CSF, beta-agonists)? Smoking? Splenectomy? Inflammatory disease? Recent surgery, trauma or exercise? Travel and parasite exposure? B symptoms? Malignancy history?
Persistent elevation
Judge persistence on the trend, the differential and film, and the laboratory comment/local pathway rather than a fixed interval or WCC cut-off.

Transient leucocytosis is common after infection, stress, exercise, or smoking. A single raised WCC does not mandate extensive investigation. Repeating the FBC avoids unnecessary anxiety and costly tests.

The differential is key. Neutrophilia (infection, inflammation, medicines, smoking; rarely CML — neutrophilia itself does not indicate bacterial infection), lymphocytosis (viral infection, CLL), eosinophilia (allergy, parasites), and monocytosis (chronic infection, CMML) each have distinct causes and management pathways.

Blood film review by a haematologist can detect morphological abnormalities (blasts, smudge cells, left shift) that automated counters miss. It is the single most important diagnostic step after confirming persistent elevation.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Identify Predominant Cell Lineage

Classify leucocytosis by the cell type that is elevated. This determines the differential diagnosis and investigation pathway.

Neutrophilia
Neutrophils >7.5 × 10⁹/L (laboratory reference intervals vary). Causes: Infection, inflammation (RA, IBD), corticosteroids, smoking, obesity, physiological stress, trauma/surgery/tissue injury, beta-agonists and other medicines, G-CSF; less commonly malignancy or CML. Neutrophilia itself does not indicate bacterial infection.
Lymphocytosis
Lymphocytes >4 × 10⁹/L. Causes: Viral infection (EBV, CMV, pertussis), CLL, lymphoma, acute stress (children). Persistent absolute lymphocytosis needs assessment of the absolute count, blood film and clinical context. CLL requires a persistent clonal B-cell lymphocytosis, generally ≥5 × 10⁹/L for at least 3 months, confirmed by immunophenotyping — not simply a raised lymphocyte count. Reactive lymphocytosis may persist after viral infection. Atypical lymphocytes, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, cytopenias or B symptoms increase urgency.
Monocytosis
Monocytes >1 × 10⁹/L. Causes: Chronic infection (TB, endocarditis), autoimmune disease (SLE, sarcoid), CMML, recovery from neutropenia. For persistent unexplained monocytosis, review the blood film, assess for infection/inflammation and discuss with haematology if persistent, progressive or associated with anaemia, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly or dysplastic changes.
Eosinophilia
Eosinophils >0.5 × 10⁹/L (mild 0.5–1.5 × 10⁹/L; persistent ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L, or eosinophilia with systemic symptoms/organ involvement, warrants further assessment — BSH 2017). Causes: Allergy, asthma, parasites (strongyloides, schistosomiasis), drugs, vasculitis (Churg-Strauss), Hodgkin lymphoma, hypereosinophilic syndrome.
Basophilia
Basophils >0.1 × 10⁹/L (rare). Causes: CML, polycythaemia vera, myelofibrosis, hypothyroidism, hypersensitivity. Not pathognomonic for CML; persistent basophilia, particularly with neutrophilia, left shift, thrombocytosis, splenomegaly or constitutional symptoms, should prompt haematology discussion.
Mixed picture
If multiple lineages elevated, consider myeloproliferative disorder (CML, PV, ET, myelofibrosis) or reactive marrow response to severe inflammation.

Neutrophilia is the most common leucocytosis pattern. Most are reactive (infection, inflammation, steroids, smoking). Persistent unexplained neutrophilia, especially with left shift, basophilia or splenomegaly, raises concern for CML.

Lymphocytosis: CLL requires a persistent clonal B-cell lymphocytosis (generally ≥5 × 10⁹/L for at least 3 months) confirmed by immunophenotyping (CD5+/CD19+/CD23+). Request flow cytometry through the local haematology pathway or according to laboratory criteria, rather than automatically for every lymphocyte count >5 × 10⁹/L.

Eosinophilia requires a full history including travel and medication. Patients who are otherwise well with mild eosinophilia (0.5–1.5 × 10⁹/L) may not require extensive testing; persistent eosinophilia ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L or eosinophilia with systemic symptoms/organ involvement warrants further assessment (BSH 2017). Before prescribing systemic corticosteroids to a patient with unexplained eosinophilia and relevant travel/exposure history, consider Strongyloides testing or specialist advice — untreated strongyloidiasis can become life-threatening after immunosuppression.

Basophilia is rare and is not pathognomonic for CML. In CML, basophils are often accompanied by the entire spectrum of myeloid maturation on blood film (left shift).

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Identify Organomegaly and Lymphadenopathy

Examine for splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and lymphadenopathy. These findings narrow the differential and guide referral urgency.

Vital signs
Fever >38°C → active infection, lymphoma. Tachycardia, hypotension → sepsis (requires same-day assessment).
Lymph nodes
Palpate cervical, axillary, inguinal chains. Unexplained generalised lymphadenopathy, or hard, fixed, painless nodes → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral (lymphoma, CLL), especially with fever, night sweats, weight loss, pruritus or breathlessness. Mobile, tender → reactive lymphadenopathy (infection).
Spleen
Percuss and palpate left upper quadrant. Palpable spleen → CML, CLL, lymphoma, myelofibrosis, infection (EBV, malaria). Measure cm below costal margin.
Liver
Percuss liver span. Hepatomegaly → infiltration (leukaemia, lymphoma), congestion (right heart failure), infection (hepatitis, abscess).
Skin
Petechiae, purpura, bruising or bleeding → thrombocytopenia with leukaemia. Vasculitic rash → vasculitis. Erythema nodosum → sarcoidosis, TB, IBD. Sweet syndrome (neutrophilic dermatosis) → AML.
Chest
Auscultate for crackles (pneumonia, pulmonary infiltration in acute leukaemia). Reduced air entry → effusion (lymphoma).
Joints
Synovitis, effusion → reactive arthritis (infection), inflammatory arthritis (RA, SLE causing neutrophilia).
Thrombosis / hyperviscosity
Signs of venous or arterial thrombosis. Neurological, visual, respiratory or cardiac symptoms with marked leucocytosis → emergency assessment.

Splenomegaly in leucocytosis is highly significant. Massive splenomegaly suggests CML, myelofibrosis, or hairy cell leukaemia. Moderate splenomegaly occurs in CLL, lymphoma, and infectious mononucleosis.

Lymphadenopathy differentiates malignant from reactive causes. Unexplained generalised lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, especially with fever, night sweats, weight loss, pruritus or breathlessness, may justify a suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12); there is no universal node-size or duration rule. Tender, mobile nodes suggest reactive hyperplasia.

Hepatomegaly + splenomegaly together strongly suggest haematological malignancy (CML, CLL, lymphoma) or chronic infection (EBV, CMV). Isolated hepatomegaly is more often due to liver pathology or heart failure.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — Bloods, Film, and Targeted Tests

Investigate based on the predominant cell lineage and clinical context. Avoid shotgun testing; target investigations to the likely differential.

Baseline bloods
Repeat FBC + differential CRP and/or ESR Renal + liver function Bone profile LDH (if concern about haematological disease, haemolysis or tissue injury). Do not order uric acid solely because the WCC is high — tumour lysis risk relates to specific haematological malignancies and treatment initiation.
Blood film
Blood film + manual differential — where indicated (persistent or unexplained elevation, cytopenias, abnormal differential, immature cells, splenomegaly or systemic concern). Identifies blasts, left shift, atypical lymphocytes, smudge cells (CLL).
Neutrophilia workup
Look for a source: CRP (infection), Chest X-ray (pneumonia) or Urine culture if clinically indicated. Persistent unexplained neutrophilia with basophilia, left shift or splenomegaly → discuss with haematology rather than requesting BCR-ABL from primary care on the WCC alone.
Lymphocytosis workup
Persistent unexplained absolute lymphocytosis: Blood film (smudge cells), EBV/CMV serology if clinically suggested. Immunophenotyping (flow cytometry for CLL) through the local haematology pathway or according to laboratory criteria — not automatically for every count >5 × 10⁹/L. Immunoglobulins if recurrent infections.
Eosinophilia workup
Full history (including travel and medication) and examination; FBC + blood film Renal/liver function + bone profile LDH ESR and/or CRP Vitamin B12 (BSH 2017). Then select further tests — Stool microscopy (parasites), Strongyloides serology, IgE, ANCA (Churg-Strauss), Tryptase (mastocytosis) — according to travel, medication, allergic disease, vasculitic symptoms, organ involvement and persistence, not for every patient. Otherwise-well patients with mild eosinophilia (0.5–1.5 × 10⁹/L) may not need extensive testing.
Monocytosis workup
Persistent unexplained monocytosis: review the blood film and assess for infection/inflammation — Blood cultures if endocarditis suspected, HIV test. Discuss with haematology if persistent, progressive or with anaemia, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly or dysplastic changes; bone marrow investigation (CMML) is specialist-led.
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT perform bone marrow biopsy in primary care. Do NOT request JAK2 mutation without haematology input. Do NOT request BCR-ABL on the WCC alone, immunophenotyping for every lymphocytosis, or bone-marrow referral based solely on a numerical threshold. Do NOT request CT abdomen unless there is a clinical indication or specialist recommendation.

Blood film review is the single highest-yield investigation. It can identify acute leukaemia, CML, CLL and infectious mononucleosis, and morphological abnormalities that automated differentials may miss.

Flow cytometry (immunophenotyping) is diagnostic for CLL (CD5+/CD19+/CD23+). It can be performed on peripheral blood and does not require bone marrow biopsy in most cases. Request it through the local haematology pathway.

LDH elevation is non-specific but indicates high cell turnover. Seen in haematological malignancy, haemolysis, tissue injury and tumour lysis syndrome. Interpret alongside the film and clinical picture; no single LDH value is a referral threshold.

Targeted investigation based on predominant cell type avoids unnecessary tests and anxiety. A patient with isolated neutrophilia does not need parasite serology; a patient with eosinophilia does not need BCR-ABL testing.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to Involve Haematology

Refer based on the clinical state, differential count, blood film findings, cytopenias, rate of change, organomegaly and symptoms — not the total WCC alone. Use the local laboratory abnormal-result pathway and haematology referral guideline.

Emergency assessment
Clinical shock or sepsis. Reduced consciousness. Severe respiratory or cardiac symptoms. Neurological or visual symptoms with marked hyperleukocytosis; suspected leucostasis. Active significant bleeding. Priapism. Suspected acute leukaemia with clinical instability. Febrile neutropenia or severe immunosuppression. WCC ≥100 × 10⁹/L in a stable patient needs urgent haematology involvement and usually hospital assessment, with transport determined by the clinical state — not an automatic 999 ambulance.
Same-day haematology
Blasts or a clearly abnormal immature-cell population. Hyperleukocytosis with symptoms or a rapidly rising count. Leucocytosis plus unexplained anaemia or thrombocytopenia. Marked splenomegaly with abnormal blood counts. Leukoerythroblastic or markedly dysplastic film. Suspected acute leukaemia or blast transformation. Severe unexplained eosinophilia with organ involvement.
Suspected cancer: haematology
NICE NG12 — very urgent FBC (within 48 hours) for pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent or recurrent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, unexplained bruising, bleeding or petechiae, or hepatosplenomegaly. Lymphoma: unexplained generalised lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, especially with fever, night sweats, weight loss, pruritus or breathlessness, may justify suspected cancer referral. Use NG12 for symptoms, not numerical WCC thresholds.
Urgent haematology referral (local pathway)
Not NG12 criteria on a count alone: persistent unexplained lymphocytosis, neutrophilia, monocytosis or basophilia, or a rising count, after reactive causes have been addressed — follow the local laboratory abnormal-result and haematology referral pathway.
Routine haematology
Persistent unexplained eosinophilia ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L (BSH 2017). Persistent unexplained monocytosis. Abnormal blood film (left shift, atypical cells) without urgent features. Persistent unexplained leucocytosis after reactive causes have been addressed (local laboratory pathway).
Primary care management
Mild leucocytosis with a clear reactive cause (infection, smoking, steroids) in a well patient with a reassuring differential and film. Transient elevation that settles once the reactive cause resolves. Neutrophilia with active infection responding to treatment.
Specialist referrals
Infectious diseases: Persistent fever, travel history, suspected TB/HIV. Respiratory: Eosinophilia with asthma exacerbations. Rheumatology: Vasculitis (eosinophilia + ANCA+).

Total WCC alone does not set urgency. Hyperleukocytosis with symptoms, a rapidly rising count, blasts or cytopenias warrants same-day haematology discussion; a stable patient with a high count needs urgent haematology involvement, with setting and transport determined by the clinical state.

Persistent lymphocytosis needs the absolute count, film and clinical context; CLL requires clonality confirmed by immunophenotyping, arranged through the local haematology pathway (BSH CLL guideline 2022). NICE NG12 covers symptom-based suspected haematological cancer referral.

Reactive leucocytosis (infection, steroids, smoking, stress) is common in primary care. It does not require haematology referral if the WCC settles once the underlying cause has been treated.

Blood film abnormalities (blasts, left shift, smudge cells) mandate referral even if WCC is only mildly elevated. Morphology is more important than absolute count in detecting malignancy.

7
Treat

Treatment — Treat the Underlying Cause

Leucocytosis is a symptom, not a disease. Treatment targets the underlying cause. Primary care can manage reactive causes; haematological malignancy requires specialist input.

Infection-related neutrophilia
Antibiotics Treat source
Treat a clinically diagnosed infection according to the relevant current antimicrobial guideline and local Greater Manchester formulary. A raised WCC is not itself an indication for antibiotics. Repeat the FBC after the infection has resolved if the count was unexplained or markedly abnormal.
Steroid-induced leucocytosis
Review medicines Do not taper for WCC
Review corticosteroids and other medicines as possible causes of neutrophilia (effect depends on dose, timing, duration and individual response). Do not taper steroids solely to correct the WCC; never stop corticosteroids abruptly when clinically required. Interpret the count alongside symptoms, CRP, differential and treatment indication.
Smoking-related neutrophilia
Smoking cessation First-line
Smoking can contribute to persistent mild neutrophilia. Offer smoking cessation (varenicline or NRT, local stop-smoking service) for general health benefit, not as a treatment with a guaranteed WCC response.

Haematological malignancy treatment: Managed by haematology. Primary care role is supportive care, monitoring for complications, and managing treatment side effects.

CLL (watch and wait)
Asymptomatic CLL does not require immediate chemotherapy. Follow the haematology monitoring plan. Treat infections promptly. Vaccinate (flu, pneumococcal). Avoid live vaccines if immunocompromised.
CML, MPNs and other haematological disorders
Managed according to the haematology treatment plan. GP role: act on the specialist monitoring plan; identify infection, bleeding, thrombosis and treatment adverse effects; provide vaccination and general preventive care; communicate deterioration to haematology. Aspirin in ET/PV is not a generic GP intervention.
Reactive eosinophilia
Antiparasitic treatment depends on the confirmed or suspected parasite, travel and exposure, weight, pregnancy and organ function — follow local or specialist (infectious diseases) advice. Stop causative drugs (NSAIDs, antibiotics) where possible.
Monitoring on treatment
For known CLL, CML or another haematological disorder, follow the specialist-written monitoring schedule rather than a generic GP schedule.

Reactive leucocytosis resolves when the underlying cause is treated. Treating infection, reviewing medicines, smoking cessation and discontinuing causative drugs address the cause. There is no role for treating the WCC itself.

CLL watch-and-wait is evidence-based. Early chemotherapy does not improve survival in asymptomatic CLL. Treatment is reserved for symptomatic disease (bulky nodes, cytopenia, B symptoms) and is haematology-led.

Parasite treatment for eosinophilia should be guided by the confirmed or suspected parasite and local or specialist advice. Stool microscopy has low sensitivity for strongyloides; seek infectious-diseases advice before presumptive treatment in high-risk patients (especially before steroids or immunosuppression).

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Reduce Risk and Optimize Health

Lifestyle interventions reduce cardiovascular and infection risk in patients with chronic leucocytosis, particularly those with haematological malignancy.

Smoking cessation Smoking can contribute to persistent mild neutrophilia and increases cardiovascular risk. Offer varenicline or NRT and refer to the local stop-smoking service, for general health benefit.
Infection prevention Hand hygiene, avoid sick contacts, annual flu vaccine, pneumococcal vaccination per UKHSA Green Book chapter 25. For CLL patients, avoid live vaccines (yellow fever, MMR) if immunosuppressed.
Dental hygiene Routine dental care is beneficial for general health. Endocarditis prophylaxis is not indicated simply because of leucocytosis, CML, CLL or splenomegaly.
Cardiovascular risk reduction Myeloproliferative disorders (PV, ET) increase thrombosis risk. Cardiovascular prevention follows the relevant disease-specific guideline; aspirin or cytoreduction in MPNs is haematology-led.
Alcohol moderation Alcohol suppresses immunity and worsens cytopenia in haematological malignancy. Recommend ≤14 units/week. Abstinence if liver involvement or on chemotherapy.
Sun protection Patients on immunosuppression (chemotherapy, steroids) have higher skin cancer risk. SPF 50+ daily, avoid midday sun, annual skin checks.
Exercise Moderate exercise (150 min/week) reduces fatigue in CLL and myeloproliferative disorders. Avoid contact sports if thrombocytopenic or splenomegaly (risk of splenic rupture).
Dietary advice No specific diet alters WCC. Nutritional and neutropenic dietary advice during chemotherapy should follow the local oncology/haematology protocol.

Smoking cessation is offered for general health benefit; it may help a smoking-related mild neutrophilia, but a WCC response is not guaranteed.

Infection prevention is critical in CLL and other lymphoproliferative disorders. Hypogammaglobulinaemia is common in CLL and increases infection risk; offer flu and pneumococcal vaccination.

Thrombosis risk is increased in myeloproliferative disorders (ET, PV). Aspirin and cytoreductive therapy are haematology-led decisions; aspirin may be contraindicated depending on platelet count, bleeding risk and specialist management.

Exercise improves quality of life and reduces fatigue in haematological malignancy. A Cochrane review (2019) showed moderate exercise reduces cancer-related fatigue by 30% (effect size 0.3). Avoid exercise if platelet count <50 × 10⁹/L (bleeding risk).

9
Safety

Follow-Up — Monitor Response and Safety-Net for Deterioration

Follow-up depends on the likely cause and the initial results. Repeat testing after the suspected reactive cause has resolved, using the laboratory comment and local referral guidance. Escalate sooner if the WCC rises, the differential becomes abnormal, cytopenias develop, blasts appear, organomegaly is found or systemic symptoms emerge.

After cause resolves
Repeat FBC once the suspected reactive cause (infection, steroids) has resolved — often within 2–6 weeks, depending on context and laboratory advice. If the WCC is rising or the differential is abnormal, request a blood film and discuss with haematology.
On repeat result
If normalised, no further routine testing. If persistent and unexplained, follow the local laboratory pathway / routine haematology referral. Review symptoms (fevers, sweats, weight loss).
Known haematological disorder
For known CLL, CML or another haematological disorder, follow the specialist-written monitoring schedule and communicate deterioration to haematology.
Safety-net emergency
Sudden severe headache, confusion, visual loss, chest pain or breathlessness (possible leucostasis). Priapism. Severe bleeding or bruising. Fever with rigors or feeling very unwell (possible sepsis).
Safety-net same-day
Rising WCC, new cytopenias or blasts on monitoring. New lymphadenopathy. New fevers >38°C with rigors. New abdominal distension (splenomegaly). Drenching night sweats or unexplained weight loss.
Re-referral criteria
Any new red-flag symptoms (B symptoms, organomegaly). WCC rising despite treatment. Development of new cytopenias (interpreted in clinical context). Patient anxiety about malignancy (haematology can reassure).

WCC trajectory is more important than absolute value. A rising WCC (e.g., 12 → 15 → 20 over 3 months) suggests malignancy or evolving myeloproliferative disorder even if each individual value is not alarm-level.

CLL progression is monitored according to the specialist plan. Contact haematology earlier for a rapidly rising lymphocyte count, new cytopenia, progressive lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, or B symptoms.

Symptoms suggesting leucostasis (headache, visual changes, confusion, breathlessness) are medical emergencies requiring emergency assessment; diagnosis and management are haematology-led.

Patient anxiety about leucocytosis is common. Clear explanation, regular monitoring, and low threshold for haematology referral reduces distress. Many patients fear cancer; early specialist reassurance (even if benign) improves quality of life.

Educational use only. Pathway based on NICE NG12 Suspected cancer: recognition and referral (symptom-based referral, not numerical WCC thresholds), BSH Guideline for the investigation and management of eosinophilia (2017), BSH Guidelines for Investigation and Management of CLL (2022), the current SPC, local antimicrobial guidance (Greater Manchester formulary) and the local haematology referral and laboratory abnormal-result pathway. Always adapt to individual patient context, local guidelines, and specialist advice. Leucocytosis is a laboratory finding, not a diagnosis — identify and treat the underlying cause.
