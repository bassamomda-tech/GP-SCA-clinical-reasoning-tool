1
Safety

Red Flags — the emergencies behind a "+glucose" dipstick

Diabetic ketoacidosis / new type 1 Thirst, polyuria, weight loss over days to weeks, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep sighing breathing, drowsiness — especially a child, teenager or lean adult. Check capillary glucose and ketones now → same-day emergency assessment. Do not send home to await fasting bloods.
Child or young person Suspected type 1 diabetes → refer immediately (on the same day) to the multidisciplinary paediatric diabetes team (NICE NG18). Do not arrange a fasting glucose, HbA1c or OGTT first.
Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state Older patient, very high glucose (typically 30 mmol/L or more), profound dehydration and confusion without significant ketones → same-day admission (JBDS-IP HHS guideline, 2022).
Pancreatic cancer — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Aged 60 or over with weight loss and any of: diarrhoea, back pain, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation or new-onset diabetes → consider an urgent direct-access CT (within 2 weeks), or ultrasound if CT unavailable. Aged 40 or over with jaundice → suspected cancer pathway referral.
Sepsis / intercurrent illness Stress hyperglycaemia with infection can tip known or undiagnosed diabetes into crisis — treat the illness and reassess glycaemia once recovered.
Pregnancy Glycosuria of 2+ or above on one occasion, or 1+ or above on two or more occasions, may indicate undiagnosed gestational diabetes → consider further testing (NICE NG3).
Euglycaemic DKA on SGLT2 inhibitors People on a "flozin" can develop ketoacidosis with only moderately raised glucose — test ketones if unwell, vomiting, dehydrated or around surgery (MHRA Drug Safety Update, March 2020).
A urine dipstick reading of glucose is usually mundane, but a few presentations behind it are emergencies. The classic trap is the lean young person with osmotic symptoms and weight loss: that is new type 1 diabetes and may already be ketoacidotic, so it needs a same-day capillary glucose and ketone check rather than a routine fasting test — and in anyone under 18, NICE NG18 asks for same-day referral to the paediatric diabetes team. The NG12 link is pancreatic cancer: new diabetes after 60 with weight loss is one of the listed combinations for urgent direct-access pancreatic imaging, and jaundice at 40 or over is a direct referral. In patients on SGLT2 inhibitors, the MHRA has warned that ketoacidosis may occur with near-normal glucose, so unwellness, not the glucose number, triggers the ketone check. Pregnancy lowers the renal threshold, which is why NG3 sets specific dipstick thresholds rather than acting on any trace.

Diagnose

The core split — hyperglycaemic vs normoglycaemic glycosuria

The physiology
Filtered glucose is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, mainly by SGLT2. Glucose spills into urine once blood glucose exceeds the reabsorptive threshold (roughly 10 mmol/L in most adults) — or when the threshold itself is lowered. So glycosuria means high blood glucose or a low threshold.
Hyperglycaemic
Diabetes (type 1, type 2, secondary, gestational), stress or acute illness, corticosteroids and other diabetogenic drugs. Confirmed by a raised plasma glucose or HbA1c.
Normoglycaemic (low threshold)
Familial renal glycosuria, pregnancy, SGLT2 inhibitors, Fanconi syndrome or other proximal tubular disease. Plasma glucose is normal.
The one test that splits them
A laboratory plasma glucose (with HbA1c where valid). Never diagnose or exclude diabetes on urine glucose alone.
Dipstick pitfalls
Reagent strips can under-read with high ascorbic acid or old strips, and a negative stick does not exclude diabetes — many people with type 2 diabetes have no glycosuria at modest hyperglycaemia.
Everything in glycosuria flows from one idea: the kidney spills glucose either because the blood level is too high or because the threshold for reabsorption is too low. That single split — hyperglycaemic versus normoglycaemic glycosuria — decides whether the patient has diabetes or simply a benign low renal threshold. Because the dipstick cannot tell these apart, the indispensable next step is always a blood test, and the commonest error is to reassure (or alarm) a patient on the strength of the urine result alone. The reverse error is just as real: a negative dipstick does not rule out diabetes, so it has no place as a screening test in someone with symptoms or risk factors.

Diagnose

History — symptoms, drugs and context

Osmotic symptoms
Thirst, polyuria, nocturia, blurred vision, recurrent thrush or balanitis, fatigue — point to true hyperglycaemia. Ask how fast they developed.
Weight and appetite
Unintentional weight loss (type 1; or the NG12 pancreatic combination in over-60s). Rapid loss in a lean patient is a red flag.
Drugs
SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin, ertugliflozin) cause glycosuria by design. Corticosteroids, thiazides, some antipsychotics, tacrolimus and ciclosporin raise glucose.
Pregnancy
Lowered renal threshold makes glycosuria common; assess against the NG3 thresholds and the woman's risk factors.
Family history
Familial renal glycosuria (benign), monogenic diabetes (several generations, onset under 25), type 1 or type 2 diabetes, autoimmune disease.
Background
Type 2 risk: overweight, South Asian, Black African or African-Caribbean family background, previous gestational diabetes, PCOS, cardiovascular disease, previous non-diabetic hyperglycaemia.
Pancreatic clues
Heavy alcohol use or previous pancreatitis, pale floating stools, back or epigastric pain, jaundice, itch — secondary diabetes from pancreatic disease.
Endocrine clues
Cushingoid features, acromegalic features, or bronze skin with arthropathy (haemochromatosis).
The history quickly biases you towards one side of the split. Osmotic symptoms argue for genuine hyperglycaemia and prompt confirmatory bloods without delay, while the same picture with rapid weight loss or vomiting escalates to a same-day pathway. NICE NG17 reminds us that type 1 diabetes in adults usually (but not always) shows one or more of ketosis, rapid weight loss, onset under 50, BMI under 25, and a personal or family history of autoimmune disease — and that age or BMI alone should not be used to rule it in or out. The single most important question in modern practice is the drug history: a patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor is expected to have glucose in the urine, and missing that leads to needless work-ups. Pancreatic and endocrine clues flag the secondary diabetes that needs its own investigation.

Diagnose

Differential diagnosis

Type 2 diabetes
Commonest; older or overweight, risk factors, gradual onset, often asymptomatic.
Type 1 / latent autoimmune
Younger or lean, rapid osmotic symptoms, weight loss, ketosis-prone; autoimmune diabetes can also start slowly in adults and be mislabelled as type 2.
Monogenic diabetes (MODY)
Young onset, strong family history over generations, not overweight, antibody-negative with preserved C-peptide.
Gestational diabetes
Pregnancy — confirm with a 75 g OGTT; see the dedicated pathway.
Secondary diabetes
Pancreatic cancer (NG12), chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis, steroids, Cushing's syndrome, acromegaly.
Renal glycosuria
Isolated low threshold with normal plasma glucose — benign, often familial (variants in the SGLT2 gene, SLC5A2).
SGLT2-inhibitor effect
Therapeutic glycosuria — expected, not pathological.
Fanconi syndrome
Glycosuria with aminoaciduria, phosphaturia, low urate ± proximal renal tubular acidosis — generalised proximal tubular dysfunction (myeloma light chains, drugs such as tenofovir, heavy metals, inherited disorders).
The differential keeps the benign and the serious causes side by side. Most glycosuria with hyperglycaemia is type 2 diabetes, but the list must stay open to type 1 in the young or lean (and to slowly evolving autoimmune diabetes in adults), to monogenic diabetes where the family history is striking, to the secondary causes — with pancreatic cancer flagged under NG12 — and to the entirely benign normoglycaemic causes. Fanconi syndrome is the clue to look beyond glucose: if the urine is also losing amino acids, phosphate and bicarbonate, the problem is the proximal tubule, and in an adult that should prompt a search for myeloma or a nephrotoxic drug.
5
Diagnose

Investigations

First-line
HbA1c and/or fasting plasma glucose. Diabetes: HbA1c 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) or above (WHO 2011) or fasting plasma glucose 7.0 mmol/L or above, or random plasma glucose 11.1 mmol/L or above with symptoms (WHO 2006). Without symptoms, repeat to confirm.
When HbA1c misleads
Do not rely on HbA1c in children and young people, suspected type 1, symptoms of short duration, pregnancy, acute illness, recent steroid start, or conditions altering red cell turnover (haemoglobinopathy, haemolysis, recent transfusion, iron deficiency, advanced CKD) — use plasma glucose.
Borderline results
HbA1c 42–47 mmol/mol falls below the diabetes threshold but indicates impaired glucose regulation (WHO 2011) — non-diabetic hyperglycaemia. Offer lifestyle support and periodic retesting.
If acutely unwell
Capillary glucose and blood ketones now. Ketones 3.0 mmol/L or above with glucose 11 mmol/L or above (or known diabetes) and acidosis defines DKA (JBDS-IP DKA guideline, revised 2023) — this is a hospital diagnosis; arrange admission rather than a venous gas in surgery.
Classify if doubt
Adults with a clinical diagnosis of type 1: measure diabetes-specific autoantibodies (NICE NG17). C-peptide and monogenic testing where the type is unclear.
Normal glucose, persistent glycosuria
Confirms renal glycosuria. If other tubular losses (phosphate, urate, bicarbonate, protein), screen for Fanconi syndrome and check a myeloma screen in adults.
Baseline once diabetes confirmed
Renal function with eGFR, urine albumin:creatinine ratio, lipids, blood pressure, weight and BMI, and referral for retinal screening — these set the cardiovascular and renal context that shapes the choice of medicines. If only urine ketone sticks are available, remember they lag behind blood ketones and can mislead in early DKA.
Pregnancy
75 g 2-hour OGTT: gestational diabetes if fasting plasma glucose 5.6 mmol/L or above or 2-hour value 7.8 mmol/L or above (NICE NG3).
Secondary screen
Driven by clues: pancreatic imaging (NG12) for new diabetes with weight loss at 60+; ferritin and transferrin saturation (haemochromatosis); steroid review; endocrine tests if Cushingoid or acromegalic.
Investigation is built around a laboratory blood glucose measure. The thresholds come from the WHO (HbA1c 48 mmol/mol from the 2011 report; fasting 7.0 and random 11.1 mmol/L from the 2006 report), and a single abnormal value needs repeating unless the patient has symptoms. HbA1c reflects the previous two to three months, so it is falsely reassuring in rapidly evolving type 1 diabetes, in children, and in pregnancy, and it is distorted by anything that changes red cell lifespan. If the patient is unwell, the work-up shifts immediately to ketones and hospital assessment. Where plasma glucose is normal but glycosuria persists, the tests have effectively made the diagnosis — renal glycosuria — and the discipline is to stop and reassure. The secondary screens, including NG12 pancreatic imaging, are deployed when the history points to them.

Refer

Referral criteria

Same-day admission
Suspected DKA or HHS; adult with suspected type 1 diabetes who is vomiting, ketotic or unwell. Do not start oral agents.
Same-day — children
Any child or young person with suspected type 1 diabetes → immediate (same-day) referral to the paediatric diabetes team (NICE NG18).
Urgent — adult type 1
Well adult with a clinical diagnosis of type 1 (no ketosis, eating and drinking) → urgent same-day discussion with the local diabetes team to start insulin.
Pancreatic — NG12
60+ with weight loss and new-onset diabetes (or another listed symptom) → urgent direct-access CT within 2 weeks (ultrasound if CT unavailable). Jaundice at 40+ → suspected cancer pathway referral.
Antenatal
Glycosuria meeting NG3 thresholds or confirmed gestational diabetes → joint antenatal and diabetes clinic.
Endocrinology (routine)
Atypical or uncertain type, suspected monogenic diabetes, suspected secondary cause (Cushing's syndrome, acromegaly, haemochromatosis).
Nephrology
Suspected Fanconi syndrome or other tubular disorder; glycosuria with falling eGFR or proteinuria.
Referral is dominated by two urgent routes and one cancer pathway. Suspected DKA or HHS go to hospital the same day, and any child with suspected type 1 diabetes is a same-day specialist referral under NG18 — never an oral-agent start or a "come back for fasting bloods" plan in primary care. A well adult with a clinical diagnosis of type 1 still needs insulin started promptly by the specialist team. New diabetes after 60 with weight loss triggers the NG12 pancreatic-imaging route, and it is worth remembering that the imaging is direct access — the GP requests it. Around these, routine endocrinology handles diagnostic uncertainty and secondary causes, nephrology handles tubular disorders, and gestational diabetes goes to the joint antenatal clinic.

Treat

Treat the cause — not the urine

Renal glycosuria / SGLT2i effect
Reassure — no treatment
Benign. Explain the low renal threshold (or the expected drug effect). No monitoring of urine glucose needed; do not start diabetes medication. Record the diagnosis so future dipsticks are not misread.
Confirmed type 2 diabetes
Education + lifestyle + medicines
Structured education, diet and activity. NICE NG28 (updated February 2026): for most adults, offer modified-release metformin with an SGLT2 inhibitor as first-line therapy, introduced one after the other (metformin first, then the SGLT2 inhibitor once metformin is tolerated); metformin alone is considered in frailty. Further choices are personalised by cardiovascular, renal, weight and frailty status (doses per BNF). Treat BP and lipids; arrange retinal and foot screening.
Suspected type 1 / ketosis
Same-day specialist — do not delay
Insulin is started under specialist care. Never trial oral agents in suspected type 1.
Gestational
Diet, metformin and/or insulin per the antenatal pathway (see dedicated algorithm).
Drug-induced
Review the need for steroids or other diabetogenic drugs; steroid-induced hyperglycaemia often needs time-of-day capillary glucose monitoring rather than HbA1c.
Secondary
Treat the underlying cause (pancreatic disease, endocrinopathy, haemochromatosis) alongside glycaemic control; pancreatic diabetes may need insulin and enzyme replacement.
Do NOT
Treat glycosuria itself, monitor urine glucose to guide therapy, or start oral agents in possible type 1.
The therapeutic principle is to treat the underlying state, never the dipstick. Renal glycosuria and the glycosuria of SGLT2 inhibitors need nothing more than explanation. Confirmed type 2 diabetes now enters the NG28 (February 2026) pathway, where modified-release metformin plus an SGLT2 inhibitor is the usual starting combination because of the heart and kidney protection the SGLT2 class brings, with metformin alone considered where frailty makes volume depletion or hypotension a real risk. Suspected type 1 must go to specialist care for insulin — trialling oral agents is dangerous. Urine glucose has no role in monitoring modern diabetes, and in anyone taking an SGLT2 inhibitor it is permanently positive.

Lifestyle

Risk-factor and self-management

Reassurance where benign For renal glycosuria, clear explanation prevents years of anxiety and repeated testing — it needs no dietary change. Suggest the patient mentions it at insurance or occupational medicals.
Diet and weight Where type 2 is confirmed or at risk, weight loss, reduced refined carbohydrate and a Mediterranean-style pattern improve glycaemia; substantial early weight loss can lead to remission.
Activity Regular physical activity improves insulin sensitivity and cardiovascular risk.
Prevention programme Non-diabetic hyperglycaemia → refer to the NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme where available and recheck HbA1c periodically.
SGLT2i counselling Genital hygiene (thrush risk), sick-day rules (pause during dehydrating illness and before surgery), and ketone awareness even with normal glucose.
Smoking and alcohol Cessation and moderation reduce cardiometabolic risk; alcohol also drives pancreatitis-related diabetes.
Pregnancy planning Women with previous gestational diabetes or risk factors benefit from preconception review and early testing in the next pregnancy.
Driving Whether the DVLA must be told depends on the treatment (especially insulin or other drugs that can cause hypoglycaemia) and the licence group — check current DVLA guidance with the patient.
Lifestyle advice is tailored to which side of the split the patient sits on. For benign renal glycosuria the highest-value intervention is reassurance, sparing the patient unnecessary diets and repeat tests. For non-diabetic hyperglycaemia, a structured lifestyle programme and periodic retesting reduce progression. For confirmed type 2 diabetes the familiar package — weight loss, dietary change, activity and smoking cessation — modifies the disease and can achieve remission. Patients on SGLT2 inhibitors need specific counselling on genital hygiene, sick-day rules and ketone awareness, because their glycosuria is intentional but carries its own small risks. Driving obligations depend on treatment and licence group, so check the current DVLA guidance rather than relying on memory.

Safety

Follow-up, safety-netting and the SCA consultation

Confirm before labelling
Diagnose diabetes on plasma glucose or HbA1c, not urine. Repeat to confirm in the asymptomatic.
Osmotic / ketotic deterioration
Same-day Thirst, polyuria, weight loss, vomiting, abdominal pain, fast breathing or drowsiness — assess for DKA, especially in the young or lean and in SGLT2i users (euglycaemic DKA).
Pancreatic safety-net
New diabetes at 60+ with weight loss, or new back pain, jaundice, pale stools or itch → complete the NG12 pancreatic route — do not attribute weight loss to "good glycaemic control".
Renal glycosuria
If truly normoglycaemic, reassure and discharge; record the diagnosis clearly to avoid repeated dipstick-driven work-ups.
Non-diabetic hyperglycaemia
Recall for a repeat HbA1c; safety-net osmotic symptoms.
Pregnancy
Glycosuria at NG3 thresholds → OGTT for gestational diabetes.
Document
Record the blood result, the conclusion (diabetes type vs renal glycosuria vs drug effect), and the safety-net advice given.
SCA phrasing
"The sugar in your urine doesn't by itself mean diabetes — sometimes the kidneys let a little sugar through even when blood levels are normal. The only way to know is a blood test, which I'd like to do today. If you start feeling very thirsty, being sick, getting tummy pain or feeling drowsy before the result is back, please don't wait — get seen the same day."
Safety-netting closes the two ways glycosuria goes wrong. The first is under-calling an emergency: osmotic symptoms with weight loss, vomiting or drowsiness — or any unwell patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor — needs a same-day ketone and glucose assessment for DKA. The second is the missed cancer: new diabetes in an older adult with weight loss should never be filed as benign without considering NG12 pancreatic imaging. For the genuinely normoglycaemic patient, the safety-net is the opposite — firm reassurance, a clear coded diagnosis and discharge, so a benign dipstick does not become a lifetime of anxious re-testing. In the SCA, explaining the kidney threshold in plain language and naming the specific symptoms that should bring the patient back the same day shows both clinical reasoning and shared understanding.
