1
Safety

Red Flags — Malignancy, Eating Disorders & Life-Threatening Complications

Anorexia (loss of appetite) is a non-specific symptom. Unintentional weight loss or unexplained appetite loss requires assessment for additional symptoms, examination findings and abnormal investigations; arrange urgent investigation, suspected-cancer-pathway referral or non-specific-symptoms-pathway referral according to clinical findings and local arrangements, and safety-net everyone who does not meet a referral threshold. Anorexia nervosa is a separate condition (labelled below) with high mortality.

Unintentional weight loss or appetite loss — assess against NICE NG12 Occult malignancy — gastric, oesophageal, pancreatic, lung, colorectal, hepatocellular, lymphoma. Palliative anorexia-cachexia syndrome (CACS) in established cancer. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) pathways: dysphagia at any age, or age 55 and over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia → suspected cancer pathway referral (upper GI); haemoptysis at 40 and over → suspected cancer pathway referral (lung); urgent chest X-ray at 40 and over with two or more unexplained symptoms including weight loss or appetite loss (one if ever smoked). Jaundice at 40 and over → suspected cancer pathway referral (pancreatic). Age 60 and over with unexplained weight loss → assess for additional features and consider urgent investigation, cancer referral or non-specific symptoms referral. Lower-GI symptoms → quantitative FIT where indicated; refer if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g, but do not delay referral with strong clinical concern. Use the current NG12 criteria directly rather than a blanket age rule.
Anorexia + vomiting + severe epigastric pain + jaundice + weight loss Upper GI malignancy (gastric, pancreatic, oesophageal) or pancreatitis/acute cholecystitis complicating chronic disease. Suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12: jaundice at 40 or over → pancreatic; 55 or over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain → upper GI). Bilirubin + ALP; imaging per the pathway. New painless jaundice with weight loss is a strong concern for pancreatic cancer and needs urgent pathway referral. Tumour markers are not routine first-line tests.
Anorexia nervosa (separate condition) — suspected eating disorder with physical compromise Assess intake, rate of weight loss, weight/BMI (age-adjusted in under-18s), lying and standing BP and pulse, temperature, hydration, muscle function, purging or water-loading, bloods, ECG and suicide/self-harm risk; refer immediately to an age-appropriate community eating-disorder service. Same-day medical assessment/admission for severe electrolyte disturbance, severe malnutrition, severe dehydration, incipient organ failure, significant ECG abnormality, recurrent syncope, severe bradycardia, haemodynamic instability, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia or significant psychiatric risk — discuss urgently with eating-disorder and acute medical teams. Use the MEED CR233 multidomain risk framework (formerly MARSIPAN); no single BMI or weight threshold decides admission, and normal bloods do not exclude serious risk.
Anorexia + confusion + polyuria + polydipsia + bone pain + constipation Hypercalcaemia — malignancy (PTHrP — squamous cell lung, breast, myeloma, lymphoma) or primary hyperparathyroidism. Serum calcium urgently — corrected Ca >3.0 mmol/L = significant; >3.5 mmol/L = emergency (IV fluids + bisphosphonate). Any symptomatic hypercalcaemia = hospital admission.
Anorexia + profound fatigue + weight loss + pigmentation + postural hypotension Addison's disease (primary adrenal insufficiency) — autoimmune, TB, metastatic (bilateral adrenal metastases — lung, breast). Suspected adrenal insufficiency → 9 am cortisol pathway and endocrine advice. Suspected adrenal crisis (vomiting, collapse, hypotension) → 999 + emergency hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV — do not delay while awaiting tests. Electrolytes: hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia (aldosterone deficiency).
Refeeding syndrome risk (NICE CG32): BMI <16, weight loss >15% in 3–6 months, little or no intake >10 days, or low K/PO4/Mg before feeding — OR two of: BMI <18.5, weight loss >10% in 3–6 months, little or no intake >5 days, alcohol misuse or relevant drugs Refeeding syndrome — rapid intracellular shift of phosphate, potassium, magnesium on refeeding → cardiac arrhythmias, cardiac failure, respiratory failure, seizures, rhabdomyolysis. If high refeeding risk: do not start an unsupervised rapid increase in calories or ONS — contact the community dietetic, eating-disorder or acute medical team urgently to agree the feeding prescription (CG32: maximum 10 kcal/kg/day, 5 kcal/kg/day in extreme cases with cardiac monitoring, building to full needs over 4–7 days), thiamine 200–300 mg daily before and for the first 10 days (oral preferred; IV only where oral/enteral is not possible or per hospital protocol, e.g. Wernicke's), electrolyte replacement and close monitoring of fluid balance, glucose, U&E, magnesium and phosphate. Follow the local Greater Manchester protocol.
The RCPsych MEED guidance (CR233; replaced MARSIPAN) uses a multidomain traffic-light risk assessment — rate of weight loss, BMI or age-adjusted measures, cardiovascular findings (bradycardia, hypotension, orthostatic changes, ECG), temperature, hydration, electrolytes and glucose, muscle function, purging and psychiatric risk — and the admission decision depends on the whole clinical picture; NICE NG69 advises against using a single BMI or weight threshold. Under-18s need age- and sex-adjusted measures, not adult thresholds. GPs who see patients with anorexia nervosa must perform or arrange an urgent physical assessment including ECG, electrolytes, glucose, and lying/standing blood pressure. The cardiac risk in severe anorexia nervosa is substantial — bradyarrhythmias (from vagal overactivity + electrolyte imbalances) and QTc prolongation (from hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia) can cause sudden cardiac death, particularly during refeeding. NICE NG69 (Eating disorders, 2017, which replaced CG9) and the RCPsych MEED guidance (2022, which replaced MARSIPAN) provide the framework. Refeeding syndrome is the most important acute complication of treating severe malnutrition and anorexia — the pathophysiology involves: during prolonged starvation, the body shifts to fat/protein catabolism. Intracellular electrolytes (phosphate, potassium, magnesium) remain relatively preserved despite depletion of total body stores. When carbohydrates are reintroduced, insulin secretion drives glucose, potassium, phosphate, and magnesium back into cells simultaneously, causing precipitous plasma falls. Hypophosphataemia (<0.3 mmol/L) is the critical trigger for cardiac failure, respiratory muscle weakness, and rhabdomyolysis. For high-risk patients NICE CG32 advises: start at a maximum of 10 kcal/kg/day (5 kcal/kg/day in extreme cases such as BMI <14 or negligible intake for >15 days, with continuous cardiac monitoring), increasing to full requirements by day 4–7; thiamine 200–300 mg daily immediately before and during the first 10 days; potassium, phosphate and magnesium supplementation unless levels are high; and close monitoring of fluid balance, clinical status, glucose, U&E, magnesium and phosphate. This is a specialist-supervised protocol, not unsupervised primary-care refeeding.

Diagnose

Classification — Causes of Anorexia

Physiological / acute illness
Acute infection (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 directly suppress appetite via hypothalamic pathways — anorexia is an adaptive sickness behaviour). Post-operative state. Pregnancy (first trimester — hyperemesis gravidarum at severe end). Medications (most common reversible cause — see step 4).
Gastrointestinal
GORD/peptic ulcer (satiety from pain anticipation), gastroparesis (early satiety, bloating, diabetes-related), chronic constipation (abdominal fullness), IBD (Crohn's — active disease + malabsorption), coeliac disease (malabsorption + anaemia), chronic liver disease (liver capsule distension + reduced hepatic synthetic function + portal hypertension).
Malignancy (most important to exclude)
Gastric, oesophageal, pancreatic (most strongly associated with early satiety and weight loss), colorectal, hepatocellular, lung, renal, lymphoma. Paraneoplastic anorexia-cachexia syndrome (CACS) — IL-6, TNF-α, proteolysis-inducing factor cause muscle wasting disproportionate to caloric restriction. Unexplained anorexia + weight loss in an adult needs assessment against NG12 criteria and safety-netting.
Metabolic / endocrine
Hypothyroidism (reduced metabolic drive + constipation → anorexia), Addison's disease (cortisol deficiency + nausea + hyperpigmentation), hypercalcaemia (malignancy/hyperparathyroidism — anorexia + constipation + confusion), uraemia (CKD/ESRD — uraemic anorexia: severe, difficult to treat), diabetes (autonomic gastroparesis, hyperglycaemia-induced anorexia).
Psychiatric
Depression (most common psychiatric cause — anhedonia + psychomotor retardation → food refusal), anxiety disorders (health anxiety, OCD food-related), PTSD, anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa (restrictive phase), ARFID (avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder).
Medication-induced
Extremely common and reversible. Key drugs: SSRIs/SNRIs (especially fluoxetine — direct appetite suppression), metformin (GI side effects), opioids (gastroparesis + nausea + constipation), digoxin (toxicity), antibiotics (GI dysbiosis), stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines), topiramate, GLP-1 agonists (liraglutide, semaglutide — profound appetite suppression by design).
The paraneoplastic anorexia-cachexia syndrome (CACS) deserves emphasis as a clinically important and pathophysiologically distinct entity from simple anorexia from reduced intake — in CACS, the weight loss is disproportionate to the degree of anorexia because of active catabolism of muscle protein (proteolysis-inducing factor, IL-6, TNF-α drive muscle wasting independent of caloric intake). This means that simply increasing caloric intake does not reverse the muscle wasting in CACS — the patient continues to lose lean body mass even with supplemental nutrition. CACS affects approximately 50–80% of cancer patients at diagnosis and is a major negative prognostic indicator (cancer patients with cachexia die earlier than matched patients without cachexia, independent of tumour stage). Understanding CACS changes the management approach: the goal is not to 'feed them more' but to address symptoms (nausea, early satiety, dysphagia, pain), optimise nutritional intake within the physiological constraints, and focus on quality of life. Megestrol acetate and corticosteroids improve appetite temporarily but do not improve survival or lean mass, and are specialist/palliative-care treatments only. This is covered in the palliative care algorithm. The GLP-1 agonist (semaglutide, liraglutide) mechanism of appetite suppression is now clinically important because these drugs are widely prescribed for type 2 diabetes and obesity. GLP-1 agonists suppress appetite via: (1) direct hypothalamic GLP-1 receptor activation reducing hunger signals; (2) delayed gastric emptying causing early satiety; (3) inhibiting the ghrelin (hunger hormone) system. The profound appetite suppression they cause (contributing to 10–15% body weight loss) is the therapeutic mechanism, but in some patients — particularly elderly patients, those with low baseline BMI, or those with concurrent illness — this appetite suppression can become a genuine clinical problem causing malnutrition. GPs must document weight at every diabetes review for patients on GLP-1 agonists and reduce the dose or stop the drug if unintended weight loss occurs.

Diagnose

Targeted History

Appetite vs intake
Distinguish: no appetite (true anorexia — hypothalamic/metabolic/psychiatric) vs appetite preserved but not eating (fear of eating — dysphagia, pain, nausea, eating disorder, dental pain) vs appetite present but early satiety (gastroparesis, gastric outlet obstruction, ascites, organomegaly).
Weight loss quantification
How much? Over what period? (patients often underestimate). Weigh in clinic and compare with previous documented weights. MUST score (Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool) where appropriate: BMI points + weight loss % points + acute illness score → ≥2 = high risk → active nutritional intervention and dietitian/nutrition-support referral per local arrangements; escalate urgently for rapid deterioration, inability to maintain intake, dysphagia, severe malnutrition or refeeding risk. MUST does not replace assessment of swallowing, intake, function/frailty, oral health, GI symptoms, medicines, eating disorders or refeeding risk. A 10% weight loss in 6 months in a 70-year-old = 7 kg — significant malnutrition.
Associated symptoms
"Alarm" features for GI malignancy: dysphagia (oesophageal), early satiety + epigastric pain (gastric), painless jaundice (pancreatic), change in bowel habit + PR bleeding (colorectal), haemoptysis (lung). Odynophagia, reflux, dyspepsia, nausea/vomiting. Respiratory symptoms, smoking/asbestos exposure. Fever, night sweats + lymphadenopathy (lymphoma). Self-harm risk. Fatigue + breathlessness + pallor (anaemia of malignancy). Depression screening (PHQ-9): low mood + anhedonia + sleep disturbance + psychomotor retardation.
Drug history
Full drug review (including SNRIs, alcohol and substance use). When did the anorexia start relative to medication changes? GLP-1 agonists (deliberate appetite suppression — is it excessive?), SSRIs (especially early treatment weeks), opioids, metformin, antibiotics, digoxin (check level), antifungals, topiramate, stimulants.
Social and functional history
Lives alone? (elderly isolation → social anorexia). Shopping/cooking capability. Dental pain (prevents eating). Financial food insecurity (ask sensitively: "Do you ever have difficulty affording food?"). Alcohol history (alcohol replaces meals + hepatic anorexia). Bereavement or depression trigger.
Eating disorder screening
Any restriction of food intake + fear of weight gain + distorted body image + compensatory behaviours (purging, excessive exercise)? SCOFF questionnaire: Sick/Control/One stone/Fat/Food — ≥2 may support further assessment, but it is not a diagnostic gatekeeper and a negative score does not exclude an eating disorder (NICE NG69). Approach sensitively without triggering shame.
The MUST (Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool) score is the recommended nutritional screening tool for primary care and hospital outpatients — it takes 3 minutes to calculate and provides a standardised risk score that determines the urgency of dietitian referral and intervention. MUST score ≥2 = high malnutrition risk, prompting active intervention and referral per local arrangements. The three components are: (1) BMI score: BMI >20 = 0 points; 18.5–20 = 1 point; <18.5 = 2 points. (2) Unintentional weight loss score: <5% = 0 points; 5–10% = 1 point; >10% = 2 points. (3) Acute illness score: if acutely ill and no nutritional intake for >5 days = 2 points. Total MUST score: 0 = low risk (routine care); 1 = medium risk (dietary advice, monitor); ≥2 = high risk (intensive dietetic input). The SCOFF questionnaire is a screening aid for eating disorders, not a diagnostic gatekeeper: (1) Do you make yourself Sick because you feel uncomfortably full? (2) Do you worry you have lost Control over how much you eat? (3) Have you recently lost more than One stone in a 3-month period? (4) Do you believe yourself to be Fat when others say you are too thin? (5) Would you say that Food dominates your life? A score of ≥2 supports further assessment; a negative score does not exclude an eating disorder. The SCOFF should be administered sensitively, with a non-judgmental framing — patients with eating disorders frequently deny or minimise symptoms, particularly weight-related questions.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Examination
Weight (BMI calculation) · Nutritional status: temporal wasting, thenar/hypothenar wasting, interosseous wasting, calf wasting, periorbital fat pad loss · Hands: palmar erythema (liver disease), koilonychia (iron deficiency), leukonychia (hypoalbuminaemia) · Skin: jaundice, pigmentation (Addison's — buccal mucosa, palmar creases), spider naevi, caput medusae · Abdomen: organomegaly, ascites, epigastric mass, succession splash (gastroparesis) · Lymphadenopathy · Mental state examination (depression, eating disorder)
Investigations — first line
FBC (anaemia of malignancy, macrocytic = B12/folate, haematological malignancy) · ESR + CRP (inflammation, malignancy, vasculitis) · U&E + eGFR (uraemia) · LFTs + GGT (liver disease, malignancy) · Ca2+ corrected (hypercalcaemia) · Glucose + HbA1c · TSH · Ferritin ± iron studies if anaemia · Albumin + ALP (albumin is not a standalone nutritional marker) · Urinalysis · TTG IgA + total IgA where appropriate · Pregnancy test where relevant · ECG if medically compromised, significant weight loss, purging, electrolyte abnormality, bradycardia, QT-risk medication or suspected eating disorder · Suspected Addison's → 9 am cortisol pathway and endocrine advice (guided by presentation)
Investigations — second line (based on first-line results)
Only when clinically indicated: US abdomen (liver, pancreas, kidneys, lymphadenopathy) · HIV / hepatitis testing (wasting + anorexia + immunocompromised features) · IGRA (TB — night sweats + weight loss) · Faecal calprotectin, endoscopy or CT via the NG12 or non-specific symptoms pathway. Do not routinely order CA19-9, CEA or CA125 for isolated anorexia
Eating disorder investigations
ECG (QTc prolongation, bradycardia — arrhythmia risk) · U&E + Mg2+ + PO4 (electrolyte disturbance from restriction/purging) · FBC + ferritin + B12 + folate · LFTs (starvation hepatitis) · Glucose · Lying and standing BP, temperature · Bone density DEXA (osteoporosis in established AN — specialist-arranged) · Thyroid (TFTs) (sick euthyroid syndrome in severe malnutrition)
The succession splash test for gastroparesis is a rarely performed but diagnostically useful bedside test — the patient fasts for 4 hours, the examiner places a stethoscope over the epigastrium and rapidly shakes the patient's torso. A splashing sound heard more than 4 hours after the last meal indicates retained gastric fluid (gastroparesis, pyloric stenosis, gastric outlet obstruction). It is quick, non-invasive, and can guide whether urgent upper GI imaging is needed. The CT chest/abdomen/pelvis investigation threshold for unexplained significant weight loss is lower than many GPs realise — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) sets site-specific weight-loss criteria — for example, consider an urgent direct-access CT (within 2 weeks) for pancreatic cancer at age 60 and over with weight loss plus diarrhoea, back pain, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation or new-onset diabetes. Outside those criteria, many areas offer a non-specific symptoms pathway that includes CT when unexplained weight loss comes with abnormal first-line results (anaemia, raised ESR/CRP, abnormal LFTs, hypercalcaemia). Access to CT follows the NG12 criteria and local non-specific symptoms pathway arrangements.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day admission
Collapse, syncope or severe postural symptoms · haemodynamic instability · severe dehydration · severe electrolyte abnormality, hypoglycaemia or suspected organ failure · severe or rapidly progressive malnutrition · bradycardia, significant ECG abnormality or prolonged QTc · eating disorder with high MEED CR233 risk on the whole clinical picture (not BMI alone) or significant suicide/self-harm risk · Hypercalcaemia corrected Ca >3.0 mmol/L with symptoms (confusion, vomiting) · Suspected adrenal crisis (collapse + hypotension + vomiting) → 999 + emergency hydrocortisone 100 mg IM — do not wait for tests · Refeeding syndrome developing (hypophosphataemia + cardiac/respiratory symptoms)
Suspected cancer pathways
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026). Reproduce the current NG12 criteria rather than a blanket age rule · Pancreatic: jaundice at 40 and over → suspected cancer pathway referral · Age 60 and over with unexplained weight loss → assess for additional features; consider urgent investigation, cancer referral or non-specific symptoms referral · Non-specific symptoms pathway or urgent imaging where symptoms are concerning but do not meet a site-specific criterion · Upper GI: dysphagia, or age 55 and over with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia → suspected cancer pathway referral; upper abdominal mass consistent with stomach cancer → suspected cancer pathway referral · Lower GI: FIT first (NICE NG12 and HTG690) — e.g. age 40 and over with unexplained weight loss and abdominal pain, or 50 and over with rectal bleeding, abdominal pain or weight loss; refer if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g, but do not delay referral where there is strong clinical concern · Lung: haemoptysis at 40 and over → suspected cancer pathway referral; urgent chest X-ray at 40 and over with two or more unexplained symptoms including weight loss, appetite loss or cough (one if ever smoked) · Haematology: unexplained lymphadenopathy (with night sweats, weight loss) → consider lymphoma referral
Urgent gastroenterology
Suspected upper GI malignancy without meeting the suspected cancer pathway criteria · Gastroparesis assessment (gastric emptying scintigraphy) · Coeliac disease confirmed (TTG IgA positive — duodenal biopsy + dietary management) · Suspected IBD with anorexia + weight loss
Eating disorders service
Suspected eating disorder (SCOFF may support, but a negative score does not exclude) → immediate referral to an age-appropriate community eating disorders team (CEDS). Severe AN with physical compromise (MEED 2022, formerly MARSIPAN) → joint CEDS + medical inpatient admission. NICE NG69 (2017): refer immediately to an age-appropriate community eating disorder service, and do not use a single measure such as BMI or duration of illness to decide whether to offer treatment — refer and manage in parallel. Under-18s: CAMHS eating disorders urgent pathway.
Psychiatry / NHS Talking Therapies
Depression-related anorexia: PHQ-9 ≥10 → NHS Talking Therapies (mild-moderate) or psychiatry referral (severe/psychotic depression). Anxiety-related food avoidance: NHS Talking Therapies CBT. ARFID: specialist ARFID pathway (emerging service — adult feeding difficulties teams at some centres).
Dietitian
MUST score ≥2 → active nutritional intervention and dietitian/nutrition-support referral per local arrangements (urgent if rapid deterioration, inability to maintain intake, dysphagia, severe malnutrition or refeeding risk). Elderly with anorexia + weight loss + frailty. Cancer cachexia. Post-treatment anorexia (surgery, chemo, radiotherapy). Refeeding protocol supervision.
NICE NG69 (2017, which replaced CG9) asks for immediate referral to eating disorder services rather than waiting for the patient to become 'ready' or for weight to reach a threshold — this reflects the evidence that early intervention in anorexia nervosa produces significantly better outcomes (higher rates of full recovery, lower mortality) than delayed treatment. The mortality of anorexia nervosa is approximately 5% at 10 years (all-cause mortality) — higher than any other psychiatric condition — and the risk of death is highest in the first years after diagnosis. Every month of delay in accessing specialist eating disorder treatment represents increased risk. GPs should make the CEDS referral immediately on identification of a likely eating disorder, while continuing to provide primary care support, regular physical monitoring, and engagement. In the under-18s, the NHS England access and waiting time standard for children and young people with an eating disorder (2015) specifies that young people with suspected eating disorders should be seen within 1 week for urgent cases and 4 weeks for routine referrals — GPs should specify 'urgent' on the referral if there is any physical compromise or rapid weight loss. ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder) is a DSM-5 diagnosis that describes food refusal based on sensory characteristics, fear of aversive consequences (choking, vomiting), or apparent lack of interest in food — without the body image disturbance of anorexia nervosa. It is increasingly recognised in adults (not just children) and is seen more frequently in patients with autism spectrum disorder, anxiety disorders, and OCD. GPs who encounter patients with longstanding selective eating patterns causing nutritional deficiency or weight loss should consider ARFID and refer to the appropriate local service (eating disorders team or psychology).
6
Treat

Treat the Underlying Cause

Malignancy / CACS
Specialist oncology-led treatment
Anorexia in cancer: treat reversible causes (nausea, constipation, pain, depression, mucositis, dysphagia). Oral nutritional supplements where appropriate. Appetite stimulants are not routine GP options: megestrol acetate is specialist/palliative-care only (limited benefit; thromboembolism, fluid retention, adrenal suppression, hypogonadism), and dexamethasone only within an oncology or specialist palliative-care plan (hyperglycaemia, infection, delirium, insomnia, proximal myopathy; does not reverse cachexia or improve survival). Palliative dietitian.
Depression
SSRI / SNRI + psychological therapy
Mirtazapine may be appropriate where there is a clear depressive disorder with insomnia or poor appetite — not as a general appetite stimulant or routine treatment for anorexia nervosa. In anorexia nervosa, medication should not be the sole treatment (NICE NG69); antidepressant choice is based on comorbidity and specialist/shared-care advice. Olanzapine is specialist-initiated only, as an adjunct in refractory AN.
Drug-induced
Remove/substitute offending drug
GLP-1 agonist-induced excess anorexia: reduce dose or switch drug. Stop or change a causative medicine only after considering its indication and withdrawal risks. SSRI-induced: review the antidepressant. Opioid-induced: treat constipation (laxative, fibre); short-term antiemetic only if needed (see nausea management). Digoxin toxicity: stop digoxin, correct electrolytes, assess need for continued digoxin.
Addison's disease
Hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day in split doses
Morning: 10–15 mg, afternoon: 5–10 mg. Fludrocortisone 50–200 mcg OD (mineralocorticoid). Endocrinology-led initiation. Sick day rules: double/triple dose during illness/surgery. MedicAlert bracelet. IM hydrocortisone kit (self-injection for adrenal crisis). Appetite returns within days of adequate cortisol replacement.
Gastroparesis
Metoclopramide + diet modification
Metoclopramide — dose (including renal/elderly adjustment) per current product information; shortest duration, generally no longer than 5 days; contraindicated in GI obstruction, perforation or haemorrhage, epilepsy and Parkinson's disease; stop if extrapyramidal symptoms; prolonged use risks tardive dyskinesia (specialist for longer use). Domperidone — restricted to nausea and vomiting; lowest effective dose (max 30 mg/day) for the shortest time (usually no longer than 1 week); avoid in QT prolongation, bradycardia, significant electrolyte disturbance, cardiac disease, severe hepatic impairment, and with QT-prolonging medicines or potent CYP3A4 inhibitors (MHRA Drug Safety Update, May 2014). Small frequent meals, low-fat/low-fibre diet. Glycaemic control (hyperglycaemia worsens gastroparesis). Erythromycin 125–250 mg TDS (motilin agonist — specialist).
Anorexia nervosa (GP role)
Physical monitoring + CEDS coordination
GP role: physical monitoring as agreed with CEDS (weight, observations, bloods and ECG according to risk), MEED CR233 (formerly MARSIPAN) physical risk assessment, liaison with CEDS, motivational support, family engagement, sick note and social support (PIP, ESA). Do not act as the eating disorder therapist — maintain the therapeutic relationship while directing specialist treatment to CEDS.
Mirtazapine is a particularly useful antidepressant for the combination of depression + anorexia + insomnia that frequently presents in elderly patients or those with chronic illness — its mechanism (noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressant, NASSA) includes potent H1 histamine receptor antagonism at 15 mg, which increases appetite and promotes weight gain. At higher doses (30–45 mg), the noradrenergic effects are more prominent and the appetite-stimulating H1 effect is relatively less pronounced — but it should be prescribed for a clear depressive disorder, not as a general appetite stimulant. Olanzapine is sometimes used by specialist teams in refractory anorexia nervosa. NICE NG69 (2017, updated 2020) advises against offering medication as the sole treatment for anorexia nervosa and does not recommend olanzapine routinely; specialist teams sometimes use it as an adjunct in severe, refractory AN where other treatments have failed. It is initiated by the eating disorders team, not in primary care, but GPs should be aware of this option when discussing treatment options with patients.

Treat

Nutritional Support

Oral nutritional supplements (ONS)
Food first where safe: dietary fortification, snacks, altered meal pattern, mealtime assistance and treatment of reversible causes. Consider an ONS when oral intake remains inadequate, swallowing is safe and the patient is malnourished or at risk — select product and quantity with dietetic or local (Greater Manchester) formulary advice, within ACBS indications, and check current product information. Assess refeeding risk first. ONS are part of total intake, not simply added calories. Offer choice of flavours. Review adherence, weight, symptoms and goals.
Nausea management
Antiemetics are not interchangeable routine options — choose according to the underlying cause, QT risk, constipation, Parkinsonism, sedation, palliative-care context and local formulary. Metoclopramide: dose per product information (renal/elderly adjustment), generally no longer than 5 days, contraindications as above (MHRA Drug Safety Update, August 2013) · Ondansetron: chemotherapy/radiotherapy-related; constipating; QT risk · Domperidone: restrictions as above (MHRA 2014) · Prochlorperazine: vestibular component; extrapyramidal and sedation risk in the elderly · Haloperidol: palliative-care context.
Enteral and parenteral (specialist)
Nasogastric (NG) feeding for eating disorders (MEED criteria — may require Mental Health Act in extreme cases with incapacity). PEG (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy) for oropharyngeal/oesophageal dysphagia, neurological dysphagia, head/neck cancer. TPN (total parenteral nutrition) for bowel failure (short gut, surgical — hospital-initiated). GPs do not initiate enteral or parenteral nutrition — role is referral + ongoing monitoring of community NG or PEG patients.
Appetite stimulants (evidence summary)
For non-specific anorexia the priority is to identify and treat the cause, not to prescribe an appetite stimulant. Megestrol acetate: specialist/palliative-care only — limited benefit; thromboembolism, fluid retention, adrenal suppression, hypogonadism. Dexamethasone: only within an oncology or specialist palliative-care plan; short-term benefit, steroid complications, no effect on cachexia or survival. Mirtazapine: only for a clear depressive disorder. Olanzapine: specialist-initiated for refractory AN. Cyproheptadine: limited evidence, sedating — not recommended routinely.
The prescribing of oral nutritional supplements (ONS) on NHS prescription is an important and underused intervention in primary care — the ACBS (Advisory Committee on Borderline Substances) specifies the conditions in which ONS can be prescribed on FP10: disease-related malnutrition, pre-operative preparation of malnourished patients, dysphagia, proven inflammatory bowel disease, following total gastrectomy, short bowel syndrome, and intractable malabsorption (specifically, not for general 'feeling unwell' or 'poor appetite' without nutritional compromise). For GP prescribers, ACBS indications, local formulary policy and product availability apply; select products with dietetic or local formulary advice and check current product information rather than relying on remembered energy/protein values. Ondansetron-induced constipation is a very common and practically important side effect — ondansetron (a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist) dramatically reduces bowel motility. In already anorexic patients, constipation further reduces appetite (abdominal fullness, bloating). Consider a laxative alongside ondansetron where constipation is a risk.

Lifestyle

Nutritional Optimisation, Mealtime Strategies & Psychosocial Support

Small frequent meals Five to six small meals per day rather than three large ones reduces the satiety/fullness barrier to eating. Each meal: 200–300 kcal maximum. High-calorie, nutrient-dense foods per mouthful (full-fat dairy, eggs, nuts, avocado, olive oil, nut butter, fortified cereals). "Eat little but eat often" — lower appetite threshold per meal.
Food fortification Add high-energy ingredients to existing foods: butter/cream in mashed potato and soups, full-fat milk in porridge and sauces, cheese grated into dishes, double cream in drinks, honey in yoghurt. Aim for 200–400 extra kcal/day without increasing volume. Fortified milk (skimmed milk powder added to full-fat milk — doubles protein content per glass) for elderly patients.
Nausea management at meals Eat at the time of day when nausea is lowest (often mid-morning). Avoid strong cooking smells (cold food has fewer volatile odorants). Sip fluids between rather than with meals (fluids increase gastric filling → satiety → less room for food). Ginger (natural antiemetic — ginger tea, ginger biscuits, ginger capsules 250 mg QDS) — Level 1b evidence for chemotherapy nausea.
Social eating Eating alone is associated with significantly reduced intake — social isolation is a major cause of anorexia in elderly patients. Meal delivery services (Meals on Wheels — local authority social services, charity-provided). Lunch clubs (Age UK, community centres). Family meals as structure. Video calls during mealtimes. OT assessment for adapted cutlery (grip, tremor), plate guards, non-slip mats — enables independent eating.
Oral health Dental pain is a common but overlooked cause of food avoidance — dental abscess, ill-fitting dentures, painful gums cause patients to avoid eating. Dental registration and treatment priority. Denture refitting or new dentures on NHS for patients with significant weight change (dentures become ill-fitting with weight loss — a vicious cycle). Soft food diet for painful dentition.
Mental health & eating disorders Non-judgmental, recovery-focused conversations about food. Avoid: food policing, meal-by-meal monitoring, power struggles (particularly in anorexia nervosa — these reinforce the disorder). Support: "I can see this is very difficult for you. I want to help you get the support you need." Involve family/carers with patient consent. Mealtime support strategies: structured mealtimes, eating with family, distraction post-meals.
Alcohol reduction Alcohol is a significant cause of malnutrition anorexia — heavy alcohol use replaces meals, causes B-vitamin deficiency (B1 Wernicke's risk), hepatic anorexia (liver capsule distension), and direct gastric mucosal inflammation impairing appetite. AUDIT-C ≥5 → brief intervention or AUDIT full → alcohol service referral. Thiamine supplementation for heavy drinkers with poor nutrition — oral in the community; parenteral thiamine is reserved for hospital/specialist protocols (e.g. suspected Wernicke's).
Activity and muscle preservation Gentle progressive resistance exercise (even chair-based) preserves lean muscle mass in elderly anorexia — without exercise, caloric supplementation primarily restores fat mass rather than lean mass. Structured exercise alongside nutritional support is standard practice in sarcopenia and frailty. Physiotherapy referral for frail/sarcopenic patients. "Nutrition + exercise together is more effective than either alone."
The social eating evidence is strong and clinically relevant — studies consistently show that elderly people living alone eat significantly less (20–30% fewer calories) than those living with others, with social mealtimes producing automatic appetite improvement independent of the food available. The mechanism involves social facilitation of eating behaviour (mirroring, reduced self-monitoring, improved mood) and the structure and ritual of a shared meal. Community interventions (lunch clubs, Meals on Wheels, befriending services) produce measurable improvements in nutritional intake and quality of life in elderly isolated patients. GPs who identify social isolation as a contributing factor in elderly anorexia should actively refer to social services and community befriending services — these are evidence-based nutritional interventions even though they are social rather than medical. The ginger antiemetic evidence base is worth knowing — a Cochrane review of ginger preparations for nausea and vomiting (including chemotherapy-induced nausea) found Level 1b evidence for efficacy superior to placebo. The mechanism involves 5-HT3 receptor antagonism (similar to ondansetron) and cholinergic effects on the gastric myenteric plexus. Ginger 250 mg capsules QDS is the studied dose. As a safe, inexpensive, widely available supplement with evidence for efficacy, it is a reasonable first-line recommendation for mild-moderate nausea contributing to anorexia in primary care, before escalating to prescription antiemetics.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Unexplained weight loss — investigation timeline
Review first-line investigation results within 2 weeks (do not leave for routine review). If results abnormal → expedite a suspected cancer pathway or urgent referral the same day. If normal → consider the non-specific symptoms pathway or targeted further investigation (not routine tumour markers) and arrange a documented review within 1–4 weeks depending on weight trajectory, intake and risk, with explicit safety-netting.
Anorexia on treatment — 4 weeks
Weight change (improving/static/deteriorating)? MUST score re-calculated. ONS compliance? Nausea controlled? Drug review completed? Underlying cause treated? If not improving: escalate investigation via the NG12 or non-specific symptoms pathway. Refer dietitian if not already referred.
Eating disorders — ongoing
Physical monitoring as agreed with CEDS according to risk: weight, BMI, pulse, BP (orthostatic), temperature, electrolytes, ECG. Document the MEED CR233 (formerly MARSIPAN) physical risk assessment at each visit. Coordinate with CEDS. Ensure patient remains engaged with treatment. Do not discharge from GP care — eating disorders are chronic, relapsing conditions requiring long-term GP support.
Cancer cachexia — palliative framing
Anorexia in established cancer with cachexia: shift goal from weight restoration to comfort and quality of life. Small portions of preferred foods. Family counselling on meaning of reduced oral intake in dying patients ("not eating is part of the dying process — forcing food causes distress, not benefit"). Palliative care team involvement. See Palliative Care Pain algorithm.
Same-day / 999 safety-net
Inability to maintain fluids, recurrent vomiting, collapse or fainting, confusion, marked weakness, palpitations or breathlessness → same-day assessment · Any physical deterioration in an eating disorder patient (bradycardia, collapse, new electrolyte disturbance, hypoglycaemia) or suicidal thoughts/inability to remain safe → 999/same-day · New confusion + vomiting + constipation + anorexia → hypercalcaemia/Addison's crisis → bloods urgently + consider admission · Refeeding syndrome developing (hypophosphataemia + respiratory deterioration) → admission
Same-week review
Weight loss accelerating (≥1 kg per week) despite treatment · New alarm feature emerging at follow-up (dysphagia, haemoptysis, jaundice, haematemesis, PR blood, persistent vomiting) → act the same day on the NICE NG12 route (suspected cancer pathway referral, or FIT for PR blood) · Anorexia + new depression developing → PHQ-9 + NHS Talking Therapies/psychiatry referral · MUST score rising despite intervention → dietitian escalation
Documentation
Working differential · weight trajectory · investigations requested · referral pathway and urgency · agreed review date · explicit safety-netting advice
Unexplained weight loss needs an active, documented plan rather than open-ended watchful waiting: review within 1–4 weeks according to trajectory and risk, and if first-line investigations are reassuring but weight loss continues, use the NG12 or local non-specific symptoms pathway. The family counselling around reduced oral intake in dying patients is one of the most important and emotionally charged conversations in palliative primary care — many families interpret reduced eating in a dying patient as 'giving up' or 'not being fed properly' and request interventional feeding (NG tube, PEG, TPN). Providing accurate, compassionate information that reduced oral intake is a normal part of the dying process (reduced metabolic demand, reduced gut absorption, central appetite suppression from disease) and that forced feeding in the dying causes discomfort without benefit or survival extension is a core GP communication skill. The Palliative Care Pain algorithm addresses this context further. NICE NG31 (Care of Dying Adults) specifically states that artificial nutrition and hydration should not be started routinely in the last days of life unless there is a specific clinical indication and it is consistent with the patient's wishes.
