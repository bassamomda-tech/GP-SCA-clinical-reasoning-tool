Confusion — acute confusion and delirium in adults For use by GPs and practice clinicians in primary care · assessment and referral guide
Scope: adults presenting with new or worsening confusion, altered behaviour, reduced attention or fluctuating alertness. This is an assessment and referral guide, not a substitute for local emergency, antimicrobial, safeguarding or medicines protocols.
Overall assessment: new confusion developing over hours to days should be treated as delirium until proven otherwise. Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention and awareness, usually fluctuating, and often has more than one precipitant. A normal cognitive score does not exclude delirium if the collateral history is incomplete. Always establish: (1) Is the patient immediately unsafe or physiologically unstable? (2) Is the change acute, chronic or acute-on-chronic? (3) What is the patient's usual cognitive and functional baseline? (4) What reversible cause or combination of causes is present? (5) Can the person be safely managed at home with reliable supervision and review? A collateral history from a relative, carer or care-home staff member is essential.

⚠️ New confusion is delirium until proven otherwise: acute fluctuating inattention demands a hunt for an organic precipitant — never assume it is "just dementia", and remember hypoactive delirium is easily missed. Always check a glucose, apply the NICE sepsis criteria, and review the actual medicines.

Safety

Immediate Safety Assessment — 999 and Same-Day Criteria

At first contact, including a home visit: capillary glucose; respiratory rate and oxygen saturation; pulse rate and rhythm; blood pressure; temperature; level of consciousness (AVPU or GCS); recent urine output; focused neurological examination; medication and recent medication-change review. Do not delay emergency transfer to complete cognitive testing or routine investigations.

Call 999 and pre-alert secondary care if there is:

Reduced consciousness Reduced or deteriorating consciousness, inability to stay awake or GCS ≤13
Suspected sepsis Any NICE high-risk criterion: objective new altered mental state, RR ≥25/min, HR >130/min, systolic BP ≤90 mmHg, no urine for 18 hours, mottled or ashen appearance, cyanosis or non-blanching petechial/purpuric rash → 999 with pre-alert [1]. A collateral history of new altered behaviour is a moderate-to-high-risk feature — use the complete NICE criteria and clinical judgement
Stroke or intracranial haemorrhage Sudden confusion with facial weakness, limb weakness, dysphasia, visual loss, new ataxia or other focal deficit
Seizure Ongoing seizure, recurrent seizure or prolonged unexplained post-ictal drowsiness
Meningitis / encephalitis Particularly fever, headache, neck stiffness and altered consciousness/cognition, or a non-blanching petechial/purpuric rash. Antibiotics must not delay transfer; give pre-hospital ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin only where delay is clinically significant, following the current local protocol [10]
Opioid toxicity Suspected opioid toxicity with respiratory depression — pinpoint pupils, slow shallow breathing, drowsiness
Serotonin toxicity / NMS Severe serotonin toxicity (serotonergic drugs + agitation, hyperthermia, tremor, clonus) or neuroleptic malignant syndrome (antipsychotic + rigidity, fever, fluctuating consciousness)
Hypoglycaemia Severe hypoglycaemia not promptly corrected or with ongoing risk of recurrence. Treat low capillary glucose immediately
Critically ill Any patient who appears critically ill or cannot be safely assessed or supervised
Arrange same-day emergency hospital assessment for:
No clear cause
Delirium with no clear, treatable cause after an appropriate primary-care assessment
Needs hospital treatment
A cause requiring IV fluids, oxygen, IV antibiotics, monitored treatment or urgent correction of significant metabolic disturbance
Head injury on an anticoagulant or antiplatelet
Head injury in a patient taking an anticoagulant (DOAC, warfarin, heparin) or an antiplatelet drug other than aspirin monotherapy. NICE advises considering CT head within 8 hours of injury, or within 1 hour if presenting more than 8 hours after injury, even with no other CT indication [2]. A confused patient will usually need ED assessment because reliable risk assessment and observation may be difficult
Alcohol withdrawal / Wernicke's
Alcohol withdrawal or suspected Wernicke's encephalopathy
Agitation or risk
Severe agitation, risk of harm or inability to provide essential care
Unsafe at home
No responsible person able to supervise the patient; carer breakdown or an unsafe home environment
Severe hypertension
Clinic BP ≥180/120 mmHg with new confusion, papilloedema, retinal haemorrhage, chest pain, heart failure or acute kidney injury → same-day specialist assessment [3]
The first minutes of assessment are about the causes that kill or maim quickly: low glucose, sepsis, hypoxia, intracranial bleeding, meningitis, toxic states and withdrawal. A glucose, a full set of observations and a short neurological examination screen for most of them, which is why they come before any cognitive test. Confusion alone does not automatically mean 999: in suspected infection, an objective new altered mental state is a NICE high-risk sepsis feature and a collateral report of new altered behaviour is a moderate-to-high-risk feature, so apply the complete NICE criteria with clinical judgement [1]. NICE recommends NEWS2 in specified acute settings and advises considering an early warning score in community settings; in primary care, use full observations, the NICE community sepsis criteria and clinical judgement rather than a single NEWS2 threshold [1]. Subdural haematoma is the classic trap: the fall may have been days ago and unwitnessed, and the only sign may be fluctuating drowsiness in someone on an anticoagulant.

Diagnose

Confirm Probable Delirium — Collateral History and 4AT

New confusion developing over hours to days should be treated as delirium until proven otherwise. A collateral history from someone who knows the person's baseline is essential.
Collateral history
What is the person normally like? When did the change begin? Was onset sudden or gradual? Does alertness or behaviour fluctuate? Any new hallucinations, agitation or withdrawal? Any recent fall, head injury or new focal symptom? Any new medicines, dose changes, omitted medicines or over-the-counter products? How much are they eating and drinking? When did they last pass urine and open their bowels? Any pain, fever, cough, breathlessness, dysuria or other infection symptoms? Recent move, hospital admission, bereavement or sleep disruption?
4AT items
1. Alertness — normal 0; clearly abnormal (for example drowsy or hyperalert) 4. 2. AMT4 (age, date of birth, place, current year) — no errors 0; one error 1; two or more errors or untestable 2. 3. Attention (months backwards) — 7 or more correct 0; starts but achieves fewer than 7, or refuses 1; untestable 2. 4. Acute change or fluctuating course — no 0; yes 4
4AT score
≥4 possible delirium, with or without cognitive impairment — investigate and act; 1–3 possible cognitive impairment; 0 delirium or severe cognitive impairment unlikely, but delirium is not excluded if acute-change information is unavailable. The 4AT is a screening tool; the diagnosis remains clinical and requires a change from baseline. NICE recommends the 4AT where indicators of delirium are present [4]
Delirium
Hours to days; fluctuates; impaired attention and altered alertness; may be hyperactive, hypoactive or mixed
Dementia
Months to years; progressive cognitive and functional decline; attention relatively preserved until later, although Lewy body dementia commonly fluctuates
Delirium superimposed on dementia
New deterioration from the person's established baseline; manage the delirium first
Depression
Usually weeks or longer; poor effort and "I don't know" responses, but attention is generally intact
Primary psychosis or mania
Usually clear consciousness and relatively preserved attention/orientation, with delusions, hallucinations or elevated mood predominating
Hypoactive delirium
Withdrawal, slowed responses, reduced mobility, sleepiness and poor intake — easily missed
Onset and fluctuation are the two features that separate delirium from dementia, and both come from the history rather than a test score. That is why the 4AT gives 4 points to the acute-change item and why the collateral account matters so much: without it the test cannot be scored properly. A patient too drowsy or agitated to be tested still scores 4 or more, so the most unwell are not falsely reassured. Hypoactive delirium is under-recognised because the patient causes no trouble; a carer who says "she's just not herself, sleeping all day" is describing it. Record the 4AT score so that the trajectory can be tracked at review.

Diagnose

Search for the Cause — PINCH ME

PINCH ME is a teaching mnemonic, not a diagnostic test or guideline in its own right. More than one precipitant is common. NICE risk factors for delirium: age 65 or over, cognitive impairment or dementia, current hip fracture, severe illness [4].
P — Pain
Occult fracture after a fall, urinary retention, faecal impaction, pressure damage, dental pain, pain related to immobility or infection. Use an observational pain assessment tool if communication is impaired
I — Infection
Chest; skin, wound or soft-tissue; biliary, dental or other abdominal; COVID-19 or influenza where clinically indicated; urinary infection only when urinary or systemic features support it. Older adults may be afebrile or hypothermic. Do not attribute confusion alone to UTI
N — Nutrition
Poor intake, weight loss or malnutrition; thiamine deficiency (heavy alcohol use, prolonged vomiting, bariatric surgery or severe malnutrition); B12 or folate deficiency where there is a background of cognitive decline or relevant clinical features. Suspected Wernicke's encephalopathy → same-day hospital assessment for parenteral thiamine and treatment of associated complications
C — Constipation
Ask when the bowels last opened. Examine for faecal loading if indicated and clinically appropriate. Review opioid and anticholinergic use
H — Hydration, hypoxia and metabolic
Dehydration or AKI; hypoxia or hypercapnia; hyponatraemia; hypercalcaemia; hypo- or hyperglycaemia; renal or hepatic failure; other electrolyte abnormalities
M — Medication and withdrawal
Check the actual medication boxes, blister packs and recent changes — not only the repeat list. Opioids; benzodiazepines and Z-drugs; anticholinergics (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, sedating antihistamines); dopaminergic or antiparkinsonian medicines; steroids; digoxin; lithium; antiepileptics; recently started or stopped medicines; alcohol, benzodiazepine or other sedative withdrawal; over-the-counter sleep aids and antihistamines. Do not stop benzodiazepines, alcohol-dependent regimens or antiepileptics abruptly without an appropriate plan
E — Environment
Recent admission, discharge, care-home move or change of room; missing or malfunctioning glasses or hearing aids; sleep disruption; catheterisation; restraint or immobility; bereavement or unfamiliar carers
PINCH ME is not a guideline tool, but it matches the precipitants NICE asks clinicians to look for [4] and keeps the search systematic. A structured sweep stops the first abnormal result being accepted as the whole answer — bacteria in the urine or a low sodium may be one contributor among several. Total anticholinergic load, added up with an anticholinergic burden scale, is a modifiable contributor to both delirium and longer-term cognitive impairment: oxybutynin, tricyclics and sedating antihistamines all cross into the brain. Constipation and retention cause confusion through discomfort and are easily missed in people who cannot describe their symptoms.

Diagnose

Focused Examination

Perform a head-to-toe examination even if the patient is difficult to assess.
Observations
Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, BP, pulse rate and rhythm, temperature, level of consciousness and capillary glucose
Neurological
Pupils, focal weakness, speech, gait if safe, neck stiffness, nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, tremor, asterixis, myoclonus, clonus and rigidity
Chest and heart
Pneumonia, heart failure, hypoxia, arrhythmia
Abdomen
Tenderness, distension, jaundice, ascites, palpable bladder and faecal loading
Skin
Cellulitis, wounds, pressure damage, bruising, dehydration, shingles and non-blanching rash
Injury screen
Scalp haematoma, hip or wrist pain, rib tenderness or reduced limb movement after a fall
Medication review
Missed doses, duplicate dosing, recent prescriptions and non-prescription medicines
Urinary retention and faecal impaction are easily missed causes, especially in older men and people taking opioids or anticholinergics, and are found only by examining the abdomen; palpate the bladder or use a bladder scanner. Pneumonia in frail older people can present with confusion and a raised respiratory rate but little fever or cough. Asterixis and myoclonus shift the differential towards metabolic or toxic causes. Unexplained bruising should prompt thought about falls on anticoagulation and, where the story does not fit, about safeguarding. Checking the medicines physically in the home often reveals double-dosing from old and new boxes or a recently started sedative that is not on the repeat template.

Investigations

Investigations should be guided by clinical findings and the likely setting of care. If admitting, do not delay transfer for primary-care tests.
Bedside (immediate)
Capillary glucose SpO₂ Temperature Bladder scan if available
Bloods — same day if managing at home
FBC U&E, creatinine, eGFR CRP LFTs Bone profile (calcium) Glucose TFTs — with a clear process for reviewing results
Consider
B12 and folate if there was pre-existing cognitive decline or relevant clinical suspicion; magnesium and phosphate if malnourished or taking a PPI or diuretic; drug levels where relevant (digoxin, lithium, antiepileptics); blood gas if hypoxia or hypercapnia is suspected and this will alter management; ECG if arrhythmia, ischaemia or QT prolongation is suspected, and before considering an antipsychotic
Urine
Do not use a positive urine dipstick or bacteriuria alone to diagnose UTI in an older person with confusion. Send urine for culture when there are urinary symptoms or signs, or when sepsis is suspected without another source. Do not prescribe antibiotics for asymptomatic bacteriuria in non-pregnant women or men [5]. Follow the local Greater Manchester antimicrobial pathway
Chest X-ray
Consider if there are chest signs, hypoxia or fever without an obvious source
Neuroimaging
CT head is generally a hospital investigation. Consider for focal neurological signs; head injury, particularly when anticoagulants or non-aspirin antiplatelets are involved; reduced consciousness; post-traumatic seizure; suspected raised intracranial pressure; or persistent or progressive symptoms without an identified cause after appropriate assessment. Not a routine investigation for every delirium with an obvious medical precipitant
NG12 Progressive neurology
Progressive, subacute loss of central neurological function → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain, or CT if MRI is contraindicated [6]. Ordinary acute confusion alone does not meet this criterion
NG12 Hypercalcaemia
Aged 60 or over with hypercalcaemia and a presentation suggestive of myeloma → very urgent myeloma investigations: FBC, calcium, plasma viscosity or ESR, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains; a Bence–Jones test is an alternative if serum free light-chain testing is unavailable [7]
Bedside glucose is mandatory in every confused patient because hypoglycaemia is both dangerous and instantly reversible. Blood tests find the silent causes — hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, AKI, hepatic dysfunction and hypothyroidism rarely announce themselves on examination. Bacteriuria without infection is common in older people, so a positive dipstick in a confused patient often leads to an unnecessary antibiotic while the true cause is missed; send culture selectively [5]. CT head has a low yield when there is a clear medical cause and no focal signs, head injury, anticoagulation or reduced consciousness. An ECG provides the baseline QTc needed before haloperidol, because both the drug and the electrolyte disturbances of delirium can prolong it. Hypercalcaemia may be the first sign of myeloma or metastatic cancer, which is why it maps to NG12.

Refer

Decide Where the Patient Should Be Managed

999 / same-day hospital
Any emergency or same-day criterion in step 1
Home management — only when all apply
The cause is reasonably clear and treatable in the community; there are no emergency features; the patient is physiologically stable; the patient can take sufficient fluids and treatment safely; a responsible person can remain with or reliably supervise them; the environment is safe; same-day results can be reviewed; daily or otherwise appropriate follow-up is available; the clinician has a low threshold for escalation
Community services
Consider local urgent community response, virtual ward, hospital-at-home or community frailty services where available
Capacity
Assess capacity for the specific decision, not simply because the person is confused (Mental Capacity Act 2005). Document the decision being considered; the information provided; whether the person can understand, retain, weigh and communicate the relevant information; best-interests reasoning if capacity is absent; family, attorney or advocate involvement; the least restrictive option
Safeguarding
Raise safeguarding concerns for suspected neglect, abuse, self-neglect or an unsafe home situation
Old-age psychiatry / geriatrics
Persistent delirium, severe distress or psychotic symptoms, diagnostic uncertainty, or advice when haloperidol is contraindicated
Memory service
Only once delirium has resolved and cognition has not returned to baseline — see step 9 [9]
Community management of delirium is appropriate only when the cause is identified and treatable at home, the person is safe, and someone can supervise and monitor; any uncertainty favours admission. Carer strain often precipitates a crisis, so asking the carer how they are coping is part of managing the patient. Capacity is decision-specific; confusion alone does not mean a person lacks capacity, and GPs are often best placed to assess it and to record best-interests decisions made with the family.

Treat

Management — Treat the Cause and Manage Distress

Every patient — treat the cause
Cause-directed treatment
Antimicrobials only when infection is clinically suspected or confirmed, following the local formulary. Oral fluids where safe; hospital if IV fluid or close monitoring is needed. Treat urinary retention and constipation or faecal impaction. Review and reduce contributory medicines. Treat hypoxia or hypercapnia. Correct glucose and significant metabolic abnormalities, in hospital where necessary. Treat alcohol or sedative withdrawal using an appropriate specialist/local protocol. Parenteral thiamine in hospital when Wernicke's encephalopathy is suspected.
Distress or risk of harm
De-escalate first, then short-term haloperidol
1. De-escalate first: reduce stimulation, use a calm approach, involve a familiar person and treat pain, thirst, retention or constipation. 2. Consider medication only if the person is distressed or poses a risk to themselves or others and de-escalation has failed or is inappropriate. 3. NICE recommends considering short-term haloperidol [4]: start at the lowest clinically appropriate dose, titrate cautiously and use for the shortest possible period, usually no more than one week, with a clear stop/review plan. In older or frail people use a substantially lower starting dose and seek senior, geriatric or old-age psychiatry advice where possible. Do not use haloperidol routinely for hypoactive delirium.
Before prescribing haloperidol
ECG, electrolytes, contraindications
Where feasible: baseline ECG and QTc; correct hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia; review other QT-prolonging medicines and interactions; consider renal and hepatic impairment, frailty and cardiovascular disease; document indication, intended duration and review date. The product information contraindicates haloperidol in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, known QTc prolongation, congenital long-QT syndrome, recent myocardial infarction, uncompensated heart failure, ventricular arrhythmia/torsades history, uncorrected hypokalaemia and with concomitant QT-prolonging medicines; it warns of QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmia, sudden death, extrapyramidal effects, neuroleptic malignant syndrome and increased mortality in older people with dementia [8]. If contraindicated, seek specialist advice.
Benzodiazepines
Not for routine use
Do not use benzodiazepines routinely to treat delirium; they can worsen confusion, falls and respiratory depression. Reserve them principally for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, using the relevant local or hospital protocol.
Haloperidol does not treat delirium; it only reduces dangerous distress while the cause is treated, which is why NICE limits it to short-term use when de-escalation has failed [4]. Antipsychotics can cause severe sensitivity reactions in Lewy body disease, and haloperidol is contraindicated in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies [8], so knowing the dementia subtype before prescribing is essential. Alternative antipsychotics are not offered as standalone GP choices: when haloperidol is contraindicated, seek specialist input or follow a local protocol. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are not an acute treatment for delirium; dementia diagnosis and treatment belong to the memory-service pathway once delirium has resolved.

Lifestyle

Non-Drug Care for Every Patient

Reassurance Calm, simple explanations and repeated reassurance; a familiar person or carer present where possible
Orientation Reorientation using name, place, date, clock and calendar
Light and sleep Good daytime light; quiet, dark nights. Avoid routine hypnotics and sedating antihistamines
Sensory aids Ensure glasses and hearing aids are available and functioning
Eating and drinking Encourage drinks and food if safe; assess swallowing if concerned
Mobilisation Mobilise with assistance where safe
Physical comfort Address pain, thirst, constipation and urinary retention
Avoid Unnecessary catheterisation, restraint, room changes and unfamiliar carers
Non-drug measures are safe and target the same factors that precipitate and prolong delirium — sensory impairment, sleep disruption, immobility, dehydration and unfamiliar surroundings. They apply to every patient, whether or not medication is also needed, and carers can deliver most of them at home or in a care home. Explaining delirium to carers as the brain's reaction to illness reduces their distress and helps them recognise deterioration.

Safety

Follow-Up, Safety-Netting and Documentation

If managed at home
Review according to severity, ideally daily during the acute phase: repeat observations; repeat 4AT or assess attention and alertness clinically; check intake, urine output and bowel function; confirm the presumed cause is improving; review blood results; confirm the carer can continue to supervise safely
Not improving
Deteriorating or not beginning to improve within 48–72 hours → reassess the diagnosis, search for a second precipitant and consider admission
If an antipsychotic was used
Review within days; stop as soon as clinically appropriate; confirm deprescribing within 1–2 weeks; document response and adverse effects
After recovery
After delirium has fully resolved — often several weeks later — repeat cognitive and functional assessment. If cognition has not returned to baseline, investigate reversible causes and refer to a memory service or appropriate specialist dementia pathway. NICE advises investigating delirium before diagnosing dementia and treating delirium first when the distinction is unclear [9]
999 safety-net
Harder to wake, new weakness or speech difficulty, a fit, a non-blanching rash or breathing difficulty, or a fall with a head injury while taking a blood thinner
Same-day GP
Worsening, stops eating or drinking, develops a fever, becomes very agitated, or not beginning to improve within two or three days
Wording
"The confusion should start to improve over the next few days as we treat the cause. Call 999 if they become harder to wake, develop new weakness or speech difficulty, have a fit, develop a non-blanching rash or breathing difficulty, or fall and hit their head while taking a blood thinner. Contact the surgery the same day if they are worsening, stop eating or drinking, develop a fever, become very agitated, or are not beginning to improve within two or three days."
Document
Baseline cognition and function; collateral history; 4AT score and clinical diagnosis; examination and observations; suspected precipitating causes; investigations and results; capacity and safeguarding decisions; referral or supervision plan; medication changes and antipsychotic stop date; follow-up and cognitive outcome
Delirium is associated with higher mortality, later institutionalisation and new dementia, so follow-up does not end when the acute episode settles. Cognitive tests during delirium are unreliable, so the dementia question is answered only after recovery [9]. A named stop date stops a short-term antipsychotic drifting onto the repeat list. For the SCA, the examiner will be listening for explicit, plain-language safety-netting for the carer, a named review plan, and an acknowledgement of carer strain.
