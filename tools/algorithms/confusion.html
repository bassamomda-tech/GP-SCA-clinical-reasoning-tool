1
Safety

Red Flags — Life-Threatening Causes of Confusion

First, is the patient safe right now? Then: is this acute (hours to days) or chronic (months)? New or suddenly worse confusion is delirium until proven otherwise. Check a capillary glucose and full observations (NEWS2) at first contact.
Hypoglycaemia Capillary glucose <4 mmol/L → oral glucose if the swallow is safe; glucagon or IV glucose and 999 if not. The quickest reversible cause
Suspected sepsis New altered mental state is a high-risk criterion in NICE NG253 (2025), alongside RR ≥25, HR >130, systolic BP ≤90, no urine for 18 h and non-blanching rash → 999 with pre-alert
Fever + neck stiffness / rash Meningitis, meningococcal disease or encephalitis → 999. For strongly suspected meningococcal disease NICE NG240 (2024) advises IV/IM ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin (dose per BNF) before transfer, but never delay transfer to give it
Stroke Sudden onset with facial droop, arm weakness, speech disturbance or visual loss → 999. Posterior circulation stroke may show mainly confusion, ataxia or vertigo
Head injury / subdural Fluctuating drowsiness days or weeks after a fall, especially on an anticoagulant → same-day ED; NICE NG232 (2023) advises considering CT head within 8 hours of injury for people on anticoagulants
Severe metabolic disturbance Known sodium <125 mmol/L, adjusted calcium >3.0 mmol/L, or severe hyperglycaemia with confusion → same-day hospital correction
Opioid or sedative toxicity Pinpoint pupils, slow shallow breathing, drowsiness → 999 and naloxone (dose per BNF) if opioid suspected
Serotonin syndrome / NMS Serotonergic drugs + agitation, hyperthermia, tremor, clonus → serotonin syndrome; antipsychotic + rigidity, fever, fluctuating consciousness → neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Both → 999
Alcohol withdrawal / Wernicke's Tremor, sweating, hallucinations after stopping drinking, or confusion with ataxia and eye-movement signs → same-day admission; parenteral thiamine (NICE CG100)
NG12 Progressive neurological loss Progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function (for example cognitive or personality change over weeks with focal features) → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain, or CT if MRI contraindicated (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), rec 1.9.1)
The first minutes of assessment are about the causes that kill or maim quickly: low glucose, sepsis, hypoxia, intracranial bleeding, meningitis, toxic states and withdrawal. A glucose, a set of observations and a short neurological examination cover almost all of them. NG253 (November 2025) replaced NG51 for people aged 16 or over and still treats a new altered mental state as a high-risk sign. NG240 changed pre-hospital practice: antibiotics before transfer are for strongly suspected meningococcal disease and must not hold up the ambulance. Serotonin syndrome is distinguished from neuroleptic malignant syndrome by speed of onset (hours rather than days), clonus and hyperreflexia rather than lead-pipe rigidity, and the drug history. A slowly progressive confusion with focal features is not delirium and fits the NG12 brain-tumour criterion, so imaging rather than a delirium screen is the priority.

Diagnose

Delirium vs Dementia vs Psychiatric — Key Distinction

This distinction drives the entire pathway. A collateral history from someone who knows the person's baseline is essential.
Delirium (acute)
Onset hours to days. Fluctuates through the day. Inattention and altered alertness. Always has a precipitant — treat the cause (NICE CG103, 2010, updated 2023)
Dementia (chronic)
Onset months to years; gradual decline in memory, language, executive function or visuospatial skills. Attention and alertness preserved until late. Lewy body dementia is the exception — fluctuation and visual hallucinations are core features
Delirium on dementia
Common, especially in care homes. Any sudden decline in known dementia needs the same search for a precipitant
Depression
Onset weeks; low mood, poor effort ("don't know"), slowed but attentive. Can mimic dementia in older people
Primary psychosis / mania
Onset days to weeks; usually alert and orientated, with delusions, hallucinations or elated mood predominant → mental health pathway
4AT screen
Alertness (clearly abnormal = 4) + AMT4 (age, date of birth, place, year: 0 / 1 / 2) + attention (months backwards: 0 / 1 / 2) + acute change or fluctuation (yes = 4). ≥4 possible delirium ± cognitive impairment; 1–3 possible cognitive impairment; 0 delirium or severe impairment unlikely. Recommended by SIGN 157 (2019)
Chronic screen
If acute causes are excluded and the story is of gradual decline: a structured cognitive test (for example 6CIT, Mini-Cog, MoCA) — but a normal score does not exclude dementia
Onset and fluctuation are the two features that separate delirium from dementia, and both come from the history rather than a test score. That is why the 4AT gives 4 points to the acute-change item and why the collateral account matters so much. The 4AT had a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.88 in a 2021 meta-analysis (Tieges et al., Age and Ageing) and can be completed in a patient too drowsy to answer, who then scores high. NICE NG97 (2018) warns against ruling out dementia on the basis of a normal cognitive test when the history is convincing, and asks that delirium, depression, sensory impairment and anticholinergic medicines are considered before a dementia referral.

Diagnose

Find the Cause — PINCH ME Framework

PINCH ME is a teaching mnemonic (clinical practice) for sweeping through delirium precipitants. Often more than one is present. NICE CG103 risk factors: age 65 or over, cognitive impairment or dementia, current hip fracture, severe illness.
P — Pain
Fracture after an unwitnessed fall, retention, impaction, pressure damage, dental pain. In dementia or after stroke, pain may show only as agitation — use an observational pain scale
I — Infection
Chest, urine (only with urinary symptoms or signs), skin, biliary, dental; COVID-19 and influenza. Older people may be afebrile
N — Nutrition
Poor intake, weight loss, thiamine deficiency (alcohol, vomiting, bariatric surgery), B12 deficiency
C — Constipation
Last bowel opening; abdominal and, if indicated, rectal examination. Treat impaction with a macrogol at the dose the BNF gives for impaction
H — Hydration / hypoxia
Dehydration, AKI, low sodium, high calcium; SpO₂ — COPD, pneumonia, PE, heart failure
M — Medication
Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, chlorphenamine), opioids, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, antiparkinsonian drugs, steroids, digoxin, lithium, recent starts or dose changes, and withdrawal of alcohol or sedatives
E — Environment
Recent move, lost glasses or hearing aids, catheter, immobility, sleep disruption
PINCH ME is not a guideline tool, but it matches the precipitants NICE CG103 asks clinicians to look for and keeps the search systematic. Dehydration and infection are frequent contributors in the community, but a structured sweep stops the first abnormal result being accepted as the whole answer. Total anticholinergic load, added up with an anticholinergic burden scale, is a modifiable contributor to both delirium and longer-term cognitive impairment: oxybutynin, tricyclics and sedating antihistamines all cross into the brain. Constipation and retention cause confusion through discomfort and are easily missed in people who cannot describe their symptoms.

Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Observations
RR, SpO₂, BP (postural if safe), pulse rhythm, temperature, consciousness, capillary glucose. A NEWS2 score of 5 or more, or 3 in a single parameter, triggers an urgent response (Royal College of Physicians NEWS2, 2017)
Cognition
4AT if acute. If chronic: 6CIT, Mini-Cog or MoCA, repeated when any delirium has resolved
Neurological
Focal signs, meningism, gait, pupils; nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia (Wernicke's); asterixis (hepatic, uraemic, CO₂ retention); clonus (serotonin syndrome); rigidity (NMS, Parkinson's); papilloedema (raised pressure)
Chest
Consolidation, crackles of heart failure, wheeze; a raised respiratory rate is often the earliest sign of pneumonia in older people
Abdomen
Palpable bladder, faecal loading, tenderness, hepatomegaly or stigmata of liver disease
Urine
Do not dipstick to diagnose UTI in people over 65: asymptomatic bacteriuria rises with age, and by 80 about half of care-home residents have bacteria in the urine without infection (UKHSA diagnostic tool, updated December 2024). Send culture only with urinary symptoms or signs, or sepsis without another source
Skin
Cellulitis, pressure damage, non-blanching rash, bruising (falls, anticoagulation, safeguarding), jaundice
Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in a confused older person gives no benefit, adds antibiotic harms and resistance, and — most importantly — lets the real cause go unfound. That is why UKHSA advises against dipstick testing in people over 65 and focuses instead on urinary symptoms and systemic signs. Pneumonia in frail older people can present with confusion and a raised respiratory rate but little fever or cough. Urinary retention is painful and confusing; palpate the bladder or use a bladder scanner, especially in men with prostatic symptoms and anyone on an anticholinergic or opioid. Asterixis and myoclonus shift the differential towards metabolic or toxic causes.

Diagnose

Investigations

Bedside (immediate)
Capillary glucose SpO₂ NEWS2 ECG (new AF, silent ischaemia, QTc before any antipsychotic) Bladder scan if available
Bloods — acute
FBC U&E CRP LFTs Calcium Glucose TFTs; add B12/folate if cognition was already declining, magnesium if malnourished or on a PPI or diuretic, and drug levels (digoxin, lithium, antiepileptics) where relevant
Infection screen
Urine culture (not dipstick) when urinary symptoms or signs; CXR if chest signs or fever without a source; blood cultures and LP are hospital investigations
Toxicology
Paracetamol level and LFTs if unexplained hepatic picture or possible accidental overdose; alcohol history and breath/blood alcohol in hospital
Neuroimaging
CT head (hospital) for focal signs, head injury — especially on anticoagulants (NICE NG232, 2023) — reduced GCS, papilloedema, or no cause after a full screen. Not required for every delirium
Dementia bloods
Before memory-service referral, exclude reversible causes with FBC, calcium, glucose (HbA1c), renal and liver function, TFTs, and B12 and folate (NICE NG97, 2018). Syphilis serology and HIV only when there are risk factors or atypical features. Structural imaging is arranged by the specialist service
NG12 Hypercalcaemia
Confusion with raised calcium in someone aged 60 or over with a presentation consistent with myeloma → very urgent (within 48 hours) myeloma tests as specified in NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)
Bedside glucose is mandatory in every confused patient because hypoglycaemia is both dangerous and instantly reversible. An ECG earns its place twice: it can reveal a silent inferior infarct or new AF as the cause, and it provides the baseline QTc that is needed before haloperidol. Hypercalcaemia can cause confusion before any other symptom and may be the first sign of myeloma or metastatic cancer, which is why it maps to NG12. B12 deficiency and hypothyroidism are reversible contributors to cognitive decline and belong in the chronic work-up; NG97 lists the core blood tests and advises structural imaging within the specialist diagnostic pathway to exclude reversible causes and help with subtype.

Refer

Referral Criteria

999
Meningism or non-blanching rash, stroke, seizure, reduced consciousness, sepsis high-risk criteria, hypoglycaemia not responding, opioid toxicity, serotonin syndrome or NMS
Same-day hospital
Delirium with no cause found in primary care, cause needing hospital treatment, electrolyte disturbance needing IV correction, head injury on an anticoagulant, alcohol withdrawal, severe agitation, confused person alone with nobody to supervise
NG12 Suspected cancer
Progressive, sub-acute loss of central neurological function → consider urgent direct-access MRI brain (NG12 rec 1.9.1). Hypercalcaemia aged 60+ consistent with myeloma → very urgent myeloma tests (NG12)
Memory service
Suspected dementia once delirium, depression, sensory impairment and anticholinergic medicines have been considered and reversible causes investigated (NICE NG97, 2018). Rapid decline over weeks to months needs a faster specialist route
Old-age psychiatry
Dementia with severe distress or psychotic symptoms, persistent delirium, or diagnostic uncertainty
Neurology
Suspected encephalitis follow-up, rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus (possible prion disease), seizure-related confusion, young-onset cognitive decline
Social care
Unsafe at home — urgent social care or rapid-response assessment. Safeguarding concern for self-neglect or suspected abuse. Assess capacity for specific decisions (Mental Capacity Act 2005)
Community management of delirium is appropriate only when the cause is identified and treatable at home, the person is safe, and someone can supervise and monitor; any uncertainty favours admission. Dementia referral should not be delayed once reversible causes are dealt with — a timely diagnosis opens access to treatment, carer support and legal planning (Lasting Power of Attorney, advance decisions) while the person can still take part. A rapidly progressive course is not typical of Alzheimer's disease and should be flagged in the referral. Capacity is decision-specific; GPs are often best placed to assess it and to record best-interests decisions made with the family.

Treat

Management — Delirium and Dementia

Delirium — treat cause
Cause-directed care + non-drug measures
Antibiotics for confirmed or clearly suspected infection, fluids for dehydration, bowel care, catheter for retention, stop or reduce culprit drugs (taper benzodiazepines and antiepileptics). Reorientation, familiar people, glasses and hearing aids, daytime light, quiet nights, mobilise.
Delirium with dangerous distress
Short-term haloperidol
Only if the person is distressed or a risk to self or others and de-escalation has failed. Usually 1 week or less, lowest clinically appropriate dose, titrate cautiously (NICE CG103); dose per BNF, ideally with specialist advice. Older people have raised cardiac and extrapyramidal risk (MHRA Drug Safety Update, December 2021). Use with caution or not at all in Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies. Benzodiazepines only for alcohol or sedative withdrawal — oral lorazepam first-line in delirium tremens (NICE CG100).
Alzheimer's or Lewy body dementia
Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor
Donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine for mild to moderate Alzheimer's; donepezil or rivastigmine for mild to moderate Lewy body dementia (NICE NG97, 2018). Started on specialist advice; dose per BNF. Watch for nausea, diarrhoea and bradycardia — check pulse and ask about syncope.
UTITreat only with urinary symptoms or signs. Per NICE NG109 (2018): nitrofurantoin 100 mg modified-release twice daily (if eGFR ≥45) or trimethoprim 200 mg twice daily (if low resistance risk) — 3 days for women, 7 days for men. Follow local formulary and culture results.
Hepatic encephalopathyUsually a hospital or specialist-led diagnosis. Lactulose titrated to two to three soft stools a day (dose per BNF); rifaximin for recurrence is specialist-initiated. Look for the trigger — infection, GI bleed, constipation, sedatives.
B12 deficiencyIntramuscular hydroxocobalamin; when there is neurological or cognitive involvement the BNF loading regimen is more intensive than for anaemia alone, so use the neurological schedule and seek haematology advice (NICE NG239, 2024). Cognitive improvement, if any, is slow.
Non-drug care is effective and safe: multicomponent programmes reduced delirium incidence from 18.4% to 10.5% in hospital trials (Burton et al., Cochrane, 2021). Haloperidol does not treat delirium; it only reduces dangerous distress while the cause is treated, and NICE CG103 was narrowed in 2023 to short-term haloperidol with an explicit reminder of the MHRA advice. This is the same approach as on the Delirium algorithm, so both pages give the same message. Antipsychotics can cause severe sensitivity reactions in Lewy body disease, so knowing the dementia subtype before prescribing is essential. The UTI regimen differs by sex because men need longer treatment (NG109), and nitrofurantoin is avoided when eGFR is below 45 except in the narrow circumstances the MHRA (2014) allows.

Lifestyle

Delirium Prevention and Dementia Support

Orientation Clock, calendar, family photographs, natural light. Explain where they are and what is happening at each contact. Familiar people reduce distress
Sensory aids Glasses clean and worn, hearing aids working with spare batteries, ear wax removed. Aids must go with the person to hospital
Hydration Offer drinks regularly — thirst lessens with age. Dehydration is both a cause and a consequence of confusion
Medication review Reduce anticholinergic load, sedatives and opioids; review after every episode of confusion and at least annually
Sleep Regular day/night routine, daytime activity, quiet dark nights. Avoid starting hypnotics
Mobilisation Keep moving; falls assessment; physiotherapy after illness
Carer support Explain delirium as the brain's reaction to illness. Offer a carer's assessment. Alzheimer's Society support line 0333 150 3456; Dementia UK Admiral Nurse helpline 0800 888 6678
Advance care planning Lasting Power of Attorney (health and welfare; property and financial affairs), advance decision to refuse treatment, preferred place of care — ideally while capacity is retained
The original multicomponent prevention trial (Inouye et al., NEJM, 1999) targeted six risk factors — cognitive impairment, sleep deprivation, immobility, visual and hearing impairment, and dehydration — and reduced the number and duration of delirium episodes. These measures translate directly to home and care-home settings. Lasting Power of Attorney requires the person to have capacity when it is made, so the conversation should happen early in dementia; waiting until a crisis often means the chance has gone and a Court of Protection deputyship is needed instead. Carer strain is one of the commonest reasons for emergency admission, so asking the carer how they are coping is part of managing the patient.

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

24–48 hours
Delirium at home: review daily with obs and 4AT — improving? cause treated? drinking and eating? carer coping? Worse or not improving by 48–72 hours → reassess and consider admission
1–2 weeks
Stop any antipsychotic, check deprescribing has happened, review results and function
After recovery
Repeat a cognitive test once delirium has fully settled (usually weeks). If cognition has not returned to baseline, investigate and refer for dementia assessment (NICE NG97)
Dementia review
At least annually: cognition and function, carer strain, medicines (including anticholinergic load), falls, driving, advance care planning
Driving
A person with dementia must tell the DVLA. If they continue to drive when unsafe and cannot be persuaded to stop, GMC confidentiality guidance (2017) supports the doctor informing the DVLA, after telling the patient
999 safety-net
Harder to wake, new weakness or speech problem, fit, fever with neck stiffness or rash, breathing difficulty, fall with head injury on a blood thinner
Same-day GP
Confusion worsening or not improving after 2–3 days, new fever, stops eating or drinking, agitation that cannot be settled, carer no longer coping
Wording
"The confusion should start to clear as we treat the cause. If they get sleepier, harder to wake, or develop weakness, a fit or a rash, call 999. If there's no improvement in two or three days, or you're struggling, call us the same day."
Delirium in older people is associated with higher mortality (hazard ratio 1.96) and with later institutionalisation and dementia, independent of age and illness (Witlox et al., JAMA, 2010), so follow-up does not end when the acute episode settles. Cognitive tests during delirium are unreliable, so the dementia question is answered weeks later. The driving position rests on the patient's legal duty to notify the DVLA and the GMC's guidance on when disclosure without consent is justified. For the SCA, the examiner will be listening for explicit, plain-language safety-netting for the carer, a named review plan, and an acknowledgement of carer strain.
