1
Safety

Red Flags — Malignancy, Vascular Emergency & Obstruction

Pulsatile epigastric/central abdominal mass + hypertension history + male age >65 + sudden severe back/flank pain + haemodynamic compromise Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) rupture. → 999. Do NOT palpate vigorously. Two large-bore IV accesses; resuscitate per local major-haemorrhage protocol; contact vascular surgery via the emergency pathway. Do not delay transfer for imaging — CT angiography is arranged in hospital only if stable enough.
Abdominal mass + unexplained weight loss (NG12 60+: >5% within 6 months) + night sweats + anaemia + age >40 Malignancy — GI (colorectal, gastric, pancreatic), lymphoma, ovarian, retroperitoneal sarcoma. → NICE NG12 route by site: quantitative FIT alongside assessment (≥10 µg Hb/g → colorectal suspected cancer pathway; a negative, unreturned or unavailable FIT must not delay referral if concern remains); gynaecology suspected cancer pathway referral for a pelvic or abdominal mass or ascites not obviously fibroids; urgent direct-access USS for a mass consistent with an enlarged liver or gallbladder; consider an upper GI suspected cancer pathway referral for a mass consistent with stomach cancer. Imaging per the receiving pathway. Do not send a routine tumour-marker panel.
Abdominal mass + jaundice + weight loss + upper back pain + painless progressive deterioration in elderly Pancreatic carcinoma or hepatic malignancy. → jaundice at 40 or over: pancreatic suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12); 60 or over with weight loss plus diarrhoea, back pain, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation or new-onset diabetes: urgent direct-access CT (USS if CT unavailable). LFTs + HbA1c; CA19-9 is not a primary-care diagnostic test.
Large tender RUQ mass + fever + history of foreign travel + cystic structure on USS Hepatic abscess (amoebic or pyogenic) or hydatid cyst. → Same-day hepatology/infectious diseases. CT liver + amoebic serology (IHA) + hydatid serology (ELISA). Percutaneous drainage.
Abdominal mass in a child + haematuria + hypertension Wilms' tumour (nephroblastoma) — most common childhood abdominal tumour. → Very urgent referral (within 48 hours) for specialist assessment (NICE NG12); same day if the child is unwell. Do not repeatedly examine or manipulate a suspected paediatric abdominal tumour.
Midline abdominal mass + bowel obstruction features (vomiting + distension + obstipation) + previous abdominal surgery Large bowel obstruction from colorectal cancer or adhesional mass. → Same-day surgical assessment (999 if unwell): nil by mouth, IV access, analgesia ± antiemetic; imaging by the hospital team, generally CT (AXR alone is insufficient). No laxatives. Do not assume a midline mass after surgery is adhesional.
The AAA rupture recognition is a time-critical primary care emergency — the classic triad (pulsatile abdominal mass + back/flank pain + hypotension) is present in only approximately 50% of cases. The other 50% present with: isolated severe back pain (misdiagnosed as musculoskeletal), isolated flank pain (misdiagnosed as renal colic — patients may have been given diclofenac and sent home with serious consequences since diclofenac in AAA rupture reduces platelet aggregation and worsens haemorrhage), or painless syncope with incidental pulsatile mass on examination. The critical rule: any male over 65 with known or suspected AAA who presents with sudden-onset back, flank, or abdominal pain must have an AAA rupture excluded as the first priority — before considering renal colic, muscular pain, or any benign diagnosis. Do not examine forcefully — a tense, tender pulsatile epigastric mass in an unwell patient is almost certainly a ruptured AAA until proved otherwise.
2
Diagnose

Abdominal Mass — Anatomical Localisation

Location and likely origin
RUQ: liver (hepatomegaly — smooth = congestive, nodular = cirrhosis/metastases; discrete mass = HCC, hepatic abscess, hydatid), gallbladder (Courvoisier sign raises suspicion of malignant biliary obstruction but is not diagnostic), right kidney. Epigastric / central: stomach (gastric carcinoma), pancreas (pseudocyst, pancreatic cancer), aorta (pulsatile = AAA), transverse colon, lymph nodes (lymphoma). LUQ: spleen (splenomegaly — notched medial border, cannot get between spleen and ribs), left kidney, pancreatic tail. RIF: caecal carcinoma, appendix mass/abscess (appendicitis with walled-off perforation), terminal ileal Crohn's mass, ileocaecal TB, ovary (right), psoas abscess. LIF: sigmoid carcinoma, diverticular mass, descending colon, ovary (left). Suprapubic / central pelvis: bladder (distended — urinary retention), uterus (pregnancy, fibroids, uterine carcinoma), ovarian cyst/tumour.
Examination — characterising the mass
Mobility: moves with respiration = liver, spleen, kidney, stomach, gallbladder. Fixed = retroperitoneal (renal, aorta, lymph nodes), large tumour with adhesions. Consistency: soft = fluid-filled cyst, lipoma; firm = bowel, solid organ; hard = malignancy, calcified structure. Pulsatility: transmitted = anything overlying aorta; expansile (radiates outward in all directions) = aneurysm. Transillumination: cystic. Resonance: resonant to percussion = bowel; dull = solid organ or ascitic fluid. Ballottement: kidney (bimanual ballottement — flicking motion between two hands) or free fluid (fluctuant).
Special examination signs
Shifting dullness + fluid thrill: ascites (>1L). Splenomegaly distinction from left kidney: cannot get between spleen and ribs (kidney — can get fingers above); moves toward RIF on inspiration (not kidney); notched medial edge; dull to percussion (kidney — resonant anteriorly from bowel). Succussion splash: gastric outlet obstruction (splashing sound on shaking patient — retained gastric contents). Hernias: examine inguinal regions, umbilicus, surgical scars (incisional).
The clinical distinction between splenomegaly and a left renal mass is a classic examination station that requires attention to specific signs — the three most reliable bedside differentiators: (1) the spleen cannot be felt above (you cannot get fingers between it and the ribs), whereas a left kidney can be ballot-ted bimanually and the examiner's hand can be placed above it; (2) the spleen moves medially and inferiorly with inspiration (toward the right iliac fossa in progressive enlargement), whereas the kidney moves downward with inspiration; (3) the spleen is dull to percussion anteriorly (no bowel between it and the abdominal wall), whereas the kidney is resonant anteriorly (bowel lies anterior to kidney). Additional distinguishing features: the notched medial border of an enlarged spleen (Traube's space — the area of resonance in the left lower costal area) is obliterated by a massively enlarged spleen. When clinical differentiation is uncertain: USS is definitive.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Duration of mass: rapid onset (malignant, inflammatory abscess, haematoma) vs slow-growing (benign cyst, lipoma, fibroid). Associated symptoms: weight loss + anorexia + night sweats (malignancy, TB, lymphoma), dysphagia (oesophageal/gastric), PR bleeding or change in bowel habit (colorectal), haematuria (renal), jaundice (hepatic/biliary/pancreatic), postmenopausal bleeding (uterine/ovarian), urinary symptoms (bladder), abdominal pain character (colicky = bowel obstruction; constant = malignancy; episodic RUQ = biliary). Foreign travel (amoebic abscess, hydatid — endemic areas). Family history (CRC, gastric cancer, BRCA — ovarian). Previous malignancy (metastatic mass). Medications (steroid use — adrenal adenoma, fatty liver, immunosuppression – atypical infections).
Examination — systematic approach
Lie patient flat with arms by sides (relaxes abdominal wall). Inspect: visible mass, scars, abdominal asymmetry. Palpate: all nine regions, start away from pain. Characterise mass: location, size (estimate in cm), shape, consistency, surface (smooth vs nodular), tenderness, mobility, pulsatility. Percussion: resonant/dull, shifting dullness (ascites). Auscultate: bowel sounds (obstruction = high-pitched), bruits (AAA, renal artery stenosis). Rectal examination where it will alter management (suspected rectal mass, impaction, rectal bleeding, pelvic pathology). Lymph nodes: a left supraclavicular (Virchow's) node raises concern for metastatic malignancy — GI, pancreatic, lung or ovarian. Record observations, weight and its trajectory, and pregnancy possibility/LMP.
Investigations
Bloods, tailored FBC, U&Es/eGFR, LFTs + bilirubin, CRP; ferritin/iron studies if anaemic; HbA1c/glucose if pancreatic disease possible · Urinalysis + pregnancy test where pregnancy is possible · FIT where colorectal pathology is possible · Imaging by likely organ pelvic/adnexal → pelvic USS (transvaginal where acceptable); enlarged liver or gallbladder → urgent direct-access USS; suspected pancreatic cancer → urgent CT per NG12; colorectal/retroperitoneal → CT or specialist-directed; obstruction/abscess → hospital · Tumour markers — targeted only CA125 per the ovarian symptom pathway; AFP only if HCC or germ-cell tumour suspected; β-hCG + AFP ± LDH for a complex ovarian mass under 40 (via gynaecology); CEA and CA19-9 are not diagnostic tests; PSA only for a prostate indication
The Virchow's node (Troisier's sign) is a physical examination finding that carries significant oncological importance — a palpable hard, non-tender node in the left supraclavicular fossa (above the clavicle, just lateral to the sternocleidomastoid muscle) raises concern for metastatic malignancy — gastrointestinal, pancreatic, lung or ovarian — with intra-abdominal spread classically via the thoracic duct. The thoracic duct (which drains abdominal and thoracic lymphatics) empties into the venous circulation at the left subclavian-jugular junction, and tumour emboli can deposit at this terminal lymph node. The classic association is gastric cancer, but Virchow's node metastases also occur from: pancreatic, ovarian, colorectal, testicular, oesophageal, hepatic, and uterine carcinomas. Any palpable left supraclavicular lymph node in an adult with an abdominal mass should prompt an urgent suspected cancer pathway referral for the likely primary site — primary lymphoma of this node is possible but much less common than metastatic disease from an abdominal primary.

Specific Mass Types — Benign vs Malignant Features

Hepatomegaly
Smooth hepatomegaly: congestive cardiac failure (tender, pulsatile in TR), hepatitis, fatty liver disease (MASLD), early cirrhosis, leukaemia/lymphoma infiltration. Nodular hepatomegaly: hepatic metastases (most common cause of discrete hepatic nodules — from colorectal, lung, breast, gastric, pancreatic primary), HCC (hepatocellular carcinoma — cirrhotic background, AFP elevated), cirrhosis (late). Single hepatic lesion: haemangioma (most common benign hepatic lesion — USS: hyperechoic, well-defined), simple cyst (anechoic), FNH (focal nodular hyperplasia), HCC, metastasis.
Splenomegaly
Mild (<5cm below costal margin): haematological (IDA, haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell disease, thrombocytopenia), infection (EBV — most common cause of acute splenomegaly in young adults; CMV, HIV, malaria, typhoid), portal hypertension (cirrhosis), autoimmune (SLE, RA — Felty syndrome). Massive splenomegaly (reaches beyond umbilicus): CML (chronic myeloid leukaemia), myelofibrosis, malaria (hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly), thalassaemia major, visceral leishmaniasis. Unexplained splenomegaly: FBC + blood film, LFTs; consider viral/infective, haematological and portal-hypertension causes; consider suspected cancer referral, especially with B symptoms (NICE NG12).
Ovarian and uterine masses
Ovarian cyst: most common pelvic mass in women. USS + CA125 + RMI (Risk of Malignancy Index). RMI = M (menopausal status) × USS score × CA125 level. RMI triages an adnexal mass (mainly postmenopausal) — it is not a primary-care test for every cyst. Thresholds differ: RCOG RMI I ≥200 → CT + gynaecological oncology MDT; NICE CG122 uses 250 — follow the local gynaecology threshold. Fibroid (leiomyoma): firm irregular uterus, heavy periods, pressure symptoms. USS confirmation. Ovarian cancer: ascites and/or a pelvic or abdominal mass not obviously fibroids → direct gynaecology suspected cancer referral — do not wait for CA125 or USS. Persistent suggestive symptoms (not every cyst or mass): 39 or under → consider urgent direct-access USS (do not use CA125 in isolation); 40+ → CA125 at or above the NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) age-specific threshold (35 IU/mL at 40–49, 31 at 50–59, 24 at 60–69, 25 at 70–79, 31 at 80+), then urgent USS; a USS suggesting ovarian cancer (for example a complex cystic-solid mass) → gynaecology suspected cancer pathway referral. A normal CA125 or USS does not exclude cancer if symptoms persist or progress.
The Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) is mainly used to triage an adnexal mass (particularly in postmenopausal patients), usually within the gynaecology pathway — it is not a primary-care test for every ovarian cyst — it combines three components: (1) menopausal status (M: pre-menopausal = 1; post-menopausal = 3); (2) USS features (U: none of the below = 1; any of multilocular cyst, solid areas, bilateral lesions, ascites, intraabdominal metastases = 3); and (3) CA125 level (IU/mL). The formula: RMI = M × U × CA125. Interpretation: RMI <25 = low risk (approximately 3% malignancy); RMI 25-250 = moderate risk (approximately 20%); RMI >250 = high risk (approximately 75% malignancy) — urgent gynaecology suspected cancer pathway referral (gynaecological oncology). Thresholds differ (RCOG RMI I ≥200; NICE CG122 250), so the local gynaecology pathway sets the operational cut-off. Important: RMI is not validated for premenopausal women with straightforward functional cysts — USS alone is usually sufficient for these, and most functional cysts (follicular, corpus luteum) resolve within 2-3 menstrual cycles without treatment.

Confirmed benign lesion (simple ovarian cyst <5cm pre-menopausal, hepatic haemangioma, fibroid with symptoms manageable) → appropriate specialist for symptomatic management.
The abdominal mass in primary care carries a significant malignancy probability that justifies a systematic urgent approach — a new palpable abdominal mass carries a high risk of malignancy in primary care, which is why NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) lists it as a trigger for FIT (colorectal), urgent direct-access USS (liver or gallbladder mass) or a suspected cancer pathway referral (pelvic or abdominal mass in women). Unlike many symptoms (which have a broad differential including benign conditions), a new palpable abdominal mass in an adult over 40 must be considered malignant until proved otherwise, and the primary care response should be: (1) FIT and urgent imaging by site; (2) a site-specific suspected cancer pathway referral if FIT is ≥10 µg Hb/g, a woman has a pelvic or abdominal mass not obviously fibroids, or clinical or imaging features are suspicious; (3) never reassure without investigation. The practice of prescribing laxatives for a 'probable constipation mass' without arranging USS is clinically risky — a sigmoid carcinoma can present as a palpable LIF mass, and a caecal carcinoma as a RIF mass, both of which may soften and partially resolve with laxative treatment, giving false reassurance.

Treat

Acute Mass Management & Benign Conditions

Appendix mass (appendix abscess / phlegmon)
Walled-off appendix abscess after perforated appendicitis. Tender RIF mass + fever + WBC elevated + resolved initial peritonism. Requires hospital surgical assessment. Management may include IV antibiotics (per the local hospital protocol and Greater Manchester antimicrobial guidance — no fixed GP regimen), bowel rest, image-guided drainage of a suitable abscess, and early or interval surgery depending on clinical and imaging findings. Routine interval appendicectomy is now variable (age, recurrence risk, appendicolith, persistent symptoms, comorbidity, local practice). In adults — especially older adults, persistent symptoms or atypical imaging — ensure follow-up to exclude caecal or appendiceal neoplasia; the surgical team decides on interval appendicectomy and colonoscopy.
Ovarian cyst — conservative management
Premenopausal, simple (thin-walled, anechoic, unilocular, <5 cm; CA125 not routinely needed): usually resolve over 2–3 menstrual cycles; RCOG Green-top Guideline 62 (2011) advises no routine follow-up. Premenopausal, simple, 5–7 cm: yearly USS follow-up may be appropriate (RCOG GTG 62, 2011 — local guidance may differ); >7 cm, persistent, enlarging, symptomatic or complex: gynaecology assessment ± MRI or surgery. Postmenopausal (RCOG GTG 34, 2025): asymptomatic simple unilocular cyst ≤3 cm — no follow-up; >3–5 cm with normal CA125 — repeat in 4–6 months, discharge after 1 year if unchanged or smaller; symptomatic, complex, persistent or enlarging → gynaecology. Ovarian cyst accident: torsion (acute severe unilateral pelvic pain + vomiting + peritonism — 999 + emergency laparoscopy); rupture (acute pain + fluid in pelvis on USS — usually self-limiting unless haemodynamic compromise). Endometrioma (chocolate cyst — typically premenopausal, CA125 mildly elevated, thick-walled on USS — gynaecology referral + GnRH consideration).
Benign conditions managed in primary care
Constipation mass (palpable loaded sigmoid/descending colon): confirm with USS if any doubt (cancer cannot be excluded clinically). Treat constipation only after assessing for obstruction, impaction and red flags (avoid high-dose osmotic/stimulant laxatives if obstruction is possible). Reassess after 4 weeks — if mass persists, refer urgently. Distended bladder (urinary retention): catheterise (acute) or USS to confirm. Pregnant uterus: always consider — urine HCG in any woman of reproductive age with pelvic mass.
The appendix mass — surgical team follow-up is a safety-critical investigation that is often omitted — a caecal carcinoma can perforate and present exactly like appendix abscess (perforated caecal carcinoma forms a pericolic mass that is clinically and radiologically indistinguishable from a perforated appendix phlegmon in approximately 5-10% of cases, particularly in patients over 50). After conservative management the surgical team should arrange appropriate follow-up — typically colonoscopy or CT, timed by them and particularly in older adults — to: (1) confirm the appendix is the source and exclude an underlying caecal carcinoma; (2) assess the caecum and terminal ileum for alternative pathology. The GP's role is to confirm this follow-up has been arranged, not to schedule it.

Treat

Malignant Abdominal Masses — Initial GP Management

While awaiting specialist review
Do not delay referral for tumour markers or imaging. Send baseline FBC, U&Es and LFTs with the referral, state the clinical findings, and let the receiving pathway arrange imaging — a blanket pre-appointment CT can delay care, adds contrast/radiation and conflicts with local pathways. Tumour markers only where a pathway specifies one (CA125 for the ovarian pathway).
Symptomatic management while waiting
Analgesia: paracetamol; if a strong opioid is needed use the local acute-pain or palliative pathway — usually an immediate-release preparation at an age- and condition-appropriate dose, with review, bowel management and safety-netting (MHRA: dependence risk). No generic modified-release morphine. Nausea: choose by mechanism — metoclopramide (MHRA: max 5 days, extrapyramidal risk, renal dose adjustment; avoid in obstruction, perforation or GI haemorrhage; caution in Parkinson's and epilepsy) or ondansetron (QT prolongation, electrolytes, constipation, interacting drugs, first-trimester caution). Constipation: only after excluding obstruction and impaction — avoid bulk-forming or high-dose osmotic/stimulant laxatives if obstruction is suspected. Weight loss: nutritional assessment; supplements per local formulary/ACBS, dietitian for significant malnutrition. Sleep: non-drug advice; assess pain, anxiety, medicines and acute illness (no amitriptyline).
What the GP should not do
Do not delay referral for tumour markers · do not prescribe strong modified-release opioids generically · do not give high-dose laxatives if obstruction is possible · do not reassure solely on a normal FIT, CA125 or ultrasound · do not arrange broad cancer-marker panels.
Patient communication
Inform the patient clearly but sensitively: "I have found a swelling in your abdomen that needs to be investigated urgently. I am referring you on the suspected cancer pathway, which means you should be seen quickly — usually within about 2 weeks, though that is not guaranteed. This pathway is used for any finding that needs prompt investigation — it does not mean cancer has been confirmed, but we need to find out what this is without delay." Avoid false reassurance ("I'm sure it's nothing serious"). Offer to speak to a family member if the patient wishes. Document the consultation carefully.
Why the GP does not front-load investigations: a broad tumour-marker panel has poor sensitivity and specificity (CEA and CA19-9 can be normal in cancer and raised in benign disease), and a blanket GP-requested CT can delay the referral, duplicate the pathway's own straight-to-test imaging and expose the patient to unnecessary contrast and radiation. The safest GP contribution is a prompt, well-documented referral with baseline bloods, plus symptom control that does not mask obstruction — which is why generic modified-release opioids, high-dose laxatives and sedating tricyclics have no place while the diagnosis is open.

Lifestyle

Communication, Nutrition & Psychological Support

Breaking news of an abdominal mass — communication principles Use SPIKES framework for breaking difficult clinical findings: Setting (private, unhurried consultation), Perception (what does the patient already suspect?), Invitation (how much does the patient want to know?), Knowledge (break news clearly, without jargon, in steps), Empathy (acknowledge emotional response), Strategy (next steps — suspected cancer pathway referral, timeline, support). Use plain language: "I found a lump" not "an echogenic lesion." Avoid euphemisms. Allow silence. Offer written follow-up letter summarising next steps.
Nutritional support during cancer investigation and treatment Weight loss is common at presentation and during treatment. Assess nutrition (weight trajectory, intake, MUST). Encourage small frequent meals. Oral nutritional supplements only per local formulary/ICB policy and ACBS indications; dietetic or specialist advice for significant malnutrition.
Psychological support during the diagnostic wait The wait for the specialist appointment causes significant anxiety — uncertainty is the most anxiety-provoking phase of cancer diagnosis. NHS Talking Therapies referral: anxiety/depression during cancer investigation or after diagnosis. Macmillan GP Facilitator: many ICBs have a Macmillan-funded GP lead who can advise on cancer care coordination. Macmillan Cancer Support (macmillan.org.uk): patient resources, benefits advice, nurse helpline (0808 808 00 00). Cancer Research UK: information on specific tumour types.
Benefits and financial support during cancer investigation Universal Credit / Sick leave: issue fit note for patients with significant symptoms or anxiety during investigation. PIP (Personal Independence Payment): relevant if cancer confirmed and patient has functional impairment. Macmillan financial guidance: benefits checks and grant applications for cancer patients. Sick pay from work: GP fit note essential.
Post-treatment surveillance Surveillance is set and usually coordinated by the treating specialist. After potentially curative treatment for non-metastatic colorectal cancer, NICE NG151 follow-up for the first 3 years includes serum CEA, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and colonoscopy (clearance colonoscopy generally within 1 year). Other tumour-marker or imaging surveillance only per the specialist's protocol — no GP-led routine USS for hepatic cysts or haemangiomas.
Fertility preservation in younger patients with abdominal malignancy If gonadotoxic chemotherapy or radiotherapy is likely, urgent discussion with the treating oncology team and fertility service is needed before treatment starts. The GP documents the issue and avoids delays in referral — the oncology team leads.
The GP's distinctive role here is generalist: honest communication, support through the diagnostic wait, nutrition and benefits signposting, and making sure nothing falls between services. Treatment-specific advice — peri-operative carbohydrate loading, neutropenic precautions, tumour-lysis prophylaxis, radiotherapy diets, tumour-marker surveillance — belongs to the specialist oncology or surgical team's protocol, and duplicating it in primary care risks contradicting it.

Safety

Follow-Up, Referral Tracking & Safety-Netting

Suspected cancer referral tracking and GP responsibility
Submit the suspected cancer pathway referral and track outcome. If patient not contacted by specialist within 14 days: chase up the referral (tracking suspected cancer referrals is a GP quality standard). Document referral date + expected response date in clinical notes. Confirm the patient understands why they are referred, check contact details and preferred communication, follow up if the referral is rejected, delayed or not attended, and reassess if symptoms progress while awaiting diagnosis.
Safety-netting while awaiting specialist review
Verbal AND written safety-net: call 999 for collapse, sudden severe abdominal or back pain, a rapidly enlarging or pulsating lump, vomiting with distension and failure to pass stool or wind, haematemesis or heavy rectal bleeding; contact us or NHS 111 the same day for worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting or inability to keep fluids down, melaena or other rectal bleeding, fever with rigors, jaundice, confusion or rapidly worsening general condition. Do not wait for the specialist appointment if acutely unwell — A&E is the appropriate route. Provide contact number for GP acute on-call service.
Diagnosis exclusion and benign confirmation
If CT/USS and specialist assessment confirms benign lesion: remove the active suspected cancer referral status from notes. Code confirmed diagnosis (e.g. hepatic haemangioma, simple ovarian cyst) in problem list. Follow any surveillance plan the specialist recommends. Inform patient clearly of the benign finding in writing.

The GP responsibility for tracking suspected cancer referral outcomes is a clinical governance standard that is frequently under-documented — GPs who refer patients on the suspected cancer pathway retain clinical responsibility during the referral period, and NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) asks the referrer to give the person information about the referral and the safety-netting plan. This includes: (1) having a system to track that the patient received an appointment (many GP practices use recall/task systems at day 10 to confirm appointment received); (2) having a system to receive and act on the specialist outcome letter (confirm receipt in the clinical record); (3) informing patients what the outcome was and what the follow-up plan is. A patient referred on the suspected cancer pathway who fails to attend their appointment ('did not attend') requires active follow-up from the GP — a simple letter or phone call, not passively waiting. The medicolegal principle: a GP who makes a suspected cancer pathway referral and then has no further knowledge of whether the patient was seen and what was found is not fulfilling the minimum duty of care.
