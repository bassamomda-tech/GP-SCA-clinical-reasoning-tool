Safety

Red Flags — Severe Hypocalcaemia, Tetany & Cardiac Emergencies

Tetany / carpopedal spasm Tetany or carpopedal spasm, or corrected calcium <1.90 mmol/L → 999 / emergency admission for IV calcium gluconate infusion. Other acute symptoms (perioral tingling, cramps) → same-day hospital assessment with an ECG. Carpopedal spasm (thumb adducted into palm, fingers extended — "obstetric hand") is a medical emergency. Do not attempt outpatient IV calcium.
Laryngospasm / stridor Laryngeal muscle spasm from severe hypocalcaemia → airway obstruction → 999. May occur acutely in post-thyroid / post-parathyroid surgery. Stridor with neck swelling after neck surgery may also be a compressive haematoma — airway emergency: 999 and urgent surgical/anaesthetic help, not just calcium.
Seizures with hypocalcaemia Hypocalcaemic seizures → 999 + IV calcium gluconate. Seizures in post-thyroidectomy patient in first 24–48 hrs = hypocalcaemia until proven otherwise. Post-operative calcium monitoring follows the operating team's plan.
Prolonged QTc on ECG Hypocalcaemia prolongs the QT interval (elongates ST segment / T wave) → risk of Torsades de Pointes and ventricular fibrillation. Perform an ECG in symptomatic, acute or severe hypocalcaemia. Urgently involve hospital services if there is marked QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmia, syncope, seizure or severe biochemical abnormality — urgency also depends on QTc reliability, other QT-prolonging drugs, and magnesium and potassium.
Severe hypocalcaemia (adjusted Ca <1.90 mmol/L) Any adjusted calcium <1.90 mmol/L, even asymptomatic → emergency hospital assessment for IV calcium (Society for Endocrinology). Do NOT attempt to correct with oral calcium alone at this level — too slow, too unreliable. IV calcium is hospital-only.
Hypocalcaemia post-neck surgery (thyroid / parathyroid) Hypoparathyroidism from inadvertent parathyroid gland removal / ischaemia → corrected calcium falls precipitously in 24–48 hrs. Recently discharged patients should follow the operating team's monitoring and supplementation plan. New perioral tingling, paraesthesia, cramps or dysphonia → same-day assessment and contact the operating team; tetany, stridor or seizures → 999.
Chvostek's sign and Trousseau's sign are the two classic clinical tests for neuromuscular excitability from hypocalcaemia. Chvostek's sign: tap the facial nerve anterior to the ear — ipsilateral facial muscle twitching = positive (sensitivity 70%, specificity 90% for hypocalcaemia). Trousseau's sign: inflate BP cuff above systolic for 3 minutes — carpopedal spasm (wrist flexion, thumb adduction, finger extension) = positive (sensitivity 94%, specificity 99% for symptomatic hypocalcaemia). Trousseau's sign is more sensitive and should be checked in all cases of suspected hypocalcaemia where the patient has perioral tingling, paraesthesia, or muscle cramps. A positive Trousseau's with a calcium of 1.9–2.1 mmol/L indicates the patient is clinically symptomatic and requires urgent treatment even though the biochemical level appears moderate. The QT prolongation mechanism of hypocalcaemia (elongation of the intracellular action potential plateau by reducing calcium-mediated channel inactivation) is important to recognise — the classic ECG finding is a prolonged ST segment (not a prolonged T wave as in other QT-prolonging conditions). Coexisting hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia (common in critically ill patients) dramatically increase the arrhythmia risk from hypocalcaemia-related QT prolongation. These electrolytes must be corrected simultaneously.

Diagnose

Corrected Calcium — Adjust for Albumin First

Why correction matters
40–45% of serum calcium is protein-bound (mainly albumin). Total calcium measurement reflects bound + free (ionised) calcium. If albumin is low (malnutrition, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome), total calcium appears falsely low — but ionised calcium may be normal. Interpret total calcium with albumin and the local reference range. If the patient is acutely unwell, symptomatic, critically ill, or the result is discordant with the clinical picture, measure ionised calcium urgently. Do not delay treatment of symptomatic hypocalcaemia while waiting for a corrected value or repeat test.
Corrected calcium formula
Corrected calcium (mmol/L) = Measured total calcium + 0.02 × (40 − albumin g/L). Example: Ca 1.95, albumin 25 → corrected Ca = 1.95 + 0.02 × (40−25) = 1.95 + 0.30 = 2.25 mmol/L (normal). Most laboratories auto-correct — check the report. The formula is only an estimate — unreliable in critical illness, major acid–base disturbance, severe hypoalbuminaemia, renal failure, paraproteinaemia, abnormal binding states, and after large-volume transfusion or IV fluids.
Normal range
Reference range is laboratory-specific (commonly about 2.20–2.60 mmol/L). Mild: 2.10–2.19 mmol/L. Moderate: 1.90–2.09 mmol/L. Severe: <1.90 mmol/L or symptomatic at any level (Society for Endocrinology). Ionised calcium (gold standard): 1.15–1.35 mmol/L (measured by blood gas analyser — most accurate).
Symptoms of hypocalcaemia
Perioral tingling (earliest) · Fingertip paraesthesia · Muscle cramps · Tetany (carpopedal spasm) · Laryngospasm · Seizures · Confusion · Prolonged QT / arrhythmia · Chronic: cataracts, calcification of basal ganglia, dental hypoplasia, dry brittle nails, dry skin, papilloedema (pseudotumour cerebri)
Clinical tests
Chvostek's sign (tap facial nerve → facial twitch) · Trousseau's sign (BP cuff above systolic × 3 min → carpopedal spasm) · Both more likely positive with acute rather than chronic hypocalcaemia (adaptation reduces neuromuscular excitability in chronic)
The corrected calcium calculation is one of the most commonly performed and most commonly wrongly interpreted calculations in primary care. Laboratories differ in whether they auto-correct calcium for albumin — some report only total calcium with a separate albumin result, requiring the GP to calculate the corrected value manually. Always check whether the calcium result reported is the corrected value. Ionised calcium (directly measured by blood gas analyser) is more accurate than calculated corrected calcium, particularly in acid-base disturbances (alkalosis reduces ionised calcium without changing total calcium — hyperventilation causes acute hypocalcaemia by respiratory alkalosis, which is why anxious hyperventilating patients develop perioral tingling and carpopedal spasm). In a patient with an arterial blood gas available, the ionised calcium is the most reliable measure. The important clinical principle in a well, asymptomatic patient is: do not prescribe calcium supplements based on a laboratory total calcium result without calculating the corrected value first — you may be treating a patient with normal ionised calcium based on a spuriously low total calcium from hypoalbuminaemia, unnecessarily exposing them to hypercalcaemia risk.

Diagnose

Aetiology — Use PTH to Direct Investigation

PTH low / inappropriately normal → hypoparathyroidism
Post-surgical (thyroidectomy / parathyroidectomy — most common) · Autoimmune (isolated or APS-1) · Familial (calcium-sensing receptor mutation — autosomal dominant) · Magnesium depletion (suppresses PTH secretion) · Infiltration (haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, granulomas) · Radiation damage to parathyroid
PTH high → secondary hyperparathyroidism (appropriate PTH response)
Vitamin D deficiency (most common cause globally — PTH rises to compensate for falling calcium) · CKD (phosphate retention → calcium-phosphate precipitation → hypocalcaemia + secondary hyperparathyroidism) · Malabsorption (coeliac, IBD, bariatric surgery — reduced calcium and vitamin D absorption) · Pseudohypoparathyroidism (PTH resistance — Albright hereditary osteodystrophy)
PTH suppressed + hypocalcaemia
Rapid bone remineralisation (hungry bone syndrome post-parathyroidectomy — bones rapidly take up calcium after years of PTH-driven resorption) · Osteoblastic metastases (prostate, breast — calcium incorporated into bone matrix) · Acute pancreatitis (calcium saponification with fat necrosis) · Severe hyperphosphataemia (e.g. tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis)
Drugs causing hypocalcaemia
Bisphosphonates (reduce bone resorption) · Denosumab (anti-RANKL — powerful hypocalcaemia risk) · Cinacalcet (calcimimetic — suppresses PTH + calcium) · Foscarnet · Loop diuretics (urinary calcium loss) · Phenytoin / phenobarbitone (hepatic degradation of vitamin D) · Proton pump inhibitors (impair calcium absorption) · Chemotherapy (cisplatin → magnesium loss → PTH suppression → hypocalcaemia)
Acute pancreatitis
Hypocalcaemia in acute pancreatitis (saponification — free calcium binds to free fatty acids in necrotic fat) is a marker of severity (corrected calcium <2.0 mmol/L is one of the Glasgow–Imrie criteria). Suspected acute pancreatitis → same-day hospital admission whatever the calcium.
Denosumab-induced hypocalcaemia is an increasingly important clinical problem — as denosumab use for osteoporosis has expanded, the risk of severe symptomatic hypocalcaemia (requiring hospitalisation) has become clinically significant. Denosumab (Prolia 60 mg SC 6-monthly for osteoporosis, or Xgeva 120 mg monthly for bone metastases) powerfully inhibits osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, removing the main source of calcium efflux from bone. Patients with pre-existing vitamin D deficiency or CKD are at highest risk. Before and during denosumab: (1) correct pre-existing hypocalcaemia and vitamin D deficiency before starting (MHRA Drug Safety Update, September 2014; in practice aim for 25-OH vitamin D >50 nmol/L), (2) ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D intake or supplementation throughout (MHRA Drug Safety Update, September 2014), and (3) monitor calcium in those predisposed to hypocalcaemia, especially eGFR <30 (MHRA; risk-stratified per product information and the current local shared-care protocol — e.g. about 2 weeks after dosing where eGFR is below 30). GPs prescribing denosumab must understand this risk and implement these precautions. Vitamin D deficiency is by far the most common cause of hypocalcaemia worldwide — the WHO estimates 1 billion people have deficient vitamin D levels. In the UK, the highest risk groups are: South Asian and Black ethnicities (reduced cutaneous vitamin D synthesis due to melanin pigmentation), housebound and elderly individuals, pregnant and breastfeeding women, patients with malabsorption syndromes, and patients on anticonvulsants or rifampicin (hepatic induction of vitamin D catabolism).

Diagnose

Investigations

First-line (all confirmed hypocalcaemia)
Repeat corrected calcium + albumin (confirm; ionised calcium if unwell or discordant) · PTH (intact PTH — key to aetiology: low = hypoparathyroidism; high = secondary) · 25-OH vitamin D (deficient <25 nmol/L; insufficient 25–50 nmol/L; sufficient >50 nmol/L) · Phosphate (low in vitamin D deficiency; high in hypoparathyroidism and CKD) · Magnesium (hypomagnesaemia suppresses PTH — must correct Mg first) · U&E + eGFR (CKD)
Second-line (context-directed — not routine)
ALP (alkaline phosphatase) — elevated in vitamin D deficiency (secondary hyperparathyroidism stimulating bone) · 1,25-OH vitamin D (active form — specialist-directed) · PTHrP (malignancy-related hypocalcaemia context) · Coeliac screen (tTGA — if malabsorption suspected) · Cortisol / Synacthen (Addison's disease can cause hypocalcaemia)
ECG
Perform in symptomatic, acute or severe hypocalcaemia. Look for: prolonged QT interval (read the QTc against local thresholds), prolonged ST segment, T-wave changes. Bradycardia. Do NOT delay ECG pending other investigation results.
CKD: calcium, phosphate and PTH
In CKD, assess calcium, phosphate and PTH as trends and interpret them together. Avoid treatment decisions based solely on a calcium–phosphate product (KDIGO 2017).
Magnesium depletion as a cause of hypocalcaemia is frequently missed and is the most important reversible cause of treatment-resistant hypocalcaemia. Magnesium is required for PTH secretion — when magnesium is depleted (<0.6 mmol/L), the parathyroid glands cannot secrete PTH in response to low calcium, producing a functional hypoparathyroidism. Additionally, even if PTH is secreted, hypomagnesaemia causes PTH resistance at the bone and kidney. The critical clinical principle: if a patient with hypocalcaemia is not responding to calcium and vitamin D supplementation, measure magnesium — and if low, replace magnesium first (IV magnesium sulfate in hospital if severely depleted). Common causes of magnesium depletion: alcoholism (most common), proton pump inhibitors (chronic use depletes intestinal Mg absorption — PPIs specifically block the TRPM6 channel responsible for intestinal Mg absorption), cisplatin chemotherapy, loop diuretics, diarrhoea. The PTH result is the key diagnostic branch point in hypocalcaemia investigation — a low or inappropriately normal PTH in the context of hypocalcaemia indicates hypoparathyroidism (the parathyroids are not responding normally to the low calcium signal); a high PTH indicates secondary hyperparathyroidism (the parathyroids are appropriately responding but cannot maintain normocalcaemia due to vitamin D deficiency, CKD, or malabsorption). This single result directs the entire subsequent workup and treatment pathway.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day hospital
Adjusted calcium <1.90 mmol/L (with or without symptoms) · Tetany / carpopedal spasm / laryngospasm · Hypocalcaemic seizure · Marked QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmia or syncope · Post-thyroidectomy / parathyroidectomy with symptomatic hypocalcaemia · Suspected acute pancreatitis (any calcium; <2.0 marks severe disease)
Endocrinology (urgent)
Hypoparathyroidism (low PTH) — requires specialist management (calcitriol + calcium monitoring + risk of over-correction into hypercalcaemia) · Pseudohypoparathyroidism · Hypocalcaemia resistant to vitamin D and calcium supplementation at 6 weeks · CKD + refractory hypocalcaemia
Nephrology / renal
CKD with hypocalcaemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism: measure calcium, phosphate, PTH, vitamin D and renal function according to CKD stage. Correct vitamin D deficiency with colecalciferol or ergocalciferol (NG203). Alfacalcidol or calcitriol is specialist-led or within a renal protocol, particularly in CKD G4–G5 or dialysis, with monitoring for hypercalcaemia and hyperphosphataemia. Do not prescribe alfacalcidol automatically for CKD 3b hypocalcaemia.
Gastroenterology
Malabsorption-related hypocalcaemia (coeliac, Crohn's, short bowel syndrome, post-bariatric surgery) — treat underlying cause + IV calcium / IV vitamin D if oral absorption inadequate. Positive coeliac serology → gastroenterology; keep gluten in the diet until assessed (NG20).
Suspected cancer
Hypocalcaemia with unexplained bone pain, pathological fracture, weight loss, a raised ALP or a high phosphate, or after denosumab for bone metastases → think osteoblastic metastases (prostate, breast) or tumour lysis. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): in anyone 60 or over with persistent bone pain, particularly back pain, or an unexplained fracture, offer FBC, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains, all together — urine Bence Jones only if free light chain testing is not available — and refer on a suspected cancer pathway if they suggest myeloma.
GP management (mild, asymptomatic)
Only if clinically well, asymptomatic, mild and stable, calcium not severely low, renal function known, magnesium checked, cause identified (usually vitamin D deficiency with raised PTH) and follow-up arranged: treat the cause; oral calcium/vitamin D per local formulary and clinical context; repeat calcium at an appropriate interval. Avoid calcitriol, alfacalcidol, PTH analogues or IV calcium in primary care unless under a formal shared-care protocol.
The management of hypoparathyroidism (low PTH hypocalcaemia) is fundamentally different from vitamin D deficiency hypocalcaemia and should not be managed in primary care without specialist input. The treatment of hypoparathyroidism uses calcitriol (active vitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol — bypasses the renal 1-hydroxylation step that requires PTH) rather than cholecalciferol (inactive, requires PTH-stimulated renal 1-hydroxylation to become active). Prescribing cholecalciferol alone for hypoparathyroidism is ineffective and potentially dangerous — it gives false reassurance that the vitamin D deficiency is being treated while the patient remains hypocalcaemic. Calcitriol dosing requires specialist monitoring because the therapeutic window is narrow — too little = persistent hypocalcaemia; too much = hypercalcaemia with hypercalciuria → nephrocalcinosis → irreversible renal damage. Recombinant PTH (teriparatide, or PTH 1-84 — rhPTH) has been used for hypoparathyroidism (Natpar, now discontinued by its manufacturer; palopegteriparatide is the newer option) and is initiated by endocrinologists for difficult-to-control hypoparathyroidism, particularly when calcitriol requirements are very high or hypercalciuria is problematic.

Treat

Oral Calcium & Vitamin D Supplementation

Vitamin D deficiency (PTH high, corrected Ca 2.10–2.19, asymptomatic)
Colecalciferol 800–1000 IU daily (maintenance) or loading dose
Follow local vitamin D guidance after considering hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, granulomatous disease, primary hyperparathyroidism, malabsorption and nephrolithiasis — not a universal treatment for hypocalcaemia. Loading (Royal Osteoporosis Society, Vitamin D and bone health) where appropriate: colecalciferol about 300,000 IU in divided doses over 6–10 weeks, then maintenance. Calcium supplements per local formulary if dietary intake poor. Repeat 25-OH vitamin D selectively (adherence, malabsorption, severe deficiency or persistent symptoms).
CKD-related hypocalcaemia
Colecalciferol/ergocalciferol first; alfacalcidol specialist-led
Measure calcium, phosphate, PTH, vitamin D and renal function according to CKD stage and context. Correct vitamin D deficiency with colecalciferol or ergocalciferol (NG203). Alfacalcidol or calcitriol is specialist-led or within a renal protocol — particularly CKD G4–G5 or dialysis, or eGFR <30 with persisting CKD–mineral bone disorder after vitamin D repletion — with monitoring of calcium, phosphate, magnesium, renal function and PTH (SPS). Phosphate management under renal guidance.
Mild, asymptomatic, stable
Oral calcium per local formulary
Perioral tingling, cramps, paraesthesia or neuromuscular irritability → same-day assessment, ECG and urgent advice — not calcium tablets in isolation; carpopedal spasm or tetany → 999. Calcium carbonate taken with food (requires gastric acid for absorption — take with meals, not fasting). Adcal 1.5 g = 600 mg elemental calcium. Calcium citrate (Calcitrate) if on PPI or achlorhydria (does not require acid for absorption). Start vitamin D simultaneously. Review at 2–4 weeks.
Severe (hospital)Hospital only — the GP action is 999 or immediate transfer; do not attempt outpatient IV calcium. Caution with digoxin, significant cardiac disease, renal failure, extravasation and concomitant magnesium deficiency. IV calcium gluconate 10–20 mL of 10% (each 10 mL = 2.2 mmol calcium) in 50–100 mL 5% glucose over 10 min with ECG monitoring → then 100 mL of 10% calcium gluconate in 1 L 0.9% NaCl or 5% glucose at 50–100 mL/h, titrated to calcium (Society for Endocrinology 2016). Repeat boluses PRN for tetany. Monitor cardiac telemetry. Correct magnesium simultaneously if low (Mg sulfate 8 mmol IV over 1 hr). Do NOT give IV calcium in same line as bicarbonate (precipitates).
Magnesium depletionCorrect magnesium first before calcium supplementation will be effective. Preparation and dose depend on severity, renal function, symptoms, oral absorption, arrhythmia and local formulary — correct as part of hospital or specialist management where severe, symptomatic or with renal impairment. Recheck Mg + PTH after correction. Remove precipitant (PPI review — switch to H2 blocker if long-term Mg depletion on PPI).
The choice between calcium carbonate and calcium citrate for oral calcium supplementation has an important practical implication — calcium carbonate requires gastric acid for dissolution and absorption, so it must be taken with food (the meal stimulates gastric acid secretion). Patients on proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) have reduced gastric acid and absorb calcium carbonate poorly — studies show 50% reduced calcium absorption in PPI-treated patients compared to those with normal gastric acidity. For patients on PPIs or with achlorhydria (e.g., post-gastrectomy), calcium citrate is the preferred formulation as it does not require acid for absorption. The practical GP instruction: "Take your Adcal/Calcichew WITH your main meal, not fasting." Many patients take their calcium supplement in the morning with water and a PPI — absorbing very little. The colecalciferol loading dose approach (300,000 IU over 6–10 weeks) is the Royal Osteoporosis Society (Vitamin D and bone health) strategy for treating vitamin D deficiency — it rapidly restores 25-OH vitamin D levels within 6–8 weeks versus the 3–6 months required with maintenance dosing alone. Loading should follow local vitamin D guidance and patient-specific contraindications (renal impairment, granulomatous disease, primary hyperparathyroidism, malabsorption, stones) — it is not a universal treatment for hypocalcaemia. The exact schedule varies by formulation: 50,000 IU (colecalciferol) weekly × 6 weeks is a convenient approach using standard UK preparations.

Treat

Hypoparathyroidism & Chronic Management

Calcitriol (active vitamin D)
Calcitriol 0.25 mcg OD–BD (starting dose). Titrate to corrected calcium target of 2.0–2.25 mmol/L (low-normal — avoid hypercalcaemia). Urinary calcium monitoring per the endocrine/renal plan — 24-hour urinary calcium may be preferred (hypercalciuria → nephrocalcinosis risk even with normal serum calcium). Renal imaging per specialist plan. Endocrinology-initiated.
Calcium supplements in hypoparathyroidism
Dose and preparation per the endocrinology plan — not a primary-care dosing decision. Take with meals. Phosphate is managed by the specialist team, interpreting calcium, phosphate and PTH together. Urinary calcium monitoring per specialist plan (avoid hypercalciuria).
Target ranges (hypoparathyroidism)
Serum calcium: 2.0–2.25 mmol/L (deliberately low-normal to reduce hypercalciuria) · Serum phosphate: 0.8–1.5 mmol/L · Urinary calcium per specialist plan · Avoid hypercalcaemia (>2.6) → hypercalciuria + nephrocalcinosis
Sick day rules (hypoparathyroidism)
If oral calcium/calcitriol cannot be retained or absorbed because of persistent vomiting or diarrhoea, contact the endocrine team or attend hospital urgently. Do not advise unsupervised dose escalation of calcitriol. Hypoparathyroid patients are critically dependent on regular oral supplementation — they have no parathyroid reserve to buffer acute calcium loss. Carry emergency calcium and medical alert ID.
PTH replacement — specialist only
Recombinant human PTH (PTH 1-84, Natpar) 50–100 mcg SC daily — its manufacturer stopped production at the end of 2024 and UK stock is being withdrawn; palopegteriparatide (Yorvipath) is the newer PTH-replacement option. Used for hypoparathyroidism not controlled on calcitriol + calcium. Reduces hypercalciuria, reduces calcitriol requirements, improves quality of life. Initiated and monitored by endocrinology. Very expensive (NHS commissioned for severe cases).

Next steps: see Chronic kidney disease · Osteoporosis · Coeliac disease

The target calcium for hypoparathyroidism is deliberately set at low-normal (2.0–2.25 mmol/L) rather than mid-normal — this reflects the paradox of treating hypoparathyroidism with calcitriol and calcium: without PTH, there is no mechanism to reduce urinary calcium excretion when serum calcium rises (PTH normally increases tubular calcium reabsorption). High-normal calcium in the absence of PTH leads to hypercalciuria even when serum calcium is within the normal range, causing progressive nephrocalcinosis and renal failure. Long-term nephrocalcinosis is the most serious chronic complication of hypoparathyroidism and is directly caused by overtreatment. The sick day rules for hypoparathyroidism are one of the most important patient education elements — unlike adrenal insufficiency (which can be treated with stress-dose steroids) or diabetes (with sliding scale insulin), hypocalcaemic crises in hypoparathyroidism are directly precipitated by missed calcium and calcitriol doses during illness. A hypoparathyroid patient who vomits for 24 hours and cannot take their calcitriol and calcium has no parathyroid gland backup and will develop symptomatic hypocalcaemia rapidly. These patients should have a written specialist emergency plan: if oral calcium/calcitriol cannot be retained or absorbed, contact the endocrine team or attend hospital urgently — no unsupervised calcitriol increases.

Lifestyle

Dietary Calcium, Vitamin D & Prevention

Dietary calcium UK reference nutrient intake: 700 mg/day for adults; around 1000 mg/day is often advised in osteoporosis or with bone-protection treatment. Good sources include milk, cheese and yoghurt, and non-dairy options such as fortified plant milks, tinned fish with bones, calcium-set tofu and green vegetables. Calcium from food is better absorbed than supplements.
Sun and vitamin D Encourage safe outdoor activity and follow UK vitamin D public-health advice. Avoid intentional sunburn or prolonged unprotected exposure. Use supplements for people at increased risk or where deficiency is confirmed, according to local guidance.
Vitamin D foods Oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines) · Egg yolk · Fortified foods (some cereals, orange juices, margarine — check label) · Beef liver. Diet alone rarely provides enough vitamin D in the UK — everyone should consider a daily 10 microgram (400 unit) supplement in autumn and winter, and people who are rarely outdoors (frail, housebound, care home) should take one all year (NHS).
Avoiding over-the-counter calcium pitfalls Calcium supplements with meals (not fasting — carbonate formulation). Do NOT take calcium and bisphosphonate together (bisphosphonate binds calcium → reduced absorption) — separate by 2+ hours. Avoid excessive calcium intake beyond what is advised or prescribed — hypercalcaemia risk, renal stones. Ensure adequate hydration.
Medical alert and sick day rules Patients with hypoparathyroidism should carry a medical alert card/bracelet (Medic Alert) stating their condition and emergency treatment (IV calcium gluconate). Follow the written specialist plan: if oral calcium/calcitriol cannot be retained or absorbed, contact the endocrine team or attend hospital urgently — do not self-increase calcitriol.
Monitoring denosumab and bisphosphonates Before denosumab: baseline calcium, renal function and vitamin D; correct hypocalcaemia and vitamin D deficiency before treatment; ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D intake or supplementation. Additional calcium check about 2 weeks after dosing in high-risk patients (e.g. eGFR <30), per product information and the local shared-care protocol. Bisphosphonates: correct pre-existing hypocalcaemia before administration and check renal function and product-specific contraindications.
The "calcium and bisphosphonate timing" principle is critically important for prescribing practice — any divalent cation (calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, aluminium-containing antacids) chelates bisphosphonates in the gastrointestinal tract, preventing their absorption. Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate) should be taken on an empty stomach, 30 minutes before breakfast, with a full glass of plain water, without any other medication or food. This is frequently not explained to patients, and taking the bisphosphonate with food, other medications or the calcium supplement prescribed alongside greatly reduces its absorption. The MHRA guidance is explicit: take bisphosphonate first thing in the morning, wait 30 minutes, then take all other medications including calcium. A practical patient instruction card is available from the Royal Osteoporosis Society. The denosumab pre-treatment vitamin D optimisation is one of the most frequently neglected prescribing steps — the MHRA received multiple yellow card reports of severe hypocalcaemia following denosumab initiation in vitamin D-deficient patients. Checking calcium, renal function and vitamin D before starting denosumab, and correcting deficiency, is essential safe prescribing; repeat checks follow individual risk and the current local shared-care protocol.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Vitamin D deficiency — after loading
Repeat 25-OH vitamin D selectively after loading when adherence, malabsorption, severe deficiency or persistent symptoms are concerns, following local vitamin D monitoring guidance. Recheck calcium to confirm it normalises. Continue maintenance colecalciferol in at-risk patients.
Mild asymptomatic hypocalcaemia (Ca 2.10–2.19)
Recheck corrected calcium, with PTH and phosphate, at 2–4 weeks after starting supplements; 25-OH vitamin D selectively after loading (see above). Target: corrected calcium ≥2.1 mmol/L. If not responding → endocrinology referral.
Hypoparathyroidism — specialist plan
Monitoring follows the endocrine/renal specialist plan — calcium, renal function and urinary calcium (24-hour collection may be preferred); renal imaging and bone density only as the specialist directs. Calcitriol dose changes are specialist-led.
Denosumab — risk-stratified
Per product information and the current local shared-care protocol: additional calcium check about 2 weeks after dosing in high-risk patients (e.g. eGFR <30, malabsorption, prior hypocalcaemia) — not a universal check after every dose. Ensure calcium + vitamin D supplements prescribed at same time as denosumab prescription.
999 safety-net
Tetany / carpopedal spasm (any cause) · Laryngospasm / stridor · Seizure · Difficulty breathing or noisy breathing · Palpitations with fainting or collapse (marked QT prolongation or arrhythmia on ECG) · Adjusted Ca <1.90 mmol/L found on repeat blood test
Same-day GP
Perioral tingling / fingertip paraesthesia (new symptoms in known hypocalcaemia patient) · Hypoparathyroid patient unable to retain or absorb oral calcium/calcitriol (vomiting or diarrhoea) — contact the endocrine team or attend hospital urgently · Post-thyroidectomy patient with any paraesthesia or muscle cramps · Newly found adjusted Ca 1.90–2.09 mmol/L in any patient (below 1.90 → emergency admission). Remote or out of hours: Chvostek and Trousseau signs and the QT cannot be assessed by telephone — an adjusted calcium below 2.10, or any symptom, needs face-to-face assessment with an ECG, and a calcium below 1.90 must never wait in an inbox overnight.
Patients recently discharged after thyroid or parathyroid surgery should follow the operating team's monitoring and supplementation plan; the GP's role is to recognise new symptoms and arrange urgent assessment. Total thyroidectomy (removal of entire thyroid gland) can cause permanent hypoparathyroidism from inadvertent removal or devascularisation of the parathyroid glands, and temporary hypocalcaemia is common in the first 24–72 hours. Patients are discharged with instructions for calcium monitoring and should contact their GP or the surgical unit if they develop any perioral or fingertip tingling. The GP receiving such a patient should check corrected calcium and PTH urgently — perioral tingling within 72 hours of thyroidectomy is treated as calcium deficiency until proven otherwise, even if the patient looks well. Corrected calcium <2.0 in this context → contact the surgical team immediately and follow their supplementation plan. Bone density in hypoparathyroidism is assessed only as the specialist directs, and is read differently from most patients — hypoparathyroidism (the absence of PTH-driven bone resorption) causes increases in bone mineral density (BMD), particularly at cortical sites. While this might seem beneficial, it can mask the trabecular bone changes that predict fracture risk in other situations, and the high BMD can generate false reassurance about fracture risk.
Educational use only. Based on Society for Endocrinology emergency guidance, British Thyroid Association post-thyroidectomy guidelines, MHRA denosumab guidance, European Society of Endocrinology Hypoparathyroidism guidelines (international), NICE TA204 (denosumab for osteoporosis), NICE NG203 (CKD), KDIGO 2017 CKD–MBD update, SPS alfacalcidol monitoring, local denosumab shared-care protocol and product information. Always adapt to individual patient context.
