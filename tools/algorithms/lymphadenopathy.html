Safety

Red Flags — Lymphoma, Metastatic Cancer & Critical Infections

Unexplained, persistent, progressive, hard, fixed, supraclavicular or generalised lymphadenopathy requires prompt assessment and appropriate referral. Do not rely on node size or tenderness alone to exclude malignancy — node features are risk indicators, none is diagnostic.

Unexplained, persistent or progressive node (hard / rubbery / fixed increases concern) Lymphoma (Hodgkin's — rubbery, B symptoms; Non-Hodgkin's — variable) or metastatic carcinoma → suspected cancer pathway referral (haematology / head and neck / relevant site). NICE NG12: suspected cancer referral for unexplained adult lymphadenopathy, taking associated symptoms into account — no requirement for the node to be hard, fixed, >1 cm or present for 6 weeks. Reactive nodes may be firm or sizeable; lymphoma nodes may be mobile or tender.
B symptoms: fever, drenching night sweats or weight loss Unexplained fever over 38°C; drenching night sweats; unexplained weight loss (conventionally over 10% of body weight over 6 months). Any one is relevant — do not require all three before considering lymphoma. Pruritus, breathlessness, alcohol-related nodal pain, fatigue and splenomegaly may also be relevant. Suspected cancer pathway referral (lymphoma) with targeted bloods. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is a specialist staging investigation, not a routine GP test before referral.
Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy (any age, any size) Concerning and deserves prompt assessment — causes include infection, lymphoma, sarcoidosis and other inflammatory disease as well as metastatic cancer. NICE NG12: urgent direct-access chest X-ray for people aged 40 and over with supraclavicular or persistent cervical lymphadenopathy; suspected cancer referral depends on the clinical findings and local pathway. Drainage patterns (e.g. Virchow's node) are useful context but not reliable enough for site-specific referral.
Mediastinal mass on CXR + lymphadenopathy Lymphoma, sarcoidosis, thymoma, germ cell tumour. Same-day or urgent haematology. Mediastinal mass with signs of SVC obstruction (facial/neck swelling, arm swelling, dilated neck veins), airway compromise (stridor, hypoxia) or neurological symptoms → urgent hospital/acute oncology assessment; 999 and emergency transfer if airway compromised. Steroids only according to the receiving specialist pathway — they may interfere with the diagnosis of lymphoma.
Generalised lymphadenopathy + rash + fever + pharyngitis in young adult Primary HIV infection (seroconversion illness) — occurs 2–6 weeks after HIV exposure. Mononucleosis-like syndrome. HIV test urgently (antigen + antibody combined — 4th generation test). Urgent sexual-health service referral where there is a significant exposure or acute retroviral syndrome; HIV RNA testing may be needed after very recent exposure or if the 4th-generation test is negative/indeterminate with high suspicion. Test for syphilis, gonorrhoea and chlamydia according to symptoms and sexual history; consider acute hepatitis and EBV/CMV in the differential. Missed HIV seroconversion = delayed diagnosis with consequences for individual and public health.
Rapidly enlarging node + overlying skin erythema + fever Suppurative lymphadenitis (bacterial — Staphylococcus aureus, group A Streptococcus). Fluctuant = abscess → incision and drainage (OMFS/surgery). Also: cat scratch disease (Bartonella henselae — cat scratch history + regional lymphadenopathy + papule at scratch site). Most immunocompetent cat-scratch patients recover without antibiotics. Lymphadenitis antibiotics per local antimicrobial guidance; a fluctuant abscess usually requires drainage — antibiotics alone are insufficient.
Persistence, growth, size over 2 cm, widespread disease and systemic symptoms are features that should trigger further investigation or referral — but there is no rule that clinicians must wait 6 weeks before acting. NICE NG12 recommends suspected cancer referral for unexplained adult lymphadenopathy, with associated symptoms informing urgency; investigations are targeted to the clinical pattern. This applies even if the node is not hard or fixed — early lymphoma can present with soft, non-tender nodes that resemble reactive lymphadenopathy. Most lymphadenopathy in primary care is benign, but the consequences of missing lymphoma are severe. Virchow's node (left supraclavicular fossa) is one of the most clinically important examination findings in medicine — it receives lymphatic drainage from abdominal organs via the thoracic duct (which empties into the left subclavian vein). A left supraclavicular node is classically associated with GI malignancy (described by Troisier in 1886), but drainage patterns are not reliable enough for site-specific referral, and supraclavicular nodes can also reflect infection, lymphoma, sarcoidosis or thoracic malignancy. Any supraclavicular node warrants prompt assessment — urgent direct-access CXR in people aged 40 and over (NICE NG12) — with suspected cancer referral according to the clinical findings and local pathway. Primary HIV seroconversion is one of the most commonly missed diagnoses in general practice — the mononucleosis-like illness (fever, sore throat, lymphadenopathy, rash, malaise) is indistinguishable clinically from EBV or CMV. Testing for HIV must be offered proactively at the time of this presentation in any patient with risk factors — the seroconversion window is short, viral load is extremely high, and transmission risk is maximal during this period.

Diagnose

Localised vs Generalised — The Primary Branch

Localised lymphadenopathy
Single node group enlarged — indicates pathology in the drainage region. Identify the drainage territory (see Step 3). Common causes: reactive (most common — URTI draining to cervical nodes), bacterial lymphadenitis, cat scratch disease, dental abscess (submandibular), scalp infection (occipital), STI (inguinal — chlamydia, syphilis, herpes, LGV), limb infection (axillary/inguinal). Persistent unexplained neck lump → head-and-neck suspected cancer pathway according to the examination and likely anatomical site.
Generalised lymphadenopathy
Two or more non-contiguous node groups enlarged simultaneously. Causes: viral infection (EBV, CMV, HIV, adenovirus, measles, rubella), bacterial (secondary syphilis, Lyme disease, brucellosis, TB), autoimmune (SLE, RA, Sjögren's), malignant (lymphoma — Hodgkin's or NHL, CLL, ALL), drugs (phenytoin, allopurinol, carbamazepine, hydralazine — drug-induced pseudolymphoma). Full history and systemic examination — not an automatic CT chest/abdomen/pelvis. FBC when there is fever, fatigue, bruising, bleeding or hepatosplenomegaly (very urgent FBC if features concerning for leukaemia, NICE NG12); blood film, LDH, CRP/ESR, renal/liver function and HIV testing guided by findings; blood cultures only if febrile or systemically unwell.
Node characteristics
Useful risk indicators, but none is diagnostic — reactive nodes may be firm or sizeable and lymphoma nodes may be mobile or tender. Tender + soft + mobile: reactive / infective (most common) · Firm + rubbery + mobile: lymphoma (Hodgkin's) · Hard + fixed + non-tender: metastatic carcinoma · Fluctuant + erythematous: suppurative lymphadenitis · Matted + firm: TB lymphadenitis (scrofula) · Violaceous + progressive in child: atypical mycobacteria
Key history
Duration and rate of change · Tender vs non-tender · Night sweats, fever, weight loss (B symptoms) · Recent infection / pharyngitis / dental work (reactive) · Animal exposure (cat scratch, toxoplasma from cat litter, brucella from cattle) · Travel history (TB, leishmaniasis, trypanosomiasis, lymphogranuloma venereum) · Sexual history (STI) · Medication changes · HIV risk factors · Occupational exposure · Pruritus · Dental, skin or limb infection · Cancer history · Airway compromise, SVC obstruction, sepsis or severe cytopenia symptoms
The distinction between reactive (tender, soft, mobile) and malignant (non-tender, firm/rubbery/hard, fixed, progressive) lymphadenopathy is the most clinically useful bedside distinction — but it has significant limitations. Lymphoma nodes are classically described as "rubbery" (the texture of an eraser) and non-tender. However, Hodgkin's lymphoma nodes can be tender (especially after alcohol intake — Hodgkin's-associated alcohol-induced pain is a rare but pathognomonic symptom), and reactive nodes in a thin patient can feel surprisingly firm. The key principle is that no single characteristic is diagnostic — it is the combination of features (consistency + mobility + tenderness + duration + patient age + associated symptoms) that determines the clinical probability. The alcohol-induced pain in Hodgkin's lymphoma (pain in affected lymph node regions within minutes of drinking alcohol) is one of medicine's most specific symptoms — it is virtually pathognomonic of Hodgkin's lymphoma and should always be asked about when taking the history. The mechanism is thought to involve eosinophil infiltration in Hodgkin's tumour tissue and prostaglandin release triggered by alcohol. Drug-induced pseudolymphoma (phenytoin being the classic example) can cause widespread lymphadenopathy indistinguishable from lymphoma — always perform a thorough medication review in generalised lymphadenopathy. Any drug change follows a risk-benefit assessment, and lymphadenopathy that persists after withdrawal still requires investigation.

Anatomical Location & Differential Diagnoses

Cervical (anterior / posterior)
Anterior cervical: dental infection, pharyngitis, tonsillitis, EBV, CMV, head and neck SCC. Posterior cervical: EBV (posterior cervical chain is the hallmark of EBV lymphadenopathy), rubella, toxoplasmosis, scalp infection, lymphoma. Occipital: scalp infection, sebaceous cyst, ringworm. Head and neck suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12, updated April 2026): persistent unexplained neck lump → suspected cancer pathway referral; unexplained neck lump or persistent unexplained hoarseness aged 45 and over → laryngeal cancer pathway; unexplained oral ulceration over 3 weeks or persistent unexplained neck lump → oral cancer pathway. The appropriate head-and-neck pathway depends on the examination and likely anatomical site. Avoid routine primary-care CT/MRI or biopsy unless specified by the local pathway — it can delay referral.
Submandibular / submental
Dental abscess, floor of mouth infection, lower lip/tongue SCC. Check dentition and oral cavity in all submandibular lymphadenopathy. Atypical mycobacterial lymphadenitis in children (violaceous skin, non-tender, progressive).
Axillary
Upper limb infection (hand / arm cellulitis, cat scratch — forearm scratch), breast cancer (sentinel node), melanoma (arm), lymphoma. Unilateral axillary node in a woman → breast examination; unexplained axillary lump aged 30+ → breast suspected cancer referral (imaging via breast clinic — ultrasound is not a substitute for referral). HIV lymphadenopathy commonly involves axilla.
Inguinal
Lower limb infection (cellulitis, tinea pedis, infected ingrown toenail — always examine the feet), STI (herpes, syphilis primary/secondary, chlamydia — LGV strain, gonorrhoea), anal cancer, penile cancer, vulval cancer. Bilateral inguinal nodes commonly reactive (small palpable inguinal nodes are common). Unilateral, persistent, progressive or hard inguinal node = investigate.
Supraclavicular
Any supraclavicular node is concerning and deserves prompt assessment. Causes include infection, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, other inflammatory disease and metastatic cancer; left/right drainage patterns are context only, not a basis for site-specific referral. → urgent direct-access chest X-ray aged 40+ (NICE NG12); suspected cancer referral according to clinical findings and local pathway. Do not watch and wait a supraclavicular node.
Mediastinal (hilar)
Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (BHL): sarcoidosis (most common cause of BHL), lymphoma, TB, primary lung cancer (unilateral), silicosis, EAA. CXR identifies BHL. Serum calcium (sarcoidosis); ACE is neither diagnostic nor a reliable activity test — do not use it alone to confirm or exclude sarcoidosis. Urgent respiratory/haematology referral. Unilateral hilar enlargement → suspected lung cancer pathway (further imaging per local pathway).
Abdominal / retroperitoneal
Lymphoma (retroperitoneal mass — "rubber hose" appearance on CT), CLL, metastatic GI cancer, TB. Not palpable unless very large. Identified on CT or USS. Staging CT is arranged by the specialist team, not by the GP before referral. Weight loss + abdominal lymphadenopathy = urgent investigation.
The axillary lymph node in women deserves specific attention — it is a key site for breast cancer sentinel node involvement, and any unilateral axillary lymphadenopathy in a woman without a clear infective cause (no ipsilateral arm or hand infection) warrants breast examination and, if aged 30 and over, breast suspected cancer referral. Breast cancer can present as an isolated axillary node without a palpable breast mass (occult primary breast cancer) — it should not be delayed. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) advises considering a suspected cancer pathway referral for breast cancer in people aged 30 and over with an unexplained lump in the axilla. Inguinal lymphadenopathy requires a full STI risk assessment and a lower limb examination — many GPs examine the groin without examining the feet, missing the infected toe, tinea pedis, or plantar wart that is draining to the inguinal chain. The most common cause of bilateral inguinal lymphadenopathy is reactive nodes from lower limb skin conditions (eczema, psoriasis, chronic tinea) — but unilateral, progressive, or hard inguinal nodes need STI testing and malignancy exclusion. LGV (lymphogranuloma venereum — Chlamydia trachomatis serovars L1–L3) causes massively enlarged, tender, matted inguinal nodes (buboes) — it is increasing in frequency in the UK, particularly in MSM, and requires referral to the sexual-health service for testing and treatment according to current BASHH guidance.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Node examination
Systematically examine all node groups: pre-auricular, parotid, submandibular, submental, anterior cervical, posterior cervical, occipital, supraclavicular (MANDATORY), axillary, epitrochlear, inguinal, popliteal. For each node: size (measure in cm), consistency (soft/firm/rubbery/hard), mobility (mobile/fixed), tenderness, overlying skin (erythema, fluctuance, sinus). Measure the largest node.
Drainage territory examination
Examine the territory draining to the affected node group: oral cavity + teeth (submandibular/anterior cervical), pharynx + tonsils (anterior cervical), scalp + ears (occipital/posterior auricular), breast (axillary in women), hand + forearm (axillary), feet + leg (inguinal), genitals + perineum (inguinal). Identify the source of drainage before attributing the lymphadenopathy as "reactive."
Systemic examination
Hepatosplenomegaly (lymphoma, CLL, EBV, CMV, portal hypertension). Rash (EBV rash, secondary syphilis — palms/soles, drug reaction). Fever (measure — temperature >38°C). Weight (calculate 3-month % loss). Joints (RA/SLE arthropathy in generalised lymphadenopathy).
First-line investigations (targeted)
Localised, clearly reactive node with an identifiable infection: treat or observe according to the clinical diagnosis; no routine blood tests if the patient is well and the node is improving; review if persistent, enlarging or atypical. Persistent, unexplained or progressive lymphadenopathy — consider: FBC · Blood film if FBC abnormal or haematological disease suspected (atypical lymphocytes = EBV/CMV; lymphoblasts = ALL; smear cells = CLL) · CRP/ESR · Renal + liver function · LDH where lymphoma or tissue injury is suspected (a normal LDH or FBC does not exclude lymphoma) · HIV 4th-generation test where appropriate · Targeted EBV/CMV, syphilis, TB or toxoplasma testing based on history · CXR (supraclavicular/persistent cervical nodes aged 40+, mediastinal/hilar concern). Do not routinely order: uric acid as a lymphoma test; ACE for nonspecific lymphadenopathy; ANA/dsDNA without connective-tissue features; CT chest/abdomen/pelvis for staging before referral; Monospot in all adults with cervical nodes.
Second-line investigations
CMV serology (monospot-negative EBV-like illness) · Toxoplasma IgM/IgG (posterior cervical + animal exposure) · Syphilis serology (TPHA + RPR) (inguinal / generalised + STI risk) · IGRA (TB) (travel history, ethnic background, systemic symptoms) · Serum calcium (sarcoidosis + BHL; ACE is not diagnostic) · ANA + anti-dsDNA (only with clinical features of connective-tissue disease) · USS node for selected superficial nodes — uncertain diagnosis, persistent or enlarging node, suspected breast/axillary/thyroid/salivary lesion, or guided core biopsy; not a substitute for an appropriate cancer referral · Blood cultures only if febrile or systemically unwell · CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (staging — specialist-arranged, not before referral)
The blood film is one of the most diagnostically rich investigations available in primary care for lymphadenopathy — it should be specifically requested alongside the FBC (not assumed to be automatically performed). A haematologist reviewing the film can identify: atypical lymphocytes (EBV, CMV, drug reaction), smear cells / CLL cells (chronic lymphocytic leukaemia — CLL is the most common leukaemia in the UK and typically presents with bilateral cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy in patients over 60), lymphoblasts (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia — paediatric emergency), Reed-Sternberg cells (rarely seen on a film in Hodgkin's — diagnosis requires lymph node biopsy), and the monocytosis of CMV. LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) may be raised in lymphoma (reflecting tumour bulk and cellular turnover) and in tissue injury — it is requested where lymphoma is suspected, not as a universal test, and a normal LDH does not exclude lymphoma. The International Prognostic Index (IPI) for lymphoma includes LDH as one of its five prognostic factors. The Monospot test (Paul-Bunnell heterophile antibody test) has variable sensitivity, particularly early in illness and in younger children — where confirmation matters, request EBV-specific serology (VCA IgM — acute infection; VCA IgG + EBNA — past infection).

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day hospital
Mediastinal mass with signs of SVC obstruction (facial/arm swelling + dilated veins), stridor, hypoxia, cerebral symptoms or rapidly progressive facial/neck swelling → urgent hospital/acute oncology assessment (contact acute oncology/respiratory/haematology urgently; 999 if airway compromised). Steroids only according to the receiving specialist pathway, particularly when lymphoma is possible · Lymphadenopathy + severe pancytopenia (FBC: Hb <70, neutrophils <1.0, platelets <50) — haematological emergency · Lymphadenopathy + airway compromise from large neck/mediastinal mass → 999
Suspected cancer: haematology
NICE NG12: unexplained lymphadenopathy in an adult — associated symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss, pruritus, breathlessness, alcohol-induced nodal pain) inform urgency; no size or 6-week threshold is required · Lymphadenopathy with B symptoms (any one), splenomegaly or abnormal blood film · Generalised lymphadenopathy with possible leukaemia → very urgent FBC · Blood film: lymphoblasts (ALL) or significant lymphocytosis
Suspected cancer: head & neck
NICE NG12: persistent unexplained neck lump → suspected cancer referral via the head-and-neck pathway appropriate to the examination and likely anatomical site (laryngeal pathway for an unexplained neck lump or persistent hoarseness aged 45 and over) · Avoid routine primary-care CT/MRI or biopsy unless the local pathway specifies · Any cervical lymphadenopathy + dysphagia or hoarse voice (laryngeal/hypopharyngeal cancer) · Cervical node + oral ulcer not healing 3 weeks
Suspected cancer: breast
NICE NG12: unexplained axillary lump aged 30 and over · Any axillary lymphadenopathy + breast lump or skin change
Urgent GP referral (within 2 weeks)
Supraclavicular or persistent cervical lymphadenopathy aged 40 and over → urgent direct-access chest X-ray (NICE NG12); lung suspected cancer pathway referral if the X-ray suggests lung cancer; lymphoma features → lymphoma suspected cancer pathway referral; otherwise suspected cancer referral according to clinical findings and the local pathway · Isolated inguinal lymphadenopathy without STI or lower limb source in adult ≥40
Sexual-health service
Inguinal lymphadenopathy + STI risk → gonorrhoea/chlamydia NAAT + syphilis serology + HIV test · LGV suspected (massive tender inguinal buboes in MSM) → urgent sexual-health referral; treatment per current BASHH guidance · Significant HIV exposure or acute retroviral syndrome → urgent sexual-health referral
Respiratory / rheumatology
Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy plus compatible pulmonary, skin, eye or joint findings (e.g. hypercalcaemia, uveitis, skin lesions; ACE alone does not confirm or exclude) → urgent respiratory · Generalised lymphadenopathy + positive ANA + arthritis → rheumatology (SLE, adult Still's, RA)
The 6-week watch-and-wait period for cervical lymphadenopathy in adults is often cited but is increasingly being challenged in clinical practice — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) sets no waiting period: suspected cancer pathway referral for a persistent unexplained neck lump, via the head-and-neck pathway appropriate to the examination and likely anatomical site (laryngeal pathway for an unexplained neck lump at age 45 and over). Observation is reasonable only where there is a clear benign/infective explanation, the patient is clinically well, the node is improving, there are no red flags and a documented review and safety-net plan are in place. Do not use an arbitrary 6-week period to delay referral where there is significant clinical concern. The practical management is: at the first consultation, if features are clearly reactive (tender, soft, associated URTI, young patient) → watchful waiting with a documented review. At the review — if persistent, enlarging or atypical → targeted investigations according to the clinical pattern, and suspected cancer pathway referral if still unexplained. If any concerning features at first presentation (hard, fixed, non-tender, patient ≥40, B symptoms) → investigate and refer at the same visit. Lymph node biopsy (excision biopsy or core needle biopsy) is arranged through the specialist pathway — avoid unplanned primary-care procedures, but specialist-directed sampling is often essential for diagnosis. The approach to biopsy is: USS-guided core needle biopsy first for accessible nodes (avoids general anaesthesia), proceeding to excision biopsy if core biopsy is non-diagnostic or insufficient tissue. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology alone is insufficient for lymphoma diagnosis — it cannot provide the architectural information needed for classification.

Treat

GP-Initiated Treatment for Infectious Causes

EBV (infectious mononucleosis)
Supportive — paracetamol + ibuprofen
No specific antiviral treatment. Rest as needed + adequate hydration (routine bed rest is not required). Avoid amoxicillin and ampicillin because of rash risk. Corticosteroids only for serious complications (with specialist advice), e.g. severe oropharyngeal oedema threatening airway, thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anaemia, neurological complications. Avoid contact sport and heavy exertion while acutely unwell and until clinical recovery, with individual assessment if splenomegaly or high-risk activity (splenic rupture risk); routine ultrasound to confirm splenic size is not needed. Return if worsening dysphagia, respiratory compromise, or neurological signs (EBV encephalitis).
Bacterial lymphadenitis
Antibiotic per local antimicrobial guidance
Choice depends on age, site, dental versus skin source, abscess formation and allergy — follow local antimicrobial guidance. Fluctuant node = abscess → usually requires drainage (antibiotics alone are insufficient). Avoid repeated antibiotics without reassessment — persistent or recurrent nodes need imaging/specialist assessment rather than serial empirical courses; not responding → hospital assessment.
Cat scratch disease
Usually supportive — antibiotics in selected cases
Bartonella henselae. Most immunocompetent patients recover without antibiotics. Azithromycin can be considered in selected cases, but the benefit is modest and it is not a default prescription. Avoid node aspiration in primary care (sinus may form). Atypical, severe, disseminated or immunocompromised disease → specialist advice.
ToxoplasmosisIn immunocompetent patients: self-limiting — symptomatic treatment only (paracetamol). Resolves in 1–2 months. Specific treatment (pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + folinic acid) only for: immunocompromised patients, pregnant women (vertical transmission risk — refer to obstetrics urgently), ocular toxoplasmosis (ophthalmology). Primary prevention: avoid eating undercooked meat, handle cat litter with gloves.
TB lymphadenitis (scrofula)Suspected TB → urgent respiratory/infectious-disease referral with appropriate microbiology and public-health involvement (NICE NG33); treatment is specialist-led. Notify UKHSA (TB is notifiable). Contact tracing (Public Health team). Avoid unplanned primary-care procedures — obtain tissue/fluid through a specialist TB/ENT/respiratory pathway with appropriate microbiology and infection-control arrangements.
The amoxicillin rash in EBV is one of the most important drug-disease interactions in primary care — if a patient with EBV infectious mononucleosis (or any active herpesvirus infection) is given amoxicillin or ampicillin, they commonly develop a widespread maculopapular rash. This rash is not a true penicillin allergy — it is a T-cell mediated reaction specific to the combination of the aminopenicillin and the EBV-altered immune state. Patients who have this reaction are NOT penicillin-allergic and should not be labelled as such in their records. The rash typically appears 5–10 days after starting amoxicillin. This matters because many patients with EBV present with a very sore throat — GPs must resist prescribing amoxicillin for the pharyngitis component without first excluding EBV (Monospot test in clinical practice — accepting the false negative rate in the first week). If antibiotics are genuinely needed for concurrent bacterial tonsillitis in EBV, use phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) rather than amoxicillin. Splenic rupture (spontaneous or traumatic) is the most dangerous acute complication of EBV. Advise avoidance of contact sport and heavy exertion while acutely unwell and until clinical recovery, with individual assessment if splenomegaly or high-risk activity is present — rather than a fixed period for everyone or routine ultrasound. This is particularly important for young athletes and must be documented in the clinical records.

Treat

Autoimmune & Drug-Induced Lymphadenopathy

Drug-induced pseudolymphoma
Implicated drugs include phenytoin (most classic), carbamazepine, allopurinol, hydralazine, captopril, gold, primidone. Do not stop essential anticonvulsants or cardiovascular medicines without a risk-benefit assessment (with the prescribing specialist where appropriate). Drug-induced hypersensitivity syndromes (fever, rash, eosinophilia, hepatitis or renal injury) require urgent assessment. Persistent lymphadenopathy after withdrawal still requires investigation. Document the suspected adverse drug reaction, but do not automatically label it as a permanent drug allergy.
SLE-associated lymphadenopathy
Treat underlying SLE (hydroxychloroquine ± low-dose prednisolone for active disease). Rheumatology to manage. Lymphadenopathy reflects disease activity — reduces with disease control. SLE also increases lymphoma risk — persistently enlarged, non-tender nodes in SLE need specialist biopsy consideration.
Sarcoidosis
Consider sarcoidosis with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy plus compatible pulmonary, skin, eye or joint findings. Refer for respiratory assessment. Avoid using ACE alone to confirm or exclude the diagnosis. Lofgren's syndrome (BHL + erythema nodosum + ankle arthritis) often resolves spontaneously, but the diagnosis should be specialist-confirmed where appropriate. Serial imaging, lung function and any corticosteroid or steroid-sparing treatment follow respiratory/specialist care — do not initiate prednisolone in primary care for nonspecific lymphadenopathy.
Reactive lymphadenopathy (confirmed benign)
No pharmacological treatment required. Reassurance + review plan. Antibiotic prescription is inappropriate for confirmed viral reactive lymphadenopathy — document this conversation in the records. Written safety-net instructions: return if the node enlarges, becomes persistent, hard/fixed, is associated with systemic symptoms or fails to improve as expected. Confirm review appointment.
Drug-induced pseudolymphoma from phenytoin is historically important and clinically relevant — patients on long-term phenytoin can develop widespread lymphadenopathy, fever, skin rash, and even hepatosplenomegaly that is histologically indistinguishable from lymphoma on biopsy (follicular hyperplasia). The key feature that distinguishes pseudolymphoma from true lymphoma is complete resolution on drug cessation. Confusingly, there is a well-documented association between phenytoin use and an increased incidence of true Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma — so any patient who develops lymphadenopathy while on phenytoin needs a risk-benefit review of the drug AND investigation to exclude lymphoma if the lymphadenopathy does not resolve. Sarcoidosis with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy warrants a brief explanation for trainees: the bilateral hilar pattern on CXR is symmetric, with node enlargement at the hilum (where the bronchi and pulmonary vessels enter the lung) bilaterally. It must be distinguished from: unilateral hilar (lung cancer, more common on the right), lymphoma hilar (often asymmetric + mediastinal involvement), and TB hilar (usually with paratracheal involvement and calcification in old disease). The classic sarcoidosis presentation (Lofgren's syndrome) in a young woman with erythema nodosum and ankle arthritis is highly suggestive of sarcoidosis — the diagnosis should be specialist-confirmed where appropriate, and tissue biopsy may not be required when this clinical triad is present.
8
Lifestyle

Patient Education, Prevention & Wellbeing

EBV recovery — rest and activity Fatigue after EBV can persist for 3–6 months (post-viral fatigue). Graded return to activity — avoid rushing back to full activity. Adequate sleep, balanced diet. Avoid contact sport and heavy exertion while acutely unwell and until clinical recovery (individual assessment if splenomegaly or high-risk activity). Most young patients recover fully within 3 months. Persistent fatigue beyond 3 months → post-EBV fatigue syndrome (PVFS) assessment.
HIV prevention (PrEP) If HIV risk identified during lymphadenopathy assessment → signpost/refer to the sexual-health service, which assesses and manages PrEP. Available free on NHS via sexual-health / online sexual health services. U=U: undetectable HIV on treatment = untransmittable. Condom use. Regular HIV testing (annually for MSM, more frequently if changing partners). Normalise HIV testing as routine healthcare.
TB prevention — BCG and screening TB is a notifiable disease. Contact tracing, latent TB testing and treatment, BCG vaccination and new-entrant screening are managed through respiratory/infectious-disease and public-health (UKHSA/local TB service) pathways (NICE NG33).
Cancer awareness Routine monthly lymph-node self-palpation is not recommended (it may increase health anxiety). Advise patients to return if a node enlarges, becomes persistent, hard/fixed, is associated with systemic symptoms or fails to improve as expected. Smokers: smoking increases head and neck cancer risk (but is not diagnostic) — smoking cessation. Alcohol: head and neck cancer risk reduction with moderation.
STI prevention (inguinal lymphadenopathy) Condom use for all STIs. HPV vaccination (Gardasil 9 — NHS schedule up to 25, sexual-health services for MSM up to 45). Regular STI screening (annually or with each new partner). Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is not a generic GP intervention — it follows current specialist sexual-health guidance and local commissioning.
Animal exposure (zoonotic infections) Cat scratch disease prevention: wash cat scratches immediately with soap and water. Toxoplasma prevention: cook meat to ≥70°C, wear gloves for gardening and cat litter. Brucella: avoid unpasteurised dairy in endemic areas. Lyme disease: tick precautions (long sleeves, insect repellent, prompt tick removal) in endemic areas (New Forest, Lake District, Scottish Highlands).
Psychological support — lymphoma diagnosis anxiety Waiting for lymphoma biopsy results is one of the most anxiety-provoking experiences for patients. Acknowledge the uncertainty explicitly: "I know waiting for these results is very difficult — I'll make sure you get your result within [timeframe]." Set a clear result communication plan at the time of referral. Macmillan Cancer Support (0808 808 0000) offers pre-diagnosis support. Document safety-net review appointment.
Post-EBV fatigue (post-viral fatigue syndrome following infectious mononucleosis) affects approximately 10–15% of patients at 6 months and 5% at 12 months — it is the most common cause of prolonged fatigue in young adults and is one of the proposed triggers of ME/CFS (myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome) in genetically susceptible individuals. NICE NG206 (ME/CFS, 2021) guidance applies to post-viral fatigue states — the previous advice to "gradually increase exercise" (graded exercise therapy) has been removed from that guideline, replaced by an energy management approach (pacing, avoiding post-exertional malaise). GPs should not advise patients with post-EBV fatigue to push through tiredness or increase activity beyond their energy envelope. The anxiety associated with unexplained lymphadenopathy waiting for biopsy results is clinically significant — studies show that the period between GP referral and biopsy result is associated with high rates of anxiety, depression, and impact on work and relationships. Setting a clear, specific communication plan at the time of referral ("I'll ring you personally with the result" or "the results clinic is on [date]") significantly reduces anxiety and improves the doctor-patient relationship. This is an RCGP SCA-assessed communication competency — the ability to explain investigation plans, timescales, and safety-net while managing uncertainty is a core GP competency.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Reactive lymphadenopathy — planned review
Review appointment booked at time of diagnosis. Reassess at the documented interval: resolving? Measure node size at both visits. If persistent, enlarging or atypical → targeted investigations and suspected cancer pathway referral if unexplained — do not use an arbitrary 6-week period to delay referral where there is significant concern. Do not give a further antibiotic course without objective reassessment.
EBV — 4 weeks + 3 months
Clinical review at 4 weeks (resolution of lymphadenopathy, fatigue improving, returned to activity?). FBC at 4 weeks if still fatigued (atypical lymphocytosis resolved?). If fatigue persisting at 3 months → post-EBV fatigue assessment (exclude thyroid, anaemia, depression). Contact sport and heavy exertion avoided until clinical recovery (individual assessment; routine USS not required).
Blood test results — 1 week
All blood tests in persistent lymphadenopathy reviewed within 1 week. Any abnormality (elevated LDH, lymphocytosis, atypical cells on film) → escalate immediately. Do not wait for an already-booked routine review if blood tests show urgent abnormalities.
After suspected cancer / specialist referral
Confirm appointment received. If lymphoma confirmed: GP co-management role — support during specialist-led treatment, blood count monitoring per the haematology plan, infection risk education, neutropenic sepsis recognition (direct to the acute oncology/haematology hotline — IV antibiotics and any G-CSF are hospital-led). Macmillan GP facilitation.
CLL (long-term watchful waiting)
Early CLL (Binet Stage A) is managed with watchful waiting — monitoring intervals are specified by haematology (e.g. FBC every 3–6 months in selected stable patients under an agreed shared-care plan). Do not calculate lymphocyte doubling time or use it in isolation to trigger urgent referral. New B symptoms, progressive nodes, splenomegaly, cytopenias, recurrent infections or suspected transformation (Richter's syndrome) → haematology review. Patient education: neutropenic sepsis awareness, vaccination schedule (avoid live vaccines on treatment).
999 / same-day safety-net
New SVC obstruction signs (face + arm swelling + distended veins) during watchful waiting · Airway compromise from rapidly enlarging cervical/mediastinal mass · Severe pancytopenia symptoms (bruising, bleeding, infection) · Febrile neutropenia in patient on lymphoma chemotherapy (temperature ≥38°C → 999 / haematology hotline)
Same-day GP / urgent
Node rapidly enlarging beyond measured baseline · New B symptoms during watchful waiting · New neurological symptoms (CNS lymphoma — headache, cranial nerve palsy) · New hepatosplenomegaly at review · Any deterioration in general condition during watchful waiting period
Febrile neutropenia in lymphoma chemotherapy patients is one of the most important emergencies that GPs will encounter in patients with haematological malignancy. The standard definition is: temperature above 38°C (or signs of sepsis) AND neutrophil count 0.5 × 10⁹/L or lower (NICE CG151). Febrile neutropenia is a medical emergency with a mortality of 5–10% even with treatment — patients must call the haematology 24-hour hotline number or attend A&E for urgent IV antibiotics per the hospital neutropenic sepsis protocol, not the GP. Every patient commencing lymphoma chemotherapy should receive a "chemotherapy alert card" — GPs should ensure this is in the patient's records and that the practice knows to advise the patient to call 999 or the haematology hotline for any temperature ≥38°C. CLL watchful waiting is specialist-led — where monitoring is shared with primary care, it follows an agreed haematology shared-care plan with specified intervals. Watch for new B symptoms, progressive nodes, splenomegaly, falling haemoglobin or platelets (autoimmune cytopenias occur in CLL) and recurrent infections, and seek haematology review rather than calculating a doubling time in isolation.
