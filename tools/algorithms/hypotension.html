Safety

Red Flags — Shock, Sepsis & Life-Threatening Hypotension

Do not diagnose shock from a BP value alone. Hypotension with altered mental state, poor perfusion, oliguria, severe dyspnoea, chest pain, hypoxia, ongoing bleeding, severe infection or deterioration is an emergency. Systolic <90 is a major warning sign read in context — shock can occur with normal BP, and with bradycardia as well as tachycardia.

Hypotension + tachycardia + cold clammy skin + confusion + mottling Circulatory shock (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, distributive, or obstructive). → 999. Do not delay transfer. If IV fluid is needed and local policy allows pre-hospital treatment: isotonic crystalloid in small aliquots (NICE NG253: 250 ml over 10–15 min, reassess after each, up to 1 L), with caution in heart failure, renal failure, frailty and suspected cardiogenic/obstructive shock. Reassess mental state, pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, capillary refill and peripheral temperature frequently.
Hypotension + fever + suspected infection source + tachycardia Septic shock. → 999. Use NEWS2 with clinical judgement (NG253: NEWS2 ≥7 high risk) — a low score does not overrule concern. Oxygen only if hypoxaemic. Pre-hospital investigations and antibiotics follow local ambulance/ICB policy and must not delay transfer.
Hypotension + severe chest pain + ST changes on ECG Cardiogenic shock from acute MI. → 999. Aspirin 300 mg PO. Avoid aggressive fluid loading (pulmonary oedema risk). Primary PCI urgently.
Sudden hypotension + urticaria + angioedema + bronchospasm + allergen exposure Anaphylaxis. → Adrenaline 0.5 mg IM anterolateral thigh → 999 immediately. Lie flat and do not stand or walk (semi-recumbent if breathing is difficult; left lateral tilt if pregnant). Repeat IM adrenaline after 5 minutes if no improvement. Antihistamines are third-line (skin symptoms only) and corticosteroids are no longer routinely recommended (Resuscitation Council UK 2021). Do NOT delay adrenaline for either.
Hypotension + severe headache + petechial rash + fever + neck stiffness Meningococcal septicaemia. → 999 immediately. If there will be a clinically significant delay, give IV/IM benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone per the local (GM) protocol — never delay transfer to give it.
Hypotension + Addisonian features: pigmentation + hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia + fatigue Addisonian crisis — cortisol and aldosterone deficiency causes profound vasodilation and volume depletion. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IM or IV immediately + 999 — do not delay for cortisol, ACTH or Synacthen. In hospital: 1 L 0.9% saline over 30 min unless contraindicated, with ongoing fluid, glucose, electrolyte and BP monitoring (NICE NG243).
NICE NG253 sets the current UK risk-stratified sepsis approach for people aged 16 and over, using NEWS2 alongside clinical judgement rather than a single universal bundle. The key primary-care action is calling 999 first; anything done before the ambulance arrives (oxygen if hypoxaemic, IV access, antibiotics) follows local policy and must not delay transfer. Addisonian crisis is frequently missed in primary care because: (1) the patient may not know they have Addison's (a crisis may be the presenting event); (2) the symptoms (fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion) are non-specific; and (3) the BP may be only marginally low before deterioration. The electrolyte pattern (hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia) combined with postural hypotension and hyperpigmentation in an unwell patient is highly suggestive. The safe action: give hydrocortisone 100 mg IM and call 999 — if the patient does not have Addison's, hydrocortisone 100 mg is not harmful. If they do and you do not give it, they may die.

Classification — Causes of Hypotension

Postural hypotension (common in primary care)
Fall ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic after standing for at least 1 minute (NICE NG136). Causes: dehydration, vasodilator medications (antihypertensives, nitrates, alpha-blockers, PDE5 inhibitors), autonomic failure (Parkinson's, MSA, diabetic neuropathy), adrenal insufficiency, post-prandial (elderly). Symptoms: dizziness on standing, pre-syncope, falls, blackouts.
Hypovolaemic
Dehydration (vomiting, diarrhoea, reduced intake — especially elderly in hot weather), GI haemorrhage (melaena, haematemesis), burns, overdiuresis. Signs: tachycardia, dry mucosae, urea raised out of proportion to creatinine, reduced skin turgor, oliguria.
Cardiac / obstructive
Low cardiac output: acute MI, severe heart failure (EF <20%), critical aortic stenosis, cardiac tamponade (Beck's triad: hypotension + raised JVP + muffled heart sounds), massive PE (S1Q3T3 on ECG), complete heart block, VT/VF.
Distributive
Sepsis (warm peripheries, bounding pulse initially), anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock (spinal cord injury — loss of sympathetic tone), Addisonian crisis (cortisol + aldosterone deficiency), liver failure (splanchnic vasodilation).
Drug-induced (extremely common)
ACEi/ARBs (first dose, volume-depleted) · Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, doxazosin — first dose) · Diuretics (volume depletion) · Nitrates · TCAs + antipsychotics (alpha-blocking) · Sildenafil + nitrates (absolute CI — life-threatening hypotension) · Beta-blockers (reduced cardiac output) · Opioids
The sildenafil (and all PDE5 inhibitors) + nitrate combination is the most dangerous drug-drug interaction in common primary care prescribing — both drugs independently cause vasodilatation and BP reduction (PDE5 inhibitors prevent cGMP breakdown → smooth muscle relaxation; nitrates directly release nitric oxide → cGMP-mediated vasodilation), and in combination can cause catastrophic hypotension resulting in syncope, MI, and death. This combination is absolutely contraindicated regardless of timing — the interaction can persist for up to 24 hours after sildenafil use. GPs prescribing nitrates must ask about sildenafil use; GPs prescribing sildenafil must check for nitrate use (including GTN spray kept for angina relief). The STOPP (Screening Tool of Older People's Prescriptions) criteria specifically identifies alpha-1 receptor blockers (tamsulosin, doxazosin) in patients prone to falls as inappropriate due to orthostatic hypotension risk — in an elderly patient presenting with falls, reviewing and stopping or reducing alpha-1 blockers is often the highest-yield single medication intervention.

Orthostatic BP Measurement Technique & Assessment

Lying-to-standing BP (NICE NG136)
(1) Rest lying down where possible → BP + HR supine. (2) Stand safely → repeat after at least 1 minute, and during the first 3 minutes if symptoms persist or syncope is suspected. (3) Positive: SBP fall ≥20 mmHg or DBP fall ≥10 mmHg. (4) Ask about symptoms at each reading. Document: "Supine BP X/X, standing 1 min Y/Y (drop Z), standing 3 min A/A, symptomatic: [yes/no]." Symptomatic drop below 20 mmHg threshold is still clinically significant.
History
Timing: on standing (postural), after meals (post-prandial — common elderly diabetics), at rest (chronic). Symptoms: dizziness, greying out, syncope, falls. Drug history (ALL medications including OTC sildenafil). Fluid intake + GI losses. Autonomic symptoms: anhidrosis, constipation, erectile dysfunction (autonomic neuropathy). Cardiac symptoms. Known Parkinson's or diabetes (autonomic failure).
Examination
BP lying + standing. HR: no compensatory tachycardia on standing = autonomic failure (MSA, diabetic neuropathy). Mucous membrane hydration. Peripheral perfusion. JVP (elevated = cardiac failure/tamponade; absent = hypovolaemia). Skin: pigmentation (Addison's), petechiae (sepsis). Cardiac: murmurs, rhythm.
Investigations
ECG (arrhythmia, MI, PE) · FBC (anaemia, infection) · U&E + eGFR (dehydration: urea raised out of proportion to creatinine; Addison's: Na low + K high) · HbA1c / TSH if clinically indicated · 9am cortisol ± synacthen (Addison's if suspected) · Troponin (if chest pain)
The orthostatic BP measurement technique is one of the most commonly performed incorrectly — many clinicians measure a single standing BP immediately after the patient stands, without the prerequisite 5 minutes of supine rest. This misses the classical orthostatic pattern (which develops over 1–3 minutes as venous pooling accumulates) and produces false-negative results. Use the NICE NG136 method and record symptoms with each reading. The absence of a compensatory tachycardia on standing is as diagnostically important as the BP drop itself — in normal orthostasis, HR rises by 10–20 bpm to compensate for reduced cardiac filling. Absence of this HR increase in the context of a significant BP drop indicates autonomic failure (the baroreflex arc is broken), pointing specifically to neurogenic causes: Multiple System Atrophy, pure autonomic failure, diabetic cardiac autonomic neuropathy, or Parkinson's disease with autonomic involvement. This finding usually warrants neurology referral.

Diagnose

Severity Classification & Differential

Haemodynamic compromise (emergency)
999: hypotension (or a normal BP in a deteriorating patient) with confusion, poor perfusion, oliguria, mottling, falling SpO₂, chest pain or ongoing bleeding — no single BP or HR cut-off defines shock. Call 999 first, treat while waiting.
Symptomatic orthostatic hypotension
Investigate and treat: Postural BP drop ≥20/10 mmHg + dizziness/syncope/falls. Identify and treat cause. Falls risk assessment. Medication review. Target symptomatic improvement.
Constitutional hypotension (young adults)
Reassure: Low BP may be a normal variant in a well, asymptomatic person — but first assess for symptoms, postural change, pregnancy, bleeding, dehydration, endocrine disease, medicines and cardiac disease.
Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension
Characteristic pattern: large postural BP drop WITHOUT compensatory HR rise on standing. Causes: Parkinson's disease, Multiple System Atrophy (MSA), pure autonomic failure, diabetic cardiac autonomic neuropathy. Neurology referral; drug treatment is specialist-guided.
Post-prandial hypotension
BP falls >15–20 mmHg within 1–2 hours of meals. Common in elderly diabetics + autonomic failure. Mechanism: splanchnic vasodilation inadequately compensated. Management: small frequent meals, avoid standing immediately post-meals, cold water before meals in selected patients (not if aspiration risk or fluid restriction); acarbose only with specialist advice.
Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) is a crucial diagnosis to recognise presenting as orthostatic hypotension — it is a progressive neurodegenerative disease causing autonomic failure (orthostatic hypotension, urinary dysfunction, erectile dysfunction, constipation) combined with parkinsonism and/or cerebellar ataxia. The key distinguishing feature from Parkinson's disease: MSA autonomic failure causes orthostatic hypotension without compensatory tachycardia because the entire sympathetic efferent system degenerates (not just the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway). In contrast, Parkinson's disease has variable and often mild orthostatic hypotension with partial HR compensation. Any patient with Parkinsonism + severe orthostatic hypotension (SBP drop >30 mmHg) + urinary dysfunction warrants neurology assessment for MSA. MSA has a worse prognosis than PD — patients deserve accurate diagnosis and appropriate support.

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day emergency
Shock (any type) · Septic shock · Anaphylaxis · Cardiogenic shock · Pituitary apoplexy · Addisonian crisis (hypotension + vomiting unable to take oral steroids) · Meningococcal septicaemia
Same-day medical admission
Sustained hypotension BP <90/60 without clear benign cause + no shock features · Suspected Addisonian crisis (first presentation with electrolyte pattern + pigmentation) · Syncope with hypotension in context of new medication in elderly patient
Cardiology (urgent)
Significant valvular disease (critical AS), cardiomyopathy, or arrhythmia (complete heart block) causing hypotension
Neurology
Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (no compensatory tachycardia) + Parkinsonism → possible MSA · Diabetic cardiac autonomic neuropathy requiring complex management
Endocrinology
Confirmed Addison's disease (initiation of hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone + sick day rules + MedicAlert) · Panhypopituitarism
GP management
Drug-induced: adjust medications (STOPP review). Dehydration: rehydrate + diuretic dose review. Constitutional: lifestyle advice. Post-prandial: dietary modification + cold water before meals. Falls assessment: NICE NG249 pathway. Unexplained TLoC: ECG and cardiovascular assessment (CG109) — urgent if abnormal ECG, exertional, heart failure, family history of sudden death, >65 without prodrome, new breathlessness or murmur.
The falls-hypotension linkage is built into NICE QS86 (Falls, updated April 2025 — statement 2: comprehensive falls assessment, which includes lying and standing blood pressure, for people aged 65 or over and people aged 50 to 64 with one or more falls risk factors) — orthostatic hypotension is one of the most prevalent modifiable fall risk factors in community-dwelling elderly patients . NICE NG249 (Falls, 2025, rec 1.2.2) includes a lying and standing blood pressure test in the falls assessment, to check for postural hypotension including lying-to-standing BP measurement. GPs should incorporate this measurement into every fall assessment. Multifactorial falls intervention (medication review, physiotherapy, OT home hazard assessment, vision check; bone health by fracture risk) reduces falls in at-risk patients. The medication review is often the highest-yield intervention — removing one antihypertensive, diuretic, or alpha-blocker from a polypharmacy elderly patient can be transformative for falls frequency and quality of life.

Treat

Treatment by Cause

Drug-induced OH
Medication rationalisation
Shared-decision medication review (STOPP as a guide, not automatic) — change one drug at a time and reassess. First-dose alpha-blocker: take at bedtime. Absolute: do not co-prescribe sildenafil + nitrates. Diuretics: review indication, volume status, renal function and electrolytes before reducing; agree any sick-day plan with the treating clinician (especially heart failure).
Dehydration
Rehydration
Adequate oral fluids tailored to comorbidity (care in heart failure, CKD, cirrhosis, hyponatraemia, fluid restriction). ORS if GI losses. Moderate-severe dehydration or unable to drink → hospital assessment. No routine diuretic "holidays" — individualise.
Neurogenic OH
Specialist-guided
Non-drug measures first. Fludrocortisone is off-label here with limited evidence (NICE ESUOM20) — fluid retention, oedema, hypokalaemia, supine hypertension, heart-failure risk; start only with specialist advice. Midodrine is licensed for severe postural hypotension due to autonomic dysfunction (ESNM61) once reversible causes are addressed. Compression + head-of-bed elevation.
Addison's disease
Hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day
Morning dose 10–15 mg, noon 5–10 mg. Fludrocortisone (NG243: 50–300 mcg daily, adjusted to response) in primary adrenal insufficiency. Endocrinology-led initiation. Sick day rules (double/triple dose with illness). IM hydrocortisone kit at home. MedicAlert bracelet. Appetite and energy return within days.
Post-prandial OH
Dietary modification
Small frequent meals. Low GI carbohydrates. Cold water before meals in selected patients (not with aspiration risk or fluid restriction). Sit for 1 hour after eating. Acarbose or octreotide only via specialist.
Fludrocortisone for neurogenic orthostatic hypotension requires careful supine BP monitoring — while fludrocortisone raises plasma volume and improves standing BP (desired), it simultaneously raises supine BP (potentially harmful in patients with cardiac disease or pre-existing hypertension). The target: eliminate symptomatic standing hypotension while maintaining supine systolic BP below 150–160 mmHg. If supine hypertension develops on fludrocortisone, the dose should be reduced and midodrine considered as an alternative (midodrine has a shorter duration of action and can be timed to avoid overnight supine hypertension — patients should not take it within 4 hours of bedtime). A cold-water bolus before meals may help selected patients with autonomic or post-prandial hypotension, but it is not suitable with aspiration risk or fluid restriction.

Treat

Pharmacological Options for Neurogenic OH

Midodrine (specialist-guided)
Alpha-1 agonist. Licensed for severe postural hypotension due to autonomic dysfunction after reversible causes are addressed (not for all low BP); per local formulary and neurology advice. Start 2.5 mg TDS. Titrate up to 10 mg TDS as needed. Do NOT take within 4 hours of bedtime (causes supine hypertension). Contraindications: severe cardiac disease, urinary retention, phaeochromocytoma. Actively monitor supine BP; assess urinary retention and cardiac disease.
Other agents
Droxidopa, pyridostigmine, desmopressin, indomethacin and octreotide are specialist or selected off-label options — not routine primary-care treatment.
Midodrine is available in the UK (licensed as Bramox since 2015, for severe orthostatic hypotension due to autonomic dysfunction) and is listed in the BNF for symptomatic orthostatic hypotension. It is the preferred second-line agent after fludrocortisone in neurogenic OH because it acts peripherally (alpha-1 agonist on peripheral vasculature) without CNS stimulation, and its shorter duration of action (3–4 hours per dose) allows dosing to be timed to waking hours, avoiding overnight supine hypertension. GPs may continue or initiate midodrine following a specialist recommendation. The three-times-daily dosing schedule should be planned around the patient's activity: typically on waking, mid-morning, and early afternoon — never in the evening. The clinical goal is symptomatic improvement in standing tolerance and reduction in fall frequency, not a specific BP target.

Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Strategies & Education

Graduated rising technique Ankle dorsiflexion exercises (10–15 pumps) before standing. Rise in two stages: sit on bed edge 30 seconds → then stand. Grip stable surface when first standing. Highest-risk time is waking in the morning (lowest BP at the end of the night, greatest tilt change). A chair beside the bed prevents falls during transitions.
Compression stockings Properly fitted compression reduces venous pooling. Wear from rising until bedtime. Must be correctly fitted (measure ankle + calf). Assess arterial supply first (significant PAD is a contraindication; thresholds vary by device and local vascular guidance). Abdominal compression binders (for truncal pooling in severe OH) as adjunct.
Fluid and salt intake Adequate fluid tailored to comorbidity — no fixed high volume in heart failure, renal impairment or fluid restriction. Salt supplementation only in selected neurogenic postural hypotension without hypertension, heart failure, CKD, oedema or cirrhosis, preferably with specialist advice (grams of salt ≠ grams of sodium). Avoid alcohol (vasodilator + diuretic). Adequate hydration is an accessible intervention.
Head-of-bed elevation Elevate head of bed 10–15 cm using blocks under bed legs (not extra pillows — pillows flex the neck without tilting the body). Creates whole-body tilt → activates renin-angiotensin system during sleep → reduces nocturnal diuresis → improves morning plasma volume. Particularly effective in supine hypertension with morning OH.
Post-prandial strategies Small frequent meals (5–6/day). Low glycaemic index. Cold water before meals if suitable. Remain seated for 1 hour post-meal. Recumbent rest after meals if severe. Avoid large single carbohydrate loads. Acarbose only via specialist.
Addison's sick day rules Sick day rule 1: double the usual daily hydrocortisone dose with fever, illness needing bed rest, or illness needing antibiotics. Sick day rule 2 (severe illness, trauma, persistent vomiting or diarrhoea): injection plus urgent medical help (Society for Endocrinology). Unable to take oral medications (vomiting): IM hydrocortisone 100 mg injection (patient/family trained). MedicAlert bracelet. Written sick day rules card. Emergency contact numbers.
Exercise in orthostatic hypotension Hydrotherapy/swimming ideal (horizontal position → no venous pooling while exercising cardiovascular fitness). Recumbent cycling as alternative. Avoid prolonged standing. Calf muscle pump activation during prolonged standing (shifting weight, marching on spot). Avoid hot environments (vasodilation).
Patient and carer education Explain mechanism clearly: "When you stand quickly, blood pools in your legs. Your heart needs a moment to pump harder to compensate — with your condition, that compensation is slower than normal." Written action plan. Recognition of pre-syncope warning signs (grey-out, nausea, sweating). Sit immediately at first warning. Falls diary to identify pattern and triggers.
Head-of-bed elevation is a counter-intuitive but evidence-based intervention that most patients have not heard of — the mechanism is that sleeping with the head elevated 10–15 degrees causes mild volume redistribution overnight (reduced renal perfusion → renin-angiotensin activation → aldosterone release → sodium retention → expanded plasma volume by morning). This effect is specific to whole-body tilt (head AND torso elevated together) — using extra pillows only flexes the cervical spine without the haemodynamic benefit. It can improve morning orthostatic symptoms in autonomic failure. The practical instruction: place 10–15 cm blocks (wooden blocks, thick books, commercially available bed risers) under the two legs at the HEAD end of the bed only, tilting the entire mattress. Document that the patient has been instructed in this technique and understands the distinction from using extra pillows.

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Drug-induced OH — 2 weeks
Repeat lying + standing BP after medication change. Symptomatic improvement? Further medication rationalisation if needed. STOPP review complete?
Neurogenic OH on treatment
Lying + standing BP during any specialist-guided drug titration. Weight (oedema), K⁺ monitoring (fludrocortisone). When stable: 3-monthly. Annual review: non-pharmacological measures in place? Neurological progression?
Addison's disease
Endocrine-led review: symptoms of under/over-replacement, BP and postural symptoms, weight, electrolytes, renin where relevant — serum cortisol is not a reliable adequacy test on oral hydrocortisone. Sick day rules reinforced at EVERY GP contact — this is the most important recurring safety message. IM kit not expired. MedicAlert bracelet worn. Sick day rules card carried.
Falls assessment
NICE NG249 pathway: lying + standing BP in all fallers, STOPP medication review, physiotherapy referral (gait + balance), vision check, home hazard assessment (OT); vitamin D, calcium or hip protectors only by fracture risk and local pathways.
Return immediately / 999
Collapse or loss of consciousness → 999 if unresponsive · Addisonian patient vomiting and unable to take oral steroids → IM hydrocortisone + 999 · New chest pain + hypotension → 999 · Sepsis features developing → 999
Same-day GP
Symptomatic OH despite optimised treatment → medication review + neurology if autonomic cause · Near-syncope or witnessed syncope → lying + standing BP + ECG same day; unexplained syncope needs cardiovascular risk assessment (CG109), not just a postural diagnosis · Worsening orthostatic symptoms on any new medication → same-day review
The Addison's sick day rules education is the highest-priority recurring safety message for any patient with adrenal insufficiency — adrenal crisis (acute cortisol deficiency during physical stress) is potentially fatal and almost entirely preventable with appropriate dose adjustment. The key failure mode: patients forget their sick day rules, or their family/partner does not know about them, and when the patient becomes too unwell to take oral steroids or loses consciousness, no one gives the IM injection. Prevention: (1) teach the rules at EVERY primary care encounter (not just at diagnosis); (2) ensure a family member or carer is trained in IM hydrocortisone administration; (3) check at each annual review that the IM kit is in date and the patient is carrying their emergency card; (4) add 'Addisonian — sick day rules education given' as a coded alert in the patient record to ensure every clinician who sees the patient reinforces this. Adrenal crises remain common and are largely preventable with good sick-day education.
