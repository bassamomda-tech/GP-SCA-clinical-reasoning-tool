1
Safety

Red Flags — Markedly Elevated Markers & Urgent Malignancy

Core principle Tumour markers rarely diagnose cancer in isolation. In primary care, use markers only where there is a defined clinical indication and an agreed pathway; symptoms, examination and appropriate imaging or FIT remain central.
Colorectal symptoms (e.g. change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, weight loss) — with or without a raised CEA CEA is not a diagnostic test for colorectal cancer and is not an NG12 referral trigger; do not use an isolated raised CEA to decide whether to refer. → Follow the NICE FIT pathway: refer urgently if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g faeces; refer directly without FIT for a rectal or anal mass or anal ulceration. Do not delay referral because FIT is negative or unavailable when clinical concern remains; use the current Greater Manchester colorectal pathway.
Ascites or unexplained pelvic/abdominal mass; or CA125 at or above the NICE age-specific threshold with an ultrasound suggesting ovarian cancer Possible ovarian cancer. → Suspected cancer pathway referral; do not delay for CA125 when there is ascites or a mass. RMI I is a secondary-care risk-stratification tool after ultrasound (NICE CG122), not a GP referral calculation.
PSA above the NICE age-specific threshold, or a prostate that feels malignant on DRE — especially with bone pain Possible prostate cancer (bone pain raises concern for metastases). → Urology suspected cancer pathway referral; a malignant-feeling prostate is referred regardless of PSA. MRI, biopsy and staging imaging are decided by the urology pathway (NICE NG131) — do not arrange a bone scan solely because PSA is above 10 ng/mL.
New liver lesion, suspicious ultrasound, progressive AFP rise or unexplained deterioration in chronic liver disease Possible hepatocellular carcinoma. → Urgent discussion with hepatology/hepatobiliary services via the local liver pathway; cross-sectional imaging is arranged through that pathway, not as an automatic GP step. AFP ≥400 ng/mL is not a standalone national suspected-cancer referral threshold. A normal AFP does not exclude HCC and a raised AFP alone does not establish it (AFP also rises in active hepatitis, hepatic regeneration, pregnancy and germ-cell tumours).
Irregular vaginal bleeding with a positive pregnancy test and suggestive ultrasound, or persistent hCG elevation after a pregnancy event Possible gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD — molar pregnancy, choriocarcinoma). → Exclude ectopic pregnancy and assess haemodynamic stability; contact the local early pregnancy/gynaecology service or GTD centre via the current regional arrangements. Do not interpret a single high hCG without pregnancy context. All diagnosed GTD should be registered with a GTD centre (RCOG Green-top Guideline 38).
Possible haematological malignancy: unexplained lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, fever, night sweats, weight loss, bruising, bleeding, recurrent infections or bone pain Assess clinically; request FBC and blood film when leukaemia is possible (NICE NG12: very urgent, within 48 hours). An abnormal film, blasts, severe cytopenias or an acutely unwell patient → urgent same-day specialist discussion. Refer persistent unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly via the local suspected cancer pathway. LDH is a non-specific marker of tissue injury and tumour burden and should not be used alone to determine referral urgency; it does not diagnose tumour lysis in an untreated patient.
Tumour markers are among the most misused tests in primary care — they are frequently requested for screening in asymptomatic patients (where they have poor positive predictive value) and their elevated results cause significant patient anxiety and cascade investigations. The fundamental principle that GPs must understand: no tumour marker is sufficiently sensitive and specific to be used as a standalone screening test in asymptomatic patients. The positive predictive value of any tumour marker depends critically on the pre-test probability: a PSA of 4 ng/ml in a 40-year-old with no symptoms has very different implications than the same result in a 70-year-old with nocturia and DRE abnormality. The two appropriate contexts for tumour marker use in primary care are: (1) investigation of symptomatic patients where a specific malignancy is suspected (CA125 for bloating in a post-menopausal woman), and (2) monitoring known malignancy for recurrence (CEA for colorectal cancer surveillance). The single exception to the no-screening rule is PSA for prostate cancer — UK primary care has an informed decision-making framework (Prostate Cancer Risk Management Programme) for PSA in asymptomatic men over 50 who request it after receiving information about the benefits and limitations.

Common Cancer Markers — Normal Ranges & Causes of Elevation

PSA (Prostate Specific Antigen)
Units: ng/ml. Age-specific referral thresholds for men with possible prostate-cancer symptoms (NICE NG12): below 40 clinical judgement; 40–49 above 2.5; 50–59 above 3.5; 60–69 above 4.5; 70–79 above 6.5; 80+ clinical judgement. Elevated by: prostate cancer (most important), benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH — gradual, proportional to volume), prostatitis (can cause very high transient PSA — recheck after treatment), urinary tract infection, recent ejaculation, vigorous exercise, urinary retention, catheterisation or urological instrumentation (consider when interpreting; follow local laboratory/PCRMP advice), DRE (take the blood sample before DRE where possible). Do not test during active UTI or suspected acute prostatitis. PSA velocity, PSA density and free-to-total PSA are secondary-care tools, not standalone primary-care referral rules.
CEA (Carcinoembryonic Antigen)
Reference range: per the local laboratory (higher in smokers). Elevated in malignancy: colorectal cancer (most useful — used for monitoring, not diagnosis), gastric, pancreatic, lung, breast, ovarian. Non-malignant elevation: smoking (common), COPD, IBD, liver disease, pancreatitis, benign bowel disease. CEA is not suitable for screening or diagnosis of colorectal cancer in primary care — for GI symptoms follow the NICE FIT pathway, not CEA. In known colorectal cancer, CEA surveillance follows the colorectal oncology plan (NICE NG151: at least every 6 months in the first 3 years after potentially curative treatment).
CA125
Reference range: per the local laboratory; NICE uses age-specific thresholds (below). Elevated in: ovarian cancer (a normal CA125 does not exclude it, particularly in early-stage disease). Non-malignant: menstruation, pregnancy, endometriosis, fibroids, PID, peritoneal inflammation, liver disease, heart failure. Aged 40 or over with persistent or frequent symptoms: CA125 at or above the NICE age-specific threshold (40–49 ≥35; 50–59 ≥31; 60–69 ≥24; 70–79 ≥25; 80+ ≥31 IU/mL) → urgent direct-access ultrasound of abdomen and pelvis. Aged 39 or under: do not use CA125 in isolation. RMI I is calculated in secondary care after ultrasound (NICE CG122).
AFP (Alpha-fetoprotein)
Reference range: per the local laboratory. Elevated in: hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), germ cell tumours (non-seminomatous testicular cancer — AFP + beta-hCG). Non-malignant: pregnancy, active hepatitis, liver regeneration. A normal AFP does not exclude HCC and a raised AFP alone does not establish it; do not use AFP as a general screening or isolated diagnostic test. HCC surveillance via the hepatology programme: cirrhosis without hepatitis B — 6-monthly ultrasound ± AFP (NICE NG50); chronic hepatitis B with significant fibrosis or cirrhosis — 6-monthly ultrasound and AFP, and selected others by age, family history and HBV DNA (NICE CG165).
Other markers
Not routinely requested in primary care; use only under specialist direction: CA19-9: pancreatic/biliary — rises with biliary obstruction, cholangitis, pancreatitis and other benign conditions, and some pancreatic cancers do not produce it; interpret only with imaging and specialist assessment, not as a standalone test for non-specific abdominal symptoms. CA15-3: breast cancer monitoring (not screening). Beta-hCG: trophoblastic disease, germ cell tumours. Calcitonin: medullary thyroid cancer. Thyroglobulin: only in specialist follow-up after differentiated thyroid cancer treatment, with anti-thyroglobulin antibodies and assay method recorded. Chromogranin A: neuroendocrine tumours — unsuitable as a general NET screening test (raised by proton-pump inhibitors, renal impairment and other conditions). S100/LDH: melanoma — not for diagnosis. CYFRA 21-1 / NSE: lung cancer (not routinely used in primary care).
Markers with a primary-care role
PSA — men with possible prostate-cancer symptoms, or informed individual testing under the PCRMP · CA125 — persistent/frequent symptoms suggestive of ovarian cancer, using NICE age thresholds · AFP — selected HCC surveillance pathways, usually under hepatology/local protocol · hCG — pregnancy-related assessment, suspected GTD or germ-cell tumour pathway · FIT — symptomatic colorectal assessment per NG12/local pathway
Not for routine cancer diagnosis in primary care
CEA · CA19-9 · CA15-3 · chromogranin A · NSE · CYFRA 21-1 · S100/LDH for melanoma diagnosis · isolated LDH for lymphoma/leukaemia · isolated thyroglobulin outside specialist follow-up
The PSA controversy in UK primary care is one of the most important clinical ethics issues in cancer screening — the NHS does not have a national prostate cancer screening programme (unlike bowel, breast, and cervical cancer) because the evidence for PSA screening reducing prostate cancer mortality is contested (screening trials such as ERSPC suggest a modest reduction in prostate cancer mortality, while ProtecT found similar prostate-cancer survival with active monitoring, surgery and radiotherapy, suggesting many PSA-detected cancers are low-grade and non-lethal). The current UK position: GPs should follow the Prostate Cancer Risk Management Programme (PCRMP) — any man over 50 who asks about PSA testing should receive balanced information about: (1) the benefits (detecting cancer early, potentially curative treatment), (2) the limitations (false positives causing unnecessary biopsies with complications, overdiagnosis of slow-growing cancers that would never have caused symptoms, PSA elevated by benign conditions), and (3) the uncertainties (whether PSA screening genuinely saves lives in the UK population). After receiving this information, if the man wants a PSA test, the GP should arrange it. GPs should never refuse a PSA test to a man over 50 who has been appropriately counselled and requests it.

PSA Interpretation in Practice

When to measure PSA
Possible prostate-cancer symptoms (NICE NG12): consider PSA and DRE in men with lower urinary tract symptoms (hesitancy, poor stream, frequency, urgency, nocturia), erectile dysfunction or visible haematuria. Asymptomatic men >50 who request testing after informed discussion (PCRMP — distinct from NG12 symptom-based referral; see the current PCRMP information). Men with family history (first-degree relative with prostate cancer age <65, or BRCA2 mutation) → offer from age 40. Before testing, explain that PSA is not specific for cancer and that an abnormal result may lead to MRI, biopsy or follow-up; consider age, ethnicity, family history, BRCA2 status, comorbidity and life expectancy. Do not test during active UTI or suspected acute prostatitis; consider recent ejaculation, vigorous exercise, urinary retention, catheterisation and urological instrumentation when interpreting (follow local laboratory/PCRMP advice).
PSA result interpretation
PSA within the age-specific range: explain and safety-net; reassess if symptoms persist or progress. PSA above the age-specific range (NICE NG12: 40–49 >2.5; 50–59 >3.5; 60–69 >4.5; 70–79 >6.5) → suspected cancer pathway referral; repeat first only if UTI/prostatitis or pre-test conditions may have raised it. Hard/irregular prostate on DRE any PSA → suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12). Do not use PSA velocity, PSA density or free-to-total PSA as standalone primary-care referral rules. After referral, MRI and biopsy decisions belong to the urology pathway (NICE NG131).
After prostate cancer treatment
After radical prostatectomy, PSA should usually become undetectable or very low. Biochemical recurrence definitions and actions depend on the treatment and the specialist protocol. Follow-up follows the written urology/oncology plan — annual DRE is not a universal GP requirement.
PSA in BPH vs cancer
BPH: PSA often modestly raised, normal DRE (smooth, symmetrically enlarged), LUTS. Cancer: PSA may be any level; hard/nodular/asymmetric DRE in palpable disease. LUTS and PSA cannot reliably separate them — use the NICE age-specific thresholds and DRE; PSA density and velocity are secondary-care tools.
The suspected cancer pathway referral for prostate cancer is straightforward for most GPs but the borderline PSA range (4–10 ng/ml with a normal DRE) is an area where clinical judgement and good documentation matter most — the probability of cancer at these levels varies with age, ethnicity, family history and prostate size. The appropriate response: if there are other clinical features (weight loss, bone pain, hard prostate, rising PSA velocity) → suspected cancer pathway referral. NICE NG12 now uses age-specific thresholds: a PSA above the range for age is a suspected cancer pathway referral for MRI-directed assessment; repeat first only if infection or pre-test conditions (recent ejaculation or vigorous exercise, active UTI or prostatitis, instrumentation) may have raised it. The MRI prostate (mpMRI) is now the standard first investigation after referral for suspected prostate cancer in the UK — it stratifies patients by PIRADS score (1–5) and guides targeted biopsy, reducing detection of clinically insignificant low-grade cancer and reducing unnecessary biopsies. The GP's referral letter should include: PSA value and date, DRE findings, LUTS severity, family history, and medication history (5-alpha-reductase inhibitors such as finasteride lower PSA — state this in the referral).

CA125 — Suspected Ovarian Cancer in Primary Care

Suspected ovarian cancer in primary care (NICE NG12)
In women, trans men and non-binary people with female reproductive organs: ascites or an unexplained pelvic/abdominal mass → suspected cancer pathway referral; do not delay for CA125 · aged 39 or under with persistent or frequent symptoms suggestive of ovarian cancer → do not use CA125 in isolation; consider urgent ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis · aged 40 or over → measure CA125 and arrange urgent ultrasound if at or above the age-specific threshold (40–49 ≥35; 50–59 ≥31; 60–69 ≥24; 70–79 ≥25; 80+ ≥31 IU/mL) · ultrasound suggests ovarian cancer → suspected cancer pathway referral · CA125 below threshold or ultrasound normal → investigate other causes and safety-net persistent or worsening symptoms
Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI — secondary care)
RMI = U × M × CA125. U = USS score (0 = no pathology; 1 = one feature; 3 = two or more features: multilocular cyst, solid areas, bilateral, ascites, intraabdominal metastases). M = menopausal status (1 = premenopausal; 3 = postmenopausal). CA125 = serum value in IU/ml. RMI I is principally a secondary-care risk-stratification tool after ultrasound: NICE CG122 recommends calculating it after ultrasound and referring women with an RMI I of 250 or more to a specialist multidisciplinary team. It is not a routine GP referral calculation; a low RMI does not safely exclude ovarian cancer, and a normal CA125 or low RMI must not override persistent symptoms, ascites, a pelvic/abdominal mass or concerning ultrasound findings.
When CA125 is NOT elevated in ovarian cancer
A proportion of early-stage ovarian cancers have CA125 within the normal range. Mucinous tumours (CA125 less sensitive — CA19-9 or CEA may be elevated instead). Borderline ovarian tumours. Therefore: a normal CA125 does not override persistent symptoms, ascites, a mass or a concerning ultrasound — investigate other causes and safety-net, with a low threshold for ultrasound.
CA125 in premenopausal women
Non-malignant elevations are very common in premenopausal women — menstruation, endometriosis, PID, uterine fibroids, early pregnancy. CA125 is much less useful as a cancer marker in premenopausal women — a mildly elevated CA125 in a young woman with pelvic pain often reflects benign disease such as endometriosis. However: do not dismiss it — under 40, CA125 is not used in isolation; consider urgent ultrasound for persistent symptoms and, if normal, investigate alternative causes and safety-net.
CA19-9 for pancreatic cancer
Not a screening test and not a standalone primary-care test for non-specific abdominal symptoms. CA19-9 may rise with biliary obstruction, cholangitis, pancreatitis, liver disease and other benign conditions; some pancreatic cancers do not produce CA19-9 (Lewis antigen-negative). Interpret only alongside appropriate imaging and specialist assessment; suspected pancreatic cancer is referred on NG12 symptom and imaging criteria.
RMI I (Jacobs et al., 1990) combines ultrasound features, menopausal status and CA125, and is used in secondary care after ultrasound to decide MDT referral (NICE CG122) — it is not the central primary-care pathway. In primary care, NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) sets the route: CA125 at or above the age-specific threshold → urgent direct-access USS → suspected cancer pathway referral if the USS suggests ovarian cancer; ascites or a pelvic/abdominal mass on examination → direct suspected cancer pathway referral. The critical point: once CA125 is raised, book the USS at once — do not let the steps drift.

Referral Pathways

Same-day / very urgent
Pregnancy with bleeding: exclude ectopic pregnancy and assess haemodynamic stability · suspected GTD (irregular bleeding, positive pregnancy test, suggestive ultrasound, or persistent hCG after a pregnancy event) → local early pregnancy/gynaecology service or GTD centre · new liver lesion or suspicious ultrasound in chronic liver disease → urgent hepatology/hepatobiliary discussion via the local liver pathway · abnormal blood film, blasts, severe cytopenias or acutely unwell → same-day haematology discussion
Suspected cancer
PSA above the NG12 age-specific threshold in a man with possible symptoms · malignant-feeling prostate on DRE, any PSA · ascites or pelvic/abdominal mass · CA125 at or above the NG12 age-specific threshold + ultrasound suggesting ovarian cancer · colorectal symptoms with FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g, or a rectal/anal mass or anal ulceration · persistent unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly (local pathway). Isolated CEA, AFP, CA19-9 or LDH values are not referral criteria.
Urology (urgent outpatient)
PSA possibly raised by infection or other confounders: repeat once these have resolved, per local laboratory/PCRMP advice — refer if still above the NG12 age-specific range
Gynaecology
Persistent symptoms with a non-suggestive ultrasound, or a suspected benign gynaecological cause (e.g. endometriosis), per local pathway · RMI I calculation and MDT referral happen in secondary care (NICE CG122)
Hepatology / hepatobiliary
Known cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B — 6-monthly ultrasound ± AFP via the hepatology surveillance programme (NG50, CG165) · progressive AFP rise, new lesion or unexplained deterioration → urgent hepatology discussion
GP role in surveillance
Surveillance must follow the written plan from the treating oncology/urology/gynaecology service. The GP checks that planned investigations occur, records results and trends, and contacts the responsible specialist team if results are abnormal or the patient is lost to follow-up.
The GTD referral to a designated centre is an RCOG mandate — gestational trophoblastic disease (hydatidiform moles, invasive moles, choriocarcinoma) is a rare but highly treatable condition that requires specialist management and registration with a GTD centre under the current regional arrangements; this centralised model gives high cure rates with appropriate treatment. hCG monitoring after molar pregnancy is managed by the GTD centres. GPs must refer any suspected molar pregnancy (irregular vaginal bleeding, a positive pregnancy test and suggestive ultrasound findings, or persistent hCG after a pregnancy event), after excluding ectopic pregnancy and assessing haemodynamic stability, to the GTD centre BEFORE surgical evacuation where possible, or immediately after, to register the patient in the national surveillance programme.

Treat

Responding to Raised Markers in Primary Care

PSA above the age-specific threshold — pending urology referral
Exclude prostatitis (if symptoms present: antibiotic for 14 days then review for acute prostatitis (NICE NG110) — see dysuria algorithm). If pre-test conditions were not met: repeat PSA under optimal conditions (after infection has resolved and confounders such as recent ejaculation or vigorous exercise are avoided — per local laboratory/PCRMP advice). If confirmed elevated: suspected cancer pathway referral. In the interim: alpha-blockers (tamsulosin 400 mcg OD) for LUTS while awaiting urology — symptom management while investigation ongoing.
Elevated CEA in known CRC — monitoring
CEA surveillance follows the colorectal oncology plan (NICE NG151: CEA at least every 6 months and at least two CT scans of chest, abdomen and pelvis in the first 3 years after potentially curative treatment). Interpret a rising result with the trend, smoking status, liver disease, imaging and symptoms, and contact the colorectal oncology/surgical team — there is no universal percentage-rise rule unless it is part of the local oncology protocol.
Raised CA125 with a normal ultrasound
Investigate other causes (e.g. endometriosis, fibroids, menstruation, liver disease, heart failure) and safety-net persistent or worsening symptoms. Under 40, CA125 is not used in isolation. Manage a suspected benign gynaecological cause per local pathway (gynaecology referral if refractory). New ascites, a mass, worsening symptoms or concerning ultrasound features → suspected cancer pathway referral.
AFP monitoring in chronic liver disease
HCC surveillance follows the local hepatology programme: cirrhosis without hepatitis B — 6-monthly ultrasound ± AFP (NICE NG50); chronic hepatitis B with significant fibrosis or cirrhosis — 6-monthly ultrasound and AFP, and selected patients without significant fibrosis by age, family history and HBV DNA (NICE CG165). A new liver lesion, suspicious ultrasound, progressive AFP rise or unexplained deterioration → urgent hepatology/hepatobiliary discussion; cross-sectional imaging via the local liver pathway. Hepatitis C: HCC risk highest in cirrhosis — surveillance per hepatology. Post-antiviral treatment for hepatitis C: HCC risk persists even in sustained virological remission (SVR) if cirrhosis was present.
The AFP + USS liver HCC surveillance programme is recommended in NICE NG50 (cirrhosis, 2016) and NICE CG165 (hepatitis B, 2013), and by EASL (international), for people with cirrhosis and selected people with chronic hepatitis B — the rationale is that HCC detected early by surveillance is more often curable than HCC detected symptomatically. The surveillance interval is 6 months. There is no national AFP threshold for referral: a progressive AFP rise, new lesion or suspicious ultrasound should prompt urgent hepatology discussion, with imaging through the local liver pathway. GPs managing patients with known cirrhosis (from alcohol, NAFLD, hepatitis B/C) should ensure that 6-monthly ultrasound surveillance (± AFP per protocol) is happening — it is frequently omitted in patients not engaged with hepatology. Adding it to the chronic liver disease template in the practice clinical record system ensures regular checking.

Treat

Monitoring Markers in Known Malignancy

Colorectal cancer surveillance (NICE NG151)
Follow the written plan from the colorectal team: CEA at least every 6 months and a minimum of 2 CT scans of chest/abdomen/pelvis in the first 3 years (NICE NG151). Colonoscopy per the specialist plan. Rising CEA: interpret with the trend, smoking, liver disease, imaging and symptoms, and contact colorectal oncology/surgery.
Prostate cancer PSA monitoring
PSA follow-up schedule, recurrence definitions and actions depend on the treatment (radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy, active surveillance) and the specialist protocol (NICE NG131). After radical prostatectomy PSA should usually become undetectable or very low. Follow the written urology/oncology plan; any rise outside it → discuss with urology.
Thyroglobulin (differentiated thyroid cancer)
Specialist follow-up only: thyroglobulin (Tg) interpreted with anti-Tg antibodies and assay method recorded, per the thyroid oncology plan. Undetectable Tg + undetectable anti-Tg antibodies = remission. Rising Tg: recurrence → neck USS + specialist oncology. Anti-Tg antibodies interfere with Tg assay (falsely low Tg) — follow antibody titres as surrogate in this scenario.
Ovarian cancer CA125 monitoring
Post-treatment CA125 testing follows the gynaecological oncology plan; relapse definitions are specialist criteria. However: early detection of CA125 relapse and early treatment does not improve survival (MRC OV05/EORTC 55955 trial — showed treating on CA125 alone before clinical relapse did not benefit patients). Use CA125 in context of symptoms, not to trigger premature retreatment.
The MRC OV05/EORTC 55955 trial finding for ovarian cancer CA125 monitoring is one of the most important counter-intuitive results in oncology — the trial showed that treating ovarian cancer recurrence based solely on CA125 rise (before clinical symptoms) did not improve overall survival, quality of life, or any patient outcome compared to waiting for clinical recurrence. This means that routine CA125 monitoring for ovarian cancer recurrence, while standard practice, should not automatically trigger chemotherapy at the first sign of CA125 rise — the decision to restart treatment should be based on symptoms, quality of life, and a full discussion between the oncologist and the patient. GPs monitoring CA125 post-ovarian cancer treatment should: record the result, communicate it to the oncology team, but not alarm patients unnecessarily about a modest rise in CA125 — the decision to treat is a specialist oncology discussion, not an automatic protocol.

Lifestyle

Informed Decision-Making, Emotional Impact & Surveillance Education

PSA informed consent conversation When a man requests PSA testing (PCRMP — separate from NG12 symptom-based referral): consider age, ethnicity, family history, BRCA2 status, comorbidity and life expectancy, and explain before testing: what PSA measures (PSA is produced by normal and cancerous prostate tissue — elevated levels can indicate cancer but also prostate enlargement and inflammation), what an elevated result means (further investigations including MRI and possible biopsy — not automatic cancer diagnosis), and what a normal result means (reassurance but cannot exclude all cancers). The Prostate Cancer UK "Is PSA testing right for me?" patient decision aid is excellent for supporting this conversation.
Anxiety management around cancer markers A raised tumour marker causes significant patient anxiety disproportionate to the clinical significance (especially mildly elevated PSA, or CA125 in a pre-menopausal woman with endometriosis). Address directly: "An elevated PSA does not mean cancer — it means we need to investigate further." Provide a clear timeline for next steps. PHQ-4 at follow-up if anxiety persists. NHS Talking Therapies referral for health anxiety if repeated attendances.
Cancer surveillance adherence Post-treatment cancer patients frequently disengage from follow-up as time passes. GP role: check that the investigations in the specialist's written surveillance plan occur, record results and trends, and contact the responsible team if results are abnormal or the patient is lost to follow-up. Add surveillance tests to the chronic disease code in clinical records. Letter or SNOMED alert for annual review.
Lifestyle in cancer survivors Physical activity: is associated with lower recurrence risk in colorectal, breast and prostate cancer. Target 150 min/week. Weight management: obesity increases cancer recurrence risk for colorectal, breast, and endometrial cancers. Mediterranean diet: strongest dietary evidence for cancer prevention (colorectal especially).
Family history and hereditary cancer BRCA1/2 mutation: breast, ovarian, pancreatic, prostate cancer risk. Lynch syndrome (MLH1, MSH2): colorectal, endometrial cancer. Take a three-generation cancer history, including tumour type, age at diagnosis, bilateral/multiple cancers, ancestry and known pathogenic variants. Refer according to the current NHS regional genetics referral criteria — this guide does not replace the local genetics pathway. Genetic testing + cascade testing for family members. Risk-reducing surgery in BRCA carriers requires specialist genetic counselling and gynaecological oncology input.
Smoking and cancer markers Smoking elevates CEA (common). Document smoking status when interpreting CEA. Smoking increases risk of: lung, bladder, colorectal, pancreatic, oropharyngeal, oesophageal, renal, and cervical cancers. Smoking cessation = most effective single cancer prevention intervention.
Alcohol and cancer Alcohol is linked to: colorectal, breast, liver, oropharyngeal, oesophageal cancers. No safe threshold for alcohol and cancer risk (linear relationship). AUDIT-C at every cancer surveillance consultation. Advise minimum alcohol use (NHS guidelines <14 units/week as a maximum, not a target).
Bowel cancer screening (NHS FIT) NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme: home FIT kits are posted to eligible adults — the age range, interval and threshold are set by the current NHS England programme (check the programme rather than relying on fixed values). A result at or above the screening threshold → specialist screening practitioner, usually colonoscopy. The screening threshold is separate from the symptomatic FIT threshold of ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE NG12); a negative FIT does not exclude colorectal cancer and does not override ongoing clinical concern. GPs should: check screening status at every consultation with relevant patients, encourage participation, and investigate symptoms regardless of screening status (screening does not replace clinical investigation).
The NHS bowel cancer screening programme with FIT (faecal immunochemical test) is one of the most cost-effective cancer screening interventions in medicine — the programme detects colorectal cancers at an earlier stage than they would otherwise be detected, reducing mortality. Participation is incomplete — GPs can significantly improve this by: actively encouraging eligible non-participants at every consultation, removing barriers (explaining the test is simple and does not require dietary restriction), and following up missed invitations. The screening threshold is far higher than the 10 µg Hb/g used for symptomatic patients (NICE NG12 / HTG690) — it is important to understand that a negative FIT does not exclude colorectal cancer in a symptomatic patient. Symptomatic patients with lower GI symptoms should be investigated clinically (quantitative FIT + clinical assessment) regardless of the result of their population screening FIT.

Safety

Follow-Up, Documentation & Safety-Netting

PSA monitoring (post-diagnosis, GP shared care)
Follow the written urology/oncology surveillance plan — annual PSA and annual DRE are not universal GP requirements (after prostatectomy, PSA should usually be undetectable or very low; a rise outside the plan → discuss with urology). Record PSA result, date, trend. Communicate rising PSA to urology promptly.
CEA monitoring (post-colorectal cancer)
Per the colorectal oncology plan (NICE NG151: CEA at least every 6 months in the first 3 years). A rising CEA → contact the colorectal team; there is no universal percentage-rise rule. Document in oncological summary. Ensure patient attending for tests.
CA125 monitoring (post-ovarian cancer)
Record each result and trend. Communicate to oncology team. Do not trigger anxiety or retreatment solely on CA125 rise without oncology input.
Unexpected raised marker — documentation
For every abnormal result, document: (1) the result, units and laboratory reference range; (2) relevant symptoms and examination; (3) possible benign causes and confounders; (4) action taken and expected timescale; (5) referral destination and urgency; (6) who is responsible for reviewing the result; (7) explicit patient advice about worsening or persistent symptoms; (8) the contingency plan if the patient does not attend or the result is not returned.
Suspected cancer / Same-day urgency
PSA above the NG12 age-specific threshold with possible symptoms, or a malignant-feeling prostate → urology suspected cancer pathway referral · ascites or a pelvic/abdominal mass, or CA125 at or above the NG12 age-specific threshold + ultrasound suggesting ovarian cancer → suspected cancer pathway referral · new liver lesion, suspicious ultrasound or progressive AFP rise in chronic liver disease → urgent hepatology discussion · colorectal symptoms → FIT, referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g (direct referral for a rectal/anal mass or anal ulceration) · abnormal blood film, blasts, severe cytopenias or acutely unwell → same-day haematology discussion
Within 2 weeks
PSA above the age-specific range on a repeat taken after infection or pre-test factors → urology suspected cancer pathway referral · CA125 rising in known post-treatment ovarian cancer → contact oncology · AFP rising trend in HBsAg-positive patient → urgent hepatology discussion
The medico-legal documentation principle for raised cancer markers is essential — a GP who identifies a raised CA125, PSA, AFP, or CEA and fails to act on it, or acts but does not document the action, is at significant medico-legal risk if a cancer is subsequently diagnosed at a late stage. The key documentation requirements: (1) record the abnormal result with date; (2) document the differential diagnosis considered and what prompted the test; (3) document the action taken (referral made, repeat test arranged, patient counselled); (4) document safety-netting given to the patient (what symptoms to return with, timeframe for review); and (5) document that the referral outcome was received and reviewed. A common error is making a suspected cancer pathway referral but not checking whether the patient was seen and the outcome received — the GP has a continuing duty of care to ensure the referral was acted upon and the result reviewed. Electronic clinical systems should flag outstanding referral outcomes for review at a defined interval (e.g., 4 weeks).
Educational use only. Tumour markers rarely diagnose cancer in isolation; this guide does not replace NG12 symptom-based referral, the local Greater Manchester suspected-cancer and FIT pathways, or specialist surveillance plans. Review at least every 6–12 months because NG12 thresholds and cancer pathways change.
