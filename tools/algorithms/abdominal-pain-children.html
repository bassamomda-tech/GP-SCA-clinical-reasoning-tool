1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude surgical emergencies and serious diagnoses first

Abdominal pain is extremely common in children and most is self-limiting, but surgical emergencies must not be missed. Do not delay suspected appendicitis, torsion or intussusception for primary-care investigations.
Peritonism Peritonism, a rigid abdomen, shock, severe systemic illness or suspected perforation → Immediate emergency hospital assessment; 999 if unstable, shocked, severely unwell or cannot be safely transported
Green / bile-stained vomiting Possible obstruction or volvulus (or intussusception) → stable child: Immediate paediatric surgical / ED assessment; shock, severe pain, marked distension, altered consciousness or rapid deterioration: 999
Suspected appendicitis Persistent or worsening localised pain, anorexia, fever, movement-related pain or peritonism → Same-day surgical — Paediatric Surgery Assessment
Intussusception features Age 6–18mo, colicky pain, pallor, drawing-up legs, "redcurrant jelly" stools → Same-day paediatric surgical / ED; 999 if shocked, lethargic or severely unwell
Testicular pain + abdominal pain Suspected testicular torsion → Immediate same-day surgical / urological assessment; do not delay for ultrasound or other investigations; 999 if unstable or cannot be transferred safely — always examine genitalia in boys with abdominal pain
Significant abdominal trauma Significant trauma, shock, peritonism, a seat-belt sign, concerning bruising or suspected non-accidental injury → Emergency hospital assessment + activate safeguarding procedures where appropriate
DKA presentation Vomiting + abdominal pain + polyuria/polydipsia → immediate capillary glucose and blood/urine ketones; suspected DKA → Emergency hospital assessment (999 if significantly unwell)
Sudden-onset severe pain Testicular or ovarian torsion, volvulus, intussusception, perforation, incarcerated hernia, renal or ovarian pathology, sickle-cell crisis where relevant, trauma; also pneumonia-referred pain → Urgent hospital assessment; 999 if unstable
Bloody diarrhoea + pain VTEC / intussusception / inflammatory bowel disease → Same-day ED
Fever + RIF + anorexia in girl Also consider ovarian torsion / pelvic inflammatory disease in post-pubertal girls → Same-day surgical / gynaecology

Appendicitis is the most common surgical emergency in children, with peak incidence at 10–12 years but can occur at any age. Perforation is more common in children than adults because children present later and diagnosis is more challenging due to atypical presentations.

Testicular torsion is time-critical — testicular viability falls with every hour of delay, so refer immediately for same-day surgical/urological assessment and do not wait for ultrasound. Boys with abdominal pain MUST have the scrotum examined. Torsion can present as lower abdominal pain with minimal testicular symptoms.

Non-accidental injury (NAI) should be considered in any child with unexplained abdominal injury, particularly with bruising on the abdominal wall, back, or buttocks. The "seat-belt" bruise pattern across the abdomen in a child not in a road traffic accident is a NAI red flag.

2
Diagnose

Characterise the pain — SOCRATES adapted for children

History is limited in young children — observe behaviour, parent report, and use age-appropriate pain tools (FLACC scale <5yr, FACES 3–7yr, NRS ≥8yr).
Site / radiation
Periumbilical → RIF migration = classic appendicitis. RIF alone = appendicitis, mesenteric adenitis, Crohn's. Generalised = constipation, functional, gastroenteritis. Epigastric = GORD, gastritis, pancreatitis. Loin to groin = renal colic (rare in children).
Onset
Sudden severe = torsion, perforation, volvulus. Gradual worsening over hours = appendicitis, UTI. Colicky (comes and goes) = constipation, intestinal obstruction, intussusception, biliary, renal. Chronic persistent = functional, IBD, coeliac.
Character
Colicky = visceral origin (bowel/ureter). Sharp / constant = peritoneal irritation. Burning epigastric = GORD / gastritis. Dull central + bloating = constipation / functional abdominal pain.
Associated symptoms
Anorexia (appendicitis — almost universal). Vomiting (surgical, GE, obstruction). Diarrhoea (GE, IBD, constipation overflow). Dysuria / frequency (UTI). Weight loss (IBD, coeliac, malignancy).
Duration
<24hrs + worsening = urgent surgical assessment. 24–72hrs = appendicitis in evolution if localising to RIF. Chronic (>4 weeks recurrent) = functional abdominal pain, IBD, coeliac, constipation.
Relieving / exacerbating
Better after defaecation = constipation / functional. Worse with movement = peritonism. Food-related = GORD, gastroparesis. School-related = functional, anxiety. Worse with periods = dysmenorrhoea / endometriosis (adolescent girls).

The classic periumbilical → RIF migration of appendicitis pain is often absent in children — atypical presentations are common and include generalised pain, diarrhoea, and vomiting. Appendicitis is primarily a clinical diagnosis. The Paediatric Appendicitis Score (PAS) is an adjunct within a local pathway, not a diagnostic test — scores do not replace serial clinical examination or surgical assessment.

Anorexia is common in appendicitis and is one of the most useful discriminating features — a hungry child with abdominal pain is much less likely to have appendicitis than an anorexic one. Always ask "Has your child been eating normally?" and "Did they want their usual favourite food today?"

3
Diagnose

Age-based differential diagnosis — most likely diagnoses by age group

0–3 months
Infantile colic (diagnosis of exclusion). Constipation. Intussusception (age 3mo+). Testicular torsion in boys. Incarcerated hernia. NAI. Always rule out organic causes before diagnosing colic; abdominal pain under 3 months where a serious cause cannot be confidently excluded → same-day paediatric assessment.
3–12 months
Intussusception (peak 6–18mo). Hernia. Colic if <3–4mo. Gastroenteritis. UTI. Constipation.
1–5 years
Constipation (most common at all ages), mesenteric adenitis (viral), UTI, gastroenteritis, intussusception (up to 5yr), appendicitis (less common <5yr but severe), trauma. Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (HSP): purpura + joint pain + abdominal pain.
5–12 years
Appendicitis (peak 10–12yr), functional abdominal pain / FAP (most common cause of recurrent pain), constipation, mesenteric adenitis, IBD (Crohn's/UC), coeliac, gastritis, UTI, renal colic. In girls: ovarian cyst, mittelschmerz, gynaecological.
Functional abdominal pain
Rome IV: recurrent pain at least 4 times per month, for at least 2 months, insufficient criteria for another functional GI disorder, and — after appropriate clinical evaluation — no better explanation from another condition. Associated with anxiety, school stress, family conflict. A positive diagnosis, but it requires structured assessment, growth review and consideration of alarm features — not a label after a brief consultation. Rome IV allows selective investigation rather than exhaustive testing. Alarm features: persistent focal RUQ or RLQ pain, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, nocturnal diarrhoea, weight loss or faltering growth, delayed puberty, fever, arthritis, perianal disease, family history of IBD or coeliac disease, abnormal examination.
Mesenteric adenitis
Viral illness + RIF pain + lymphadenopathy on USS. Diagnose only after important surgical diagnoses have been considered. Self-limiting. Managed with analgesia and reassurance. Can mimic appendicitis — USS helps distinguish but does not always.

Functional abdominal pain (FAP) / centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS) is the most common cause of recurrent abdominal pain in school-age children, but not all recurrent pain is functional. It is not a diagnosis of exclusion after exhaustive negative investigations — after structured assessment, growth review and checking for alarm features, making a positive diagnosis with confidence and communicating it well to families prevents unnecessary tests and builds therapeutic alliance.

HSP (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura / IgA vasculitis) must not be missed — it presents as colicky abdominal pain (in 60%), arthritis (in 80%), and purpuric rash on buttocks and legs (100%). Renal involvement (haematuria, proteinuria) requires urinalysis monitoring over 6 months (UK Kidney Association). Any child with purpuric rash + abdominal pain = HSP until proven otherwise.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — systematic approach

General impression
Lying very still (peritonism) vs. writhing / rolling (colic). Pallor = significant pathology. Jaundice = hepatobiliary. Dehydration signs. Ill-looking = urgent. Smiling, playing with phone = reassuring for functional.
Abdomen — observation
Distension (obstruction, organomegaly, constipation). Scars (previous surgery — adhesions). Visible peristalsis (pyloric stenosis in infant). Bruising (NAI, trauma).
Abdomen — palpation
Start away from the pain. McBurney's point (2/3 from umbilicus to ASIS) = appendicitis. Rovsing's sign (LIF pressure → RIF pain) = peritoneal irritation. Rebound / guarding = peritonism. Hepatomegaly, splenomegaly. Faecal mass (constipation).
Genitalia (boys)
Examine scrotum in EVERY boy with abdominal pain — torsion can present as abdominal pain without obvious testicular symptoms. High-riding, horizontal testicle + pain = suspected torsion → immediate same-day surgical/urological assessment (999 if unstable); do not delay for ultrasound.
Genitalia (girls)
Consider pelvic pathology in post-pubertal girls. Adnexal tenderness, PID. Offer a confidential pregnancy test to post-menarchal patients where pregnancy is possible — ectopic pregnancy is a surgical emergency.
Hernial orifices
Groin, umbilicus, scars — examine for incarcerated hernia. Tender irreducible hernia = incarcerated/strangulated hernia → same-day surgical assessment; 999 if unstable or peritonitic.
Perianal / rectal
Inspect for fissures (constipation) and perianal disease (Crohn's). Digital rectal examination is not routinely required — only by a competent professional when clinically indicated (NICE CG99). Blood on nappy / underwear → further assessment.
Skin / joints
Purpuric rash (HSP), jaundice, pallor, clubbing (IBD), arthritis. Examine joints if HSP suspected.

The examination of the scrotum in boys with abdominal pain is life-altering if torsion is present. Testicular torsion is missed when the genitalia are not examined. A child with severe abdominal pain presenting at 2am who "just has a tummy ache" may have testicular torsion. Check every time.

Children with peritonism lie very still because movement exacerbates pain. Driving over a speed bump or bumpy car journey increases pain (Dunphy's sign). A child who arrives in the waiting room running around is unlikely to have peritonitis. Observe the child's behaviour before and during the consultation.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — targeted by diagnosis

Urine dipstick / MSU
When UTI is plausible Consider urine testing with dysuria, frequency, new bedwetting, malodorous/cloudy urine, haematuria, fever without a clear source, loin/suprapubic tenderness, vomiting, reduced intake, previous UTI or diagnostic uncertainty (NICE NG224). Not routinely needed aged 3 months+ when another infection clearly explains the presentation, unless the child remains unwell or there is uncertainty. Under 3 months with suspected UTI → urgent paediatric assessment and urine culture. Positive leukocyte esterase or nitrite → culture according to age and clinical status. Pyuria can occur with appendicitis but is not a reliable discriminator.
Blood glucose + urine ketones
Check capillary glucose and ketones in children with abdominal pain and vomiting when there is polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, dehydration, altered consciousness, deep/rapid breathing, recurrent unexplained vomiting or clinical concern about diabetes/metabolic disease. Low threshold in an ill child, but not mandatory in every mild viral illness.
Pregnancy test (urine hCG)
Offer a confidential pregnancy test to post-menarchal patients where pregnancy is possible — particularly with lower abdominal or pelvic pain, delayed or abnormal menstruation, vaginal bleeding, sexual activity, syncope or shoulder-tip pain. Record the urine test as positive or negative per the assay instructions. A positive test with pain, bleeding, syncope or haemodynamic instability → urgent gynaecological assessment; serum hCG and pelvic ultrasound are hospital investigations.
FBC + CRP
Appendicitis is primarily a clinical diagnosis. FBC, CRP, renal function and, where appropriate, group-and-save may support assessment in hospital but cannot exclude early disease; raised values are non-specific. Use scoring systems (e.g. Paediatric Appendicitis Score) only as adjuncts within a local pathway. Do not delay same-day referral for blood tests.
Stool calprotectin
Targeted — suspected IBD Consider in children with chronic GI symptoms where IBD is suspected and a local paediatric pathway supports testing (NICE DG11). Interpret with symptoms, growth, FBC, CRP, albumin and stool microbiology. Do not use a single cut-off to rule IBD in or out. Not a reassurance test in acute diarrhoea or recent infection, rectal bleeding, suspected surgical disease, marked weight loss or faltering growth, severe anaemia, a significant abdominal mass or peritonism. A normal result does not override strong clinical concern.
Coeliac serology (tTG IgA + total IgA)
Recurrent abdominal pain + any of: faltering growth, iron deficiency anaemia, fatigue, diarrhoea, family history. Total IgA and IgA tTG first (NICE NG20); IgG-based test if IgA deficient. Ensure the child is eating gluten regularly before testing; do not start a gluten-free diet before diagnosis is confirmed. Positive serology → paediatric gastroenterology; it is not diagnostic in primary care.
AXR
Not for diagnosis of idiopathic constipation — no routine abdominal X-ray or ultrasound (NICE CG99). Useful if suspected bowel obstruction (erect film — air-fluid levels, absent bowel gas). Hospital-initiated.
USS abdomen
Hospital-initiated. Generally first-line imaging for suspected appendicitis in children (limited by obesity / bowel gas), with MRI or CT in hospital if inconclusive and concern persists. Intussusception (target sign). Ovarian pathology. Imaging must not delay surgical referral in a clinically concerning child.

Faecal calprotectin is a neutrophil protein released during gut mucosal inflammation — raised in Crohn's and UC. NICE DG11 supports it as an option for children with suspected IBD within a quality-assured local paediatric pathway; it is a targeted test interpreted alongside symptoms, growth and blood results, not a stand-alone rule-in or rule-out or a reassurance test.

Normal blood results (FBC, CRP) do NOT rule out early appendicitis. Use serial examination and clinical judgement rather than a single score, laboratory threshold or ultrasound result. This is a high-stakes clinical decision. If you have a clinical gestalt that "something is wrong," refer even with normal bloods.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria

999
999 or emergency transfer: shock, severe systemic illness or reduced consciousness; peritonism with instability or suspected perforation; suspected testicular or ovarian torsion; suspected DKA with significant illness; severe obstruction/volvulus; severe intussusception features; severe haematemesis; ectopic pregnancy with haemodynamic compromise; severe abdominal trauma.
Same-day surgical
Suspected appendicitis; persistent focal right lower quadrant pain with fever or anorexia. Bilious vomiting in a stable child. Suspected intussusception. Age <5yr with significant abdominal pain (higher perforation risk, harder to assess). Incarcerated hernia. Severe bloody diarrhoea with abdominal pain. Do not delay for primary-care investigations.
Same-day paeds/ED
Unable to exclude surgical diagnosis. Significant dehydration. Pain uncontrolled. HSP with severe or persistent abdominal pain, GI bleeding, suspected intussusception, scrotal/testicular involvement, hypertension, nephritic or nephrotic syndrome, reduced renal function or significant persistent proteinuria. Age <3 months with abdominal pain where a serious cause cannot be confidently excluded. Raised glucose/ketones (suspected DKA → emergency if unwell).
Paeds outpatient (urgent)
Suspected IBD (alarm features ± raised calprotectin within the local paediatric pathway). Positive coeliac serology (paediatric gastroenterology). Recurrent unexplained pain >1 month not responding to simple measures. Weight loss + pain. Jaundice + pain.
Primary care manage
Only when the child is well: functional abdominal pain — after appropriate assessment. Confirmed uncomplicated constipation without red flags. Mild gastroenteritis. Mesenteric adenitis — only after important surgical diagnoses have been considered. Mild, self-limiting viral illness.
Safeguarding
Consider safeguarding if there are unexplained injuries (e.g. bruising over abdomen / back / buttocks), inconsistent or changing explanations, delay in seeking care, concerning caregiver–child interactions, fearfulness, or other information suggesting abuse or neglect. Follow local safeguarding procedures and discuss with the designated safeguarding lead. A parent expressing persistent concern should prompt careful reassessment and safety-netting, not be treated as a red flag for abuse.

Children under 5 with appendicitis have higher perforation rates than older children because: they cannot localise pain well, parents and clinicians attribute pain to constipation or GE, and the omentum is poorly developed so cannot contain perforation. Any child under 5 with significant unexplained abdominal pain should have a low threshold for same-day surgical assessment.

IBD (Crohn's disease) in children can present insidiously over months with abdominal pain, fatigue, and weight loss without obvious bowel symptoms. Perianal disease (fistulae, skin tags) is a recognised feature of Crohn's. Examine the perianal region and refer early — early treatment prevents bowel damage and growth failure.

7
Treat

Treatment — condition-specific management

Functional abdominal pain
Explanation + CBT / pain management First-line
Positive diagnosis after structured assessment. Validate pain as real. Explain brain-gut connection. CBT and gut-directed hypnotherapy can be considered (local availability varies); mindfulness. Low-FODMAP diets only specialist/dietitian-led. Medication is usually not required in primary care. Avoid excessive medical investigation. Do not routinely test for or eradicate H. pylori.
Constipation-related pain
Macrogol + toilet training
Positive diagnosis from history and examination (NICE CG99). Assess and treat faecal impaction before maintenance; macrogol 3350 with electrolytes first line for disimpaction and maintenance, age-specific dosing (see constipation algorithm). Improvement may take time. Do not diagnose constipation if red flags (abdominal distension with vomiting, faltering growth, constipation from the first weeks of life, delayed meconium) are present.
Persistent typical reflux symptoms
PPI (weight-banded dose per BNFC) — 4-week trial
Consider only for persistent, typical heartburn, retrosternal or epigastric pain after appropriate assessment (NICE NG1) — not for nonspecific abdominal pain or isolated regurgitation. Reassess response; refer if symptoms do not resolve or recur. Do not routinely test for or eradicate H. pylori in children with functional abdominal pain — testing is specialist-led for a clear indication such as suspected ulcer disease.
Analgesia — acute pain
Age/weight-based dosing per BNFC and local formulation guidance: paracetamol 15 mg/kg 4–6-hourly (maximum 4 doses in 24 hours; check the BNFC maximum daily dose) ± ibuprofen 5–10 mg/kg three times daily if age ≥3 months (maximum 30 mg/kg/day). Use lower doses or avoid with renal impairment or dehydration; avoid ibuprofen in significant dehydration, renal disease, GI bleeding or NSAID-sensitive asthma. Do NOT withhold analgesia pending surgical assessment — appropriate analgesia improves cooperation and examination quality, but reassessment remains necessary.
Mesenteric adenitis
Self-limiting — analgesia, fluids, reassurance. Duration 1–2 weeks. Follow-up if not improving to exclude missed appendicitis. Antibiotics not indicated (viral aetiology).
HSP management
Analgesia — paracetamol; avoid NSAIDs, or use only with specialist/renal advice, if there is renal involvement, dehydration, GI bleeding or hypertension. Urinalysis frequently over 6 months (e.g. weekly for the first 4–6 weeks, then monthly); BP at diagnosis and when renal involvement is suspected or present. Steroids are not routine primary-care treatment — severe abdominal pain or GI bleeding may need specialist-directed corticosteroids after intussusception and other surgical causes are excluded.
UTI with abdominal pain
Manage per NICE NG224: urine culture according to age and clinical status; antibiotic choice per local guidance with age/weight-specific BNFC dosing and contraindications checked; under 3 months → urgent paediatric assessment; upper UTI/pyelonephritis needs a different regimen. Reassess if symptoms do not settle.

The evidence strongly supports giving analgesia before surgical assessment — three RCTs and a Cochrane review (2011) found analgesia reduces distress without changing surgical outcomes. Withholding analgesia in a child with abdominal pain is poor practice, but signs must still be reassessed after it is given.

For functional abdominal pain, gut-directed hypnotherapy and CBT are more effective than medication in reducing pain episodes and school absenteeism. Medication is usually not required in primary care; antispasmodics (mebeverine, hyoscine) have weak evidence in children. Avoid unnecessary PPI prescribing — gut microbiome effects.

8
Lifestyle

Supportive measures — especially for functional abdominal pain

Normalise and validate Explain that functional pain is real, common, and not dangerous. Avoid catastrophising language ("nothing wrong"). Say instead: "Your gut is very sensitive — it's a real signal from a very sensitive nervous system." This reduces anxiety and pain amplification.
Maintain normal activities Support normal activity, including school attendance, while recognising individual circumstances. Agree a management plan with the family and, where helpful, the school.
Low-FODMAP diet Fermentable carbohydrates trigger IBS-type symptoms. Should only be specialist/dietitian-led. Not appropriate without dietitian support — nutritional risk in growing children.
Stress and anxiety management School bullying, academic pressure, family conflict are common triggers. Address with parents and school. Refer CAMHS if anxiety is significant. Mindfulness apps (Headspace for Kids, Calm) are useful adjuncts for children ≥8yr.
Regular eating pattern Skipping meals triggers pain in many children. Regular breakfast, lunch, and dinner. Avoid high-fat, high-spice, and carbonated drinks if symptomatic. Adequate fibre (fruits, vegetables, wholegrains).
Adequate hydration 6–8 cups fluid/day. Dehydration causes GI dysmotility and pain sensitisation. Water preferred. Limit fizzy drinks (carbonation causes bloating and pain).

School absenteeism is the most important functional outcome in paediatric chronic abdominal pain. Children who miss school develop educational deficits, social isolation, anxiety, and depression — all of which amplify pain. Supporting normal activity, including school, while recognising the child's individual circumstances is a central part of management.

The gut-brain axis explains functional abdominal pain mechanistically — visceral hypersensitivity, altered gut motility, and central sensitisation all contribute. Explaining this brain-gut connection using age-appropriate language (e.g., "your stomach has its own little brain that gets stressed when you're worried") has been shown to reduce parent catastrophising and improve treatment compliance.

9
Safety

Follow-up, Safety-Netting & Monitoring

Acute pain — first episode
Review within 24–48hrs if: suspected appendicitis not meeting 999 threshold, amber features, diagnostic uncertainty. Earlier if deteriorating.
Functional pain
Review 4–6 weeks — has the intervention (diet, CBT, school support) helped? Pain diary useful. Growth parameters at each visit. Red flags review.
IBD / coeliac follow-up
Once referred and diagnosed: GP role includes monitoring growth, nutrition, medication side effects (e.g., azathioprine monitoring: FBC, LFTs 3-monthly). Annual influenza and pneumococcal vaccination (immunosuppressed).
HSP monitoring
Frequent urinalysis (blood + protein) over 6 months — e.g. weekly for the first 4–6 weeks, then monthly (UK Kidney Association). BP at diagnosis and when renal involvement is suspected or present. Urgent paediatric/renal referral for hypertension, nephritic or nephrotic syndrome, reduced renal function or significant persistent proteinuria; same-day for severe or persistent abdominal pain, GI bleeding, suspected intussusception or scrotal involvement.
Call 999 if:
Pain suddenly much worse with the child severely unwell. Green vomit with the child unwell or deteriorating (otherwise same-day). Child becomes floppy / pale. Abdomen becomes hard / rigid. Severe difficulty breathing (pneumonia-referred abdominal pain).
Same-day if:
Persistent or worsening pain, or pain becoming localised. Fever developing or worsening. Anorexia persisting. Green vomiting in a stable child. New mass palpable. Urinary symptoms develop. A parent who remains concerned — reassess carefully rather than reassure.
Documentation key points
Record exact location of maximal tenderness, guarding, rebound, bowel sounds, genitalia exam (boys). Signed and timed. If referring for suspected appendicitis — document clinical features that prompted referral clearly.

Time-based safety-netting is critical for abdominal pain — "come back if it gets worse" is insufficient. Specify: "If the pain is getting worse, moving to one place, or your child stops eating, becomes feverish or more unwell, go straight to A&E — do not wait for a GP appointment." This specificity correlates with better decision-making by parents and earlier presentation for surgical assessment.

HSP nephritis develops weeks after the initial presentation and is the most serious complication. Frequent urinalysis over 6 months (weekly early, then monthly) with BP checks at diagnosis and when renal involvement is suspected is essential and is often a primary care responsibility. In practice this follow-up urinalysis and BP monitoring is usually led by the GP, so agree who is doing it at discharge.
