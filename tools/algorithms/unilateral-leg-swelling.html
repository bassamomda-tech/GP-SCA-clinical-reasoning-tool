1
Safety

Red Flags — The Limb- and Life-Threatening Causes First

One swollen leg is a DVT until excluded — and the conditions that kill or cost a limb (PE, phlegmasia, compartment syndrome, necrotising infection, acute ischaemia) are recognised on the first look. Bilateral swelling follows the ankle swelling algorithm.

Breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis or collapse With a swollen leg → suspected PE → 999
Massive, tense, blue or white leg Phlegmasia cerulea or alba dolens (iliofemoral DVT threatening the limb) → 999
Pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment After trauma, fracture, crush, prolonged immobility or on anticoagulants → compartment syndrome → 999 emergency orthopaedics
Pain out of proportion to the redness, rapid spread, skin necrosis, crepitus, systemic toxicity Necrotising soft tissue infection → 999
Cold, pale, pulseless, painful limb Acute limb ischaemia (not true swelling, but can be confused) → 999 vascular
Sepsis Fever with NEWS2 or NG253 high-risk features and spreading cellulitis → 999; moderate risk → Same-day
Suspected DVT Any unexplained unilateral swelling with pain, tenderness or pitting → Same-day two-level Wells and the NG158 pathway (step 4) — today, not next week
Pregnant or within 6 weeks postpartum → Same-day assessment per RCOG/local pathway (NG158 does not cover pregnancy); LMWH, not a DOAC, if treating before imaging
Swelling with a pelvic or abdominal mass, groin nodes, or known cancer → Suspected cancer image or refer per NICE NG12 site criteria; unexplained lymphadenopathy → consider suspected cancer pathway referral

DVT, ruptured Baker's cyst, cellulitis and calf muscle injury look alike, and clinical features alone cannot exclude a DVT — which is why NICE NG158 builds the assessment around a structured score, a D-dimer and imaging within a fixed time. PE presents as breathlessness or chest pain, so ask about the chest in every patient with a swollen leg.

Compartment syndrome and necrotising fasciitis share one early clue: pain out of proportion to what you can see. Both need surgery within hours.

2
Diagnose

History — Onset, Risk Factors and the Knee

Onset
Hours to days: DVT, cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst, muscle tear, haematoma, compartment syndrome. Weeks to months: venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, pelvic mass, post-thrombotic syndrome
VTE risk factors
Surgery or hospital admission in the last 12 weeks, immobility, plaster cast, long travel, active cancer or treatment, previous VTE, pregnancy or postpartum, combined hormonal contraception or HRT, thrombophilia, obesity, IV drug use (groin injection)
Pain & mechanism
Sudden "kick" in the calf during exercise → gastrocnemius tear. Known knee arthritis with sudden calf pain → ruptured Baker's cyst. Trauma, crush or long lie → compartment syndrome
Infection clues
Fever, rigors, spreading redness, broken skin, tinea between toes, leg ulcer, insect bite, previous cellulitis
Chest
Breathlessness, pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, palpitations, syncope → PE
Chronic & cancer
Previous DVT in that leg (post-thrombotic syndrome), varicose veins, previous pelvic or groin surgery, lymph node dissection or radiotherapy, weight loss, night sweats, bowel, bladder or gynaecological symptoms
Medicines
Anticoagulants (haematoma, compartment bleed), oestrogens, tamoxifen, chemotherapy; calcium channel blockers cause bilateral rather than unilateral oedema

VTE risk factors raise the pre-test probability but their absence does not exclude DVT — up to a third of DVTs are unprovoked. Baker's cyst rupture typically follows exertion in someone with knee arthritis and can cause calf swelling, tenderness and later a crescent of bruising below the malleolus; it can coexist with DVT, so imaging is still needed.

3
Diagnose

Examination — Both Legs, Then the Abdomen and Nodes

Observations
Temperature, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, SpO₂ — tachycardia or hypoxia with a swollen leg → PE
Measure
Calf circumference 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity on both legs; a difference of 3 cm or more scores on Wells
Look & feel
Pitting, warmth, erythema (spreading edge — mark it), tenderness along the deep veins, collateral superficial veins, skin changes (haemosiderin, lipodermatosclerosis, varicose eczema), ulceration, entry wounds, tinea
Knee
Popliteal fullness or effusion, crescent bruising at the ankle (ruptured Baker's cyst)
Compartments
Tense, woody compartments, pain on passive toe or ankle movement, paraesthesia — late signs include pallor and pulselessness
Vascular
Foot pulses, capillary refill, temperature — needed before any compression
Lymphatic
Non-pitting, toe and foot dorsum involvement, Stemmer's sign (positive supports lymphoedema)
Abdomen, pelvis, groin
Inguinal lymph nodes, abdominal or pelvic mass, hepatosplenomegaly; pelvic or rectal examination if indicated

Examining both legs together makes asymmetry obvious. Do not use Homan's sign — it is neither sensitive nor specific. An unexplained unilateral swelling without DVT needs an abdominal, pelvic and groin examination, because extrinsic compression of the iliac veins or lymphatics (pelvic tumour, lymphadenopathy, May–Thurner anatomy) presents exactly like this.

4
Diagnose

DVT Pathway — NICE NG158

Score
Two-level DVT Wells score: 2 or more = DVT likely; 1 or less = DVT unlikely. Assess for PE as well
DVT likely (Wells 2 or more)
Proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours. If it cannot be done within 4 hours: D-dimer, then interim therapeutic anticoagulation and ultrasound within 24 hours. Ultrasound positive → treat as DVT. Ultrasound negative and D-dimer positive → stop interim anticoagulation and repeat the scan 6–8 days later
DVT unlikely (Wells 1 or less)
D-dimer with the result within 4 hours (if not possible, interim anticoagulation while waiting). Negative → DVT unlikely; stop any interim anticoagulation and look for another cause. Positive → ultrasound within 4 hours, or interim anticoagulation and ultrasound within 24 hours
D-dimer cautions
Consider an age-adjusted threshold over 50 (NG158). Raised by infection, inflammation, cancer, pregnancy, recent surgery and older age — a positive result is not a diagnosis
Interim anticoagulation
Choose one that can continue if DVT is confirmed — apixaban or rivaroxaban; dose per SPC (treatment-phase loading regimen). Take FBC, U&E with creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault), LFT, PT and APTT but do not wait for results; check bleeding risk, weight, pregnancy and interacting drugs. LMWH in pregnancy, severe renal impairment or where a DOAC is unsuitable
Other imaging
Ultrasound also shows Baker's cyst, haematoma, muscle tear or superficial thrombosis. Unexplained swelling with a negative scan and no local cause → abdominal/pelvic imaging per findings

NICE NG158 sets clear time limits: imaging within 4 hours, or a D-dimer and interim anticoagulation with imaging within 24 hours. A DOAC that can be continued avoids switching if the scan is positive. In people with a likely DVT but a negative proximal ultrasound and a positive D-dimer, a repeat scan at 6–8 days picks up calf clots that have propagated.

Superficial vein thrombosis near the saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction can extend into the deep system, so a scan is needed when it is in the thigh or close to a junction (CKS).

5
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Thrombotic
DVT · superficial vein thrombosis
Pitting, calf tenderness, risk factors; tender cord along a superficial vein (scan if near a junction)
Infective
Cellulitis · necrotising infection
Acute spreading warmth and redness, fever, portal of entry; pain out of proportion → necrotising
Knee & muscle
Ruptured Baker's cyst · calf tear
Knee arthritis, sudden pain, ankle crescent bruise; "kick" during exercise
Compartment & bleed
Compartment syndrome · haematoma
Trauma, crush, anticoagulants; tense compartment, pain on passive stretch
Venous
Venous insufficiency · post-thrombotic
Gradual, worse through the day, varicosities, skin changes; previous DVT in that leg
Lymphatic
Lymphoedema
Non-pitting, foot dorsum and toes, Stemmer positive; after cancer surgery or radiotherapy, or primary
Extrinsic compression
Pelvic mass · lymphadenopathy
Gradual, unexplained, with weight loss, abdominal or pelvic symptoms; May–Thurner (left iliac vein)
Other
Gout, fracture, dependent oedema
Hemiplegic limb, unilateral dependency, ankle fracture, acute gout or Charcot foot in diabetes

When the scan excludes DVT, the differential is guided by onset and examination. In people with diabetes and neuropathy, a hot swollen foot with normal or minimal pain may be an acute Charcot foot, which needs same-day offloading and specialist foot team referral.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria

999
Suspected PE with instability, phlegmasia, compartment syndrome, necrotising infection, acute limb ischaemia, high-risk sepsis
Same-day
DVT pathway (local DVT service, ambulatory clinic or emergency department) — scan within 4 hours, or D-dimer and interim anticoagulation with a scan within 24 hours; pregnancy or postpartum (maternity / medical team); cellulitis with moderate sepsis risk or Eron class III–IV; suspected acute Charcot foot (foot protection service)
Suspected cancer · NICE NG12
Pelvic or abdominal mass on examination or imaging → site-specific pathway; unexplained lymphadenopathy → consider suspected cancer pathway referral. Unprovoked DVT: assess for other cancer symptoms and signs and investigate or refer if present — no routine extensive screening
Vascular
Superficial vein thrombosis near a junction (same-day scan), venous ulceration, symptomatic varicose veins with skin changes (NICE CG168)
Lymphoedema service
Suspected lymphoedema once a DVT and an extrinsic cause have been excluded
Orthopaedics / MSK
Baker's cyst with recurrent rupture or significant knee pathology

Most areas run an ambulatory DVT service that delivers the NG158 timeframes; refer there the same day rather than by routine letter. Lymphoedema referral should follow exclusion of DVT and of a pelvic or abdominal cause, because a new unilateral lymphoedema can be the first sign of malignancy.

Treat

Management at This Consultation

Suspected DVT, scan not within 4 hours
Interim anticoagulation Start today
Apixaban or rivaroxaban, dose per SPC, after baseline bloods (do not wait for results), unless contraindicated — then same-day specialist advice. LMWH in pregnancy
Confirmed proximal DVT
DOAC for at least 3 months
Apixaban or rivaroxaban, dose per SPC; creatinine clearance by Cockcroft–Gault; specialist advice for active cancer, triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome, severe renal impairment, pregnancy
Cellulitis
Flucloxacillin (NICE NG141)
Usual adult dose: 500 mg to 1 g four times a day for 5–7 days (1 g is off-label). Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin 500 mg twice a day, or doxycycline per NG141. Mark the edge; treat tinea; elevate
Ruptured Baker's cyst / calf tear
Analgesia, rest, elevation
Only once DVT is excluded by ultrasound; paracetamol ± topical or oral NSAID if suitable; treat the knee
VenousVenous insufficiency — elevation, exercise and compression hosiery once arterial supply is adequate (pulses ± ABPI); emollient for varicose eczema
LymphoedemaSuspected lymphoedema — skin care, exercise, weight management, refer for specialist compression; diuretics do not help
SuperficialSuperficial vein thrombosis away from junctions — NSAID if suitable, warm compresses; scan if thigh or near a junction
PrecipitantsReview combined hormonal contraception or HRT if DVT is confirmed; plan contraception

Next steps: see Deep vein thrombosis · Pulmonary embolism · DOACs, antiplatelets & anticoagulation · Cellulitis & erysipelas · Varicose veins

NICE NG158 recommends interim therapeutic anticoagulation when imaging cannot be done within 4 hours, using an anticoagulant that can be continued if DVT is confirmed; apixaban and rivaroxaban fit this and need no heparin lead-in. Doses differ between the initial and maintenance phases and by renal function, so prescribe from the SPC.

NICE NG141 gives flucloxacillin as first choice for non-severe cellulitis, with clarithromycin, erythromycin (pregnancy) or doxycycline for penicillin allergy. Compression without checking the arterial supply risks ischaemic injury.

8
Lifestyle

Self-Care and Prevention

Mobilise Walk as tolerated once anticoagulated — bed rest is not needed for DVT.
Elevation Raise the leg above hip level when sitting; for cellulitis and venous swelling.
Skin care Daily emollient, treat athlete's foot, avoid cuts — reduces cellulitis recurrence, especially with lymphoedema.
Travel On long journeys walk regularly and stay hydrated; discuss travel within the early weeks of a DVT.
Weight & activity Weight management and calf exercises help venous and lymphatic return.
Anticoagulant card Carry it; tell dentists, pharmacists and surgeons; avoid over-the-counter NSAIDs and aspirin.

Early mobilisation with anticoagulation is safe and improves symptoms. Recurrent cellulitis is driven by oedema and broken skin, so skin care and oedema control are the main preventive measures.

9
Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Safety-net 999
Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, coughing blood or collapse; the leg becomes cold, white or blue; severe pain that is getting rapidly worse; vomiting blood, black stools or a bleed that will not stop on anticoagulation; any head injury on anticoagulation (emergency department)
Safety-net same-day
Redness spreading beyond the marked line, fever or feeling unwell, swelling increasing despite treatment, new numbness or tingling
Within 24 hours
Confirm the scan happened and the result was acted on; stop interim anticoagulation if DVT excluded (unless repeat scan planned at 6–8 days)
2–3 days
Cellulitis review if not improving (NG141)
3 months
DVT: review provoked or unprovoked, bleeding risk and whether to continue (see the DVT protocol)
Scan negative, swelling persists
Review in 1–2 weeks: re-examine abdomen, pelvis and groin; consider imaging, lymphoedema or venous referral

The highest-risk moment is the gap between the consultation and the scan: a documented plan for who books it, when, and who acts on the result closes that gap. A negative scan with persisting unexplained swelling needs a planned re-look for an extrinsic cause.
