Safety

Red Flags — Neurological, Malignant, Drug-Toxicity & Systemic Causes

Most taste disorders — loss of taste (ageusia/hypogeusia) or distorted taste (dysgeusia) — are transient (viral, drug-induced), oral, or due to impaired smell. Act urgently when taste change comes with neurological signs, malignancy features, drug toxicity or systemic disease. Sudden-onset distortion of smell or taste is rarely due to structural neurological disease and usually resolves within a few months (NICE NG127). Do not label isolated taste loss as cancer or a neurological emergency without objective supporting findings.

Sudden taste loss + facial weakness/palsy on the same side Bell's palsy (facial nerve VII — chorda tympani branch carries taste from anterior 2/3 tongue) or Ramsay Hunt syndrome (VZV reactivation in the geniculate ganglion — ear pain + vesicles in the ear canal, on the pinna or in the mouth + SNHL/vertigo). Vesicles may appear after the palsy or not at all (zoster sine herpete) — examine the ear, pinna and mouth. Taste loss alone occurs in Bell's palsy and does not itself indicate Ramsay Hunt. → Facial weakness with limb weakness, speech disturbance or forehead sparing → 999 (stroke). Bell's (uncomplicated, adult, within 72 hours): oral corticosteroid per local protocol + eye protection. Ramsay Hunt: same-day specialist assessment; start antiviral promptly, ideally within 72 hours; adjust for renal impairment, immunosuppression, severe otological disease, hearing loss or vertigo, delayed presentation and pregnancy.
Sudden taste loss + dysphagia, dysarthria, tongue deviation/wasting or palatal palsy Objective lower cranial-nerve palsy (IX, X, XII) or multiple cranial-nerve signs (jugular foramen syndrome): posterior fossa or skull-base lesion (nasopharyngeal carcinoma, glomus jugulare), carotid artery dissection, brainstem vascular disease, inflammatory or post-infectious neuropathy, surgery/trauma, malignancy. → Urgent neurology/ENT assessment and appropriate imaging; 999 if acute stroke signs or rapidly progressive bulbar or respiratory symptoms.
Taste loss + progressive dysphagia + hoarse voice + neck mass Head and neck malignancy (oropharyngeal, laryngeal, or hypopharyngeal cancer). Refer using NICE NG12 criteria: persistent unexplained hoarseness at 45 and over, unexplained neck lump, persistent oral ulceration, persistent oral lump or red/white patch (usually via dental/oral pathway); dysphagia → urgent upper-GI/endoscopic assessment. Taste disturbance alone is not a suspected cancer criterion. HPV-associated oropharyngeal SCC (especially base of tongue — presenting with taste alteration + ear pain ± neck node).
Taste/smell disturbance + unilateral nasal obstruction, epistaxis, facial pain, orbital symptoms or visible mass → Urgent ENT/head-and-neck assessment through the local pathway (NICE NG12 has no taste-loss or sinonasal criterion). Chronic rhinosinusitis, polyps and post-viral dysfunction are much more common than sinonasal malignancy.
Metallic or bitter taste + suspected digoxin or lithium toxicity Metallic taste alone is non-specific and is not a drug emergency. Digoxin: with nausea, vomiting, anorexia, visual disturbance, confusion, palpitations, bradycardia or arrhythmia — arrange urgent ECG; check U&E/eGFR, potassium, magnesium and calcium; obtain a digoxin concentration at least 6–8 hours after the last dose (unless acute overdose requires toxicology advice); withhold digoxin and seek urgent acute medical/toxicology advice. Usual therapeutic range approximately 0.7–2.0 nanograms/mL (approximately 0.9–2.6 nmol/L, SPS), but toxicity can occur within range (renal impairment, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, older age, interacting drugs) — do not use a single concentration in isolation to diagnose or exclude toxicity. Lithium: vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion → urgent assessment, ECG, lithium level and U&E. 999 if unstable (arrhythmia, reduced consciousness, seizure).
Credible heavy-metal exposure Metallic taste alone is not an indication for urgent heavy-metal testing. Ask about specific occupational, environmental or supplement exposure (contaminated supplements, cosmetics, imported remedies or water, retained bullets, industrial/hobby exposure) and discuss with toxicology/occupational medicine before requesting targeted testing.
Taste loss + mouth ulcers/mucositis, sore throat or fever on chemotherapy or methotrexate Possible neutropenic sepsis or methotrexate toxicity (mucositis impairs taste-cell renewal). Fever or feeling unwell on systemic anticancer therapy → refer immediately for emergency assessment (acute oncology/ED; 999 if severely unwell) — do not wait for an FBC (NICE CG151). On methotrexate: withhold it and arrange an urgent same-day FBC; seek specialist advice. Chemotherapy taste changes are common and can contribute to malnutrition and treatment non-compliance.
New taste disturbance + HIV risk factors / known HIV + oral white plaques or ulcers Oral candidiasis, hairy leukoplakia, or CMV oral ulcers in HIV. Diagnose oral candidiasis clinically. Offer HIV testing according to risk and clinical context. CD4 count belongs to specialist HIV management — HIV clinic referral.
Bitter/metallic taste + excessive thirst + polyuria + weight loss New-onset diabetes mellitus (ketosis causes a sweet/fruity/acetone breath and may alter taste perception). Check glucose/HbA1c; suspected severe hyperglycaemia/DKA → emergency assessment.
Transient, repetitive unpleasant tastes without an external stimulus May represent a focal seizure aura (e.g. temporal lobe lesion) → neurological assessment for possible epilepsy (NICE NG127). Seizure, reduced consciousness or meningism with taste disturbance → 999.
Unexplained taste/smell loss >3 months, or earlier with red flags Consider neuroimaging (MRI brain) for unexplained loss of taste or smell lasting more than 3 months (NICE NG127); ENT assessment through the local pathway. Earlier if focal neurological signs, seizures/recurrent taste hallucinations, progressive headache, cognitive or personality change, visual symptoms, cranial-nerve abnormalities, or unilateral nasal obstruction, crusting or bleeding. Do not routinely arrange neuroimaging for an isolated, recent taste disturbance with a normal examination and an apparent viral, medication-related or oral cause. Do not routinely refer adults with normal neuroimaging, or adults whose taste or smell loss began immediately after a head injury (NICE NG127).
The chorda tympani branch of the facial nerve is the anatomical key to understanding taste loss with facial palsy — taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue is carried by the chorda tympani (a branch of the facial nerve CN VII), which travels through the middle ear before joining the lingual nerve. In Bell's palsy (idiopathic facial nerve inflammation), the entire facial nerve is affected, including the chorda tympani branch, causing: ipsilateral taste loss from the anterior tongue + ipsilateral loss of lacrimation (greater petrosal nerve) + hyperacusis (nerve to stapedius). The patient may not volunteer taste loss unless specifically asked — 'Have you noticed any change in how food tastes?' should be a routine question in any facial palsy assessment. Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus) involves the geniculate ganglion of the facial nerve and typically causes: severe otalgia (ear pain, often the first symptom) + ipsilateral facial palsy + vesicles in the external ear canal/auricle (sometimes on the soft palate) + sensorineural hearing loss + vertigo. It has a worse prognosis than Bell's palsy, and incomplete recovery is more common. Treatment is time-sensitive: it requires same-day specialist assessment; start antiviral treatment promptly, ideally within 72 hours, and discuss corticosteroid treatment with the specialist/local pathway.

Diagnose

Loss or Distortion? True Taste vs Flavour (Retronasal Smell)

First establish whether the patient has true gustatory loss or predominantly impaired smell/flavour perception. Most patients who say they have "lost their taste" have lost their sense of smell. If smell loss is the main problem, use the Loss of Smell (Anosmia) pathway alongside this one.

Terminology
Ageusia: complete loss of taste. Hypogeusia: reduced taste sensitivity. Dysgeusia: altered/distorted taste perception (food tastes wrong — often metallic, bitter, sweet, chemical or rotten). Phantogeusia: persistent taste sensation without a stimulus (phantom taste — usually metallic or sour). Parosmia: distorted smell, experienced as distorted flavour.
True taste (gustation)
Detects 5 primary qualities via taste receptor cells in taste buds on papillae (fungiform, circumvallate, foliate): sweet (T1R2/T1R3), salty (ENaC), sour (PKD2L1), bitter (T2R family — most numerous, evolutionary toxin detection), umami (T1R1/T1R3 — glutamate/amino acids). Cranial nerves: anterior 2/3 tongue = CN VII (chorda tympani branch); posterior 1/3 tongue = CN IX (glossopharyngeal); epiglottis/larynx = CN X. True taste is rarely completely absent in benign disease — true ageusia is much rarer and more likely to indicate a structural or neurological cause.
Retronasal olfaction — most of "taste"
Aromatic volatile compounds released during chewing travel retronasally from the nasopharynx to the olfactory epithelium. This accounts for much of flavour perception — what people describe as "tasting" coffee, wine, strawberries, herbs. Post-viral anosmia (the most common cause of patient-reported "taste loss") eliminates retronasal olfaction while leaving true gustation intact: "everything tastes the same" or "food has no flavour" usually means anosmia with intact taste.
Simple bedside distinction
Ask separately about sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami detection, and about smell, nasal obstruction and retronasal flavour: "Can you taste the sweetness of sugar or the saltiness of salty food?" If yes, the problem is smell (flavour) rather than true taste. Prepared-solution tongue tests are not validated for routine GP use; formal gustatory testing is specialist (see Step 4).
Salivary function
Saliva is essential for taste — it dissolves tastant molecules and delivers them to taste receptor cells. Xerostomia (dry mouth) from any cause significantly impairs taste. Treating dry mouth often dramatically improves reported taste loss (Step 9).
Zinc and taste
Zinc is a cofactor for gustin (carbonic anhydrase VI) — a zinc metalloprotein secreted in saliva that is essential for taste receptor maturation and maintenance. Serum zinc has biological and pre-analytical limitations and is not a universal test for dysgeusia (see Step 4).
Understanding the physiology of flavour is essential for accurate diagnosis and patient education — if a GP tells a patient who has post-COVID anosmia that they have 'lost their sense of taste,' the patient will search for ageusia treatments, not anosmia treatments. The correct explanation is: 'You have lost your sense of smell, and because most of what we experience as taste is actually smell, your food has no flavour. But your actual taste receptors — for sweet, salt, sour, bitter — are working normally.' This explanation is accurate, helps the patient understand why antihistamines won't help, and correctly directs them toward olfactory training. The gustin (carbonic anhydrase VI) — zinc connection is mechanistically important: gustin is secreted by serous acinar cells of the parotid gland into saliva, where it acts as a trophic factor for taste receptor cell maturation and maintenance. In zinc deficiency, gustin activity falls, taste bud maintenance is impaired, and hypogeusia and dysgeusia result. Even so, do not empirically prescribe high-dose zinc for idiopathic or post-viral dysgeusia (see Step 7).

Causes of Taste Loss & Distortion

Post-viral (including COVID-19)
Upper respiratory infection is the most common cause — viral damage to taste receptor cells or smell-dependent taste impairment (anosmia). Also rhinovirus, influenza, HSV, EBV. COVID-19 is the most common post-COVID sensory complaint (see Step 5). Recovery depends on the cause, age, severity and duration.
Drug-induced (very common — most common iatrogenic cause)
Many drugs cause taste disturbance (check BNF/SPC): Metronidazole (metallic — very common) · Metformin (metallic/bitter) · ACE inhibitors (bitter metallic; captopril most of any ACEi) · Clarithromycin (bitter metallic aftertaste — dose-dependent) · Terbinafine (prolonged dysgeusia, may persist for months after stopping) · Lithium (metallic) · Allopurinol · Omeprazole/PPIs · Antiepileptics (carbamazepine, topiramate) · Antidepressants/SSRIs · Calcium channel blockers (amlodipine) · Statins · Methotrexate (mucositis) · Chemotherapy · Zinc-chelating drugs (penicillamine, captopril — deplete zinc) · anticholinergic/xerogenic drugs (dry mouth).
Nutritional deficiency
Zinc deficiency: possible reversible cause (gustin cofactor) — consider where there is malnutrition, malabsorption, restrictive diet, chronic diarrhoea, alcohol dependence or a relevant medication such as penicillamine. B12, folate, iron: all can cause hypogeusia and dysgeusia. Test selectively according to clinical risk (Step 4).
Oral and dental causes
Dry mouth (xerostomia), poor oral hygiene (periodontal disease — bacterial volatile sulphur compounds cause dysgeusia), dental infections (abscess — metallic/bitter taste), oral candidiasis, ill-fitting dentures, after dental procedures (local anaesthetic — temporary numbness). GORD (acid/bitter taste).
Neurological
CN VII (chorda tympani) injury — trauma, Bell's palsy, Ramsay Hunt, middle-ear or parotid/submandibular surgery, dental anaesthesia · CN IX palsy · CN X involvement · Head injury (taste or smell loss immediately after a head injury — do not routinely refer, NICE NG127) · stroke affecting the nucleus tractus solitarius or thalamic taste pathway · MS (demyelination of central taste pathways) · temporal lobe lesion (gustatory aura/seizure).
Systemic disease and malignancy
Chronic kidney disease (uraemic dysgeusia — metallic/"fishy", worse in ESRD) · liver disease (hepatic dysgeusia) · hypothyroidism (reduced taste sensitivity) · diabetes mellitus (neuropathy, candidal overgrowth) · Sjögren's syndrome (dry mouth; bilateral parotid swelling + dry eyes + arthralgia) · sarcoidosis (bilateral parotid enlargement + uveitis + taste loss = Heerfordt syndrome) · malignancy (paraneoplastic + cachexia-related; head and neck cancer — Step 1) · head and neck radiotherapy (direct damage to taste buds and salivary glands).
Lifestyle and age
Smoking (dose-dependent taste impairment), alcohol, vaping and recreational drugs, mouth breathing. Age-related ("presbygeusia"): taste sensitivity declines physiologically from the 6th decade — fewer fungiform papillae, slower taste-cell turnover, more medicines. Clinically relevant when it contributes to malnutrition (food tasteless → poor appetite → weight loss). Usually a diagnosis of exclusion after correctable causes are addressed.
Drug-induced dysgeusia is far more common than most clinicians appreciate and is the most commonly missed cause in primary care — for many patients the taste change is the most bothersome side effect of their medication. The mechanism varies by drug: (1) drugs excreted in saliva affect taste directly at the receptor level (metronidazole, metformin — both achieve high salivary concentrations, producing a persistent metallic taste); (2) drugs that chelate zinc impair taste receptor function (captopril — its sulfhydryl group chelates zinc — and penicillamine; zinc is essential for carbonic anhydrase VI in taste buds); (3) drugs that reduce salivary flow (anticholinergics, antihistamines, antidepressants) cause dry mouth → altered taste by reducing the aqueous medium for taste molecule delivery to receptors. Terbinafine deserves special mention: it is lipophilic, accumulates in keratin-rich tissues including taste receptor cells and is released slowly, so a bitter or metallic dysgeusia can be severe enough to cause weight loss and depression and may persist for months after stopping (case reports up to 12 months). Patients who completed a course for onychomycosis may not connect the drug — ask specifically about terbinafine use in the past 12 months, and warn patients starting terbinafine about this effect. Clarithromycin commonly causes a bitter metallic taste — patient education prevents early discontinuation of important antibiotic courses.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Duration and onset (acute = viral, drug; chronic = nutritional, neurological, idiopathic). Type: ageusia/hypogeusia (reduced) vs dysgeusia (distorted) vs phantogeusia; which tastes are affected (metallic/bitter/sweet/absent). Unilateral vs bilateral (unilateral = CN VII, IX, structural). Taste, smell or both? Associated smell changes (most "taste" loss is actually anosmia — ask), nasal obstruction or rhinitis. Recent URTI or COVID-19 infection. Head injury. Drug history (complete, including OTC, herbal and supplements; terbinafine in the past 12 months). Dental history (recent dental work, dry mouth, gingivitis, oral infections). Occupational, environmental or supplement exposure. Smoking, vaping, alcohol and recreational drugs, mouth breathing. Weight and nutritional intake. Oral symptoms (dry mouth, burning mouth, lesions). GI symptoms (reflux — acid/bitter taste). Systemic symptoms (weight loss, dysphagia — malignancy; polyuria — diabetes; cold intolerance — hypothyroid; renal, hepatic, Sjögren's features; chemotherapy). Neurological symptoms (facial palsy, dysphagia, hoarse voice, speech change).
Examination
Oral cavity: dental hygiene, periodontal health, dental abscess, mucosa health, xerostomia (dry mouth — reduced saliva pooling), oral candida (white wipeable plaques), glossitis (smooth red tongue = deficiency), mucosal lesions, hairy leukoplakia, denture fit. Cranial nerves (if neurological features): CN VII (Bell's — close eyes, raise eyebrows, show teeth; forehead sparing suggests stroke), CN IX/X (palatal movement — uvula should rise centrally on "ah"; gag reflex), CN XII (tongue deviation or wasting); taste distribution (anterior 2/3 = CN VII; posterior 1/3 = CN IX). Ear and pinna: vesicles (Ramsay Hunt). Nose: obstruction, polyps, unilateral signs. Neck: lymphadenopathy. Parotid glands: swelling, tenderness (Sjögren's, sarcoidosis, infection).
Taste testing
In primary care, distinguish true taste loss from loss of smell using history and simple patient-reported testing (Step 2). Prepared-solution tongue tests are not validated for routine GP use (preparation variability, olfactory contamination, local oral disease, risk of swallowing). Formal gustatory testing, electrogustometry and validated taste-strip testing are specialist investigations.
Investigations
Targeted — not routine (risk-based, not for every patient): HbA1c/glucose (diabetes symptoms or risk factors) · TSH/TFTs (thyroid symptoms) · B12 + folate (deficiency risk, malnutrition, malabsorption or neurological features) · FBC/ferritin (anaemia, dietary risk, weight loss or poor intake) · U&E/eGFR + LFTs (systemic disease or medication toxicity suspected) · Serum zinc (only when deficiency risk is plausible — see below) · HIV test (according to risk and clinical context) · Digoxin or lithium level + ECG (only if prescribed and toxicity suspected). Specialist/targeted: ANA + anti-Ro/La (SSA/SSB) and rheumatology assessment for convincing Sjögren's features · Serum ACE only selectively — not a diagnostic test for sarcoidosis · CXR / further imaging according to clinical suspicion · MRI brain ± ENT (unexplained >3 months or red flags — NICE NG127) · formal olfactory/gustatory testing via ENT/specialist services. Do not routinely request: ESR/CRP for isolated dysgeusia, serum zinc in every patient, salivary-flow testing in primary care, CT/MRI without red flags or persistent unexplained symptoms, CD4 count in primary care, COVID serology to confirm a remote cause, formal smell testing in primary care, urgent heavy-metal testing for metallic taste alone.
Serum zinc testing for taste disorders should be targeted — zinc deficiency is a potentially reversible cause of hypogeusia and dysgeusia, and testing is worth considering in at-risk groups: elderly patients (reduced dietary intake + reduced absorption), patients taking zinc-chelating drugs (penicillamine, captopril, tetracycline), vegetarians and vegans (plant phytates inhibit zinc absorption), malnutrition, malabsorption (coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, short bowel syndrome, bariatric surgery), chronic diarrhoea, restrictive diet, alcohol dependence, long-term PPI therapy, and unexplained alopecia, dermatitis or poor wound healing. Serum zinc behaves as a negative acute-phase reactant — it falls during inflammation/infection and may be low-normal while tissue zinc is deficient — so interpret it against the local laboratory range, with albumin and inflammatory status. In primary care, history and simple patient-reported testing usually separate true taste loss from olfactory-driven flavour loss; the majority of patients reporting taste loss after COVID-19 who undergo formal gustometry have normal true taste function, confirming anosmia as the actual problem.

Post-Viral & COVID-19 Smell and Taste Impairment

Mechanism of post-COVID olfactory/gustatory dysfunction
SARS-CoV-2 infects sustentacular cells (support cells) of the olfactory epithelium via ACE2 receptors → inflammation → olfactory sensory neuron dysfunction. Taste dysfunction is largely secondary to anosmia (flavour perception = smell + taste), but direct injury to taste buds also occurs (ACE2 is expressed on taste-bud supporting cells). COVID-19 anosmia/dysgeusia is typically sudden in onset, often without nasal congestion or rhinitis (distinguishes it from other viral anosmia).
Recovery patterns
Ask whether smell is also impaired — much apparent taste disturbance is retronasal olfactory dysfunction. For post-viral or post-COVID smell/taste disturbance, explain that recovery is variable and may continue for months; offer a tailored assessment for ongoing symptoms (NICE NG188). Parosmia (distorted smell — food smells burnt, rotten, or chemical): particularly distressing; may develop weeks after acute infection; correlates with neural regeneration. Phantosmia (phantom smells without stimulus): may accompany recovery.
Olfactory training — reasonable, low-risk option for associated smell loss
Hummel et al. (Laryngoscope 2009) and subsequent trials: olfactory training (smell training) is a reasonable, low-risk intervention for post-viral smell loss; evidence for drug or supplement treatment remains limited. Protocol: 4 essential oils (rose, lemon, cloves, eucalyptus — representing 4 odour categories: flowery, fruity, spicy, resinous). Sniff each oil for 20 seconds twice daily. Concentrate intensely on the memory/image of the smell. Continue × minimum 12 weeks. Available as commercial kits or use individual essential oils. AbScent charity (abscent.org) provides free resources and support community. Full smell-training detail: Loss of Smell pathway.
Referral for persistent loss
Persistent, troublesome smell/taste disturbance despite olfactory training: consider ENT assessment; any imaging or formal smell testing is specialist-led. Do not routinely arrange MRI olfactory bulb sequences, COVID serology to confirm a remote cause, or formal smell testing in primary care. Do not use routine omega-3, or systemic or intranasal steroids, solely for post-COVID dysgeusia — no universal pharmacological treatment is supported (NICE NG188).
Parosmia is one of the most distressing sequelae of COVID-19 olfactory damage — it is a distorted smell perception where familiar odours are perceived as disgusting (coffee smelling like petrol, meat smelling like rotting, or sweet foods smelling like chemicals). Parosmia can occur with persistent post-COVID smell dysfunction and is characterised by: onset several weeks after the acute infection (rather than at the same time); worsening with strong smells; specific trigger foods or household smells; and profound impact on quality of life, appetite, and mental health. Paradoxically, parosmia is a positive prognostic sign — it indicates that olfactory nerve fibres are regenerating (misrouted regenerating axons interpret incoming signals incorrectly, creating the distorted perception). Most patients with parosmia eventually recover, though the timeline is months to years. The management: continue olfactory training (evidence suggests it accelerates parosmia resolution), avoid major trigger foods temporarily (adapt diet around the parosmia without eliminating all food groups — dietitian referral if significant weight loss), and address the psychological impact (anxiety and depression are extremely common — PHQ-9 and GAD-7, NHS Talking Therapies referral).

Refer

Referral Pathways

Urgent
Emergency (999 / same-day hospital): acute facial weakness with limb weakness, dysphasia or other stroke signs · rapidly progressive bulbar or respiratory symptoms · severe digoxin or lithium toxicity with bradyarrhythmia, syncope, confusion or instability · suspected neutropenic sepsis on chemotherapy (NICE CG151) · suspected severe hyperglycaemia/DKA · taste disturbance with reduced consciousness, seizure or meningism. Same-day or urgent specialist: Bell's palsy with incomplete eye closure or atypical neurological findings · Ramsay Hunt syndrome · objective lower cranial-nerve palsy (dysphagia, dysarthria, palatal weakness, vocal-cord palsy, tongue wasting or multiple CN signs) → urgent neurology/ENT assessment and imaging · suspected digoxin or lithium toxicity (urgent ECG and bloods, withhold the drug, acute medical advice) · recurrent focal taste hallucinations · significant weight loss, dehydration or nutritional compromise
Suspected cancer: ENT
NICE NG12 head and neck features: persistent unexplained hoarseness at 45 and over · unexplained neck lump · persistent oral ulceration · persistent oral lump or red/white patch (usually via dental/oral pathway) · dysphagia → urgent upper-GI/endoscopic assessment. Taste disturbance alone is not a suspected cancer criterion.
Urgent (local pathway): ENT / head & neck
Taste/smell disturbance with unilateral nasal obstruction, crusting, epistaxis, facial pain, orbital symptoms or visible mass · persistent unilateral oral/nasal symptoms · unilateral pharyngeal mass or tonsillar asymmetry with taste change · suspected sinonasal or oropharyngeal malignancy
ENT / rhinology (routine)
Unexplained taste/smell loss persisting beyond 3 months → consider neuroimaging (NICE NG127) and ENT assessment; do not routinely refer if neuroimaging is normal · persistent troublesome post-viral smell/taste disturbance despite olfactory training · chronic rhinosinusitis or nasal polyps contributing to mixed taste/smell loss not responding to topical treatment (assessment for FESS or dupilumab) · formal olfactory/gustatory testing; specialist smell and taste clinics exist at a small number of centres for persistent isolated ageusia
Neurology
Taste disorder + neurological features (diplopia, vertigo, other cranial-nerve signs) · facial palsy that is atypical, recurrent, bilateral, progressive or not recovering as expected → facial-palsy/ENT/neurology pathway (typical uncomplicated Bell's palsy: routine referral not needed — NICE NG127) · recurrent transient unpleasant tastes without stimulus (?focal seizure aura) · suspected MS (other demyelinating features) · stroke affecting taste pathways · suspected thalamic or temporal lobe lesion · suspected Parkinson's disease only with motor features (bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, gait or balance disturbance) or other neurological signs — refer quickly and untreated; smell or taste disturbance alone is not an indication for referral and objective smell testing is not used routinely (NICE NG71)
Oral medicine / dentistry
Burning mouth syndrome + taste disturbance · persistent burning, unexplained or unilateral oral lesions, ulceration, induration or cervical lymphadenopathy (dental/oral-medicine or head-and-neck assessment) · periodontal disease, dental infection · xerostomia (salivary gland assessment) · chronic oral candidiasis
Rheumatology
Suspected Sjögren's syndrome (xerostomia + dry eyes + taste loss; anti-Ro/La positive) · sarcoidosis-related taste loss (Heerfordt syndrome)
Renal / hepatic
Uraemic dysgeusia → renal team to optimise CKD management or dialysis adequacy · hepatic dysgeusia → manage underlying liver disease
Dietitian
Taste loss with clinically significant weight loss, malnutrition risk or difficulty maintaining adequate intake · cancer-related dysgeusia (chemotherapy, radiotherapy) impairing nutritional intake · older people with taste loss and unintentional weight loss · severe parosmia restricting diet · food modification strategies for specific dysgeusia types
GP management
Primary-care management is appropriate when the disturbance is recent and follows viral illness, or there is a clear medication or oral cause, the examination is reassuring, and there are no neurological, cancer or nutritional red flags (treatment: Steps 7–10).
Bell's palsy with taste involvement helps localise the lesion — the chorda tympani branch of CN VII leaves the facial nerve within the facial canal. If taste is preserved, the lesion is distal to the chorda tympani exit; if taste is impaired, the lesion is more proximal (geniculate ganglion or above). This does not change initial management but provides prognostic information — more proximal lesions with taste involvement tend to have slower and less complete recovery, so document the presence or absence of taste in all Bell's palsy presentations. Dietitian referral for cancer-related dysgeusia (common in chemotherapy patients) is an underused intervention — specific strategies include plastic cutlery, serving food cool or at room temperature, marinating meat in fruit juices, using tart flavours (lemon, vinegar), and non-meat protein sources (dairy, eggs, legumes) when meat tastes unpleasant.

Treat

Treat Reversible Causes — Stepwise

Step 1 Address causeDrug review: identify the likely culprit and stop or reduce it if clinically safe — review the temporal relationship, indication and alternatives with the prescriber; do not change therapy automatically or stop important treatment without weighing benefit and risk (e.g. a strongly suspected ACE inhibitor: consider an alternative after reviewing the indication, blood pressure, renal function and potassium; terbinafine → stop if the course is complete; clarithromycin → complete the course with the patient informed). Treat dry mouth (Step 9). Treat confirmed oral candidiasis per NICE CKS: miconazole oral gel first line (check interactions, e.g. warfarin, statins), nystatin if miconazole is unsuitable; fluconazole 50 mg once daily for 7 days if extensive or severe. Periodontal or dental infection: dental referral. GORD: PPI + lifestyle (acid reflux causing sour/bitter dysgeusia). Hypothyroidism: levothyroxine. Diabetes, CKD, liver disease: optimise the underlying condition.
Step 2 NutritionalZinc: only prescribe zinc when deficiency or a strong clinical indication is established; do not use high-dose zinc routinely for post-viral or idiopathic dysgeusia (details: Step 8). B12: replace per cause (NICE NG239; doses per BNF) — IM hydroxocobalamin if neurological features, autoimmune gastritis (pernicious anaemia) or malabsorption; oral cyanocobalamin for diet-related deficiency without neurological features. Folate: folic acid 5 mg once daily for 4 months (start B12 first if B12 is also low). Iron: ferrous sulphate 200 mg once daily (or alternate days if not tolerated; BSG 2021). Review response according to cause and severity.
Step 3 Symptom managementMetallic/bitter dysgeusia: rinse the mouth with bicarbonate of soda solution before meals (used in oncology for chemotherapy-related metallic taste); use plastic cutlery; marinate meat in fruit acids; citric acid or sugar-free lozenges. Phantogeusia: recurrent taste hallucinations or phantogeusia need neurological/ENT assessment (Step 6). Avoid empirical benzodiazepine or herbal receptor-blocking treatment in primary care. Flavour and diet strategies: Step 10.
The most useful intervention in taste loss is a systematic medication review: list every drug (including OTC and herbal) the patient takes, check each for taste-related adverse reactions in the BNF/electronic medicines compendium, and review the temporal relationship and alternatives with the prescriber. The bicarbonate of soda mouthwash strategy for metallic dysgeusia comes from oncology practice — metallic taste in chemotherapy patients is partly caused by acidic by-products of taste receptor cell damage, and rinsing with an alkaline bicarbonate solution before meals partially neutralises this, improving perceived food taste. The same advice can be applied to drug-induced metallic dysgeusia from any cause.

Treat

Treatment by Specific Cause

Bell's palsy
Oral corticosteroid per local protocol (within 72 hours); antiviral not routinely required
Uncomplicated adult Bell's palsy within 72 hours (NICE NG127): regimens commonly used include prednisolone 25 mg twice daily for 10 days, or 60 mg daily for 5 days followed by a taper — follow the current local formulary/SPC. Antivirals add little (Sullivan et al, NEJM 2007) — seek specialist advice if one is considered. Stroke assessment if the forehead is spared, or limb weakness or speech disturbance is present. Eye care where closure is incomplete: lubricating drops by day and ointment/taping at night; urgent ophthalmology advice if the eye cannot be closed, corneal pain, visual change or suspected corneal injury. Routine neurology referral is not required for uncomplicated Bell's palsy; refer if atypical, recurrent, bilateral, progressive, with other cranial-nerve signs, or not recovering as expected (Step 11).
Ramsay Hunt syndrome
Aciclovir 800 mg 5× daily × 7 days (or valaciclovir 1 g TDS × 7 days) + corticosteroid per specialist/local pathway
Same-day specialist assessment. Start antiviral promptly, ideally within 72 hours; discuss corticosteroid regimen and tapering with the specialist/local pathway. Renal-dose adjustment of aciclovir/valaciclovir; seek advice in immunosuppression and pregnancy. Audiology/ENT assessment for sensorineural hearing loss and vestibular symptoms. Eye care (lubricating drops, tape eye at night); urgent ophthalmology if eye closure is impaired or ocular symptoms. Serial facial nerve assessment. Prognosis worse than Bell's — incomplete recovery is more common.
Zinc deficiency
Elemental zinc per local formulary/SPC (confirmed deficiency or strong indication only)
Do not confuse the salt dose with the elemental zinc dose (a 220 mg zinc sulphate tablet may contain approximately 50 mg elemental zinc). Define the duration and review clinical response (a repeat serum zinc alone does not establish response). Prolonged or high-dose use can cause nausea, copper deficiency, anaemia, neutropenia and neurological complications — check copper status if supplementation is prolonged or repeated. Investigate the underlying cause. Dietary sources: Step 10.
Drug-induced dysgeusia
Stop, reduce or switch the culprit if clinically safe (with the prescriber)
If it cannot be stopped (e.g. metformin for diabetes), warn the patient it often improves over weeks to months; try a modified-release formulation (better tolerated) and take with food. Terbinafine-related dysgeusia may persist for months after stopping.
Post-viral / post-COVID smell and taste
Olfactory training × 12+ weeks (for associated smell loss)
4 essential oils twice daily (Step 5). AbScent resources. Nasal saline irrigation (Sterimar or Neilmed — reduces sinonasal inflammation). Explain that recovery is variable and may continue for months. PHQ-9 (depression complicates recovery). Evidence for drug or supplement treatment (including omega-3 and steroids) remains limited — not recommended routinely.
Sjögren's xerostomia
Pilocarpine (specialist-initiated; local formulary/SPC) + saliva substitutes
Increases salivary flow from remaining functional acinar cells. Contraindicated/caution: uncontrolled asthma, acute iritis, narrow-angle glaucoma, significant cardiac disease, peptic ulcer disease, urinary obstruction. Monitor BP, sweating, urinary frequency. Hydroxychloroquine and other disease treatment are specialist-led.
Post-radiotherapy
Saliva substitutes (e.g. Glandosane, Salivix) ± pilocarpine via specialist/dental pathway
Radiation damages salivary glands irreversibly in the radiation field; pilocarpine only if functional gland tissue remains. Artificial saliva for overnight use. Dietary adaptation and dietitian (Step 9).
Uraemic dysgeusia
Optimise CKD management or dialysis adequacy (renal team)
Adequate dialysis reduces the uraemic solute accumulation driving dysgeusia. Zinc only if deficiency is established, guided by the renal team and local formulary/SPC. Individualise protein and calorie targets with the renal dietitian.
The olfactory training protocol (smell training) for post-viral anosmia is a reasonable, low-risk intervention for post-infectious smell loss (it is not a proven treatment for all taste disorders) — a systematic review and meta-analysis (Pekala et al., Int Forum Allergy Rhinol 2016; 10 studies, 639 patients) found that olfactory training improved smell function compared with controls. The mechanism is neuroplasticity — repeated activation of olfactory neural pathways stimulates axonal regeneration and cortical reorganisation of olfactory processing areas. The original Hummel protocol (2009) used 4 scents representing the 4 odour categories in the Henning odour prism (flowery, fruity, resinous, aromatic/spicy), sniffed for 20 seconds per odour, twice daily. Subsequent research (Altundag 2015) showed that 'modified' smell training — changing the 4 scents every 12 weeks — was more effective than using the same 4 scents continuously. Patient motivation and adherence are the main determinants of response. Pilocarpine is a muscarinic (M3) agonist that stimulates remaining functional salivary acinar cells; side effects (sweating, flushing, increased lacrimation, urinary frequency) are dose-dependent. Initiation in Sjögren's is typically specialist-led (rheumatology), and GPs can continue and adjust the prescription; for post-radiotherapy xerostomia it produces a modest improvement in salivary flow and patient-reported taste and quality of life.
9
Treat

Xerostomia, Burning Mouth & Refractory Dysgeusia

Xerostomia (dry mouth) — a common driver of taste disorder
Causes: anticholinergics (tricyclics, antihistamines, oxybutynin and other bladder antimuscarinics, antipsychotics), antidepressants, diuretics, opioids, Sjögren's syndrome, head/neck radiotherapy, mouth breathing, dehydration. Management: (1) Identify and reduce anticholinergic burden (STOPP criteria — anticholinergic cognitive burden score). (2) Saliva substitutes: Biotène gel, A.S. Saliva Orthana spray, BioXtra gel. (3) Saliva stimulants: sugar-free gum or pastilles; pilocarpine via the specialist/dental pathway only where functional glandular tissue remains (Sjögren's or post-radiotherapy — Step 8; not a universal taste-loss treatment). (4) Dental review (caries risk high with xerostomia — fluoride varnish; chlorhexidine only for a clear dental indication and limited duration); twice-daily fluoride toothpaste and denture care. (5) Adequate hydration.
Burning mouth syndrome (BMS) — pharmacological
Burning mouth syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion. Assess for candidiasis, trauma, dental disease, xerostomia, iron/B12/folate deficiency, diabetes, thyroid disease, medication effects and oral mucosal disease before diagnosing primary burning mouth syndrome. Persistent burning, unexplained oral lesions, unilateral lesions, ulceration, induration or cervical lymphadenopathy require dental/oral-medicine or head-and-neck assessment. Do not start clonazepam, gabapentin, alpha-lipoic acid or other neuropathic-pain treatment automatically in primary care without a clear diagnosis and consideration of dependence, sedation and local specialist guidance. Cognitive behavioural therapy may help. Oral medicine/dentistry referral for formal diagnosis.
Post-radiotherapy and cancer-related taste loss
Most severe at 4–6 weeks of radiotherapy, partial recovery over 1–2 years, may be permanent. Zinc supplementation only for suspected/confirmed deficiency (elemental zinc per local formulary, with oncology advice). Dietary adaptation: intensify flavour (spices, umami — MSG, soy sauce), food texture focus when taste impaired. Dietitian referral. Megestrol acetate (appetite stimulant) if significant weight loss — oncology/specialist advice.
Anticholinergic burden and xerostomia is a critically important interaction in elderly patients — anticholinergic drugs (tricyclic antidepressants, oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, diphenhydramine, chlorphenamine, procyclidine, hyoscine, first-generation antihistamines, antipsychotics, antiemetics) suppress parasympathetic stimulation of salivary glands, causing dry mouth and the associated taste impairment, caries and worsening dysphagia. In elderly patients with polypharmacy, the cumulative anticholinergic burden can be substantial — the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) score quantifies this. High ACB scores are associated not only with dry mouth and constipation, but also with cognitive impairment, falls, and urinary retention. GPs who identify taste disorder + dry mouth in an elderly patient should specifically calculate the anticholinergic burden and consider whether any anticholinergic drugs can be stopped or substituted. The STOPP criteria specifically identify antidepressants with anticholinergic properties, bladder antimuscarinic drugs for overactive bladder, and first-generation antihistamines as potentially inappropriate in older adults. Burning mouth syndrome affects primarily peri- and post-menopausal women: chronic burning of the tongue, lips or palate without visible lesions, often with altered (metallic, bitter) taste and a dry-mouth sensation, typically absent on waking and worst in the evening. It is idiopathic in most cases but can be secondary to nutritional deficiency (iron, B12, zinc, folate), dry mouth, oral candidiasis, contact hypersensitivity (dental materials) or psychosocial factors.

Lifestyle

Diet, Flavour, Oral Hygiene & Support

Oral hygiene optimisation Good oral hygiene directly impacts taste — dental plaque, periodontitis (bacterial volatile sulphur compounds), and poor mucosal health all impair taste perception. Twice-daily brushing (electric toothbrush) with fluoride toothpaste + daily flossing + tongue cleaning (tongue scraper), denture care, regular dental and hygienist appointments; treat any active periodontal or dental infection and confirmed candidiasis promptly. Use chlorhexidine only for a clear dental/oral indication and for a limited duration — it can itself cause taste disturbance, unpleasant taste, mucosal irritation, staining and allergy, and is not a treatment for unexplained dysgeusia.
Flavour intensification for reduced taste Increase flavour intensity rather than adding salt or sugar excessively: herbs, spices, garlic, lemon; umami foods (MSG, soy sauce, miso, parmesan, mushrooms, tomatoes) activate preserved umami receptors at reduced concentrations. Spices (black pepper, chilli, mustard) stimulate trigeminal nerve endings — still perceived with ageusia. Texture contrast (crunchy with soft) and temperature variation compensate for reduced taste. Plastic cutlery if metallic dysgeusia.
Zinc-rich foods For zinc deficiency contributing to taste disorder: red meat (beef, lamb), oysters (highest zinc density), shellfish, poultry, eggs, legumes (chickpeas, lentils, kidney beans), seeds (pumpkin, hemp), nuts (cashews, almonds), fortified breakfast cereals. Vegetarians/vegans: fortified foods; soaking and sprouting legumes/seeds reduces phytate content, improving zinc bioavailability.
Nutritional monitoring Monitor weight, appetite and dietary intake — taste loss reduces appetite and food enjoyment. Dietitian referral if clinically significant weight loss, malnutrition risk or difficulty maintaining adequate intake. Individualise protein and calorie targets, particularly in CKD and older adults. Fortified foods and oral nutritional supplements for older patients with anorexia from dysgeusia; small, frequent, calorie-dense meals.
Parosmia dietary management Identify specific trigger foods (common: coffee, meat, onions, eggs, garlic, alcohol) with a food diary. Avoid major triggers during the most severe phase while maintaining adequate nutrition with acceptable foods; smoothies and soups help disguise trigger smells. Dietitian referral if significant weight loss.
Smoking cessation Smoking causes dose-dependent, cumulative taste impairment (direct toxic damage to taste receptor cells, reduced olfactory function, reduced and altered saliva). Taste and smell may improve within weeks of stopping — a concrete, patient-relevant motivational message. NHS Stop Smoking Service referral.
Alcohol reduction Chronic alcohol use causes B vitamin (B1, B12, folate) and zinc deficiency, direct taste impairment and reduced saliva quality. AUDIT-C screening and brief intervention. Full AUDIT if AUDIT-C is 5 or more; specialist alcohol service referral if the AUDIT score suggests dependence (NICE CG115).
Hydration and salivary stimulation Sip water regularly to maintain salivary flow. Sugar-free gum or lozenges (xylitol-containing) after meals. Biotène gel at night; bedroom humidifier. Avoid alcohol-containing mouthwashes and limit caffeine and alcohol (dry mouth). Review anticholinergic and other xerogenic medicines.
Medication review habit Dysgeusia is one of the most under-reported drug side effects — ask specifically: "Has your sense of taste changed since starting any of your medications?" Identifying the culprit drug is the single most rewarding intervention in taste loss management.
Support resources AbScent (abscent.org): free smell training resources, parosmia/anosmia community. Fifth Sense (fifthsense.org.uk): UK charity for smell and taste disorders — helpline, patient stories, medical resources. OlfactoryMatters (olfactorymatters.com): further research and clinical resources.
Psychological support Persistent taste loss or dysgeusia significantly impacts quality of life: reduced enjoyment of meals, social isolation, weight loss, depression and anxiety. Acknowledge: "Not being able to taste your food properly affects much more than just meals." PHQ-9 at each review; NHS Talking Therapies referral if depression. Mindfulness-based approaches for parosmia (focusing on texture, colour and temperature of food).
The umami receptor strategy is scientifically grounded — the umami receptor (T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer, sensitive to glutamate and nucleotides) is often partially preserved in conditions causing dysgeusia. Umami-rich foods (parmesan, soy sauce, miso, anchovies, sun-dried tomatoes, dried shiitake mushrooms) make food more palatable even when sweet/salty/bitter perception is diminished; this comes from culinary medicine and oncology nutrition, where umami enhancement is standard advice for chemotherapy-related dysgeusia. The AbScent charity was founded by a chemosensory scientist who herself experienced post-viral anosmia and provides smell-training guides, a peer-support community (particularly valuable for parosmia) and clinician information; Fifth Sense provides similar UK support. GPs should routinely signpost post-viral taste/smell patients to these resources — combined with GP support (olfactory training, mental health assessment) this improves wellbeing and adherence.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Post-viral taste disorder (including COVID)
Follow-up at 3 months: improvement? Olfactory training being done correctly? Parosmia developed? PHQ-9 for depression (extremely common). If unexplained and persistent beyond 3 months: consider neuroimaging (NICE NG127) and ENT assessment; targeted bloods only (zinc only if risk factors support testing); neurology if neurological features. Persistent isolated ageusia with normal smell at 3 months → ENT/specialist taste assessment.
Post Bell's palsy / Ramsay Hunt
Review taste and facial nerve recovery: at 1 week (not worsening), 3 weeks (partial recovery expected) and 3 months. No sign of improvement by 3 weeks → urgent ENT referral. Persistent weakness or synkinesis, or not recovering as expected: facial-palsy/ENT/neurology pathway (specialist decides on EMG and rehabilitation). Eye: corneal health (ophthalmology if any eye complications). Avoid labelling persistent taste loss as permanent without specialist assessment.
Drug-induced dysgeusia
Follow-up at 4–8 weeks after stopping/changing the drug: taste normalising? Recovery varies by drug; terbinafine-related dysgeusia may persist for months (prolonged tissue half-life). If not resolving — consider other causes (nutritional, structural). If the drug cannot be changed: tolerance often develops over months.
Nutritional / zinc replacement
Review according to cause: deficiency corrected? Taste improving (a repeat serum zinc alone does not establish response)? Weight stable? Avoid long-term high-dose zinc without monitoring (zinc and copper if supplementation is prolonged). Address the underlying cause and ongoing dietary adequacy. Persistent symptoms despite correction of reversible factors → reconsider the diagnosis and ENT/neurology assessment.
Call 999 / go to A&E now
Facial weakness with new limb weakness or speech disturbance (stroke) · Seizure, reduced consciousness or meningism · Fever or feeling unwell on chemotherapy (neutropenic sepsis — or call the acute oncology helpline immediately) · On digoxin or lithium with collapse, palpitations, very slow pulse or confusion
Same day — contact the GP / NHS 111
New taste loss with facial weakness or numbness (Bell's / Ramsay Hunt), ear pain or rash in or around the ear · Eye pain, visual change or unable to close the eye → urgent ophthalmology · New difficulty swallowing, slurred speech or tongue/palate weakness · Metallic taste with nausea, vomiting, visual disturbance, confusion, tremor or unsteadiness on digoxin or lithium → urgent ECG, drug level and U&E; withhold the drug and seek acute medical advice · Mouth ulcers or sore throat on methotrexate · Recurrent taste hallucinations
Within a week — see the GP
Worsening of taste disorder despite treatment · New dysphagia or hoarse voice appearing during follow-up · Taste loss + new neck lump, or a mouth ulcer, lump or red/white patch persisting beyond 3 weeks (suspected cancer pathway assessment) · Clinically significant weight loss (dietitian + nutritional screen) · New unilateral nasal blockage or bleeding · Facial palsy not recovering as expected · Persistent unexplained taste loss at 3 months → re-assess, investigate, refer
Most Bell's palsy patients achieve complete or near-complete recovery with corticosteroid treatment within months. However, a minority have incomplete recovery, and some develop synkinesis (involuntary facial movements linked to intentional movements, caused by aberrant nerve regeneration — e.g. the eye closes when smiling), hemifacial spasm, or crocodile tears (lacrimation when eating). Persistent weakness or synkinesis is best managed through a facial-palsy/ENT/neurology pathway rather than a routine EMG at a fixed time point — specialist assessment may include EMG, facial rehabilitation physiotherapy, botulinum toxin for synkinesis and, in selected cases, surgery. The GP should ensure these patients have follow-up arranged rather than being discharged with incomplete recovery. For persistent unexplained taste loss despite correction of reversible factors, specialist smell and taste services can provide electrogustometry, formal threshold testing and access to research, even if access is geographically limited.
