1
Safety

Red Flags — Cord Compression, MSCC & Motor Neurone Disease

Foot drop is a sign — not a diagnosis. Localise the lesion first. UMN foot drop (spasticity, hyperreflexia, upgoing plantars) suggests a spinal cord or brain lesion — urgency depends on onset, progression, associated cord signs and clinical stability. Do not attribute to peripheral nerve without full neurological examination.

Sudden-onset limb weakness can be a stroke even when restricted to one limb and even when FAST is negative — exclude hypoglycaemia and use the local stroke pathway (NICE NG128). Severe or rapidly progressive bilateral weakness, especially with bulbar or respiratory symptoms, or foot drop with reduced consciousness, severe systemic illness or suspected sepsis → emergency hospital assessment.

Bilateral foot drop + saddle anaesthesia + sphincter dysfunction Cauda equina syndrome — immediate emergency assessment for severe low-back pain radiating into the leg with any new urinary retention, difficulty initiating urination or altered urinary sensation, urinary or faecal incontinence, loss of anal tone, saddle/perineal sensory disturbance, sexual dysfunction, or bilateral sciatica / progressive leg weakness (NICE NG127). Urgent MRI and management via the local spinal pathway — there is no universal 48-hour surgical rule. Foot drop alone does not diagnose cauda equina syndrome.
Foot drop + spastic paraparesis + hyperreflexia + upgoing plantars Cervical or thoracic myelopathy — spinal cord compression above the L4/L5 level. UMN signs (Babinski, clonus, hyperreflexia, spasticity) with progression, gait disturbance or sphincter symptoms, or severe/rapidly worsening signs → emergency assessment and urgent spinal MRI. Stable, non-progressive UMN signs still need urgent specialist assessment. Fever or systemic illness with back pain and neurological deficit (possible spinal infection) → emergency hospital assessment.
Known malignancy + new back pain + any leg weakness Malignant spinal cord compression (MSCC) — metastasis from lung, breast, prostate, myeloma, lymphoma. Immediately contact the local MSCC coordinator or acute oncology service for new limb weakness or foot drop, gait disturbance, sensory loss/paraesthesia or sensory level, radicular or band-like pain, bladder/bowel dysfunction, or progressive, severe, mechanical or nocturnal spinal pain or pain aggravated by coughing/straining. Oncological emergency: whole-spine MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 hours when neurological signs or symptoms are present (NICE NG234). With neurological symptoms/signs: dexamethasone 16 mg orally (or parenteral equivalent) as soon as possible per local MSCC pathway, continue 16 mg daily until surgery/radiotherapy, reduce gradually afterwards, stop if MSCC excluded. Discuss urgently with the specialist first if lymphoma or another haematological malignancy is possible. Do not give dexamethasone routinely for unexplained UMN foot drop without suspected MSCC.
Foot drop + contralateral arm weakness or facial weakness Cortical or internal capsule lesion — anterior cerebral artery stroke (leg > arm weakness), parasagittal meningioma, or brain tumour affecting the leg area of the motor homunculus. Sudden onset → 999 / stroke pathway even if FAST-negative; exclude hypoglycaemia (NICE NG128). Gradual onset with contralateral limb or face involvement → urgent specialist assessment and brain imaging.
Progressive weakness + fasciculations + no sensory loss + hyperreflexia AND wasting in the same muscles Motor neurone disease — combined UMN and LMN signs raise concern for MND but are not diagnostic; structural, inflammatory, metabolic and peripheral nerve causes must be excluded. Look for progressive spread, wasting, fasciculations, stiffness, brisk reflexes, bulbar or respiratory symptoms. Refer to neurology without delay and state the MND concern in the referral (NICE NG42). MND Association: 0808 802 6262.
Foot drop + systemic features (weight loss, fever, night sweats) Spinal infection (epidural abscess — Staphylococcus aureus, TB / Pott's disease), primary or metastatic spinal tumour, or paraneoplastic neuropathy. Any combination of unexplained foot drop + systemic symptoms = serious spinal or systemic pathology → emergency assessment if fever/systemic illness with back pain and neurological deficit; otherwise urgent specialist assessment and imaging via the appropriate pathway.
The UMN versus LMN distinction in foot drop determines the entire investigation and management pathway. Upper motor neurone foot drop (brain or spinal cord lesion above L4) is characterised by: spastic increased tone, hyperreflexia, extensor plantar response (Babinski sign upgoing), clonus, minimal wasting early on. Lower motor neurone foot drop (nerve root, plexus, or peripheral nerve) is characterised by: flaccid weakness, absent or reduced reflexes, progressive wasting with time, fasciculations (anterior horn cell or nerve root lesion), sensory loss in the relevant territory. The Babinski sign is the most important single finding — a unilateral upgoing plantar response in a patient with foot drop means the lesion is in the corticospinal tract or cortex, regardless of the pattern of weakness. Malignant spinal cord compression (MSCC) is an oncological emergency. NICE NG234 advises contacting the MSCC coordinator immediately for suspected MSCC and arranging whole-spine MRI as soon as possible, always within 24 hours when neurological signs or symptoms are present; dexamethasone 16 mg is for people with neurological symptoms or signs of MSCC, not for unexplained UMN foot drop. Early treatment, before severe deficit, gives the best chance of preserving mobility. MND may present with isolated unexplained weakness and cannot be diagnosed from foot drop, fasciculations or a single UMN/LMN pattern alone. Key alert: painless progressive asymmetric limb weakness without sensory symptoms, particularly with fasciculations or mixed UMN and LMN signs = refer to neurology without delay and specify the concern (NICE NG42).

Anatomical Localisation — Where Is the Lesion?

Common peroneal nerve at fibular head (most common cause)
The CPN wraps superficially around the fibular neck, where it is vulnerable to pressure. Results in: weak dorsiflexion + weak eversion, preserved inversion, variable sensory change over dorsum of foot + lateral lower leg; no upper motor neurone signs. Causes: prolonged leg crossing, hospital bed positioning (lateral rotation), plaster cast pressure, squatting, weight loss (loss of peroneal fat pad), ganglion cyst, Baker's cyst, fibular head fracture. No back pain. No SLR.
L4/L5 nerve root radiculopathy
L4/L5 disc herniation or foraminal stenosis: weak dorsiflexion + weak eversion AND weak inversion (tibialis posterior = L5 root, not CPN) + back pain ± radiation + positive SLR ≤60°. Sensory loss: dorsum of foot (L5) or medial foot/ankle (L4). Weak inversion suggests root or more proximal lesion rather than CPN alone, but no single muscle test is definitive. Reflexes may be preserved. Imaging is not routine for uncomplicated sciatica (NICE NG59) — see Investigations.
Lumbosacral plexopathy
Multiple root levels affected simultaneously. Severe unilateral leg pain preceding weakness (diabetic amyotrophy — Bruns-Garland syndrome). Also: retroperitoneal haematoma (anticoagulants), pelvic malignancy, post-radiation, obstetric injury. NCS/EMG + MRI lumbosacral plexus (specialist). Weight loss + asymmetric leg weakness = malignant plexopathy until excluded.
Spinal cord (myelopathy)
Cervical or thoracic cord compression: UMN foot drop (spastic, hyperreflexic, upgoing plantars), may be bilateral or progressive, sensory level, gait disturbance, bladder or bowel dysfunction, neck or thoracic pain, possible arm involvement with cervical disease. MS (common in young adults — Lhermitte's sign, relapsing course). Urgent spinal imaging (usually MRI of relevant region or whole spine); emergency assessment if progressive, with sphincter symptoms or gait disturbance.
Motor neurone disease (ALS)
Progressive painless asymmetric weakness, wasting, fasciculations, stiffness, mixed UMN + LMN signs, bulbar or respiratory symptoms, no sensory symptoms, no identifiable compressive cause — raises concern but is not diagnostic alone. Foot drop is a common early ALS manifestation — often mistaken for L5 radiculopathy or CPN palsy initially. NCS normal sensory; EMG shows widespread denervation beyond a single nerve territory. Refer to neurology without delay, stating the MND concern (NICE NG42).
Brain / cortical
Anterior cerebral artery stroke (leg > arm area of motor cortex), parasagittal meningioma, cerebral tumour, MS cortical plaque. Contralateral face or arm involvement (even subtle), cortical signs (dysphasia, neglect, visual field deficit, apraxia), ataxia, headache, seizures or altered consciousness suggest intracranial origin. Sudden onset → stroke pathway; otherwise urgent specialist assessment and brain imaging.
Localisation patterns
Dorsiflexion + eversion weak, inversion relatively preserved → common peroneal neuropathy, particularly at fibular head · Dorsiflexion, eversion + inversion weak → L5 radiculopathy or more proximal lesion · Great-toe extension weak with L5 sensory symptoms → L5 radiculopathy · Plantarflexion weak with reduced ankle jerk → S1 or tibial-nerve involvement · Spasticity, brisk reflexes, clonus or extensor plantar → UMN lesion (brain or spinal cord) · Weakness with wasting and fasciculations → LMN or motor neurone disorder; specialist assessment. No single muscle test reliably distinguishes all peripheral nerve and root lesions — interpret with pain, sensory loss, reflexes and the rest of the examination.
The anatomical localisation of foot drop is one of the most intellectually satisfying clinical exercises in general practice because the neurological examination findings directly map to the anatomy. The single most useful examination test is tibialis posterior function (foot inversion): preserved inversion + weak eversion + weak dorsiflexion = CPN at fibular head (tibialis posterior is supplied by the tibial nerve, not the CPN). Weak inversion + weak eversion + weak dorsiflexion = L5 nerve root or more proximal lesion (all three movements involve L5 root-innervated muscles). This is quick to examine and helps separate the two most common causes, but no single muscle test is definitive — interpret it with pain, sensory loss, reflexes and the rest of the examination. Diabetic amyotrophy (Bruns-Garland syndrome) deserves emphasis as a cause of foot drop that is frequently misdiagnosed. It presents as acute severe unilateral thigh and hip pain (often worse at night) followed within days to weeks by rapid-onset proximal and sometimes distal leg weakness including foot drop. The pain precedes and is more prominent than the weakness — the opposite of most disc herniations. It is caused by ischaemic injury to the lumbosacral plexus (vasculitis of the vasa nervorum supplying the plexus) in the setting of diabetes. The clinical picture of a diabetic patient with severe unilateral leg pain + weakness + weight loss mimics malignant plexopathy, and both must be excluded. NCS/EMG is essential to characterise the distribution of nerve damage. Recovery over months to years is the norm with strict glycaemic control, but complete recovery is uncommon. Recognising this as a distinct diabetic complication (rather than attributing it to L5 disc herniation) ensures the patient receives the correct investigation and management.

Diagnose

Targeted Neurological Examination

History — clarify
Exact onset (sudden, days, weeks, months) · progression (static, improving, worsening) · unilateral or bilateral · low-back, radicular, hip or knee pain · numbness, paraesthesia or sensory level · bladder, bowel or sexual symptoms · saddle/perineal sensory change · falls, tripping, functional impairment · recent surgery, prolonged bed rest, anaesthesia or admission · leg crossing, prolonged squatting/kneeling, pressure at fibular head · weight loss · plaster cast, tight splint, knee injury or local trauma · diabetes, alcohol excess, renal disease, nutritional deficiency, systemic inflammatory disease · current or previous cancer (breast, lung, prostate, renal, thyroid, myeloma, lymphoma) · family history of neurological disease · bulbar or respiratory symptoms, fasciculations, cramps, weakness elsewhere · occupation and driving requirements.
Gait observation
Steppage gait (high-stepping hip and knee flexion to clear the dropped foot). Observe foot clearance, toe drag, circumduction or hip hiking, knee or ankle collapse, spastic gait, balance and falls risk — assess whether the patient can walk safely. Heel-walking screens for dorsiflexion weakness — do not ask the patient to heel-walk unsupervised if at risk of falling. Observe foot clearance, toe drag, circumduction (hemiplegic gait), bilateral steppage (systemic neuropathy or bilateral cord lesion).
Ankle dorsiflexion power (MRC grading)
"Pull your foot up towards your head against my hand." MRC 5 = normal; 4 = weak against resistance; 3 = against gravity only; 2 = with gravity eliminated; 1 = flicker; 0 = nil. Record MRC grades with side-to-side comparison for hip flexion/extension/abduction, knee flexion/extension, ankle dorsiflexion, great-toe extension, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion. MRC ≤3, particularly if not improving, warrants urgent neurology/specialist referral. Serial grading at each follow-up quantifies recovery or deterioration — "some weakness" is insufficient documentation.
Eversion vs inversion — the key test
Eversion (turn sole outward): peroneus longus/brevis — common peroneal nerve (superficial branch). Inversion (turn sole inward + plantarflex): tibialis posterior — tibial nerve from L5 root. CPN palsy at fibular head: weak eversion + weak dorsiflexion, normal inversion. L5 radiculopathy: weak eversion + weak dorsiflexion + weak inversion. This comparison helps separate the two most common causes, but no single muscle test reliably distinguishes all nerve and root lesions. Perform at every assessment.
Reflexes, tone and plantar responses
Knee jerk (L3/L4): preserved in CPN and L5 lesions; absent in L4. Ankle jerk (S1): preserved in CPN and L5; absent in S1 or tibial nerve. Plantar response: flexor (downgoing) = LMN or normal; extensor (Babinski upgoing) = UMN — cord or brain. Tone: spastic (UMN — increased, clasp-knife) vs flaccid (LMN — reduced). Sustained or clearly pathological ankle clonus, extensor plantars, a sensory level or spasticity → urgent assessment for spinal cord or brain disease. Check upper-limb reflexes if a cord or brain lesion is possible. Fasciculations: inspect tibialis anterior at rest (MND, L5 root irritation).
Sensory examination
Map and record the distribution rather than relying on a label. CPN: variable sensory loss over lateral lower leg + dorsum of foot; deep peroneal nerve: first web space. L5 root: often dorsum of foot + great toe, but dermatomes overlap. S1 root: lateral border of foot + heel + posterior calf. Tibial nerve: sole of foot. Map findings to dermatomal chart — document clearly. Check vibration and joint position sense if a posterior-column or generalised neuropathy is possible.
Fibular head and lumbar spine
Inspect and palpate fibular head for local swelling, trauma, scars or pressure injury. A positive Tinel's sign at the fibular head may support local peroneal nerve irritation but is neither necessary nor diagnostic. SLR (L4/L5/S1 root — supine, raise leg passively, positive ≤60° with sciatica reproduction). Slump test. Lumbar paraspinal tenderness and ROM. Femoral stretch test (L2/L3/L4 — prone, flex knee). Neck flexion/Lhermitte's (cord). Examine hip and knee where indicated, spine for focal tenderness, other limbs for weakness/wasting/fasciculations/sensory signs; cranial nerves, coordination and upper limbs if a central lesion is possible; assess respiratory function urgently if weakness is progressive or generalised.
The MRC power grade documentation is a clinical governance and medicolegal standard — a foot drop case with serial documented power grades (e.g., tibialis anterior MRC 2/5 at first visit → 3/5 at 6 weeks → 4/5 at 3 months) tells a coherent story of recovery that guides management decisions. Without baseline documentation, it is impossible to know if a patient is improving (reassuring conservative management) or static/deteriorating (indicating escalation to surgical intervention). Equally important from a medicolegal perspective: a patient who sustains a permanent foot drop and alleges that the GP failed to act promptly will have a much better outcome for the clinician if serial objective power grades were documented at each visit. Ankle clonus is an important reflex test for UMN pathology — sustained or clearly pathological clonus indicates a corticospinal tract lesion. Foot drop with sustained clonus, extensor plantars, a sensory level or spasticity should prompt urgent assessment for spinal cord or brain disease; urgency of imaging depends on progression, associated cord signs and clinical stability. Never record this as "normal" without actually testing it. A positive Tinel's sign at the fibular head may support local peroneal nerve irritation, but it is neither necessary nor diagnostic — specialist neurophysiology localises the lesion.

Investigations

Targeted primary-care bloods
Do not order a blanket panel for every foot drop. Consider: HbA1c + glucose (diabetic neuropathy, diabetic amyotrophy) · FBC · CRP and/or ESR (where infection, inflammatory disease, vasculitis or malignancy is clinically possible) · B12 + folate (peripheral neuropathy contributing to functional threshold) · TSH (hypothyroid neuropathy) · U&E + eGFR (uraemic neuropathy) · LFTs (alcohol neuropathy) · CK (if myopathy possible) · other tests guided by history and examination
Only if clinically indicated
Serum protein electrophoresis + serum free light chains where features suggest paraproteinaemia or myeloma (anaemia, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia, weight loss, persistent bone pain). Do not routinely request tumour markers (CA125, CEA, AFP), PSA simply because of foot drop, ANA/ANCA/RF/anti-CCP or IGRA without supporting clinical features.
Imaging — targeted, not routine
NICE NG59 advises against routine non-specialist imaging for uncomplicated low-back pain or sciatica — but significant, progressive or unexplained objective weakness is not uncomplicated sciatica and warrants urgent specialist assessment. Do not routinely request MRI of spine, brain and plexus simultaneously. Suspected MSCC urgent whole-spine MRI, always within 24 h if neurological signs/symptoms · Suspected cauda equina emergency spinal assessment + urgent MRI via local pathway · UMN signs, bilateral weakness, sensory level or bladder/bowel symptoms urgent spinal imaging, usually MRI of relevant region or whole spine · Sudden focal deficit emergency stroke assessment + brain imaging in hospital · Persistent foot drop without red flags specialist assessment; MRI lumbar spine if L5 radiculopathy suspected and result will alter management · Suspected MND or generalised neuropathy neurology with neurophysiology.
Nerve conduction studies (NCS) + EMG
Specialist investigations — must not delay emergency imaging or referral where cord compression, cauda equina, MSCC or stroke is suspected. Arranged by neurology. Confirms CPN palsy: reduced peroneal conduction velocity / amplitude across fibular head, localises to fibular head vs sciatic nerve vs L5 root. EMG: denervation in tibialis anterior without tibialis posterior involvement = CPN distribution (excludes L5 root). For MND: widespread denervation beyond one nerve territory. Essential before surgical decompression at fibular head.
Ultrasound / MRI fibular head
Not a standard first-line investigation. Specialist neurophysiology may localise a suspected focal peroneal neuropathy; ultrasound or MRI around the fibular head may be considered if a structural lesion (e.g. ganglion cyst, Baker's cyst) is suspected.
The choice between MRI lumbar spine and MRI whole spine is one of the most important imaging decisions in foot drop management. NICE NG59 advises against routine imaging for uncomplicated sciatica in a non-specialist setting; MRI lumbar spine is arranged when L5 radiculopathy is suspected and the result is likely to change management. MRI whole spine is required when: there are any UMN signs (the cord lesion may be cervical or thoracic — ordering lumbar MRI alone will be reported as normal and the myelopathy will continue to progress), when the foot drop is bilateral (single L4/L5 disc herniation very rarely causes bilateral foot drop), when the patient has known malignancy (MSCC can be at any spinal level), or when the clinical picture is atypical. A common medicolegal error pattern is: patient presents with foot drop + spasticity + upgoing plantar → GP orders MRI lumbar spine → reported as "mild degenerative changes" → myelopathy continues for months → delayed diagnosis of cervical myelopathy or thoracic MSCC. The lesson: if in doubt, order whole spine or let neurology arrange imaging. A ganglion cyst at the fibular head, typically arising from the proximal tibiofibular joint, is a structural cause of CPN palsy that may be surgically treatable. Ultrasound or MRI around the fibular head may be considered by the specialist if a structural lesion is suspected, particularly when there is no convincing compressive history or weakness fails to improve.

Referral Pathways & DVLA

999 / Same-day
Cauda equina features → immediate emergency assessment via local spinal pathway · Suspected MSCC → MSCC coordinator / acute oncology now · Sudden-onset foot drop or associated focal deficit → 999 / stroke pathway (even if FAST-negative) · Rapidly progressive weakness · UMN signs with progression, gait disturbance or sphincter symptoms · Fever or systemic illness with back pain and neurological deficit · Rapidly progressive bilateral weakness or bulbar/respiratory symptoms
Urgent neurology / specialist (generally within 2 weeks or per local pathway)
New foot drop without an obvious reversible compressive cause · Progressive weakness over days to weeks · MRC ≤3, particularly if not improving · Bilateral foot drop without cauda equina features · Suspected MND (refer without delay — NICE NG42) · Suspected vasculitic neuropathy or mononeuritis multiplex · Persistent or progressive foot drop with inconclusive initial assessment · Any foot drop with brisk reflexes or other signs of brain/spinal disease
Neurosurgery / spinal surgery
Use the local spinal or neurology pathway rather than a generic "after 4–6 weeks" rule — progressive motor deficit may justify earlier spinal surgical assessment. NICE NG59: consider spinal decompression for sciatica when non-surgical treatment has not improved pain or function and imaging is concordant. Cervical myelopathy + UMN foot drop → anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. CPN compression from structural lesion (ganglion cyst, osteochondroma) → surgical decompression via neurosurgery or orthopaedics. Cauda equina (from referral step above).
Orthotics (urgent AFO)
Assess falls risk immediately. Refer to orthotics if foot clearance is impaired or falls risk is significant — do not wait for investigation results. Consider an ankle–foot orthosis based on functional assessment and the underlying diagnosis. NHS orthotics referral via GP or physiotherapy.
Physiotherapy + rehabilitation
Consider physiotherapy for gait assessment and retraining, strength and range of movement, balance and proprioception, safe use of walking aids, falls prevention, and functional goals including return to work. Stroke-related foot drop: manage through the stroke pathway — AFO (NICE NG236) or FES depending on goals, gait pattern and rehabilitation assessment. FES assessment via neurology/rehabilitation medicine for UMN foot drop (stroke, MS, incomplete SCI) — NICE HTG178 (formerly IPG278, 2009). Specialist neurological physiotherapy for MND (pacing, energy conservation, postural management).
Driving and DVLA
The side of the foot drop does not by itself determine DVLA notification, and an automatic car does not automatically make left foot drop safe. The test is whether the person can safely control the vehicle — braking, acceleration, steering, clutch where applicable and emergency manoeuvres. Advise: do not drive if foot drop or associated weakness makes control unsafe; there is a legal duty to notify DVLA if the condition is likely to affect safe driving or persists as a relevant disability; DVLA, not the GP, decides licensing and may request medical information or a driving assessment; check current GOV.UK guidance on limb disability and driving. If the patient refuses to stop driving despite a significant safety concern, follow GMC confidentiality guidance and consider notifying DVLA after telling the patient. Document the advice, the patient's understanding, current driving status, vehicle type and any occupational-driving implications.
The urgency of surgical referral for L4/L5 disc herniation causing foot drop is determined by the power grade and the trajectory, using the local spinal or neurology pathway rather than a generic waiting period — NICE NG59 does not set a universal waiting period for foot drop, and progressive motor deficit may justify earlier spinal surgical assessment. Mild, stable weakness can be managed conservatively initially with close follow-up, but any deterioration mandates prompt referral. The orthosis does not treat the cause but helps prevent secondary harm — falls and fall-related injuries in patients who drag the foot — so assess falls risk early and refer to orthotics when foot clearance is impaired or falls risk is significant. Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES) for UMN foot drop is supported by NICE HTG178 (formerly IPG278, 2009; normal arrangements for clinical governance, consent and audit) and is underused in UK clinical practice — in the evidence reviewed for NICE HTG178, walking speed rose by a mean of 27% in a case series of 111 people after stroke using surface FES, and by 23% (vs 3% with conventional therapy) in a small RCT of implanted FES; a meta-analysis found a mean gain of 0.18 m/s over conventional therapy. NHS FES services are available through rehabilitation medicine and neurology departments — GPs should be aware of this option for stroke patients who have persistent foot drop despite physiotherapy.

Treat

Conservative Management

AFO — Ankle-Foot Orthosis
Rigid polypropylene AFO (holds foot at 90° — prevents toe-drag). Hinged AFO (allows plantarflexion — better for active patients with preserved plantarflexion). Posterior leaf spring (lightweight flexible — mild foot drop only). Prescription: NHS orthotics referral. Must fit comfortably inside wide-fitting shoe (advise patient to buy shoes with extra toe box depth). Choice based on functional assessment and the underlying diagnosis. Inspect skin regularly once an orthosis is supplied, particularly with sensory loss or diabetes.
CPN compression — remove the cause
Cease leg crossing (most common avoidable cause). Reposition in hospital bed (lateral rotation padding at fibular head — foam wedge). Remove or bivalve plaster cast. Avoid squatting for prolonged periods. Pad the fibular head with foam or gel padding if pressure is unavoidable. Arrange physiotherapy and orthotic assessment if needed, and specialist follow-up if weakness is severe, progressive or fails to improve. Do not promise recovery within a fixed period — it depends on whether the lesion is demyelinating or axonal and on the degree and duration of nerve injury.
L4/L5 radiculopathy analgesia
Encourage appropriate activity; avoid prolonged bed rest. Consider an NSAID (e.g. ibuprofen 400 mg TDS or naproxen 500 mg BD) only after assessing gastrointestinal, renal, hepatic and cardiovascular risk — lowest effective dose for the shortest period, consider gastroprotection; NICE NG59 advises against paracetamol alone for low back pain. Do not routinely offer opioids for acute low-back pain — if considered, short-term only when NSAIDs are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective. NICE NG59 (2016, updated 2020; 2026 update left medicines advice unchanged): do not offer gabapentinoids (pregabalin, gabapentin), other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids or benzodiazepines (e.g. diazepam) for sciatica — no overall benefit and evidence of harm. Do not offer opioids for chronic sciatica; avoid prolonged opioids — poor long-term evidence, high dependence risk.
Physiotherapy for radiculopathy
Physiotherapy addresses gait retraining, falls prevention, strength and range of movement, balance and proprioception, safe use of walking aids, and functional goals including return to work. Do not present McKenzie press-ups, nerve glides or a fixed exercise schedule as universally effective or appropriate — exercises should be selected after assessment and modified if they worsen radicular pain or neurological deficit.
Neuropathic pain for CPN + radiculopathy
Non-radicular peripheral neuropathic pain (e.g. peroneal neuropathy — burning, electric, allodynia): NICE CG173 allows a choice of amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin, with regular review and attention to renal function, sedation, dependence, respiratory depression and driving. This is distinct from sciatica / L5 radiculopathy, for which NICE NG59 advises against gabapentinoids. Gabapentinoids are Schedule 3 controlled drugs — a substance-misuse history requires careful risk assessment, monitoring and compliance with controlled-drug requirements rather than being an absolute bar. Follow the current Greater Manchester (GMMMG) gabapentinoid pathway for sequencing and controlled-drug requirements. Review pain regularly and document response.
Physiotherapy for radiculopathy should be individualised: exercises (including McKenzie-type extension or nerve glides) are selected after assessment and modified if they worsen radicular pain or neurological deficit — no single programme or fixed schedule is universally effective. Diazepam for acute lumbar muscle spasm is a common habit but NICE NG59 advises not to offer benzodiazepines for low back pain or for sciatica — there is no overall evidence of benefit and there is evidence of harm, and the paraspinal spasm of acute disc herniation settles with time, activity and NSAID analgesia. GPs should explain this to patients who expect a muscle relaxant. Prescribing diazepam, especially as a PRN for recurring back pain episodes, creates iatrogenic benzodiazepine dependence — this is a well-documented prescribing error in back pain management. The same NG59 recommendation covers pregabalin and gabapentin for sciatica: the neuropathic pain guideline (CG173) applies to other neuropathic pain such as a peroneal neuropathy, not to sciatica. NICE NG59 also advises not offering opioids for chronic sciatica.

Treat

Cause-Specific Treatment

Surgical decompression (CPN structural compression)
Ganglion cyst or osteochondroma at fibular head compressing CPN: surgical excision via neurosurgery or orthopaedics. Post-operative physiotherapy essential. Specialist follow-up with serial power grading; recovery varies with the degree and duration of nerve injury.
L4/L5 discectomy
Decided by the spinal surgical team via the local pathway — no universal waiting period. NICE NG59: consider spinal decompression for sciatica when non-surgical treatment has not improved pain or function and imaging is concordant; progressive motor deficit may justify earlier assessment. Post-operative physiotherapy and graded return to activity per surgical team.
Stroke rehabilitation (UMN foot drop)
Manage through the stroke pathway and specialist rehabilitation (NICE NG236). Treatment may include physiotherapy, an ankle–foot orthosis (where swing-phase foot clearance or stance-phase control affects walking; assessed and provided by qualified professionals as part of stroke rehabilitation) or FES depending on goals, gait pattern and rehabilitation assessment — NICE HTG178 (formerly IPG278, 2009) for FES in UMN foot drop (stroke, MS, incomplete SCI). Botulinum toxin injection to plantarflexors (if calf spasticity limiting AFO use — specialist rehabilitation medicine). Secondary prevention: antiplatelet + statin + antihypertensive + lifestyle modification.
MND — multidisciplinary management
Do not initiate disease-modifying treatment in primary care — neurology/MND services coordinate diagnosis, riluzole, respiratory assessment, orthotics, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, nutrition, psychological support and advance care planning (NICE NG42). Edaravone (IV infusion) is not licensed in the UK. Multidisciplinary MND team: neurologist, MND specialist nurse, physiotherapist (pacing, splinting), occupational therapist, speech therapist, dietitian (PEG timing), respiratory team (NIV — non-invasive ventilation, extends life and improves quality). Advance care planning: early discussion of wishes, DNACPR, LPA. Referral to MND Association care network.
Vasculitic neuropathy (mononeuritis multiplex)
Systemic vasculitis treatment: prednisolone 60 mg/day initially (rheumatology/neurology-led). Cyclophosphamide for severe/ANCA-associated vasculitis (Wegener's/GPA). Rituximab for refractory cases. Diabetic vasculitic amyotrophy: strict glycaemic control + methylprednisolone pulse (specialist). Partial recovery expected over months with treatment. Serial NCS to monitor progress.
Riluzole for MND is the only licensed pharmacological treatment for ALS in the UK — it works by reducing glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity (a proposed mechanism of motor neurone death in ALS). The clinical benefit is modest, but it is currently the only available neuroprotective treatment in the UK and is started by the specialist team after diagnosis. The decision to start riluzole is made by the neurologist, and GPs continue the prescription — it requires LFT monitoring — ALT monthly for the first 3 months, then every 3 months for the rest of the first year, then periodically (BNF; hepatotoxic in approximately 2% of patients). The multidisciplinary approach to MND is the most evidence-based aspect of its management — studies show that patients managed in specialist MND multidisciplinary clinics (which include all the above specialists) have significantly better quality of life and longer survival compared to those managed by neurology alone. Respiratory assessment and the timing of non-invasive ventilation (NIV), decided by the MND/respiratory team, is one of the most important interventions, improving survival, sleep quality and dyspnoea. GPs should be proactive in connecting MND patients with the MND Association regional care network and specialist MND clinic at the time of diagnosis. The botulinum toxin injection to plantarflexors for stroke-related UMN foot drop is a specialist rehabilitation intervention that can significantly improve AFO tolerance — in stroke patients with spastic equinus (plantarflexion spasticity that holds the foot in a fixed dropped and inverted position), the rigidity of the spastic calf muscles makes AFO fitting and gait retraining difficult. Botulinum toxin injection (guided by ultrasound or EMG) to gastrocnemius and/or soleus reduces this spasticity, allowing the AFO to position the foot correctly and enabling more effective physiotherapy-guided gait training.

Lifestyle

Falls Prevention, Adaptation & Self-Management

AFO compliance and footwear Wear the AFO during all ambulation — not just outdoors. Ensure shoes accommodate the AFO (wider, deeper toe box — Cosyfeet, Hotter, or wide-fit shoes). Remove the AFO only for bathing and sleeping. Inspect the skin under the AFO daily (especially over the fibular head and malleoli) — pressure sores can develop undetected in patients with sensory loss. Report any redness or skin breakdown to GP or orthotist immediately.
Home hazard reduction Occupational therapy home assessment for trip hazards: loose rugs (remove), uneven thresholds (threshold ramps), poor lighting (install night lights on stairs). Bathroom: grab rails by toilet and shower, shower chair, non-slip mat. Stairs: bilateral handrails, stair lift if climbing stairs unsafe. Open-plan living on one level if stairs are a major barrier. NHS community OT referral — document and chase outcome.
Safe footwear Well-fitting, closed-toe shoes with a low flat heel, rigid sole, and good ankle support. Trainers with ankle support are appropriate. Avoid slippers (no grip, no ankle support — highest fall risk footwear in older adults). Avoid high heels, flip-flops, or any backless footwear. For neuropathic patients (diabetes): feet cannot feel pressure, heat, or stones — inspect inside shoes before putting them on every time.
Postural awareness and gait compensation Compensatory gait strategies while awaiting AFO or recovery: consciously lift the knee higher on the affected side (high-stepping). Slow down — rushing increases trip risk. Look where you are walking — visual compensation for proprioceptive loss. Tap the outside of the foot on the ground to confirm ground contact. Use a stick on the contralateral side (ipsilateral stick use is common but biomechanically incorrect — contralateral stick is more effective).
Driving safety Do not drive if foot drop or associated weakness makes safe control of the vehicle (braking, acceleration, steering, clutch, emergency manoeuvres) unsafe — this depends on function, not on which side is affected. Notify DVLA if the condition is likely to affect safe driving or persists as a relevant disability; DVLA, not the GP, decides licensing and may arrange a driving assessment. Check current GOV.UK limb disability guidance. Discuss alternative transport (community transport, taxis, Motability if eligible). Document the advice given.
Exercise and strengthening Exercises should be selected and progressed by a physiotherapist after assessment — no fixed exercise schedule is universally appropriate. Options may include dorsiflexor, calf and hip strengthening, balance and proprioception work, and hydrotherapy where available. Stop or modify any exercise that worsens radicular pain or weakness.
Psychosocial support Foot drop causes significant psychological impact: fear of falls, loss of independence, embarrassment, social withdrawal, inability to work. CBT for health anxiety and fear of falling (NHS Talking Therapies referral). MND: MND Association support groups, specialist social worker, carer support. Stroke Association (0303 3033 100) peer support and community re-engagement programmes. Signpost to PIP (Personal Independence Payment) and Blue Badge schemes where persistent mobility impairment may qualify — eligibility is decided by the scheme, not by the diagnosis.
Position avoidance (prevention) Patient education to prevent recurrent CPN compression: never cross legs for more than a few minutes. When hospitalised: request that the nursing team position the lateral fibula away from bed frame and mattress edge. Avoid prolonged squatting or kneeling and direct pressure on the fibular head. Be aware that marked weight loss is a recognised risk factor for peroneal compression.
The contralateral walking stick recommendation is frequently misunderstood — both patients and some clinicians instinctively use a walking stick on the same side as the weakness. However, the biomechanically correct position for a walking stick is the contralateral hand because: when the weak leg is in the stance phase (bearing weight), the contralateral arm is naturally forward (normal reciprocal arm swing). If the stick is in the contralateral hand, it contacts the ground simultaneously with the weak leg, providing lateral support at the moment of maximum weight-bearing on the affected side. This is how elderly people with hip OA are taught to use sticks, and it applies equally to foot drop where the weak ankle cannot resist lateral sway during stance. Using the stick on the same side as the weakness means it contacts the ground when the strong leg bears weight — providing no benefit at the moment it is most needed. Hydrotherapy, where available and selected by a physiotherapist, uses buoyancy to allow active dorsiflexion movements that may be impossible against gravity on land in severe foot drop. The skin inspection under the AFO is a patient safety instruction that is critical in patients with peripheral neuropathy (diabetic or otherwise) — sensory loss means that pressure sores developing under the AFO are not felt, and significant ulceration can occur before the patient notices. This is particularly relevant in diabetic patients, in whom foot ulcers substantially increase amputation risk. The instruction to inspect the skin daily, remove the AFO if any redness persists beyond 30 minutes after removal, and attend podiatry for any skin break must be given explicitly and documented.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Initial review — appropriate timeframe (sooner if severe or progressive)
Repeat and document MRC grades (compare to baseline — improving, static, or deteriorating?). Reassess gait and falls; check for new bilateral symptoms; recheck reflexes, tone and plantars; review sensory distribution and pain; check bladder, bowel and saddle symptoms. AFO fitted and worn? Orthotics referral outcome. Physiotherapy started? MRI result reviewed? Neurology referral appointment confirmed? Driving safety reassessed and DVLA advice documented. Any new symptoms (bilateral involvement, bladder/bowel, saddle anaesthesia) → immediate escalation. Pain control adequate — neuropathic medication effective?
Review at 3 months
CPN compression: if not improving after the compressive cause is removed, → NCS (if not already done) + neurosurgery/neurology escalation. L4/L5 disc: physiotherapy completed? Pain improving? If MRC still ≤3 → neurosurgery referral urgently. Functional review: walking, stairs, work, driving status.
Review at 6 months
Recovery depends on whether the lesion is demyelinating or axonal and on the degree and duration of injury — do not promise full recovery by a fixed time. Persisting weakness → specialist neurophysiology for axonal damage extent → prognosis discussion. L4/L5: if not operated, is recovery ongoing? Re-refer if plateau at <4/5. Document driving status: is driving safe, has DVLA been notified where required, has a driving assessment been arranged? Patient's occupation review.
MND — frequency increases
Monthly visits initially as disease progresses. Anticipatory prescribing (syringe driver for end-stage — morphine + midazolam + glycopyrronium). DNACPR discussion and documentation. Advance care plan. LPA (Lasting Power of Attorney) — advise early while patient has capacity. Carer support — GP letter for carer's allowance, social services referral, MND Association. Involve palliative care team from diagnosis.
999 / Same-day safety-net
Escalate immediately (emergency assessment via local pathway) for: new bladder, bowel or sexual dysfunction · saddle or perineal numbness · new bilateral leg weakness · rapid worsening of strength · new UMN signs or sensory level · new arm, facial, speech or visual symptoms (999 / stroke pathway) · cancer history with new spinal pain or neurological change (MSCC coordinator) · new swallowing difficulty, breathlessness at rest or when lying flat
Urgent GP same week
New contralateral leg symptoms developing (bilateral = cord or systemic neuropathy — escalate imaging) · Falls or injury related to foot drop since last visit · Pressure sore under AFO identified · Neuropathic pain not controlled despite adequate pharmacological treatment · Patient continuing to drive despite a significant safety concern — follow GMC confidentiality guidance; consider notifying DVLA after telling the patient
Documentation
Onset and progression · functional impact and falls · full neurological examination · MRC grades · reflexes, tone and plantar responses · sensory findings · red flags specifically assessed and present/absent · working localisation and differential diagnosis · investigations and results · referral urgency and pathway · driving advice · orthotic, physiotherapy and safety-netting plan
The serial MRC grade documentation at each follow-up visit is the single most important quality indicator for foot drop management — it is the metric that drives every key management decision: continue conservative management (improving), escalate investigation (static), refer urgently via the local spinal/neurology pathway (deteriorating, or MRC ≤3 not improving). A GP who documents MRC grades at every visit is able to make these decisions objectively. A GP who documents "foot drop — ongoing" without a power grade is unable to demonstrate appropriate clinical decision-making if the case is ever reviewed. A prognosis discussion for persisting CPN palsy is an important component of patient-centred care — recovery depends on whether the lesion is demyelinating or axonal and on the degree and duration of nerve injury, so avoid fixed recovery promises. This conversation should address: realistic functional prognosis, longer-term AFO requirements, driving safety (based on function, not side), and whether specialist review for surgical options is appropriate. The patient continuing to drive when foot drop makes vehicle control unsafe is a public safety issue — if a patient is known to the GP to be driving unsafely and refuses to stop or notify DVLA, the GP has a legal right (and in some circumstances an obligation) to notify the DVLA directly without patient consent, having first informed the patient of this intention. The GMC guidance "Confidentiality: patients' fitness to drive and reporting concerns to the DVLA" details this process. Documentation of the conversation, the patient's refusal, and the subsequent DVLA notification is essential.
