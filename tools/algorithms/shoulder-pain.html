Step 1

Safety — Red Flags & Emergency Exclusion

Safety
Screen for can't-miss diagnoses BEFORE assuming a musculoskeletal cause. Shoulder pain is a common cardiac and thoracic referred pain site.
Shoulder pain + chest discomfort, dyspnoea, diaphoresis, nausea, jaw/arm pain, syncope or exertional symptoms Possible acute coronary syndrome → immediate assessment; emergency transfer according to clinical severity 999. Perform an ECG without delaying transfer.
Shoulder tip pain + hypotension + recent trauma or surgery Sub-diaphragmatic haemorrhage (ruptured spleen, ectopic pregnancy) — referred diaphragmatic pain → 999
Hot, red, swollen joint + systemic fever + rigors Septic arthritis of glenohumeral joint — the joint is deep, so severe pain with marked restriction of passive movement, fever or systemic illness may be septic even without obvious erythema or swelling. Do not inject the joint or give a steroid injection until infection is excluded → Same-day ED / orthopaedics (aspiration, blood cultures, specialist management)
Shoulder pain + neurological deficit in arm / hand Pancoast tumour (apex lung) — brachial plexus invasion; also consider cervical cord compression (myelopathy → urgent MRI via spinal/neurology pathway; known cancer → MSCC pathway, NICE NG234) → Urgent CXR; suspected cancer pathway if CXR suggests lung cancer
Acute severe pain + unable to move shoulder + after dislocation Acute dislocation / fracture — anterior most common → Same-day ED. Check axillary nerve (sensation lateral deltoid).
Shoulder pain + weight loss + night pain + age >50 + no trauma Malignancy (primary or metastatic bone disease — breast, prostate, lung) → Imaging + targeted bloods as clinically indicated; CXR / suspected cancer pathway per NICE NG12 criteria
New major loss of active elevation with preserved passive movement Urgent assessment for acute rotator cuff tear, fracture, dislocation or neurological disease (axillary/suprascapular nerve injury, cervical radiculopathy, stroke); also acute inflammatory arthritis or pain inhibition. Urgency is greatest after trauma or with objective weakness → Urgent shoulder/orthopaedic referral if traumatic. Atraumatic degenerative cuff tears in older adults are common and do not automatically require urgent referral.
Age ≥50 + new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication or visual symptoms (incl. diplopia) Possible giant cell arteritis — shoulder pain alone is not a GCA indication. Start glucocorticoids promptly when suspicion is significant; do not wait for ESR/CRP or biopsy → Urgent local fast-track GCA pathway; same-day specialist/ophthalmology if visual symptoms
Acute shoulder pain + Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) Pancoast tumour involving stellate ganglion → Urgent CXR / urgent lung referral
Shoulder pain referred from cardiac or abdominal sources accounts for a significant proportion of missed diagnoses in primary care. Shoulder-tip pain may be referred from the diaphragm, pleura, gallbladder, liver, pericardium or other structures (e.g. sub-diaphragmatic haemorrhage, abscess, ectopic pregnancy) — interpret it in clinical context rather than as a specific "phrenic nerve" sign. Pancoast tumours are particularly important — they arise at the lung apex, can be hard to see on a standard CXR (a normal film does not exclude one if suspicion persists), and cause a characteristic triad of arm pain, Horner's syndrome, and hand weakness. NICE NG12 does not list shoulder pain itself as a criterion; the lung triggers are an urgent chest X-ray (within 2 weeks) for people aged 40 and over with the specified unexplained symptoms (e.g. weight loss in someone who has ever smoked), and a suspected cancer pathway referral if the X-ray suggests lung cancer or with unexplained haemoptysis at 40 and over. Septic arthritis of the glenohumeral joint carries significant morbidity and mortality and must be aspirated urgently.
Step 2

Diagnose — Structured History

Diagnose
A structured history narrows the differential before examination.
Onset & mechanism
Traumatic (fall on outstretched hand → RC tear, dislocation, ACJ injury) vs atraumatic (insidious → frozen shoulder, OA, tendinopathy, referred pain). Sudden severe → dislocation, massive RC tear. Gradual → rotator-cuff-related pain, frozen shoulder, OA.
Location & radiation
Anterior: AC joint, biceps tendon, GH joint. Lateral deltoid: rotator cuff, subacromial. Posterior: infraspinatus, posterior capsule. Radiation down arm with neck pain or dermatomal symptoms: consider cervical radiculopathy. Shoulder tip: referred from diaphragm, pleura, gallbladder, liver or pericardium — consider clinical context.
Movement restriction pattern
Painful arc: rotator-cuff-related pain, RC tear. Marked restriction of active and passive movement: adhesive capsulitis, GH OA, fracture, dislocation or septic arthritis. Active restricted, passive relatively preserved: cuff weakness/tear, pain inhibition or neurological disease. Painful active and passive movement: many conditions — not diagnostic alone. Painless full range: likely referred.
Night pain
Severe night pain waking from sleep is characteristic of rotator cuff tears and frozen shoulder. Also consider malignancy if age >50 + no mechanism
Associated symptoms
Neck pain + paraesthesia down arm → cervical origin. Clicking/clunking → instability, labral tear. Bilateral → inflammatory arthritis, PMR (age ≥50, bilateral shoulder/hip girdle pain and stiffness, raised inflammatory markers). Age ≥50 + new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication or visual symptoms → GCA. Preceding viral illness → reactive arthritis.
Occupation & activities
Overhead activity (painting, swimming, throwing) → rotator-cuff-related pain. Heavy lifting → RC tear. Sedentary + prolonged immobility → frozen shoulder risk (also: diabetes, thyroid disease).
Diabetes / thyroid
Diabetes mellitus 5× increased risk of adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder). Hypothyroidism associated. Always ask.
The movement restriction pattern is a useful, but not absolute, diagnostic discriminator. True frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) causes global restriction of both active AND passive movements — passive external rotation is the first and most severely restricted. In rotator cuff tears, passive movement is relatively preserved but active is restricted — as also seen with pain inhibition or neurological disease. A painful arc supports rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain but does not identify a single anatomical lesion. Diabetes is the strongest risk factor for frozen shoulder; prevalence is 10–20% in people with diabetes (vs 2–5% general population). Night pain is important — it should prompt active consideration of malignancy and RC tear, with imaging where it will change management.
Step 3

Diagnose — Classification by Diagnosis

Diagnose
Classify the shoulder condition to direct treatment. Most presentations fit one of seven patterns.
Rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain
Pain with active elevation, a painful arc and pain/weakness on resisted testing, with or without subacromial bursitis or tendinopathy. Worse overhead. Hawkins–Kennedy, Neer, Jobe and painful-arc tests are interpreted as a cluster — no single test confirms a cuff tear or proves subacromial impingement. No significant passive restriction. Most common shoulder diagnosis in primary care
Rotator cuff tear
Partial: similar to rotator-cuff-related pain + weakness. Full thickness: unable to actively abduct, lag signs positive (drop arm sign, external rotation lag). Passive range preserved. Distinguish acute traumatic tears from common atraumatic degenerative tears in older adults. USS or MRI if the result will change management
Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)
Pain and restriction of both active AND passive glenohumeral movement, particularly external rotation, after excluding other causes. Often described as freezing (pain dominant), frozen (stiffness dominant) and thawing phases, but the natural history is variable — recovery may take longer and some patients retain symptoms or limitation.
AC joint pathology
Localised tenderness at ACJ. Scarf test positive (cross-body adduction). Post-traumatic (Grade I–VI sprain), OA, osteolysis. No glenohumeral restriction.
GH osteoarthritis
Elderly, global restriction, crepitus. X-ray: loss of joint space, osteophytes. Less common than rotator-cuff-related pain. Consider referral for shoulder replacement when symptoms substantially affect quality of life and non-surgical management is ineffective or unsuitable (NICE NG226) — not on age, BMI or a score alone
Cervical referred pain
Neck pain ± radiating arm pain in dermatomal pattern. Full painless shoulder range (or minimally restricted). Dermatomal symptoms, sensory loss, reflex change or myotomal weakness → neurological assessment and cervical radiculopathy pathway (NICE NG127). Spurling's test may support but is not required.
Inflammatory / PMR
Bilateral shoulder/hip girdle pain and stiffness, age ≥50, raised inflammatory markers. Exclude mimics and assess for GCA.
Glenohumeral instability
Younger patients, history of dislocation or subluxation episodes. Apprehension test positive. May have labral tear (SLAP lesion). Shoulder/orthopaedic referral (imaging per specialist)
Accurate classification prevents inappropriate treatment and imaging. Rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain is the most common diagnosis but "impingement" is frequently over-diagnosed — cervical referred pain is commonly mistaken for shoulder pathology. The key differentiator is passive shoulder range: if passive movement is full and painless, the glenohumeral joint itself is unlikely to be the source. Frozen shoulder is frequently misdiagnosed and overtreated with cortisone without physiotherapy — the condition often improves but has a variable natural history, and treatment decisions should account for this. For suspected cuff tear, ultrasound or MRI may be appropriate depending on local expertise, availability and the clinical question — ultrasound is operator-dependent; do not routinely request both. ACJ pathology is identified by the scarf test and point tenderness at the joint line, not generalized shoulder pain.
Step 4

Diagnose — Targeted Examination

Diagnose
Examine systematically: Look → Feel → Move → Special Tests. Always examine the neck.
Inspection
Wasting (supraspinatus, infraspinatus fossa) → RC tear / nerve injury. Asymmetry, swelling. Scars. Winging of scapula (long thoracic nerve injury).
Palpation
ACJ tenderness (ACJ pathology). Greater tuberosity (RC insertion). Biceps groove (bicipital tendinopathy — Speed's test). Axilla for lymph nodes.
Active range
Flexion (0–180°), abduction (0–180°), external rotation (arm at side, 60–90°), internal rotation (hand behind back). Note painful arc (60–120°).
Passive range
Marked restriction of active and passive movement → adhesive capsulitis, GH OA, fracture, dislocation or septic arthritis. Passive relatively preserved but active restricted → cuff weakness/tear, pain inhibition or neurological disease. Painful active and passive movement occurs in many conditions and is not diagnostic alone.
Rotator-cuff pain tests
Hawkins–Kennedy: forward flex 90° + IR. Neer: passive forward flex with IR. Interpret with Jobe and painful arc as a test cluster — no individual test confirms a rotator-cuff tear or proves subacromial impingement.
Rotator cuff strength
Empty can (Jobe): abduction 90°, 30° forward, thumb down — supraspinatus. ER lag sign: infraspinatus/teres minor. Lift-off test: subscapularis. Drop arm sign: full thickness supraspinatus tear.
AC joint test
Scarf test: cross-body adduction — pain at ACJ → ACJ pathology. Point tenderness at ACJ.
Instability tests
Apprehension test: 90° abduction + ER → fear of dislocation → anterior instability. Sulcus sign: inferior instability.
Cervical spine screen
ALWAYS examine neck. Neck pain, dermatomal symptoms, sensory loss, reflex change or myotomal weakness → full neurological assessment and cervical radiculopathy pathway (NICE NG127). Spurling's test may help but is not a diagnostic requirement. Myelopathic signs (gait disturbance, hand clumsiness, hyperreflexia) → urgent MRI via spinal/neurology pathway.
Neurovascular
Axillary nerve: sensation lateral deltoid (post-dislocation). Radial pulse. Horner's syndrome → Pancoast. Systemic signs: fever, lymphadenopathy, weight loss.
The passive vs active range distinction is a key, though not absolute, examination finding for shoulder diagnosis. Neck examination is non-negotiable — cervical radiculopathy is a common mimic of shoulder pain. The drop arm sign (inability to slowly lower the arm from 90° abduction) has high specificity for full-thickness supraspinatus tear. Hawkins–Kennedy and Neer tests have poor specificity — use them as part of a test cluster rather than as proof of impingement. Infraspinatus wasting in the posterior shoulder fossa is highly significant and indicates either RC tear or suprascapular nerve entrapment — do not miss this. Axillary nerve function must be assessed after every shoulder dislocation before and after reduction.
Step 5

Diagnose — Investigations

Diagnose
Most shoulder diagnoses are clinical. Imaging changes management in specific situations only.
Plain X-ray shoulder
AP + axillary views. Indicated for: trauma (fracture/dislocation), calcific tendinopathy (calcium deposit visible), GH OA (joint space loss), suspected ACJ OA, suspected bone lesion or malignancy, significant loss of function, inflammatory or infective features, or persistent symptoms where the result will change management (age alone is not an indication). NOT routinely needed for impingement or frozen shoulder
Ultrasound shoulder
For suspected cuff tear, ultrasound or MRI depending on local expertise, availability and the clinical question. Ultrasound is useful for full-thickness tears, biceps pathology and dynamic assessment. Also: biceps tendinopathy, subacromial bursitis, calcific tendinopathy (guides aspiration/barbotage). Operator-dependent. If cuff tear suspected and result will change management
MRI shoulder
Useful where the diagnosis is uncertain, complex pathology is suspected (partial tears, labral/SLAP tears, Bankart lesion, instability) or surgery is being considered — not automatically "gold standard" and not needed before rehabilitation in most non-traumatic cases. Do not routinely request both USS and MRI. MR arthrogram for labral lesions. Suspected cervical myelopathy → urgent MRI via spinal/neurology pathway. Usually requested by orthopaedics pre-operatively
Blood tests
NOT routinely needed for typical shoulder presentations. Targeted only: FBC, CRP/ESR if infection, inflammatory arthritis, PMR or malignancy suspected (RF, anti-CCP if inflammatory arthritis) · calcium, ALP, renal function or protein electrophoresis only with relevant features (bone pain, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, renal dysfunction, myeloma suspicion) · PSA only with a prostate-related indication · TFTs if symptoms/signs of thyroid disease.
CXR
Indicated if: Pancoast tumour suspected (apical opacity — request apical lordotic view), symptoms meeting NICE NG12 criteria (e.g. weight loss in someone who has ever smoked) — not solely because the patient is over 50 with shoulder pain.
Do NOT request
Routine MRI without prior clinical assessment and targeted indication. CT for soft tissue pathology (use MRI). Isotope bone scan in primary care — request via secondary care.
Over-investigation of shoulder pain is a major NHS resource issue — most rotator-cuff-related pain and frozen shoulder presentations require no imaging and should proceed directly to physiotherapy. However, ultrasound is appropriate and cost-effective when a rotator cuff tear is clinically suspected (weak empty can, drop arm sign, age >60 with acute-on-chronic pain). Ultrasound has excellent specificity for full-thickness tears. MRI is more sensitive for partial tears but should generally be requested by orthopaedics when surgical planning is needed. CXR is the first test when Pancoast tumour is in the differential — apical lesions can be missed on a plain film, so persisting suspicion warrants discussion with the respiratory team despite a normal CXR. Raised ESR/CRP supports PMR, and a dramatic response to corticosteroid supports but does not prove the diagnosis — mimics must be excluded.
Step 6

Refer — Referral Criteria & Urgency

Refer
Most shoulder conditions are managed in primary care with physiotherapy. Refer when red flags, surgical pathology, or treatment failure.
999
Suspected acute coronary syndrome (emergency transfer per severity; ECG without delaying transfer). Acute dislocation with vascular compromise. Septic arthritis with haemodynamic instability.
Same-day ED
Acute traumatic dislocation / fracture. Suspected septic arthritis (severe pain, restricted passive movement, fever or systemic illness — swelling may be subtle; aspiration and blood cultures). Neurovascular compromise post-injury. Rapidly progressive neurological deficit → urgent spinal/neurology pathway.
Urgent chest X-ray
NICE NG12 (lung): urgent chest X-ray (within 2 weeks) for people aged 40 and over with 2 or more unexplained symptoms (cough, fatigue, shortness of breath, chest pain, weight loss, appetite loss), or who have ever smoked and have 1 or more. Suspected cancer pathway referral if the chest X-ray suggests lung cancer or if aged 40 and over with unexplained haemoptysis. Suspected Pancoast (Horner's, T1 wasting): discuss urgently / refer.
Urgent orthopaedics
Acute rotator cuff tear with significant trauma, acute loss of active elevation, objective weakness or inability to perform activities of daily living; suspected fracture, dislocation or neurological injury: urgent referral to be seen in the next available specialist shoulder clinic (BESS/BOA). Do not make all atraumatic weakness in an older adult an urgent surgical referral. Recurrent glenohumeral dislocation. Higher-grade ACJ injury: specialist assessment depending on deformity, neurovascular status, pain and function, associated fracture, occupational/sporting needs and local pathways.
Routine orthopaedics
Atraumatic (degenerative) RC tear, including large tears in older people, not improving with rehabilitation. GH osteoarthritis substantially affecting quality of life where non-surgical management is ineffective or unsuitable (NICE NG226 — not based on age, BMI or a score alone). Frozen shoulder — specialist MSK if diagnosis uncertain, severe disability, uncontrolled pain, persistent despite appropriate conservative treatment or atypical features (not simply because symptoms persist). Calcific tendinopathy failed conservative management (consider barbotage/USS-guided needling). SLAP tear / labral pathology.
Rheumatology
Suspected inflammatory arthritis (RA, psoriatic, ankylosing spondylitis). PMR — clinical assessment (bilateral girdle pain/stiffness, age ≥50, inflammatory markers, mimics excluded, GCA assessed); refer per local pathway if atypical, refractory, relapsing, diagnostically uncertain or needing prolonged/high-dose steroids. Suspected GCA (age ≥50 + new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication or visual symptoms) — start glucocorticoids promptly when suspicion is significant (do not wait for ESR/CRP or biopsy) and refer urgently via the local (e.g. Greater Manchester) fast-track GCA pathway; visual symptoms → same-day specialist/ophthalmology. Shoulder pain alone is not a GCA indication. Systemic connective tissue disease.
Primary care manage
Rotator-cuff-related pain — physiotherapy ± subacromial corticosteroid injection. Frozen shoulder — analgesia + physiotherapy ± intra-articular injection. Cervical radiculopathy — physiotherapy; refer per NICE NG127 criteria. ACJ Grade I–II sprain.
The vast majority of shoulder pain is safely managed in primary care — rotator-cuff-related pain, mild RC tendinopathy, and frozen shoulder should all receive a trial of physiotherapy before referral. However, an acute traumatic cuff tear needs urgent referral, ideally to the next available specialist shoulder clinic (BESS/BOA), because early repair gives better results before tendon retraction and fatty infiltration; atraumatic degenerative tears are referred routinely if not improving with rehabilitation. PMR is commonly started on prednisolone 15 mg daily in primary care after clinical assessment (BSR/BHPR 2009; EULAR/ACR 2015 range 12.5–25 mg) — a dramatic response supports but does not prove the diagnosis, so exclude mimics, assess for GCA, and review response and steroid toxicity. Recurrent dislocation requires Bankart repair assessment — each dislocation risks further labral damage and Hill-Sachs lesion enlargement, increasing instability.
Step 7

Treat — Treatment Pathway by Diagnosis

Treat
Treatment depends on diagnosis. Physiotherapy is first-line for most conditions. Corticosteroid injections are adjunctive, not curative.
Rotator-Cuff-Related Shoulder Pain
Physiotherapy First-line
Individualised rotator cuff strengthening + scapular stabilisation programme — continue beyond 6–8 weeks where beneficial. Referral to MSK physio via self-referral or GP referral.
Frozen Shoulder (Adhesive Capsulitis)
Analgesia + Intra-articular injection Staged
Freezing phase: GH joint corticosteroid injection most effective. Frozen phase: intensive physiotherapy. Thawing: maintain range. Hydrodilatation via radiology if refractory.
PMR
Prednisolone commonly 15mg OD start
After clinical assessment: bilateral shoulder/hip girdle pain and stiffness, age ≥50, inflammatory markers, mimics excluded, GCA assessed. Response supports but does not prove PMR — review response and steroid toxicity. Consider bone protection (per NOGG/local guidance). Taper per current local/BSR guidance; refer if atypical, refractory, relapsing, uncertain or needing prolonged/high-dose steroids.
Calcific Tendinopathy
NSAID + Physio First-line
Oral NSAID if no contraindication (lowest dose, shortest time, PPI where appropriate). Physio. If refractory: USS-guided barbotage (needling + lavage) — refer. Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) also effective.
Step 1Analgesia: Paracetamol — dose adjusted for actual weight, liver disease, alcohol use and frailty; check for duplicate paracetamol-containing medicines. Topical NSAID (diclofenac 1% gel) — first-line for musculoskeletal pain (NICE NG226 principle). Low abuse potential.
Step 2Oral NSAID: e.g. naproxen at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, with PPI where appropriate (per current SPC/local formulary). Caution: CKD, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, GI ulcer/bleeding, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, pregnancy, elderly
Step 3Corticosteroid injection: Subacromial corticosteroid injection ± local anaesthetic — drug and dose per BNF / local injection protocol. Lateral or posterior approach. Avoid if infection is possible. Caution in diabetes (transient hyperglycaemia). Repeated injections may have tendon/cartilage implications — follow the local injection protocol (no universal national maximum or interval) and reconsider repeating if the first gave no meaningful response. Benefit is short-term (weeks).
Step 4Physiotherapy referral: If not already started. Rotator cuff strengthening programme essential. Exercise therapy equivalent to injection at 12 months. Injection works best as an adjunct alongside physiotherapy.
Step 5Secondary care: USS or MRI if cuff tear suspected and the result will change management. Refer for specialist (MSK interface / shoulder) opinion if symptoms persist despite adequate rehabilitation. Subacromial decompression is not recommended for subacromial pain (BMJ Rapid Recommendation 2019: strong recommendation against).

Injection technique note: Subacromial injection — lateral approach: needle 1cm below and medial to the lateral acromion edge, directed medially. Use aseptic technique. Avoid if: local skin infection or possible joint infection, previous reaction. Caution in diabetes — warn of transient hyperglycaemia. Repeat only if the first gave clear benefit, with an interval per local protocol.

Subacromial corticosteroid injection gives short-term pain relief for subacromial pain, but the effect is not durable (weeks) without physiotherapy. Trials suggest exercise therapy and injection have similar outcomes by 12 months — injections are adjuncts that facilitate engagement with rehabilitation. Topical NSAIDs are recommended first-line for localised musculoskeletal pain before systemic NSAIDs (lower GI/renal/CV risk). Hydrodilatation (high-volume arthrographic distension) is a secondary-care option for frozen shoulder that is not settling, alongside MUA and capsular release (BESS frozen shoulder pathway 2025). Prednisolone for PMR should be started without undue delay once clinically assessed and mimics considered — the ESR/CRP are supportive but not diagnostic, and treatment should not be withheld pending rheumatology in a patient with typical features.
Step 8

Lifestyle — Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Lifestyle
Lifestyle modification is treatment, not optional advice. Exercise is the most effective long-term intervention for most shoulder conditions.
Physiotherapy exercise programme Rotator cuff strengthening + scapular stabilisation reduces pain and improves function in rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain. In CSAW (Beard et al., Lancet 2018), subacromial decompression gave no clinically important benefit over arthroscopy only (placebo) or no treatment, so exercise-based rehab remains the default.
Ergonomic advice Offer practical ergonomic advice based on symptoms and work demands, but avoid implying that a particular posture or screen-time limit is necessary to prevent structural damage. Encourage regular movement, graded return to normal activity and an individualised exercise programme.
Activity modification Avoid provocative overhead activities during acute phase. Gradually reintroduce. Do NOT completely rest — disuse causes secondary muscle weakness and prolonged recovery.
Heat / ice application Ice 15–20 min up to 4×/day for acute inflammation. Heat for chronic stiffness (frozen shoulder — warm shower/bath before exercises). No evidence one is superior; patient preference.
Weight management Obesity increases shoulder pain burden and reduces physiotherapy response. BMI reduction reduces mechanical load. Brief intervention + referral to local weight management service.
Glycaemic control (diabetes) Optimising HbA1c reduces risk of frozen shoulder progression and improves recovery. Individualised HbA1c target, usually 48 or 53 mmol/mol depending on treatment (NICE NG28). Coordinate with diabetes annual review.
Sleep position advice Avoid lying on affected shoulder — causes nocturnal compression. Use pillow support. Side-lying with pillow between arms (for frozen shoulder) maintains gentle stretch.
Self-management resources Arthritis UK shoulder pain leaflet. Physio-specific home exercise programme (Chartered Society of Physiotherapy resources). NHS shoulder exercises video resources.
The CSAW trial (Beard et al., Lancet 2018) randomised patients to arthroscopic subacromial decompression, investigational arthroscopy only (placebo) or no treatment: decompression gave no extra benefit over arthroscopy only, and neither surgical group differed from no treatment by a clinically important amount. A BMJ Rapid Recommendation (2019) makes a strong recommendation against decompression for subacromial pain, so conservative care centred on exercise is the default. Ergonomic advice should be practical and based on symptoms and work demands, without implying that a particular posture prevents structural damage. Sleep disruption from shoulder pain is a significant contributor to quality of life impairment and should be addressed — simple positional advice (pillow support) may help. Glycaemic control in diabetes is one of the few modifiable risk factors for frozen shoulder and is an important co-management target.
Step 9

Safety — Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Safety
Review according to severity, diagnosis and response. Reassess earlier for worsening weakness, neurological symptoms, fever, systemic features or severe uncontrolled pain. Persistent symptoms despite an appropriate rehabilitation programme should prompt review of the diagnosis and consideration of imaging or specialist MSK referral.
After injection
Review is not required after every injection — useful if symptoms are severe, the diagnosis is uncertain or diabetes is present (glucose). If no improvement at all → reconsider diagnosis. Ensure physiotherapy appointment booked.
Interim review
Review physiotherapy engagement and response. Imaging only if it will change management based on the working diagnosis (not automatic USS). Consider repeat injection only if the first gave meaningful benefit. Reassess diagnosis if atypical response.
3 months
If persistent — formal reassessment. USS or MRI if cuff tear suspected, not yet imaged and the result will change management. Consider secondary care referral. Review analgesia and wean if improving.
Persistent symptoms
Persistent symptoms despite appropriate rehabilitation → review the diagnosis; consider imaging or specialist MSK/shoulder referral (orthopaedic referral is not automatic for every persistent complaint). Continue exercise where beneficial — "failure" is not defined solely by attendance or a fixed duration. Reconsider malignancy if new features (NICE NG12 criteria).
PMR monitoring
ESR + CRP at 4 weeks (should normalise on prednisolone). Review response and steroid toxicity (glucose, BP, bone, mood). Monthly while tapering. Individualised taper per current local/BSR guidance (e.g. BSR/BHPR 2009: 15 mg for 3 weeks, 12.5 mg for 3 weeks, 10 mg for 4–6 weeks, then reduce by 1 mg every 4–8 weeks). Usually 1–2 years of treatment.
Safety-net 999
Chest pain, sweating, jaw pain with shoulder pain → cardiac. Sudden severe dyspnoea. Signs of vascular compromise in arm. Sudden visual loss with headache or jaw claudication (GCA).
Safety-net same-day
Fever or systemic illness with severe pain and loss of movement (septic arthritis — swelling may be subtle). New or worsening neurological deficit or weakness in arm. Severe uncontrolled pain. New Horner's syndrome. Visual symptoms (GCA).
Red flag review
New onset weight loss, night sweats, or pain worse at rest in any shoulder pain patient → prompt reassessment with targeted bloods and imaging per clinical features and NICE NG12 criteria. Do not wait.
Review timing should reflect severity, diagnosis and response — persistent symptoms despite an appropriate rehabilitation programme should prompt diagnostic re-evaluation and consideration of imaging, not just another injection. Injection without physiotherapy has poor long-term outcomes — the exercise programme must be reinforced at every review. PMR relapse is common during steroid taper and presents as return of bilateral girdle stiffness and rising ESR/CRP — patients should know to return if this happens. Safety-netting for GCA is particularly important in PMR patients — a minority of people with PMR develop GCA. Visual symptoms in a PMR patient are a same-day emergency (start glucocorticoids promptly per the local fast-track GCA pathway and arrange same-day specialist/ophthalmology assessment). Failure to improve after adequate conservative management should always prompt re-evaluation for missed diagnosis, particularly malignancy.

Pathway based on: NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) · NICE NG226 (OA 2022 — shoulder OA referral; not a general shoulder-pain guideline) · NICE NG127 (cervical radiculopathy referral) · NICE NG234 (spinal metastases and MSCC) · local fast-track GCA pathway (e.g. Greater Manchester) and BSR GCA guideline 2020 · local MSK/shoulder referral guidance · current SPCs and local injection/prescribing protocols · BSR/BHPR PMR guideline 2009 (update in development) · BESS frozen shoulder pathway 2025 · British Elbow and Shoulder Society (BESS) guidelines · CSAW trial (Lancet 2018) · BMJ Rapid Recommendation on subacromial decompression (2019) · Chartered Society of Physiotherapy shoulder guidelines. Always adapt to individual patient context and local pathways.
