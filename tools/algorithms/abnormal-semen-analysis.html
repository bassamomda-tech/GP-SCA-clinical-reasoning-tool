Safety

Red Flags — Testicular Malignancy, Genetic Disorder & Severe Oligozoospermia

Any unexplained intratesticular mass or non-painful enlargement/change in testicular shape or texture (any age) Possible testicular cancer — most common in younger men, but assess at any age; cryptorchidism is the major risk factor for testicular malignancy. → Urgent testicular ultrasound and suspected urology cancer-pathway referral. Do not delay referral while awaiting tumour markers. AFP, β-hCG and LDH are specialist diagnostic/staging tests; normal results do not exclude testicular cancer. A small or atrophic testis alone is not a cancer red flag — it needs andrological assessment.
Azoospermia + bilateral testicular atrophy + gynaecomastia + tall stature + elevated FSH/LH + low testosterone Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) — most common chromosomal cause of azoospermia. → Refer suspected Klinefelter syndrome for andrology/genetics assessment; karyotype. Do not start exogenous testosterone in a man actively seeking fertility unless discussed with the fertility specialist, because testosterone suppresses LH/FSH and can further suppress spermatogenesis. Testosterone replacement may be indicated for confirmed hypogonadism after fertility plans have been addressed. Micro-TESE/TESE may retrieve sperm in selected men, but success is variable and must be discussed by a specialist.
Azoospermia + absent vas deferens on palpation + normal testis size + normal FSH Congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) — associated with CFTR mutation (cystic fibrosis carrier or mild CF). → Refer to andrology/reproductive medicine. Arrange CFTR testing for the affected man and offer partner testing/genetic counselling, because the reproductive risk depends on both partners' variants (coordinated through the genetics/fertility service). Consider renal imaging where congenital unilateral absence of the vas deferens is identified, because renal anomalies may coexist. Surgical sperm retrieval (PESA/TESA) is specialist-led.
Azoospermia or sperm concentration <1 million/mL + small testes + raised FSH Suggests impaired spermatogenesis (possible non-obstructive azoospermia). Small testes and raised FSH are not diagnostic of Sertoli-cell-only syndrome or maturation arrest — do not label as irreversible before specialist assessment. → Prompt andrology/reproductive medicine. If idiopathic: karyotype + Y-chromosome microdeletion (AZF) testing; genetic counselling if abnormal.
Abnormal semen analysis + erectile dysfunction + reduced libido + morning erections absent + fatigue + low testosterone Hypogonadism — secondary (pituitary: prolactinoma, Cushing's, haemochromatosis) or primary. → Morning total testosterone (repeat on a second occasion if low/borderline) + LH/FSH to distinguish primary from secondary hypogonadism; consider SHBG/free testosterone. Prolactin if low testosterone with low/inappropriately normal gonadotrophins, galactorrhoea, headaches, visual symptoms or suspected pituitary disease. No routine morning cortisol. Pituitary imaging if prolactin is significantly or persistently raised or other pituitary symptoms/signs. Azoospermia with low testosterone and low/normal LH/FSH → specialist endocrinology/andrology.
Sudden-onset, non-reducible or isolated right-sided varicocele, or one that does not decompress when supine May indicate renal or retroperitoneal pathology (e.g. renal vein obstruction) and warrants further assessment; an ordinary left-sided varicocele is usually idiopathic. → Scrotal and renal/retroperitoneal ultrasound; discuss with urology.
The CBAVD-CFTR connection is the most clinically important genetic implication of an abnormal semen analysis that GPs must recognise — congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) presents in adults as obstructive azoospermia (normal testicular function and FSH, absent vas deferens on clinical examination, normal volume but absent sperm due to blockage). It is directly caused by mutations in the CFTR gene (the cystic fibrosis gene) — approximately 80% of men with CBAVD carry at least one CFTR mutation, and approximately 10-15% carry two mutations. The clinical consequence: if a man with CBAVD (and therefore likely CFTR mutation) uses surgical sperm retrieval (PESA) for IVF with a partner who is also a CFTR carrier, there is a 25% risk of having a child with cystic fibrosis. CFTR testing of the affected man, with partner testing and genetic counselling coordinated through the genetics/fertility service, is needed before fertility treatment in CBAVD — the reproductive risk depends on both partners' variants. GPs who find absent vas deferens on examination in a young man presenting with infertility must refer to andrology AND flag CFTR testing.

Diagnose

WHO 2021 Reference Values for Semen Analysis

WHO 2021 lower reference limits (5th centile)
Volume: ≥1.4 mL (previously 1.5 mL). pH: ≥7.2. Sperm concentration: ≥16 million/mL (previously 15). Total sperm count: ≥39 million/ejaculate. Progressive motility (PR): ≥30% (previously 32%). Total motility (PR + NP): ≥42%. Morphology (strict Kruger criteria): ≥4% normal forms. Vitality (live sperm): ≥54%. Leucocyte concentration: <1 million/mL. These are 5th-centile reference limits, not absolute fertility/non-fertility thresholds: a value below a reference limit does not diagnose infertility, and a value above it does not guarantee fertility.
Semen analysis terminology
Oligozoospermia: sperm concentration <16 million/mL. Terms such as "severe" are not standardised — use the local laboratory definition. For NICE genetic testing the key thresholds are <1 million/mL (Y-microdeletion) and persistent <5 million/mL (consider karyotype). Asthenozoospermia: progressive motility <30%. Teratozoospermia: morphology <4% normal. Oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT) syndrome: all three parameters abnormal — most common abnormal result. Azoospermia: no sperm in ejaculate. Obstructive (normal FSH, normal testis volume) vs non-obstructive (elevated FSH, small testes) — suggestive features only; normal testes and FSH do not prove obstruction. Cryptozoospermia: sperm present only after centrifugation. Aspermia: no ejaculate (retrograde ejaculation, obstruction). Necrozoospermia: >96% dead sperm.
Pre-analytical variables — why the sample matters
Abstinence: 2-7 days, according to the laboratory protocol. Too short: reduces total count. Too long: reduces motility + increases DNA fragmentation. Sample: complete ejaculate (not just first portion — fructose-rich seminal vesicle secretion is in late ejaculate). Collect the complete sample into the laboratory container and deliver it within the laboratory's specified time and temperature requirements. Report any loss of sample. Avoid interpreting one borderline result in isolation. Repeat analysis: all abnormal results must be confirmed on a SECOND sample, ideally 3 months later (as soon as possible if azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia; NICE NG257), before clinical decisions are made.
The repeat semen analysis before clinical decision-making is a fundamental principle that is often bypassed in primary care — semen quality is highly variable between samples from the same individual, with substantial intra-individual variation in sperm concentration. A single abnormal result may not represent the man's average semen quality — it could reflect: a recent febrile illness (fever >38.5°C causes temporary spermatogenic suppression for approximately 3 months — the time for a complete spermatogenic cycle), an inappropriately short or long abstinence period, a technical laboratory error, or incomplete sample collection. NICE NG257 (2026): if the first semen analysis is abnormal, offer a repeat confirmatory test, ideally 3 months after the initial analysis to allow a full cycle of spermatozoa formation — but as soon as possible if azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia is found — before proceeding to further investigations or treatment. GPs who receive a single abnormal result should request a repeat, provide patient education on correct sample collection technique, and advise avoiding febrile illness-related spermatogenic suppression.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Duration of infertility (primary = never conceived; secondary = previously conceived). Coital frequency and timing (aim for every 2-3 days around ovulation). Previous paternity (if secondary infertility — new partner or interval of time may mean male factor has changed). Childhood history: cryptorchidism (orchidopexy — incomplete correction may impair spermatogenesis), testicular torsion (ischaemic damage), mumps orchitis (usually after puberty → orchitis → atrophy). Medications: sulfasalazine (reduces sperm count — reversible), anabolic steroids (suppresses HPG axis), testosterone supplementation (suppresses LH/FSH → reduces endogenous sperm production), chemotherapy (alkylating agents — potentially permanent azoospermia), nitrofurantoin, colchicine. Recreational: cannabis (impairs sperm motility), alcohol, cocaine. Occupational/environmental: heat exposure (laptop use, prolonged sitting, saunas), pesticide exposure, radiation. STIs: chlamydia/gonorrhoea epididymo-orchitis causing obstructive azoospermia. Family history (CF — CBAVD). Erectile/ejaculatory symptoms. Previous chemotherapy/radiotherapy and pelvic, inguinal or testicular surgery.
Examination
Offer examination of the scrotum and testes (testes, epididymides and vasa) after two abnormal semen analyses, or sooner where clinically indicated. Testicular size and consistency: small, soft testes (non-obstructive azoospermia); normal size, normal consistency (obstructive azoospermia or variable spermatogenesis). Varicocoele: dilated pampiniform plexus veins — examine standing and with Valsalva (left side most common; ultrasound is not a substitute for clinical examination). Sudden-onset, non-reducible or isolated right-sided varicocele, or one not decompressing supine → further assessment for renal/retroperitoneal pathology. Graded I (palpable on Valsalva), II (palpable without Valsalva), III (visible). Epididymis: fullness or induration (obstructive pathology). Vas deferens: palpate bilaterally (CBAVD if absent). Secondary sexual characteristics: gynecomastia, body hair (hypogonadism). Systemic features: anosmia (Kallmann syndrome — hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism + anosmia).
Investigations
Semen analysis × 2 (repeat ideally 3 months apart — sooner if azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia; 2-7 days abstinence each) · FSH + LH + testosterone (morning) (consider after two abnormal analyses or with clinical features of hypogonadism; repeat testosterone if low/borderline; FSH elevated = impaired spermatogenesis; LH + testosterone low = hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism) · Prolactin (if low testosterone with low/inappropriately normal gonadotrophins, galactorrhoea, headaches, visual symptoms or suspected pituitary disease) · Karyotype (idiopathic azoospermia; consider if persistent sperm concentration <5 million/mL — not for mild, isolated abnormalities) · Y chromosome microdeletion (AZFa/b/c) (idiopathic azoospermia or sperm concentration <1 million/mL — determines sperm retrieval feasibility) · CFTR mutation panel (idiopathic suspected obstructive azoospermia or a vasal abnormality, particularly absent vas deferens) · USS testes (for a mass or clinical concern — not routine for every abnormal semen analysis, and not to find or treat subclinical varicoceles)
Do not order routinely in primary care: sperm DNA fragmentation testing (NICE: not part of routine assessment) · routine morning cortisol · pituitary MRI solely for a mildly raised prolactin · karyotype for mild, isolated abnormalities. Genetic counselling before assisted conception if a karyotype abnormality, Y-chromosome microdeletion, CFTR variant or CBAVD is found.
The Y chromosome microdeletion testing in severe male infertility is a clinically consequential genetic investigation — Y chromosome microdeletions in the AZFa, AZFb, or AZFc regions are an important cause of non-obstructive azoospermia and very low sperm concentration; NICE NG257 advises testing in idiopathic azoospermia or sperm concentration <1 million/mL. The clinical significance: complete AZFa or AZFb deletions have negligible sperm-retrieval prospects, and surgical sperm retrieval should not be offered in these circumstances; AZFc deletions (the most common) may still permit sperm retrieval. The most important implication: AZF deletions are transmitted on the Y chromosome to any male offspring born via ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection) — a son will carry the same deletion and may be similarly infertile. Genetic counselling before ICSI is mandatory for all couples where the male partner has a Y chromosome microdeletion.

Causes — Classification & Specific Conditions

Pre-testicular (hypothalamic-pituitary) causes
Hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism (HH):: low FSH + low LH + low testosterone. Causes: Kallmann syndrome (GnRH deficiency + anosmia — bilateral cranial nerve 1 involvement; X-linked or autosomal), hyperprolactinaemia (prolactinoma), Cushing syndrome, haemochromatosis (iron deposition in pituitary), pituitary adenoma, anabolic steroid use (exogenous androgen suppresses FSH/LH), obesity (insulin resistance → leptin dysregulation → reduced GnRH pulse). Treatment: specialist-led gonadotrophin therapy (not testosterone replacement) can restore spermatogenesis in HH.
Testicular (primary) causes
Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY): most common chromosomal cause of non-obstructive azoospermia. Elevated FSH + LH, low testosterone, small firm testes, gynaecomastia. Refer to andrology/genetics. Micro-TESE/TESE may retrieve sperm in selected men (success variable — specialist discussion). Do not start exogenous testosterone while seeking fertility unless advised by the fertility specialist. Cryptorchidism: bilateral = significant spermatogenesis impairment; unilateral = less severe. Orchidopexy before age 2 years reduces (but does not eliminate) infertility risk. Varicocoele: impairs spermatogenesis via testicular hyperthermia and reflux of adrenal metabolites. Consider specialist radiological or surgical treatment for a palpable clinical varicocele with abnormal semen parameters and no more appropriate explanation, taking female fertility factors into account; do not promise improvement in semen parameters or pregnancy.
Post-testicular (obstructive) causes
CBAVD: congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens (CFTR mutation). Normal FSH, normal testes, azoospermia. Epididymal obstruction: post-infective (chlamydia, gonorrhoea) — EPIDIDYMO-ORCHITIS HISTORY. Ejaculatory duct obstruction: post-inflammatory or post-surgical; semen volume and pH are important clues; transrectal ultrasound is specialist-directed. Retrograde ejaculation: sperm in post-ejaculate urine (diabetes, spinal cord injury, retroperitoneal surgery, alpha-blocker use — tamsulosin). Diagnose: post-ejaculatory urine analysis for sperm where retrograde ejaculation is suspected (particularly diabetes, pelvic surgery or relevant medication).
The varicocoele and male infertility relationship is an area of ongoing clinical debate — varicocoele is the most common identifiable cause of male infertility (found in approximately 15% of men in the general population, but in approximately 35-40% of men presenting for primary infertility evaluation). The physiological basis: a left varicocoele impairs testicular thermoregulation (the pampiniform plexus normally cools arterial blood flowing to the testis — venous reflux from the varicocoele warms the testis, impairing spermatogenesis, which requires temperature 2°C below core body temperature) and allows reflux of adrenal venous blood carrying cortisol and other metabolites that impair spermatogenesis. The treatment evidence: individual benefit is uncertain. Treatment is considered for a palpable clinical varicocele (assessed standing and with Valsalva) with abnormal semen parameters, relevant reproductive goals and no more appropriate explanation; subclinical (ultrasound-only) varicoceles should not be treated, grade II–III is not an automatic indication for embolisation, and the choice of microsurgical repair, embolisation or other surgery is specialist-led. NICE NG257 (2026) reversed the CG156 advice not to offer varicocele surgery as a fertility treatment: it now advises considering radiological or surgical treatment of a varicocele detected on clinical examination in men trying to conceive who have reduced semen parameters, taking female fertility factors into account.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Suspected cancer · urology
Unexplained intratesticular mass or non-painful enlargement/change in testicular shape or texture, at any age: urgent ultrasound and suspected cancer pathway (concurrent with semen analysis work-up — must not be delayed, including while awaiting tumour markers).
Reproductive medicine / andrology (prompt referral)
Azoospermia (confirmed on 2 samples) · Sperm concentration <1 million/mL · Absent vas deferens · Small testes with raised FSH · Clinical (palpable) varicocele + abnormal SA · Hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism (low/normal FSH + LH with low testosterone — consider endocrinology) · Previous chemotherapy/radiotherapy where fertility preservation is urgent
Clinical genetics
Karyotype abnormality (e.g. Klinefelter) · Y-chromosome microdeletion · CFTR variants · CBAVD (CFTR testing of the man; partner testing and counselling coordinated by genetics/fertility service) · Family history of CF + semen analysis abnormality
GP management + repeat sample
Mild OAT (all parameters borderline, above severe thresholds): repeat SA in 3 months. Lifestyle optimisation (smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, weight management, avoid heat). Review medications (sulfasalazine, anabolic steroids — counsel to stop). If repeat SA normal: reassure. If repeat SA abnormal: reproductive medicine referral.
Couples pathway
Male factor infertility + female partner assessment both needed: refer as a couple to reproductive medicine. With an abnormal semen analysis there is no need to wait a further 6–12 months before referring. NICE recommendations should be separated from local NHS commissioning criteria: actual NHS-funded IVF access, number of cycles, BMI criteria, smoking criteria, previous children, sterilisation history and residency requirements depend on the local ICB (Greater Manchester) pathway.
The GP couple-centred approach to infertility investigation is the most efficient clinical pathway — male and female partner investigations should be initiated simultaneously, not sequentially (waiting for male results before investigating the female partner or vice versa wastes months). NICE NG257 recommends offering both partners assessment and investigation. The GP primary investigation set: for the male partner — two semen analyses, then scrotal/testicular examination ± testosterone and gonadotrophins if abnormal (USS only if a mass or clinical concern); for the female partner — day 2-5 FSH/LH/oestradiol + day 21 progesterone + TFTs + pelvic USS + chlamydia/gonorrhoea screen. Both partners investigated and results collated before reproductive medicine referral optimises the specialist consultation, as the couple can be directed immediately toward the appropriate pathway (IUI, IVF, ICSI, donor sperm, donor oocyte) rather than requiring multiple consultations for further GP investigations.

Treat

Medical and Surgical Treatment Options

Gonadotrophin therapy for hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism
FSH (Gonal-F, Puregon) + hCG (Pregnyl) injections: can restore spermatogenesis in hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism. Gonadotrophin treatment should be initiated and monitored by endocrinology/andrology or reproductive medicine. Do not use exogenous testosterone to treat fertility-related hypogonadism. Monitoring intervals and decisions about adding FSH, sperm retrieval or donor sperm should follow the specialist protocol. Clomifene citrate (off-label, anti-oestrogen increasing FSH/LH): for mildly idiopathic oligospermia — limited evidence base.
Varicocelectomy / varicocoele embolisation
Surgical: subinguinal microsurgical varicocelectomy (lowest recurrence + complication rate). Percutaneous embolisation: radiologically guided, less invasive. Choice of modality (microsurgical repair, radiological embolisation or other surgery) is specialist-led. Indication (NICE NG257): varicocoele detected on clinical examination + reduced semen parameters in a man trying to conceive, taking female fertility factors into account, with no more appropriate explanation for the infertility. Not for subclinical (ultrasound-only) varicoceles; grade II–III alone is not an automatic indication for embolisation. Do not promise improvement in semen parameters or pregnancy. Complications include testicular artery injury and hydrocele.
Surgical sperm retrieval (SSR)
PESA (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration): for obstructive azoospermia (CBAVD, post-vasectomy — reversal failed or partner's age). TESA (testicular sperm aspiration): simple needle aspiration. TESE/mTESE (testicular sperm extraction / micro-TESE): open testicular biopsy — for non-obstructive azoospermia (Klinefelter, AZFc deletion). Sperm from SSR used directly for ICSI (in vitro fertilisation with intracytoplasmic sperm injection). Freeze remaining sperm for future use. Donor sperm is an alternative to discuss, particularly for obstructive azoospermia/CBAVD or failed retrieval. Sperm retrieval success is variable and must be discussed by a specialist: AZFc deletions may still permit retrieval, but complete AZFa or AZFb deletions have negligible prospects and surgical sperm retrieval should not be offered.
The micro-TESE (microsurgical testicular sperm extraction) for Klinefelter syndrome represents one of the most significant advances in male infertility treatment — Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) was historically considered untreatable from a fertility perspective. However, some men with Klinefelter syndrome have focal areas of spermatogenesis within the testis (areas where X-inactivation allows normal spermatogenic cell function). Micro-TESE uses an operating microscope to identify and selectively biopsy these dilated, vascularised tubules containing active spermatogenesis, while avoiding the fibrotic pale tubules containing only Sertoli cells. The retrieved sperm are then used for ICSI. Predictors of sperm retrieval success in Klinefelter mTESE: younger age (spermatogenesis deteriorates with age — early referral is critical), testosterone above the lower limit of normal, lower FSH levels. GPs who diagnose or suspect Klinefelter syndrome in a young man wishing future paternity should refer promptly to andrology (and avoid starting testosterone without specialist advice) — even if the patient is not currently planning a family, early sperm banking (if any sperm are present) may preserve future options.

Treat

Assisted Reproduction & Unexplained Infertility

IVF and ICSI pathways
IVF (in vitro fertilisation): conventional insemination (sperm placed with egg in culture dish) — for mild-moderate male factor or unexplained infertility. ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection): single sperm injected directly into egg — consider when semen parameters are abnormal, with the decision based on severity, the number and quality of available sperm, previous fertilisation history and the fertility team's assessment. Do not use ICSI when semen parameters are normal solely because infertility is unexplained. NHS funding depends on the local ICB pathway — NICE recommendations are not the same as local commissioning.
NHS fertility eligibility (England)
NICE NG257 (2026) recommends offering IVF before the 42nd birthday in specified circumstances (e.g. a diagnosed cause for which other treatments are unsuitable or unsuccessful, or unexplained infertility after 2 years of regular unprotected intercourse or 12 cycles of artificial insemination) — this is not a guaranteed NHS entitlement. Additional criteria often applied by ICBs (local, not in NICE NG257): no previous sterilisation by either partner; no children from current relationship; BMI 19-30 in woman; not smoking (both partners, or 3 months stopped). Note: local NHS commissioning criteria vary significantly from NICE NG257 — some ICSs have more restrictive criteria (e.g., female age <35, only 1 cycle). Actual NHS-funded IVF access, number of cycles, BMI and smoking criteria, previous children, sterilisation history and residency requirements depend on the local ICB pathway — check the Greater Manchester fertility commissioning policy.
Unexplained infertility
NICE NG257 (2026): unexplained infertility after 2 years of regular unprotected intercourse — discuss options and either consider up to 4 cycles of IUI with gonadotrophin ovarian stimulation before IVF, or offer IVF directly. Do not offer ovarian stimulation (e.g. clomifene, letrozole) as a stand-alone treatment for unexplained infertility. Lifestyle optimisation: weight management, folic acid (for the person who may become pregnant), alcohol reduction.
The NHS IVF funding inequity across England is a significant patient and GP advocacy issue — the number of NHS-funded IVF cycles offered varies between ICB areas and often differs from NICE NG257 recommendations, and some areas offer very limited NHS-funded IVF. This creates a postcode lottery of fertility care that is explicitly at odds with the principle of equitable NHS access. GPs should: (1) be aware of their local ICS fertility commissioning criteria (available from the ICS website or the local fertility unit); (2) advise couples accurately about what is funded locally vs what requires self-funding; (3) advocate for patients who appear to meet NICE NG257 criteria but are denied on local criteria grounds; (4) signpost to Patient Advisory Liaison Service (PALS) for patients who wish to challenge commissioning decisions. The HFEA (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority) website (hfea.gov.uk) provides reliable information on local fertility treatment options and success rates by clinic.

Lifestyle

Lifestyle Optimisation for Semen Quality

Smoking cessation — semen quality impact Smoking is associated with poorer semen quality; stopping smoking is advised. NHS Stop Smoking Service referral at every infertility consultation. Nicotine replacement therapy: safe during fertility investigations and IVF preparation.
Alcohol reduction Heavy alcohol consumption (>14 units/week) significantly impairs sperm quality — reduces testosterone, impairs hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis function, and directly damages developing sperm. NICE NG257: drinking within the UK CMO low-risk limit (14 units/week) is unlikely to affect semen quality, but excessive alcohol intake is detrimental. Light-moderate alcohol (≤14 units/week): minimal evidence of semen quality impairment. Advise reducing heavy alcohol use.
Heat avoidance Spermatogenesis requires a temperature approximately 2°C below core body temperature — any chronic testicular hyperthermia impairs sperm production. Advise avoiding excessive scrotal heat exposure if relevant (e.g. saunas, hot tubs, tight underwear, laptop on lap), while acknowledging that evidence for specific heat sources is limited.
Weight management Obesity (BMI >30) is associated with impaired semen quality. Mechanisms: aromatisation of testosterone to oestrogen in adipose tissue reduces FSH/LH drive to testes; elevated scrotal temperature from adiposity; insulin resistance dysregulating steroidogenesis. Optimise weight and physical activity. NICE NG257: men with a BMI of 30 kg/m² or over have an increased risk of reduced fertility.
Nutritional supplements — evidence assessment NICE does not recommend a specific antioxidant, folate, zinc, selenium, CoQ10 or L-carnitine regimen for male infertility. Do not recommend commercial fertility supplements as standard treatment.
Folic acid Folic acid is routinely recommended for the person who may become pregnant. Evidence for routine supplementation in male partners is insufficient, so it is not routinely advised for men.
Cannabis and recreational drug avoidance Cannabis significantly impairs sperm quality: THC (delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol) is associated with reduced sperm motility, impairs acrosome reaction (required for fertilisation), and reduces testosterone. Effects are dose-dependent and largely reversible with cessation (within 3-6 months — one spermatogenic cycle). Cocaine: directly toxic to sperm via vasoconstriction. Anabolic steroids: profoundly suppress HPG axis → azoospermia during use; recovery after cessation takes 6-18 months and may be permanent in long-term users.
Medicines and exogenous testosterone Avoid exogenous testosterone and anabolic steroids if fertility is desired. Review medicines, including sulfasalazine, testosterone, anabolic steroids, chemotherapy and selected other drugs.
Psychological support during infertility investigation Male infertility carries significant psychological burden — shame, guilt, identity challenges, relationship stress. Men are less likely to seek psychological support than women but are equally affected. PHQ-9 + GAD-7 at infertility review for both partners. Relate (relate.org.uk): relationship counselling for infertility-related stress. Fertility Network UK (fertilitynetworkuk.org): peer support. Male factor infertility community (RESOLVE, US-based): men-specific peer support. Acknowledge explicitly that infertility is a shared couple problem, not a male "failure."
The anabolic steroid-induced azoospermia is an increasingly prevalent cause of male infertility that is frequently not volunteered by patients without direct questioning — anabolic androgenic steroids (testosterone enanthate, nandrolone, stanozolol, oxandrolone, boldenone) are used by an estimated 3-5% of men in the UK, predominantly by gym-going men aged 18-35. Exogenous androgens profoundly suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis by negative feedback, reducing FSH to near-zero and halting spermatogenesis completely within 3-4 months of use. The semen analysis typically shows: azoospermia or near-azoospermia with normal testicular volume (the testes are not atrophied — they contain normal seminiferous tubules with adequate Sertoli cell function, but no active spermatogenesis due to absent FSH stimulation). Recovery after cessation: most men recover spermatogenesis within 6-18 months, but approximately 10-15% have prolonged recovery or permanent azoospermia, particularly long-term heavy users. GPs asking about anabolic steroid use should do so in a non-judgmental way: 'Do you use any supplements, performance-enhancing drugs, or anabolic steroids for training purposes?'

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & NHS Fertility Pathway

GP timeline for infertility investigation
Month 1: confirm infertility definition (offer referral after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse if no known cause; refer at presentation if the person trying to become pregnant is aged 36 or over, or either partner has a known/suspected cause or predisposing factor such as abnormal semen analysis, cryptorchidism, previous chemotherapy or pelvic/testicular surgery) + first SA + female investigations simultaneously. Month 2-3: repeat SA + hormones (if first SA abnormal) + female cycle assessment. Once an abnormal result is confirmed on repeat, refer the couple to reproductive medicine without waiting a further 6-12 months. Document all counselling and investigations clearly.
Monitoring on gonadotrophin therapy
Gonadotrophin treatment should be initiated and monitored by endocrinology/andrology or reproductive medicine. Do not use exogenous testosterone to treat fertility-related hypogonadism. Monitoring intervals and decisions about adding FSH, sperm retrieval or donor sperm should follow the specialist protocol.
Post-TESE/ICSI follow-up
GP role: emotional support + physical recovery monitoring. After micro-TESE: scrotal haematoma, infection (antibiotics if needed). After IVF/ICSI: early pregnancy scan at 7-8 weeks (reproductive medicine or GP EPAS). Miscarriage support if cycle fails. Recurrent IVF failure: psychological support + further specialist review.
Safety-netting for male infertility patients
Advise prompt review for any new testicular lump, swelling, change in shape or texture, or pain; severe testicular pain or suspected torsion needs same-day emergency assessment. Sudden-onset, non-reducible or isolated right-sided varicocele, or one not decompressing supine: further assessment including renal/retroperitoneal ultrasound.
Suspected cancer · urology
Unexplained intratesticular mass or non-painful enlargement/change in testicular shape or texture (any age) detected during infertility assessment — do not await tumour markers
Prompt andrology/reproductive medicine
Azoospermia confirmed × 2 · Absent vas deferens (CBAVD + CFTR testing) · Sperm concentration <1 million/mL · Suspected pituitary failure or significant hyperprolactinaemia · Previous chemotherapy/radiotherapy where fertility preservation is urgent · Klinefelter features (small firm testes + gynaecomastia + tall stature)
The concurrent investigation of both partners from the outset is one of the most evidence-based yet poorly implemented aspects of UK fertility guidance (NICE CG156, now NICE NG257, 2026) — audit data consistently shows that in UK primary care, the male partner is investigated, on average, 3-6 months later than the female partner. This sequential approach wastes months of the couple's reproductive window, particularly significant where the person trying to become pregnant is aged 36 or over (where ovarian reserve declines rapidly and time-to-pregnancy is a critical variable). The GP implementation: at the first infertility consultation, initiate investigations for BOTH partners simultaneously: SA referral for the male partner + day 2-5 FSH/LH and day 21 progesterone request for the female partner + USS pelvis + chlamydia screen for both. This approach compresses the total investigation time from 9-12 months (sequential) to 3-4 months (simultaneous), significantly improving the timeliness of specialist referral.
