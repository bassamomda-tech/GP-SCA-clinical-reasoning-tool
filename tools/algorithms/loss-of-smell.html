Safety

Red Flags — Intracranial Pathology & Malignancy

Persistent or unexplained unilateral smell loss, particularly with unilateral nasal obstruction, bleeding, crusting, facial pain/swelling, a mass or neurological signs, requires ENT assessment through the local rhinology/head-and-neck pathway. An isolated unilateral reduction in smell without nasal or neurological findings is not automatically sinonasal cancer, but should be reviewed if persistent or progressive. NICE NG12 has no standalone unilateral anosmia criterion. Anosmia with new neurological features needs urgent assessment.

Anosmia + headache + visual field loss + personality change Olfactory groove meningioma — compresses olfactory tracts then optic chiasm (Foster Kennedy: ipsilateral optic atrophy + contralateral papilloedema). Urgent same-day assessment and neuroimaging (local pathway).
Persistent unilateral smell loss + unilateral obstruction, epistaxis, crusting or facial swelling Possible sinonasal malignancy (esthesioneuroblastoma, SCC, adenocarcinoma — especially woodworkers/nickel workers) → urgent ENT/head-and-neck referral (local pathway; NICE NG12 has no sinonasal criterion).
Several people in the same environment with headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, weakness or collapse Consider carbon-monoxide exposure, particularly with faulty heating or poor ventilation. Remove the person from the source, call 999 if symptomatic or significantly exposed, and follow emergency oxygen/poisoning protocols (oxygen therapy managed by emergency services/ED). Loss of smell is not a specific diagnostic feature.
Anosmia + tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, balance or gait disturbance Possible Parkinson's disease. Ask about smell loss when assessing possible Parkinson's disease, but do not diagnose Parkinson's disease or refer solely because of anosmia. Refer if anosmia is accompanied by tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, balance or gait disturbance, or other parkinsonian features (NICE NG71). Objective smell testing is not for routine diagnosis.
Anosmia since birth + hypogonadism + absent puberty Suspect congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism/Kallmann syndrome in someone with lifelong smell loss and absent or incomplete puberty. Refer to endocrinology for early-morning gonadotrophin/sex-steroid assessment, other pituitary assessment and specialist imaging/genetic testing where appropriate. MRI alone does not confirm the diagnosis.
Anosmia + weight loss + night sweats + lymphadenopathy Paraneoplastic neuropathy or skull base malignancy → urgent ENT referral (local pathway); check whether the lymphadenopathy meets an NG12 route (for example an unexplained neck lump).
Olfactory groove meningioma is an important intracranial cause of anosmia — it can grow silently, reaching large sizes before symptoms beyond anosmia occur, and is misattributed to ageing or sinusitis for years. Foster Kennedy syndrome (anosmia + ipsilateral optic atrophy + contralateral papilloedema) is the classic triad. Anosmia with new neurological symptoms or signs needs urgent assessment. NICE NG127: consider neuroimaging for adults with unexplained loss of smell lasting more than 3 months; do not routinely refer adults with loss of smell and normal neuroimaging. Hyposmia is common in Parkinson's disease but is non-specific — it also occurs with ageing, rhinitis, prior viral infection, smoking and Alzheimer's disease. NICE advises that objective smell testing should not be used for routine differential diagnosis of parkinsonian syndromes, except in research.

Classification — Causes of Anosmia

Conductive (transport)
Obstruction prevents odorant molecules reaching olfactory epithelium. Causes: chronic rhinosinusitis (most common overall), nasal polyps, severe allergic rhinitis, deviated septum, adenoid hypertrophy. Usually reversible with treatment.
Sensorineural
Damage to olfactory receptor neurons or olfactory bulb/tract. Causes: post-viral (most common sensorineural — includes COVID-19, rhinovirus, HSV), head trauma (shearing of olfactory nerve filaments at cribriform plate), neurodegenerative (PD, Alzheimer's), toxic (cocaine, cadmium, benzene), post-radiotherapy.
Post-COVID anosmia
SARS-CoV-2 infects sustentacular (supporting) cells via ACE2 receptors in olfactory epithelium. Many recover over weeks to months; a minority have persistent hyposmia/parosmia. Parosmia (distorted smell) commonly occurs after post-viral olfactory dysfunction and may improve over time, but it is not a reliable marker of recovery.
Drug-induced
Intranasal zinc (withdrawn — irreversible), intranasal cocaine (epithelial destruction), chemotherapy, prolonged topical decongestants (rhinitis medicamentosa — blocks olfactory cleft).
Congenital
Kallmann syndrome (anosmia + hypogonadism). Isolated olfactory bulb aplasia. Ciliopathies (Bardet-Biedl, Usher syndrome).
Nutritional
Zinc deficiency — test only where deficiency is clinically plausible; routine serum zinc testing and supplementation are not supported. B12 deficiency — check B12/folate if clinical features or risk factors.
Post-COVID anosmia mechanism: SARS-CoV-2 preferentially targets sustentacular (support) cells of the olfactory epithelium via ACE2 receptors, causing massive local inflammation that disrupts olfactory receptor neuron function. Crucially, olfactory receptor neurons themselves are relatively spared (explaining the high recovery rate). Recovery occurs through neurogenesis from basal stem cells — facilitated by olfactory training. Parosmia (distorted smell — familiar things smelling like burning, chemicals, or faeces) can occur during recovery, but it does not prove recovery and may be persistent or severely disabling.

Assessment — History, Examination & Targeted Investigations

History
Onset: sudden (viral, trauma, CO) vs gradual (polyps, PD) · Unilateral (structural, tumour) vs bilateral · Nasal blockage, rhinorrhoea, sinusitis symptoms · Head trauma timing · COVID history · Smoking, cocaine and other intranasal substances · Medication review · Occupation (woodworking, nickel — sinonasal cancer) · Neurological symptoms (PD, AD) · Puberty/hypogonadism history if congenital anosmia is suspected (Kallmann) · Duration and trajectory · Taste disturbance and nutritional effect · Epistaxis, facial pain
Examination
Anterior rhinoscopy: nasal polyps (grey/white glistening masses in middle meatus), mucosal oedema, septal deviation · CN I gross test (coffee, vanilla, peppermint — one nostril at a time) · CN II visual fields (olfactory groove meningioma) · Cranial-nerve and neurological examination when indicated: tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia · Neck: lymphadenopathy
Formal olfactory testing (specialist)
Sniffin' Sticks or UPSIT may be used in specialist smell or research services, but should not be a routine GP investigation. Do not use objective smell testing to diagnose Parkinson's disease.
Investigations
CT paranasal sinuses (polyps, sinusitis, tumour, Lund-Mackay score) · Neuroimaging (MRI brain) consider for unexplained loss >3 months (NICE NG127) — protocol set by radiology/specialist service; ENT/rhinology assessment may be more appropriate when nasal symptoms predominate · Targeted bloods only (not a routine panel): B12/folate if clinical features or risk factors · TSH if thyroid disease suspected · Glucose/HbA1c if metabolic risk · FBC/ferritin if weight loss, poor intake or anaemia suspected · Endocrine tests if delayed puberty, amenorrhoea, infertility or other hypogonadism features (consider sex, age, pubertal stage, menstrual history; specialist guidance) · Zinc only where deficiency is clinically plausible. COVID serology is not useful for establishing the cause of a remote smell disturbance.
Formal tests such as Sniffin' Sticks or UPSIT belong in specialist smell or research services; they are not a routine GP investigation. For practices without formal testing: a gross 3-item screen (coffee, vanilla, eucalyptus) takes 2 minutes. Inability to identify any of the three = significant anosmia. CT sinuses is the first-line imaging for conductive anosmia — it shows polyp extent, sinus opacification, bony erosion (malignancy), and Lund-Mackay score (influences surgical decision-making). MRI is reserved for soft tissue characterisation, olfactory bulb visualisation (Kallmann), and intracranial pathology. NICE NG127: consider neuroimaging for unexplained loss lasting more than 3 months; do not routinely refer adults with normal neuroimaging. Do not routinely request MRI for all unexplained cases.

The unilateral vs bilateral distinction is the most useful first branch point in anosmia assessment. Bilateral anosmia is more likely to be systemic (post-viral, nutritional, neurodegenerative, bilateral polyps). Unilateral symptoms are more concerning when accompanied by unilateral nasal obstruction, epistaxis or blood-stained discharge, crusting, facial pain or swelling, a visible mass or polyp, orbital symptoms, cranial-nerve signs, or persistent progressive symptoms — these need ENT assessment through the local rhinology/head-and-neck pathway. An isolated unilateral reduction without nasal or neurological findings is not automatically malignancy, but should be reviewed if persistent or progressive.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day
999/ED: anosmia with acute confusion, reduced consciousness or suspected CO poisoning · New focal neurological signs, seizure or meningism · Severe headache with visual disturbance or papilloedema · Sinonasal symptoms with orbital signs, rapidly progressive facial swelling or intracranial features
Urgent referral (local pathway): ENT / head & neck
Persistent unilateral nasal obstruction, crusting, blood-stained discharge or epistaxis · Visible nasal mass or polyp that is unilateral/atypical · Facial swelling, pain or cranial-nerve signs · Persistent unexplained unilateral smell loss · Destructive nasal disease, septal perforation or suspected cocaine-induced vasculitis · CT finding of sinonasal mass or bone erosion · Olfactory groove meningioma (also neurosurgery)
ENT (routine)
Bilateral anosmia from CRS/polyps not responding to 12 weeks nasal steroid + saline douching · FESS consideration · Biologic therapy (dupilumab NICE TA1134) assessment for severe CRSwNP — specialist treatment, not a primary-care option
Neurology
Anosmia with tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, balance or gait disturbance, or other parkinsonian features (do not refer for anosmia alone) · Unexplained loss >3 months: consider MRI brain or specialist ENT/neurology assessment per local pathway (NICE NG127); do not routinely refer if neuroimaging is normal
Endocrinology
Suspected Kallmann syndrome/congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (lifelong anosmia + absent or incomplete puberty) · Hypothyroid/acromegaly macroglossia causing obstructive anosmia
Primary care
Appropriate when bilateral loss follows a viral infection, there are no neurological or sinonasal red flags, anterior nasal examination is reassuring, and symptoms are improving or of relatively recent onset. Use saline irrigation where nasal symptoms are present, intranasal corticosteroid for allergic rhinitis/CRS with correct technique, olfactory training for persistent post-viral/COVID-related dysfunction, and safety-net with review if symptoms persist beyond 3 months or worsen.
Dupilumab (Dupixent) for severe CRS with nasal polyps (NICE TA1134, 2026) is one of the most effective treatments for polyp-related anosmia — it is an IL-4/IL-13 receptor antagonist (same biologic as for severe atopic eczema) that dramatically reduces polyp size and restores olfaction in patients with severe CRSwNP uncontrolled by steroids or surgery. NICE TA1134 eligibility (adults): add-on to intranasal corticosteroids, not controlled by systemic corticosteroids or sinus surgery, at least 1 previous sinus surgery, SNOT-22 score of at least 50, and only if the company provides it according to the commercial arrangement. 300 mg SC every 2 weeks, specialist-initiated — this is specialist treatment, not a primary-care option. GPs should refer appropriate refractory polyp patients to ENT for dupilumab assessment.

Treat

Treatment by Cause

CRS with polyps (conductive)
Mometasone furoate 2 sprays (100 micrograms) into each nostril once or twice daily (BNF, nasal polyps) — head-forward position, spray directed away from the septum. Minimum 12 weeks. Oral corticosteroids only on ENT advice for severe CRS with nasal polyps — not routine for isolated post-viral anosmia. Saline douching BD (NeilMed 240 ml). Review at 12 weeks → ENT if not responding (ENTUK GIRFT pathway: adequate medical therapy before considering surgery).
Allergic rhinitis
Intranasal steroid + oral non-sedating antihistamine (loratadine 10 mg OD). Allergen avoidance. Immunotherapy (SLIT/SCIT) for severe HDM/grass pollen disease.
Post-viral / COVID anosmia
Olfactory training (British Rhinological Society recommended): two smell sets or four familiar odours (e.g. rose, eucalyptus, lemon, cloves) — about 20 seconds each, twice daily, for at least 12 weeks; continue longer if there is gradual improvement. The exact odours and switching interval are not essential. There is insufficient evidence to recommend a drug treatment for persistent COVID-related olfactory dysfunction (ESCMID 2022). Do not offer routinely: PEA/luteolin, alpha-lipoic acid, zinc, vitamin A or other supplements, theophylline nasal drops, PRP injections outside specialist or research services, or empirical systemic prednisolone for isolated post-viral anosmia.
Nutritional
B12/folate replacement per standard protocol if deficiency confirmed. Routine zinc supplementation is not supported — zinc excess can itself cause copper deficiency and neurological disease.
Olfactory training (OT) mechanism — repeated exposure to odorants activates residual olfactory receptor neurons and promotes axonal regeneration via neuroplasticity, helping re-establish olfactory neuron-to-bulb synaptic connections. The conscious attentional focus is part of what drives neuroplasticity — passive sniffing is less effective than actively trying to recall what the smell used to be like. Olfactory training is reasonable and low risk; the exact odours and switching interval are not essential. Continue for at least 12 weeks, and longer if there is gradual improvement. Fifth Sense charity (fifthsense.org.uk) provides training kits and patient resources.

Treat

Persistent Anosmia & Parosmia

PRP injections (experimental/specialist)
Platelet-rich plasma injected into olfactory cleft — delivers PDGF, VEGF, EGF growth factors supporting olfactory neuron regeneration. Experimental — only within specialist or research services; not a routine treatment option.
Parosmia management
Parosmia commonly occurs after post-viral olfactory dysfunction and may improve over time, but it is not a reliable marker of recovery. Offer reassurance, olfactory training and practical dietary support (avoid trigger foods, food diary, texture-based flavour enhancement; Fifth Sense parosmia dietary guide). Check weight and nutritional intake; consider dietitian referral where intake is restricted; advise food safety because spoiled food may not be detected; assess mood, anxiety and social impact. Refer to a smell clinic or ENT if persistent, causing weight loss, or significantly affecting nutrition and quality of life.
PD / neurodegeneration
No treatment restores olfaction in PD (Lewy body olfactory bulb pathology — not dopamine-responsive). Nutritional monitoring. Dietitian if weight loss. Acknowledge impact.
Theophylline nasal drops
Specialist-formulated. Phosphodiesterase inhibitor increases cAMP in olfactory receptor neurons. Experimental — specialist rhinology or research settings only; not for routine use.
Evidence for supplements and pharmacological treatments (including alpha-lipoic acid, PEA/luteolin and zinc) is limited or insufficient, so they should not be offered routinely; PRP and theophylline remain experimental/specialist options. Parosmia education matters — it can occur during recovery and may improve over time, but it is not a reliable marker of recovery and can be persistent or severely disabling. Monitor nutrition, mood and quality of life, and refer to a smell clinic or ENT if it persists or affects nutrition.

Lifestyle

Olfactory Training, Safety & Quality of Life

Olfactory training kit Two smell sets or 4 familiar odours, e.g. rose, eucalyptus, lemon, cloves. Small amounts on cotton pads in glass jars. Twice daily: 20 seconds each, eyes closed, concentrated attention. Continue for at least 12 weeks, and longer if there is gradual improvement. The exact odours and switching interval are not essential; formal smell scores are not needed in primary care.
Home safety Fit smoke detector + CO detector + gas leak detector (tested weekly). Cannot rely on smell to detect fire, gas, CO, or food spoilage. Strict use-by date compliance. Inform household members. Photoelectric smoke alarms preferred (detect smouldering fires earlier).
Nutritional maintenance Smell contributes substantially to flavour — appetite may decrease and weight loss can occur. Enhance by: texture (crunchy), temperature (hot), visual presentation, strong umami/salt/sweet tastes. Weigh monthly. Dietitian referral where intake is restricted or weight is falling.
Emotional impact Loss of smell causes profound grief (inability to smell infants, partners, food, nature, memories). Assess mood, anxiety and social impact. Acknowledge: 'This is a significant loss — it affects much more than most people realise.' Fifth Sense charity peer support. NHS Talking Therapies if depression develops.
Rhinitis management NeilMed saline douching BD (240 ml). Consistent morning nasal steroid use — head-forward-and-down position maximises olfactory cleft deposition. Direct the spray away from the septum. Intranasal steroids can themselves cause epistaxis and nasal irritation: check technique and inspect the nasal mucosa. If recurrent bleeding or crusting develops, reassess the indication, consider a temporary pause and use saline/moisturisation, while maintaining treatment if the benefit clearly outweighs the local adverse effects. Use a low-bioavailability preparation where prolonged therapy is needed (BSACI). Topical decongestants (xylometazoline/oxymetazoline): short-term use only per local formulary/product guidance because of rebound congestion — they are not a treatment for persistent anosmia.
Occupational safety Gas engineers, fire safety, food safety inspectors, perfumers — notify employer and occupational health. Equality Act 2010 may apply (disability if substantially affecting daily life). Reasonable workplace adjustments may be required. GP supporting letter for OH/employer available.
Food safety education for anosmic patients is one of the most clinically important aspects of management — anosmia represents a genuine public health safety risk because smell is the primary early warning system for fire (smoke), gas leaks, CO, and food spoilage. Fitting a CO detector is especially important because CO is odourless and a patient with smell loss may also miss smoke or gas leaks. The Equality Act 2010 implication is worth documenting — persistent anosmia (lasting >12 months, affecting daily activities including cooking, eating, work, and safety) may meet the disability definition, entitling the patient to reasonable adjustments at work. A GP letter documenting the condition and its functional impact supports the patient's claim.

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Post-viral anosmia — 3 months
Olfactory training commenced? Weight and nutritional intake stable? Parosmia affecting nutrition, mood or quality of life (→ smell clinic/ENT)? Unexplained loss persisting >3 months → consider neuroimaging or specialist ENT/neurology assessment per local pathway (NICE NG127). If improving → continue training, review at 6 months.
CRS / polyps on treatment — 12 weeks
Polyp / symptom improvement? Not responding despite adequate medical therapy → ENT referral (ENTUK pathway); oral corticosteroids only on ENT advice.
Unexplained unilateral anosmia
Persistent or progressive, or with nasal/neurological findings → ENT assessment via local rhinology/head-and-neck pathway; track referral outcome. An isolated unilateral reduction without nasal or neurological findings is not automatically sinonasal cancer — review if persistent or progressive.
Neurological causes
PD referral outcome. Motor symptom monitoring 6-monthly. Meningioma: neurosurgery follow-up. Annual neurological review if anosmia + neurological features.
Return immediately
Acute confusion, reduced consciousness, seizure or suspected CO exposure → 999 · Severe headache + visual disturbance + anosmia → urgent same-day assessment · Unilateral nasal bleeding + anosmia → urgent ENT referral immediately · New neurological symptom at any follow-up
Same-week GP
Significant weight loss since last review · Symptoms persisting beyond 3 months or worsening · New neurological symptom developing · Persistent parosmia affecting nutrition → dietitian
The 3-month review point for post-viral anosmia is the clinical decision gate — for unexplained loss lasting more than 3 months, consider neuroimaging or specialist ENT/neurology assessment according to local pathway (NICE NG127); do not routinely refer adults with normal neuroimaging. Setting realistic long-term expectations is important: a minority of post-COVID anosmia patients will have persistent impairment. These patients need long-term support: food safety counselling, nutritional monitoring, psychological support, and signposting to Fifth Sense charity — rather than repeated investigation.
