1
Safety

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

Sudden-onset new ataxia or unsteadiness should be treated as possible posterior-circulation stroke until excluded (NICE NG127 and NG128). Refer immediately through the local stroke/emergency pathway when there is a focal neurological deficit, acute vestibular syndrome not explained by BPPV or postural hypotension, abnormal trained HINTS examination, reduced consciousness, severe headache or vomiting — a cerebellar stroke can rapidly cause fatal brainstem compression and hydrocephalus.

Acute ataxia + headache / vomiting / ↓GCS Sudden cerebellar signs, occipital headache → 999 Cerebellar stroke/haemorrhage — risk of brainstem compression & hydrocephalus
Ataxia + other acute focal deficit Dysarthria, diplopia, dysphagia, vertigo, weakness, sensory loss, visual symptoms, new deafness, or acute vestibular syndrome with abnormal trained HINTS → 999 Posterior circulation stroke (FAST may be negative — use this pathway)
Confusion, ophthalmoplegia or ataxia in an at-risk patient Full triad not required. Especially alcohol misuse, hyperemesis, malnutrition → Emergency Wernicke's encephalopathy — emergency hospital assessment (999 if unstable); urgent parenteral thiamine per local protocol, before or alongside glucose where feasible; do not delay treatment of severe hypoglycaemia
Ataxia + headache worse on waking / papilloedema Posture-related headache, vomiting, visual change → Same-day Posterior fossa tumour / raised ICP — urgent imaging
Child with sudden-onset or rapidly progressive ataxia Post-infectious, or with vomiting/headache → Same-day Immediate referral to acute paediatric services (NICE NG127) (posterior fossa tumour, acute cerebellitis, ingestion). 999 if reduced consciousness, persistent vomiting, severe headache, meningism, focal neurological deficit, suspected ingestion or inability to walk safely
Drug toxicity (phenytoin / carbamazepine / lithium) Nystagmus, slurred speech, drowsiness → Same-day Clinical review, medication reconciliation and urgent levels; do not advise abrupt cessation without a plan. Emergency hospital assessment for significant antiepileptic or moderate lithium toxicity; 999 if collapse or severe neurological features
Fever + ataxia + meningism Headache, photophobia, neck stiffness → 999 Meningoencephalitis / cerebellar abscess
Rapidly progressive ataxia over days–weeks Subacute decline, weight loss, malignancy history → Urgent Suspected cancer pathway referral for neurological assessment (NICE NG127); same-day if headache, vomiting, papilloedema or a new focal deficit. Paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration, demyelination, prion disease
Subacute ataxia + paraesthesia, numbness, weakness or urinary symptoms Recreational nitrous oxide use → Urgent Nitrous oxide-induced subacute combined degeneration — urgent neurological assessment; do not delay treatment while awaiting investigations (normal serum B12 does not exclude it)

Posterior circulation strokes are often missed because the standard FAST tool is insensitive to cerebellar/brainstem signs — isolated acute ataxia, vertigo or diplopia can be the only feature. A cerebellar infarct/haemorrhage can swell over the first few days, compressing the brainstem and obstructing CSF flow (hydrocephalus) — a neurosurgical emergency. Wernicke's encephalopathy is under-diagnosed (the classic triad is present in only a minority): give parenteral thiamine urgently to any at-risk patient with confusion, ophthalmoplegia or ataxia, before or alongside glucose where feasible — but never delay treatment of severe hypoglycaemia.

2
Diagnose

History — Speed of Onset Is the Key Discriminator

The tempo of onset narrows the differential more than anything else. Always clarify exactly how quickly it came on and whether it is progressive.

Onset / tempo
Hyperacute (mins–hours): stroke, drug toxicity. Acute (hours–days): post-infectious cerebellitis (children), Wernicke's, demyelination, intoxication. Subacute (weeks): tumour, paraneoplastic, B12/thiamine deficiency, nitrous oxide. Chronic/progressive (months–years): hereditary, MS, alcohol, hypothyroidism
Alcohol & nutrition
Alcohol intake (acute intoxication, chronic cerebellar degeneration, Wernicke's). Diet/weight loss, bariatric surgery, hyperemesis — thiamine deficiency. Vegan/malabsorption — B12
Drug history
Antiepileptics (phenytoin, carbamazepine), lithium, sedatives, chemotherapy, aminoglycosides, metronidazole. New drug or dose change? Recent overdose? Ask specifically about recreational nitrous oxide ("NOS", "laughing gas", balloons or canisters), including frequency, quantity and last use, and about paraesthesia, numbness, weakness, urinary symptoms and gait disturbance.
Associated symptoms
Vertigo/hearing loss (vestibular — but acute hearing loss with vertigo can indicate posterior circulation ischaemia; don't automatically label it labyrinthitis). Headache/vomiting (posterior fossa). Limb weakness/sensory loss/visual change (stroke, MS). Numbness + falls in the dark (sensory ataxia — dorsal column)
Systemic / red-flag
Fever (infection), weight loss/known cancer (paraneoplastic), cognitive change. Recent viral illness (post-infectious cerebellitis, esp. children)
Family history
Hereditary ataxias (Friedreich's, spinocerebellar ataxias), consanguinity, similar problems in relatives, age of onset
Functional impact
Falls, mobility, driving, swallow/speech, hand function. Establishes urgency, support needs and DVLA discussion

Onset tempo is the most powerful triage tool in ataxia. Sudden onset = vascular or toxic until proven otherwise (emergency); subacute progression over weeks should raise the spectre of a tumour or paraneoplastic syndrome; slowly progressive over years suggests a degenerative, hereditary or nutritional cause that can be worked up in primary care. A careful drug and alcohol history identifies the large group of reversible ataxias — antiepileptic toxicity, lithium toxicity, and thiamine/B12 deficiency are all treatable and must not be missed.

3
Diagnose

Localise — Cerebellar vs Sensory vs Vestibular

Cerebellar ataxia
Wide-based gait, past-pointing, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor, nystagmus, scanning/slurred speech. Romberg: no marked deterioration on eye closure — but the test is not interpretable if the patient cannot stand safely with eyes open. Midline lesion → truncal ataxia; hemisphere lesion → ipsilateral limb ataxia
Sensory ataxia
Loss of proprioception (dorsal columns / peripheral nerve). Romberg positive (much worse with eyes closed / in the dark). Stamping gait, looks at feet. Causes: B12 deficiency (including nitrous oxide), diabetes, tabes dorsalis
Vestibular ataxia
Veers to one side, with vertigo, nausea, nystagmus. No limb incoordination. Acute peripheral (labyrinthitis/neuronitis) vs central (see vestibular pathways). Severe truncal ataxia or inability to walk unaided is concerning for central disease. Acute hearing loss with vertigo can indicate posterior circulation ischaemia. HINTS only in continuous acute vestibular syndrome, by a trained and experienced examiner — it is not a substitute for neurological assessment or imaging when central features are present
Acute vs chronic
Re-confirm tempo from history — drives whether this is an emergency referral (acute) or a primary-care work-up (chronic)
Common reversible causes
Alcohol intoxication, antiepileptic/lithium toxicity, Wernicke's (thiamine), B12 deficiency (including nitrous oxide-related), hypothyroidism — all treatable; screen for these

The Romberg test is the key bedside discriminator: a positive Romberg (steady with eyes open, unsteady when closed) indicates a sensory ataxia — the patient relies on vision to compensate for lost proprioception. A positive Romberg can also reflect vestibular loss. If the patient cannot stand safely with eyes open (as in severe cerebellar ataxia), the test is not interpretable. Distinguishing peripheral vestibular causes (benign) from central ones (stroke) acutely uses the HINTS examination only in acute vestibular syndrome and only when performed by a trained, experienced examiner; if one is unavailable, refer immediately through the stroke pathway.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Gait & stance
Wide-based, unsteady, unable to tandem-walk (cerebellar) — do not test unsupported standing or tandem gait without assistance if at high falls risk. Romberg: assess whether the patient can stand safely with eyes open; marked deterioration on eye closure supports sensory or vestibular ataxia; not interpretable if they cannot stand with eyes open. Truncal ataxia (can't sit/stand unsupported = midline cerebellar)
Cerebellar signs
Finger-nose (past-pointing, intention tremor), heel-shin, rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia), rebound, nystagmus, dysarthria. Lateralise (ipsilateral to hemisphere lesion)
Cranial nerves / eyes
Nystagmus (direction), ophthalmoplegia (Wernicke's, brainstem), diplopia, fundi for papilloedema (raised ICP). Hearing (vestibular)
Peripheral neuro
Tone, power, reflexes, plantars. Proprioception & vibration (dorsal columns — B12, sensory ataxia). Peripheral neuropathy signs
Vital signs / general
BP & conscious level, capillary glucose, temperature (infection), signs of chronic alcohol/liver disease, thyroid status, cachexia/lymphadenopathy (malignancy)
Vestibular (if acute vertigo)
Use HINTS (head-impulse, nystagmus, test of skew) only in adults with continuous acute vestibular syndrome and only if the examiner is trained and experienced — not for chronic ataxia, brief positional vertigo or episodic dizziness without spontaneous nystagmus. An abnormal HINTS result — normal head-impulse, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation — requires immediate neuroimaging/stroke assessment. If a trained examiner is unavailable, refer immediately through the stroke pathway. A "negative" HINTS is reassuring only when performed correctly in the appropriate syndrome

Truncal ataxia (inability to sit or stand unsupported) localises to the cerebellar vermis (midline) and is a feature of alcohol-related degeneration and midline tumours (e.g. medulloblastoma in children). Lateralised limb ataxia is ipsilateral to a cerebellar hemisphere lesion. Always examine the fundi: papilloedema in a patient with ataxia and headache mandates urgent imaging before any lumbar puncture. Documenting proprioception and vibration sense is essential — a sensory ataxia from B12 deficiency is reversible if caught early but causes irreversible cord damage if missed.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — Screen for Reversible Causes

Acute ataxia
Do NOT delay referral for tests — emergency imaging via the acute stroke/neurosurgical pathway (non-contrast CT, CT angiography/perfusion, or MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging as appropriate). Check capillary glucose and treat hypoglycaemia immediately. If Wernicke's suspected → emergency hospital assessment with urgent parenteral thiamine
Initial primary-care bloods (chronic/subacute)
FBC, U&Es, LFTs (alcohol), glucose/HbA1c, B12 & folate, TFTs, calcium & albumin, ESR/CRP (if systemic disease suspected), coeliac serology (consider — NICE NG20), plus medication and alcohol review. A normal initial blood screen does not justify reassurance if ataxia is progressive.
Drug levels
Phenytoin, carbamazepine, lithium levels if on these (ataxia = toxicity). Review recent dose changes / interactions. Phenytoin levels are affected by albumin, renal disease and interactions — consider a corrected or free level.
Targeted / specialist-directed
Vitamin E, copper and caeruloplasmin, HIV/syphilis serology, thyroid antibodies, autoimmune testing. Suspected nitrous oxide: FBC/MCV, B12 and folate, methylmalonic acid and homocysteine where available (a normal serum B12 does not exclude it), MRI spine/brain as directed by neurology. Paraneoplastic antibodies + malignancy screen if subacute/cancer history (specialist)
Imaging
MRI brain is usually the preferred investigation for chronic or subacute unexplained ataxia (cerebellum, posterior fossa, demyelination), usually via neurology. Acute onset requires emergency stroke/neurosurgical assessment and urgent CT/MRI according to the local pathway; do not arrange outpatient MRI as a substitute for emergency assessment
Genetic testing
For suspected hereditary ataxia (family history, young onset) — arranged by neurology/genetics, with counselling
When to refer not test
Don't sit on acute ataxia awaiting outpatient bloods. Don't request MRI/EEG/genetics from primary care without specialist input — refer neurology

A panel of simple bloods identifies the common reversible ataxias that primary care can act on: B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, alcohol/liver disease, and antiepileptic or lithium toxicity. Lithium toxicity is a particular trap — ataxia, coarse tremor and confusion signal a level that can rise rapidly to fatal levels, so check it urgently and agree a same-day plan with acute/specialist advice before withholding doses. MRI is far superior to CT for the cerebellum and posterior fossa and is the imaging of choice for non-acute ataxia, but it should be requested through neurology, who also coordinate paraneoplastic and genetic work-up.

6
Refer

Referral — Match Urgency to Tempo

999
Sudden-onset new ataxia/unsteadiness, especially with ↓GCS, severe headache/vomiting, dysarthria, diplopia, dysphagia, limb weakness, sensory loss or visual symptoms; acute vestibular syndrome with abnormal HINTS; suspected intracranial haemorrhage or posterior fossa swelling; unstable suspected Wernicke's; meningoencephalitis; lithium toxicity with collapse or severe neurological features
Emergency hospital assessment
Not necessarily 999: clinically stable suspected Wernicke's; significant antiepileptic toxicity without instability; moderate lithium toxicity without collapse or severe neurological features; unexplained acute ataxia in a stable patient who can be transferred safely
Same-day
New unexplained ataxia without a clear benign cause; suspected raised ICP/posterior fossa lesion; new ataxia in a child (emergency transfer if red features); suspected drug toxicity where the patient is stable
Neurology — urgent
Rapidly (days–weeks) progressive unsteady gait → suspected cancer pathway referral for neurological assessment (NICE NG127); subacute progressive ataxia (weeks), suspected MS/demyelination, suspected paraneoplastic (with malignancy screen); suspected nitrous oxide-induced neurological disease
Neurology — routine
Gradually progressive unsteady gait (NICE NG127) — refer after or alongside the initial reversible-cause screen; suspected hereditary ataxia (→ genetics + counselling)
Stroke pathway
Acute ataxia of vascular origin → hyperacute stroke unit (thrombolysis/thrombectomy window; neurosurgery for cerebellar haematoma/swelling)
Primary care manages
Confirmed reversible causes: replace B12, treat hypothyroidism, manage drug toxicity with an agreed specialist/acute-care plan, alcohol support + thiamine. Reassess after treatment
DVLA
Advise the patient not to drive if ataxia, impaired coordination, visual disturbance, cognitive impairment, sedating medication or unpredictable episodes could impair safe vehicle control. Advise them to check the current DVLA medical standards (including Group 2 requirements) and notify DVLA where required for their diagnosis or functional impairment. Document the discussion

The referral destination follows directly from the onset tempo established in the history. Acute ataxia is treated as a stroke/emergency; subacute progression earns an urgent neurology referral (to catch tumours, demyelination and paraneoplastic syndromes within a useful window); chronic ataxia is worked up more deliberately once reversible causes are excluded. Driving is an important and often-forgotten conversation — advise not driving where symptoms could impair safe vehicle control, and advise checking the current DVLA standards and notifying DVLA where required.

7
Treat

Treat the Cause + Reversible-Cause Actions

Suspected Wernicke's
Parenteral thiamine Urgent
Emergency hospital assessment; parenteral thiamine urgently according to the local hospital protocol (generic IV or IM thiamine per local availability — IM Pabrinex discontinued). In hospital, high-dose parenteral thiamine for a minimum of 5 days unless Wernicke's is excluded, then oral (NICE CG100); preparation and dose per the local hospital protocol. Give before or alongside glucose where feasible, but do not delay treatment of confirmed severe hypoglycaemia. Full triad not required.
Drug toxicity
Same-day review + levels Reversible
Suspected drug toxicity requires same-day clinical review, medication reconciliation and urgent level testing where indicated. Do not advise abrupt cessation of antiepileptic medication or lithium without specialist/acute-care advice, except where directed by the treating team; withhold further doses only after risk assessment and with a treatment plan. Lithium toxicity → urgent (may need admission/dialysis)
B12 deficiency
Hydroxocobalamin Replace
IM B12 loading then maintenance (treat B12 before folate). Do not delay treatment of neurological B12 deficiency while awaiting all results; assess for pernicious anaemia and malabsorption; ask about nitrous oxide (if suspected: parenteral B12 and urgent specialist advice, even if serum B12 is not low). Neurological recovery may be incomplete if treatment is delayed; seek specialist advice for severe/subacute neurological presentations — see B12 prescription card
Alcohol-related
Thiamine + alcohol support Prevent progression
Offer prophylactic oral thiamine to harmful or dependent drinkers who are malnourished or at risk of malnourishment, have decompensated liver disease, are in acute withdrawal, or before and during planned assisted withdrawal (NICE CG100); alcohol cessation support. Suspected Wernicke's encephalopathy requires parenteral thiamine and hospital assessment
AcuteStabilise & refer — for acute ataxia, ABCDE, capillary glucose (treat hypoglycaemia immediately), urgent parenteral thiamine if Wernicke's suspected, and emergency referral; do not delay for tests
ReversibleCorrect what you can — replace B12, treat hypothyroidism, manage drug toxicity with a plan, alcohol cessation + thiamine. Many ataxias improve substantially
SymptomaticRehabilitation — physiotherapy (balance/gait), occupational therapy (aids, home safety), SALT for speech/swallow. Falls prevention
DefinitiveSpecialist-directed — stroke pathway, MS disease-modifying therapy, tumour management, genetic counselling for hereditary ataxia

A meaningful proportion of ataxia is reversible, and primary care can make a real difference: replacing B12 before the dorsal-column damage becomes permanent, recognising antiepileptic or lithium toxicity and arranging same-day review, treating hypothyroidism, and giving thiamine to at-risk drinkers. For the many ataxias without a curative treatment (hereditary, degenerative), multidisciplinary rehabilitation — physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech and language therapy — is the evidence-based mainstay and substantially improves function, safety and quality of life.

8
Lifestyle

Rehabilitation, Safety & Support

Falls prevention Home hazard assessment, grab rails, good lighting, footwear. Falls are the major cause of injury in ataxia — OT review.
Physiotherapy Balance & gait training, core stability, walking aids (frame/stick). Maintains mobility and independence. For stroke-related ataxia, rehabilitation should be task-specific, repetitive and progressive (National Clinical Guideline for Stroke).
Speech & swallow SALT for dysarthria and dysphagia — aspiration risk with cerebellar/brainstem disease. Coughing or choking with food, wet voice, recurrent chest infections or inability to maintain oral intake → urgent swallow assessment. Communication aids.
Alcohol Cessation halts (and may partially reverse) alcohol-related cerebellar degeneration. Offer prophylactic oral thiamine where at risk (NICE CG100). Brief intervention + support services.
Driving (DVLA) Advise not to drive if ataxia, impaired coordination, visual disturbance, cognitive impairment, sedating medication or unpredictable episodes could impair safe vehicle control; check current DVLA medical standards and notify DVLA where required. Document advice. Reassessment depends on cause & recovery.
Nutrition Address deficiencies (B12, thiamine, vitamin E). Dietitian if swallow impaired or weight loss. Coeliac diet if gluten ataxia.
Psychological & social Progressive/hereditary ataxia carries significant psychological impact — signpost Ataxia UK, peer support, carer support, benefits advice.
Medication review Avoid/limit sedatives, alcohol, and drugs that worsen coordination. Review antiepileptic doses (never stop abruptly without specialist advice). Caution combining CNS depressants. Advise avoiding recreational nitrous oxide.

For chronic and progressive ataxias there is rarely a cure, so the goals shift to maximising function, preventing complications and supporting quality of life. Falls and aspiration are the principal causes of harm — proactive OT, physiotherapy and SALT input prevent injury and hospital admission. Ataxia UK provides specialist information and peer support that patients and carers value highly. Continued alcohol use in alcohol-related cerebellar degeneration drives ongoing, eventually irreversible, loss — cessation is the single most effective intervention.

9
Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

After reversible-cause treatment
Reassess clinically after correcting B12/thyroid/drug levels — partial or full recovery expected. Persisting/progressive ataxia despite correction → neurology
Drug-level monitoring
Recheck antiepileptic/lithium levels after dose change and confirm resolution of ataxia. Lithium: ongoing level + renal/thyroid monitoring
Chronic ataxia review
Periodic review of mobility, falls, swallow, mood and carer strain. Reassess aids and therapy input. Coordinate with specialist/MDT
Safety-net 999
Sudden worsening, new headache/vomiting/drowsiness (cerebellar swelling/hydrocephalus), new focal deficit, confusion or ophthalmoplegia (Wernicke's), fever with headache, neck stiffness or photophobia (meningoencephalitis), airway compromise or suspected acute brainstem stroke
Safety-net same-day
Rapidly progressive unsteadiness, new neurological symptoms, signs of drug toxicity, fever with neurological change
Aspiration / swallow
Coughing or choking with food, wet voice, recurrent chest infections or inability to maintain oral intake requires urgent swallow assessment (risk of aspiration pneumonia). Suspected acute brainstem stroke or airway compromise requires emergency assessment
DVLA review
Driving status reassessed according to cause and recovery; patient checks current DVLA standards and notifies DVLA directly where required; GP supports with information on request; document the discussion

After an acute cerebellar stroke the danger period extends over the first few days as oedema peaks — a patient who becomes drowsy or develops worsening headache and vomiting may be developing hydrocephalus or brainstem compression and needs emergency reassessment. For chronic ataxias, the recurring safety issues are falls and aspiration: a low threshold for SALT swallow assessment prevents aspiration pneumonia, the commonest cause of death in advanced cerebellar disease. Clear safety-netting and documented driving advice protect the patient and are medico-legally important.
