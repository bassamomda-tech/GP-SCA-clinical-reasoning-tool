Safety

Red Flags — Severe Anaemia, Thalassaemia Major & Malignancy

Severe anaemia + chest pain, dyspnoea at rest, syncope, haemodynamic compromise, heart failure or active bleeding → Same-day hospital assessment. Transfusion is a hospital decision based on symptoms, haemodynamic state, comorbidity and urgency (NICE NG24: restrictive threshold around 70 g/L where there is no major haemorrhage or acute coronary syndrome); in non-bleeding adults use single-unit transfusion followed by clinical reassessment and repeat Hb. Identify and control the bleeding source. Iron replacement remains necessary after transfusion.
Confirmed IDA in a post-menopausal woman or man of any age Occult GI blood loss, including possible malignancy, must be excluded. → GI investigation, usually bidirectional endoscopy (OGD + colonoscopy; CT colonography if colonoscopy unsuitable), unless there is a clear alternative explanation and the local pathway advises otherwise — consider upper and lower GI pathology even without GI symptoms. Offer quantitative FIT per NICE NG12/local colorectal pathway: FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → colorectal suspected cancer referral; FIT <10 (or not returned) does not exclude GI pathology and should not override strong clinical concern. Coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA + total IgA). Urine dipstick/microscopy for haematuria. Do not assume dietary cause.
MCV <70 fl + Hb very low + splenomegaly + ethnic background (Mediterranean, South/Southeast Asian, Middle Eastern, African) Consider thalassaemia major/intermedia. → Urgent haematology. Haemoglobin electrophoresis + HPLC. Transfusion-dependent thalassaemia = iron chelation therapy mandatory (deferasirox/desferrioxamine). Genetic counselling.
Microcytosis + normal or high ferritin + high CRP in patient with chronic inflammatory disease Anaemia of chronic disease with co-existing iron deficiency (functional iron deficiency). → Interpret ferritin with transferrin saturation, CRP/ESR, blood count and reticulocyte Hb where available — no single test in isolation; TSAT <20% supports iron deficiency despite a normal ferritin. Oral iron first unless it is ineffective, not tolerated, poorly absorbed or rapid repletion is needed; IV iron is not automatically preferred because inflammation is present.
Microcytic anaemia + blood transfusion history + iron overload symptoms (bronze skin + hepatomegaly + diabetes + heart failure) Transfusional haemosiderosis. Markedly raised ferritin → haematology for assessment (e.g. liver MRI iron quantification) and chelation decisions.
Microcytosis + sideroblastic anaemia features + history of lead exposure, alcohol, or isoniazid use Sideroblastic anaemia — ringed sideroblasts in bone marrow. → Haematology (consider MDS if other cytopenias or abnormal film). Lead level if exposure suspected. Pyridoxine (B6) trial (hereditary sideroblastic anaemia).
Microcytosis (MCV below 80 fl) combined with an iron deficiency pattern in a man or post-menopausal woman is a red flag for gastrointestinal malignancy that must not be attributed to dietary causes without investigation — the BSG guidelines are explicit: iron deficiency anaemia in a post-menopausal woman or a man of any age mandates bidirectional GI investigation (OGD + colonoscopy) regardless of whether there are GI symptoms (FIT, offered per NICE NG12, guides colorectal cancer referral but does not replace bidirectional endoscopy). Approximately 10-15% of IDA in these groups has an underlying GI malignancy (colorectal, gastric, or oesophageal cancer). The investigation must be bidirectional because approximately 10% of patients have lesions in both the upper and lower GI tract. Coeliac disease should also be excluded in every patient with IDA (anti-tTG IgA + total IgA) — it is present in approximately 5-7% of IDA patients. The GP who prescribes iron without investigating the cause in a post-menopausal woman or man has made a clinical error, regardless of whether there are GI symptoms.

Diagnose

Causes of Microcytosis — Classification

Iron deficiency (most common)
Causes: blood loss (GI most important in men + post-menopausal women; menstruation most common in pre-menopausal women; also NSAIDs, urinary tract loss, blood donation, previous surgery), reduced absorption (coeliac disease, achlorhydria, post-gastrectomy, H. pylori, IBD), increased demand (pregnancy, growth, EPO therapy), inadequate intake (vegan/vegetarian diet, poverty). Serum ferritin <15 mcg/L strongly indicates absent iron stores; <30 mcg/L generally supports iron deficiency; a higher threshold (around 45 mcg/L) may improve sensitivity where inflammation is present (BSG 2021). Ferritin may be falsely normal or raised with inflammation, liver disease, infection or malignancy. MCV typically <80 fl. Hypochromic microcytic red cells on film. Pencil cells characteristic.
Thalassaemia (alpha and beta)
Alpha-thalassaemia: 1 or 2 gene deletions = thalassaemia trait (asymptomatic, MCV low, Hb normal). 3 deletions = HbH disease (haemolytic anaemia, splenomegaly). 4 deletions = hydrops fetalis (stillbirth). Alpha-thal trait commonly has normal Hb electrophoresis — DNA testing is specialist/laboratory-led, particularly when reproductive risk is relevant. Beta-thalassaemia trait: HbA2 usually raised (commonly ≥3.5%); iron deficiency can lower HbA2 and mask beta-thal trait — correct confirmed iron deficiency before relying on HbA2. Beta-thal major: Hb F predominant, severe anaemia from infancy, transfusion-dependent.
Anaemia of chronic disease (ACD)
Microcytosis less common than in IDA — ACD usually normocytic or borderline microcytic. Mechanism: hepcidin rise (from IL-6 in inflammation) blocks intestinal iron absorption + releases iron from macrophages into plasma. Ferritin: normal or elevated (acute phase reactant). Serum iron: low. TIBC: low or normal (unlike IDA where TIBC is elevated). Transferrin saturation <20% in both IDA and ACD. MCV usually 75-85 fl in ACD.
Sideroblastic anaemia
Hereditary (ALAS2 X-linked — pyridoxine-responsive) or acquired (MDS, alcohol, lead toxicity, isoniazid, chloramphenicol). Microcytic hypochromic cells + elevated serum iron + elevated ferritin + elevated transferrin saturation (iron-loaded but cannot be incorporated into haem). Ring sideroblasts in bone marrow (mitochondrial iron loading). Haematology-led diagnosis.
The Mentzer index (MCV ÷ RBC count) is a screening heuristic with limited diagnostic performance — it cannot reliably distinguish iron deficiency from thalassaemia trait in an individual patient and should not be used alone to diagnose or exclude thalassaemia, or to trigger DNA testing. Persistent microcytosis with normal ferritin and no evidence of inflammation should prompt haemoglobinopathy investigation through the local laboratory, interpreting red-cell indices, MCH, Hb analysis/HPLC, HbA2, HbF and family origin together. The clinical importance: giving iron to a patient with thalassaemia trait (who has normal iron stores) is ineffective and may cause unnecessary investigation. Conversely, missing IDA in a patient thought to have thalassaemia trait can cause ongoing investigation for a 'genetic condition' while GI blood loss goes untreated.

Investigation Strategy — Iron Studies Interpretation

Iron deficiency — confirming the pattern
Ferritin <15 mcg/L strongly indicates absent iron stores; ferritin <30 mcg/L generally supports iron deficiency (BSG 2021): treat + investigate cause. Ferritin may be falsely normal or raised with inflammation, liver disease, infection or malignancy — interpret it alongside transferrin saturation (<20% supports iron deficiency), CRP/ESR, the blood count, reticulocyte haemoglobin where available and clinical context. No single test should be interpreted in isolation, and there is no fixed CRP cut-off. Useful primary set: FBC + MCV, MCH, RDW + Serum ferritin + CRP/ESR (if inflammation suspected) + transferrin saturation (where ferritin may be misleading) + reticulocyte Hb / % hypochromic cells (where available) + renal function + coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA + total IgA) + urine dipstick (haematuria); reticulocyte count where clinically indicated. Serum iron is highly variable and should not be overemphasised. Soluble transferrin receptor, zinc protoporphyrin and hepcidin are not routinely recommended (BSH 2021).
IDA vs ACD vs mixed: iron study pattern
IDA: Ferritin LOW | Serum iron LOW | TIBC HIGH | TS <20%
ACD: Ferritin NORMAL/HIGH | Serum iron LOW | TIBC LOW/NORMAL | TS <20%
Mixed IDA+ACD: Ferritin VARIABLE | Serum iron LOW | TIBC LOW | TS <20%
Thalassaemia workup
Persistent microcytosis with normal ferritin and no inflammation → Hb analysis / HPLC (HbA2, HbF) interpreted with MCH, red-cell indices and family origin (beta-thal trait: HbA2 usually ≥3.5% — may be lowered by iron deficiency, so correct confirmed iron deficiency first; alpha-thal trait: commonly normal) · DNA analysis (alpha-thalassaemia — specialist/laboratory-led, particularly when reproductive risk is relevant; not triggered by the Mentzer index alone) · Ferritin ± TSAT (a normal ferritin does not absolutely exclude iron deficiency in inflammation, liver disease or infection) · Partner testing (carrier identified and pregnancy planned or ongoing — via the NHS Sickle Cell and Thalassaemia Screening Programme)
Normal ferritin + persistent microcytosis
Review CRP and transferrin saturation · consider Hb analysis/HPLC and thalassaemia trait · review lead, alcohol and isoniazid exposure · consider sideroblastic anaemia or MDS if other cytopenias or abnormal film · refer to haematology if unexplained or clinically significant.
The iron studies pattern is one of the most useful and underutilised diagnostic discriminators in haematology — the combination of serum ferritin, serum iron, TIBC, and transferrin saturation helps distinguish iron deficiency anaemia from anaemia of chronic disease, although no single test should be interpreted in isolation and serum iron is highly variable. The key distinguishing feature: in IDA, the body depletes iron stores (low ferritin) and upregulates iron absorption by producing more transferrin (high TIBC). In ACD, inflammation-driven hepcidin blocks iron release from macrophage stores — iron is actually trapped in the reticuloendothelial system (ferritin is elevated as an acute phase protein; TIBC is low or normal because the liver reduces transferrin synthesis in response to IL-6). The transferrin saturation (serum iron ÷ TIBC × 100%) is below 20% in both conditions — it does not discriminate. The clinical trap: in a patient with both IDA and ACD (common in IBD, RA with GI blood loss from NSAID use, or malignancy with nutritional iron deficiency), the ferritin may appear normal because the ACD component elevates it while the IDA component depletes it — interpret ferritin with transferrin saturation, CRP and reticulocyte haemoglobin where available (a ferritin threshold around 45 mcg/L may improve sensitivity); sTfR, zinc protoporphyrin and hepcidin are not routinely recommended (BSH).

Thalassaemia — Carrier Identification & Pregnancy Planning

UK antenatal thalassaemia screening
All pregnant women in the UK are offered haemoglobinopathy screening (ideally before 10 weeks). High-prevalence areas (South and East London, Midlands, parts of Yorkshire): all women screened. Low-prevalence areas: family origin questionnaire first. If mother is a carrier: partner testing offered. If both parents are carriers, the fetal risk depends on the exact maternal and paternal genotype (DNA testing may be required for alpha-zero thalassaemia risk) — refer for specialist haemoglobinopathy/genetic counselling and discuss reproductive options, including prenatal diagnosis, with specialist genetics/haemoglobinopathy services.
GP-identified thalassaemia trait
Incidental finding of persistent microcytosis with normal ferritin and normal Hb: request haemoglobin analysis/HPLC via the local laboratory. Beta-thalassaemia trait (HbA2 usually ≥3.5%): explain it is a carrier state, not a disease, with no symptoms. Document in clinical record. Advise: inform partner; if pregnancy is planned or ongoing, arrange testing of the biological partner through the NHS Sickle Cell and Thalassaemia Screening Programme. Specialist haemoglobinopathy/genetic counselling if the partner is also a carrier — the risk depends on the exact genotypes (do not quote a universal 25%). A low MCV in thalassaemia trait is not an indication for iron therapy — confirm concurrent iron deficiency with ferritin and, where appropriate, transferrin saturation before prescribing.
Haemoglobin variants
HbS (sickle cell trait + sickle cell disease — SS, SC, S-beta). HbC, HbE, HbD-Punjab: variable severity when combined with HbS or each other. HbH disease (alpha-thal 3 deletions): mild-moderate haemolytic anaemia, splenomegaly, no transfusion needed in most cases. Haemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC (high performance liquid chromatography) identifies and quantifies all haemoglobin variants.
Partner thalassaemia testing after a carrier is identified in primary care is a critical step that is frequently not completed — the clinical consequence of missed partner testing is a couple where both partners are thalassaemia trait carriers who conceive without knowing their risk, and present in pregnancy at 20+ weeks with a thalassaemia major fetus on anomaly scan. This is avoidable: if thalassaemia trait is identified in a patient of reproductive age, their partner must be tested before the next pregnancy, and ideally before conception. The GP's responsibility: document thalassaemia trait clearly in the clinical record, provide patient education, and advise partner testing. If the patient is pregnant at the time of diagnosis, the antenatal team will coordinate partner testing — but if identified outside pregnancy, the GP must take proactive action. The Sickle Cell and Thalassaemia Society (sicklecellsociety.org) provides excellent patient information resources.

GP management
IDA with identified source: oral iron + treat cause. Thalassaemia trait: reassurance + partner testing advice + pregnancy counselling. Coeliac + IDA: GFD + iron; ongoing follow-up per the coeliac pathway.
The bidirectional GI investigation requirement for IDA — OGD plus colonoscopy — must both be performed, not performed sequentially only if the first is normal. The BSG IDA guideline states clearly that both investigations should be arranged simultaneously in men and post-menopausal women with IDA, because approximately 10% of patients have relevant pathology in both the upper and lower GI tract. Common clinical errors: (1) arranging OGD only (finding oesophagitis and attributing IDA to it, missing a concurrent right-sided colorectal cancer); (2) arranging colonoscopy only (finding left-sided polyps but missing gastric cancer); (3) arranging FIT alone without endoscopy — FIT is a screening tool, not a diagnostic tool; a negative FIT does not exclude upper GI pathology and does not eliminate the need for OGD in a man with IDA. The GP referral should request both OGD and colonoscopy simultaneously, with a cover letter explaining: IDA confirmed (ferritin level + Hb), sex and menopausal status, GI symptoms if any, family history of CRC or gastric cancer.

Treat

Iron Replacement — Oral and IV (See Low Ferritin Algorithm)

Oral iron — dosing and administration
Ferrous sulphate 200 mg once daily (65 mg elemental iron per tablet; one tablet a day, or on alternate days if not tolerated — BSG 2021, BNF): take according to the product instructions; it may be taken with food if GI adverse effects would otherwise prevent adherence, although absorption may be reduced. Review medicines and dietary factors that may reduce absorption (calcium-containing products, antacids, acid suppression) — separate where clinically practical, but prioritise adherence. Expected response: Hb rise of approximately 10 g/L within 2 weeks (many primary-care protocols check at 4 weeks). If Hb fails to rise appropriately: check adherence, ongoing blood loss, incorrect diagnosis, malabsorption, inflammation, renal disease and haemoglobinopathy before escalating treatment. Reticulocyte peak at day 7-10 (confirms diagnosis). Continue for approximately 3 months after Hb normalises, or according to the underlying cause and response, to replenish stores (ferritin >30 mcg/L is not a universal target — interpret trends and clinical context). If not tolerated (constipation, nausea): one tablet on alternate days, another oral preparation (elemental iron content and tolerability vary individually) or parenteral iron — alternate-day dosing (BALTAR trial: equivalent efficacy to daily with fewer side effects from reduced hepcidin rise).
IV iron — indications
Consider IV iron when oral iron is not tolerated (confirmed — not poor adherence), ineffective, contraindicated or poorly absorbed (e.g. coeliac, IBD, post-bariatric), or when rapid repletion is clinically necessary (e.g. pre-operative optimisation; CKD per renal team; pregnancy from the second trimester under obstetric/haematology supervision). It is not automatically preferred because inflammation is present. GMMMG formulary first-choice options are ferric carboxymaltose and ferric derisomaltose — follow local protocols and use the product-specific dose calculation based on body weight and haemoglobin (do not default to 1,000 mg for every adult). Observe during and for at least 30 minutes after infusion for hypersensitivity. Monitor phosphate selectively (MHRA: repeated high-dose or long-term ferric carboxymaltose, or risk factors such as vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption, IBD, osteoporosis or hyperparathyroidism, or symptoms); reassess persistent hypophosphataemia.
Treating the cause alongside iron
Heavy menstrual bleeding: manage per NICE NG88 (e.g. LNG-IUS, tranexamic acid). H. pylori: test and treat if positive, per local pathway. Coeliac disease: strict GFD improves iron absorption. NSAID-related GI blood loss: stop NSAID if possible; PPI gastroprotection if essential. Review dietary inhibitors (e.g. tea/coffee with meals) where practical, prioritising adherence.
IV ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject) management in primary care is an area where UK pathways are actively expanding — many ICBs now have pathways for GP-initiated IV iron infusion in day units without specialist referral for specific indications (confirmed IDA with failed/intolerable oral iron, IBD, pre-operative anaemia, obstetric anaemia). GPs who identify patients meeting these criteria should: (1) check local ICB formulary for the IV iron pathway; (2) arrange pre-infusion blood tests (FBC, ferritin, CRP, renal function); (3) refer to the appropriate day unit with a completed referral form; (4) arrange post-infusion monitoring (FBC + ferritin at 4-6 weeks; serum phosphate selectively according to repeated dosing, risk factors and symptoms, per MHRA). IV iron in pregnancy is arranged under obstetric/haematology supervision, not routinely initiated in general practice. The GIRFT (Getting It Right First Time) programme has highlighted that IV iron is significantly underutilised in UK primary care, resulting in avoidable hospital admissions for severe IDA and unnecessary delays in surgical procedures.

Thalassaemia Management — GP Role

Thalassaemia trait (carrier state)
No treatment required. Normal life expectancy. No anaemia or only very mild anaemia. A low MCV in thalassaemia trait is not an indication for iron therapy (unnecessary + potentially harmful long-term). Confirm concurrent iron deficiency with ferritin and, where appropriate, transferrin saturation before prescribing. Document clearly in clinical record: "Thalassaemia trait — iron treatment not required; refer to genetics if pregnancy planned." Avoid misdiagnosis as IDA and repeated unnecessary iron treatment.
HbH disease (alpha-thalassaemia, 3 deletions)
Chronic mild-moderate haemolytic anaemia (Hb typically 7-10 g/dL). Splenomegaly. Jaundice. Haemolysis exacerbated by: infections, oxidant drugs (dapsone, primaquine, sulfonamides — check before prescribing). Haematology follow-up. Folic acid 5 mg OD (increased erythropoiesis demand). Transfusion only if Hb drops acutely (haemolytic crisis). Avoid iron supplementation unless concurrent iron deficiency confirmed.
Beta-thalassaemia major (transfusion-dependent)
GP awareness: patients on monthly transfusion + iron chelation (deferasirox, deferoxamine). GP role: manage intercurrent infections promptly (infection precipitates haemolytic crisis), monitor chelation side effects (deferasirox: renal function + LFTs annually; audiology and ophthalmology for deferoxamine). Annual cardiac MRI (iron overload monitoring). Hydroxyurea in some patients (increases HbF — reduces transfusion requirement). HSCT: only curative option. Haematology-led.
Pregnancy with thalassaemia trait
Refer early to obstetrician/haematologist with haemoglobinopathy experience. Ensure partner tested. Folic acid 5 mg OD from pre-conception. If both partners carry beta-thalassaemia trait, the risk of an affected pregnancy is 25%; for alpha-thalassaemia the risk depends on the specific genotype (particularly alpha-zero deletion) — specialist haemoglobinopathy/genetic counselling, with reproductive options including prenatal diagnosis discussed by specialist services. Register with haemoglobinopathy specialist centre.
Iron supplementation must not be given to patients with thalassaemia trait without confirming concurrent iron deficiency — this is a common and important prescribing error in primary care. Thalassaemia trait causes microcytosis and a mildly low MCV that looks superficially identical to early IDA. However, in thalassaemia trait, the iron stores are normal (ferritin is normal or elevated), and the microcytosis is caused by reduced haemoglobin chain synthesis rather than iron deficiency. Prescribing iron to a thalassaemia trait patient who does not also have IDA: (1) is ineffective (MCV and Hb will not improve); (2) exposes the patient to unnecessary GI side effects; and (3) if continued long-term, may cause iron overload (particularly in alpha-thalassaemia carriers who may have slightly elevated ferritin even at baseline). The safe practice: always check ferritin before prescribing iron for microcytosis. If ferritin is normal and microcytosis persists without inflammation: arrange haemoglobin analysis/HPLC before starting iron (do not rely on the Mentzer index).

Lifestyle

Iron Absorption Optimisation & Thalassaemia Carrier Awareness

Maximising dietary iron absorption Haem iron (meat/fish/poultry): 15-35% absorption — not significantly affected by dietary factors. Non-haem iron (plants/fortified foods): 2-8% absorption — highly variable. Enhancers: vitamin C (orange juice, peppers, kiwi, broccoli — increases Fe3+ to Fe2+ reduction). Meat factor (MFP factor — eating meat alongside plant iron increases non-haem iron absorption). Inhibitors: tea and coffee (polyphenols — separate by 1 hour from iron-rich meals), calcium/dairy (separate from iron tablets where practical, prioritising adherence), phytates in wholegrains (soaking/sprouting reduces), zinc supplements (compete with iron).
Iron tablet tips for tolerability Take according to the product instructions. Once-daily dosing is now standard (BSG 2021); if nausea/constipation occur, taking it with food (reduces absorption but improves adherence) or alternate-day dosing can help. Alternate-day dosing (BALTAR trial): equivalent efficacy to daily with significantly fewer GI side effects. Another oral preparation (e.g. ferrous fumarate or gluconate) may be tried — elemental iron content differs and tolerability is individual. Ferric maltol (Feraccru): licensed for IDA in IBD — good tolerance, superior absorption profile.
Vegetarian and vegan iron strategies Iron requirement on plant-based diet approximately 1.8x higher than omnivore diet. Key plant iron sources: fortified breakfast cereals (up to 8mg/serving), lentils (3.3mg/100g cooked), tofu (3.5mg/100g), pumpkin seeds (9mg/100g), spinach (2.7mg/100g cooked). Always combine with vitamin C. Check FBC and ferritin if symptoms suggest deficiency. Cooking in cast-iron cookware contributes small amounts of iron.
Thalassaemia carrier awareness and pregnancy Every patient with known thalassaemia trait should understand: it is a carrier state, not a disease. It affects your children only if your partner is also a carrier. Before pregnancy: tell your partner, encourage them to get tested. If both are carriers, the chance of an affected child depends on the exact genotypes — specialist counselling explains the risk and reproductive options, including prenatal diagnosis. Sickle Cell and Thalassaemia Society (sicklecellsociety.org) — patient information and support. UK Thalassaemia Society (ukts.org).
Antenatal iron monitoring In pregnancy, interpret Hb and ferritin using the current maternity/BSH pathway (anaemia: Hb <110 g/L first trimester, <105 g/L second/third trimester; ferritin <30 mcg/L supports depleted stores) — diagnostic thresholds and thresholds for giving iron are not interchangeable. Treat confirmed iron deficiency. Refer or discuss with obstetrics/haematology when anaemia is severe, late in pregnancy, unresponsive or oral iron is not tolerated. IV iron is generally considered from the second trimester onwards under specialist/local protocol — not routinely initiated in general practice. Neonatal iron stores depend on maternal iron status — severe maternal IDA risks neonatal iron deficiency.
Monitoring after GI investigation for IDA Following good-quality negative bidirectional endoscopy, consider small-bowel and renal-tract investigation if IDA persists, recurs or responds inadequately to iron replacement (capsule endoscopy is the preferred small-bowel test when indicated). Long-term iron may be appropriate where the cause is irreversible or remains unidentified after appropriate evaluation; individualise follow-up blood tests according to recurrence and cause. Document: investigations performed, results, action taken, safety-netting. Recurrence of IDA without new GI pathology identified → gastroenterology for small-bowel and renal-tract assessment.
Adherence support for iron treatment Iron treatment requires 3-4+ months for full store repletion — poor adherence is extremely common because patients feel better once Hb is normalised (typically 4-8 weeks) and stop taking tablets. Explain: stopping iron when you feel better leaves your stores depleted, which will cause IDA to return within months. Set a clear end-date: "take iron tablets every day until [date — 3 months after Hb normalised]." Pill organiser or phone alarm.
The duration of iron supplementation after Hb normalisation is one of the most important patient education points that prevents IDA relapse — most patients stop taking iron when their symptoms resolve (usually 4-8 weeks into treatment). However, at the point when the Hb normalises, the iron stores (ferritin) are still depleted. The bone marrow's priority is to restore the Hb to normal first, using all available iron for haemoglobin synthesis — only after this is the remaining iron stored. Stopping iron when Hb normalises leaves a patient with replete Hb but near-empty stores (ferritin often still below 10-15 mcg/L), meaning the next episode of increased demand (heavy period, minor illness, dietary restriction) will cause IDA to return rapidly. The message to patients: 'We will check your blood test in 3 months — you need to keep taking the iron tablets until then, even when you feel completely better. This is because it takes your body longer to refill its iron stores than it takes to fix the anaemia.'

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

IDA treatment monitoring
FBC at about 4 weeks (expect an Hb rise of approximately 10 g/L within 2 weeks). Ferritin after the course — interpret trends and clinical context (ferritin >30 mcg/L is not a universal target). Continue iron for approximately 3 months after Hb normalises, or according to the cause and response. If Hb fails to rise appropriately: check adherence, ongoing blood loss, incorrect diagnosis, malabsorption, inflammation, renal disease and haemoglobinopathy before escalating treatment.
After GI investigation
OGD + colonoscopy results reviewed and documented. If both normal (good quality): small-bowel (capsule endoscopy) and renal-tract investigation if IDA persists, recurs or responds inadequately to iron; individualise follow-up rather than mandatory annual ferritin testing. Close the loop: confirm referral outcome received.
Thalassaemia trait management
Document in clinical record + summary. Advise partner testing if reproductive age. Genetic counselling referral if both partners carriers. Avoid prescribing iron without confirming concurrent IDA (ferritin).
Coeliac-related IDA
Ongoing coeliac follow-up per NICE NG20 and the local coeliac pathway. Recheck FBC + ferritin until iron deficiency is corrected; strict GFD improves iron absorption.
Urgent referral
IDA in post-menopausal woman or man without GI investigation → GI investigation now (usually bidirectional endoscopy) + FIT per NICE NG12 (colorectal suspected cancer referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g; FIT <10 does not exclude pathology) · Inadequate Hb response on oral iron → check adherence, ongoing loss, diagnosis, malabsorption, inflammation, renal disease and haemoglobinopathy; then consider IV iron · Thalassaemia both partners carriers + pregnancy → urgent genetics + obstetrics
Within 2 weeks
New GI symptoms during iron treatment → OGD or colonoscopy urgently · Ferritin not normalising after 3 months iron → malabsorption screen (coeliac, H. pylori) · Persistent microcytosis with normal ferritin and no inflammation → haemoglobin analysis/HPLC (consider family origin)
The clinical governance requirement for closed-loop referral management applies critically in the IDA context — IDA is one of the most common diagnoses associated with missed cancer diagnoses in UK medicolegal claims. The typical claim pattern: GP identifies IDA in a 62-year-old man → prescribes iron without arranging GI investigation → patient's Hb improves on iron → GP assumes problem resolved → patient returns 8 months later with symptomatic stage IV colorectal cancer. Prevention: every diagnosis of IDA in a man or post-menopausal woman must be followed by: (1) arrangement of bidirectional GI investigation (OGD + colonoscopy); (2) documentation that the referral was sent; (3) a system to confirm the referral was received and acted upon; and (4) review of the results when available. If a patient declines investigation, this must be documented: 'Patient fully informed of investigation requirement and cancer risk. Patient declines investigation — reasons documented. Patient safety-netted to attend urgently if symptoms develop.'
