Red Flags — Meningococcal Sepsis & Life-Threatening Haematological Emergencies

Suspect meningococcal disease and transfer urgently; consider the whole clinical picture. Purpura >2 mm, a rapidly spreading non-blanching rash, or meningitis features plus a non-blanching rash are particularly concerning (NICE NG240). Do not wait for blood results in a clinically unwell patient.

Non-blanching rash + fever + systemically unwell Suspected meningococcal / other bacterial septicaemia → 999 and pre-alert. Do not delay transfer to give antibiotics; give IV/IM ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin (dose per current BNF/BNFC and local protocol) when strongly suspected or if transfer will be significantly delayed, unless severe immediate allergy to that drug. Do NOT wait for investigation results. Glass test helps identify a non-blanching rash but does not exclude serious infection.
Purpura + fever + haemodynamic compromise Purpura fulminans — DIC from sepsis. Pale, mottled, tachycardic, cold peripheries, prolonged capillary refill, altered consciousness → 999. Parenteral antibiotic only if it does not delay transfer.
Petechiae above clavicles + no fever + forceful coughing / vomiting Possible Valsalva petechiae (raised intrathoracic pressure). Facial/neck petechiae after vomiting, coughing fit or crying. Diagnosis of exclusion — before reassurance: check temperature and observations, examine the whole child, confirm the rash is not spreading, consider recent infection, medication, thrombocytopenia and safeguarding. Distribution alone does not rule out serious disease. Explicit safety-netting.
Purpura + joint pain + abdominal pain + haematuria in child IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura / HSP) — most common vasculitis in children. Palpable purpura on buttocks/legs + arthritis + colicky abdominal pain + renal involvement (haematuria, proteinuria). Check BP + urinalysis. Same-day paediatrics if severe/persistent abdominal pain, GI bleeding, significant joint involvement or inability to mobilise, hypertension, significant proteinuria/haematuria, renal impairment, dehydration or systemic illness; otherwise structured paediatric/GP follow-up per local pathway.
Purpura + pallor + lymphadenopathy + hepatosplenomegaly Acute leukaemia (ALL/AML) — bone marrow failure → thrombocytopenia + anaemia + infection. Child or young person: immediate specialist assessment (NICE NG12, updated April 2026); adult: very urgent FBC within 48 hours. FBC: pancytopenia or blast cells on film = same-day haematology.
Petechiae / bruising in child without adequate explanation Possible physical abuse. Document developmental stage, location, size, colour and pattern of lesions. Bruising or purpura in a pre-mobile infant: treat as a safeguarding concern requiring urgent paediatric/safeguarding assessment while considering bleeding disorders and other medical causes — the two can coexist. Do not discharge without an appropriate safety plan where there is significant concern.
Purpura + thrombocytopenia <20 × 10⁹/L Severe ITP or other thrombocytopenic disorder — significant bleeding risk. Same-day haematology discussion (a safety trigger, not a treatment algorithm); 999 if significant bleeding. Platelet transfusion in ITP is specialist-led and generally reserved for life-threatening or critical-site bleeding.
Purpura + thrombocytopenia + anaemia/haemolysis, renal impairment or neurological symptoms Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) or haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) — microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia + thrombocytopenia + organ failure. Emergency hospital assessment / 999. Request FBC + film, renal function, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin and reticulocytes only where this does not delay transfer; do not wait for ADAMTS13 results before contacting haematology. Plasma exchange (TTP) or supportive care (HUS) — haematology/renal emergency.

Meningococcal septicaemia is the most rapidly fatal bacterial infection in the UK — it can kill a previously well individual within 12–24 hours of first symptoms. The classic purpuric non-blanching rash (the "glass test" rash) represents petechial and purpuric haemorrhage from septic emboli and DIC, and indicates established septicaemia. By the time the rash appears, the patient is already critically ill. NICE NG240 (2024) advises not delaying transfer to give antibiotics: give ceftriaxone (preferred when available) or benzylpenicillin before transfer when meningococcal disease is strongly suspected or a clinically significant delay is expected, provided this does not delay transfer. Check doses against the current BNF/BNFC and local emergency medicines protocol. Meningococcal disease can occur without a rash, and absence of fever does not exclude serious infection in an infant or immunocompromised patient. The glass test helps identify a non-blanching rash but is not diagnostic: a negative or equivocal result does not exclude serious infection, lighting and skin tone affect interpretation, and rashes may evolve from blanching to non-blanching. NICE advises that rashes may be difficult to see on brown, black or tanned skin — look for petechiae in the conjunctivae. Valsalva petechiae (from coughing, vomiting, or straining) are a benign cause of petechiae suggested by: distribution confined to the face/neck, absence of fever, a well child with normal observations, and a clear precipitating event (coughing fit, vomiting episode, prolonged crying). This is a diagnosis of exclusion — distribution alone should not be used to rule out serious disease; examine the whole child, consider thrombocytopenia, medication and safeguarding, and give explicit safety-netting.

The Glass Test & Blanching vs Non-Blanching

Glass test technique
Press a clear glass tumbler firmly against the rash. Blanching (rash disappears under pressure) = vascular dilatation — erythema, morbilliform viral rash, urticaria. Non-blanching (rash persists under pressure) = blood outside vessels — petechiae (<2 mm), purpura (2–10 mm), ecchymosis (>10 mm). The glass test is not a diagnostic test for meningococcal disease: a negative or equivocal result does not exclude serious infection; lighting and skin tone affect interpretation (inspect conjunctivae and all skin surfaces, including nappy areas); rashes may evolve from blanching to non-blanching. Urgent assessment if the patient is unwell or the rash is spreading, purpuric, unexplained, widespread or with concerning symptoms; a well child with clearly localised Valsalva petechiae may be managed with careful assessment and safety-netting.
Petechiae (≤2 mm)
Pinpoint non-blanching red/purple dots. Causes: thrombocytopenia (ITP, leukaemia, aplastic anaemia, bone marrow infiltration), meningococcaemia, vasculitis, Valsalva (localised above clavicles), amyloid angiopathy (periorbital petechiae after Valsalva — "raccoon eyes" in amyloid)
Purpura (2–10 mm)
Larger non-blanching lesions. Palpable purpura (raised, can be felt) = vasculitis (IgA vasculitis, ANCA vasculitis, cryoglobulinaemia). Flat purpura = thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy. Stellate/irregular purpura = DIC (meningococcal, other sepsis). Dependent purpura (lower legs) = vasculitis or stasis.
Ecchymosis (bruise >10 mm)
Coagulopathy (haemophilia, anticoagulant), thrombocytopenia, trauma, vasculitis or safeguarding concern. Pattern and location important for NAI assessment. Bruises in unusual sites (buttocks, ears, neck, soft tissue areas) or bruises in non-mobile infants → safeguarding.
Systemically well vs unwell
Most important clinical discriminator. Well child/adult + petechiae confined to face/neck after coughing, vomiting or crying → possible Valsalva or viral petechiae — a diagnosis of exclusion after observations, whole-body examination and explicit safety-netting; distribution alone should not rule out serious disease. Well child + unexplained petechiae elsewhere → urgent FBC + film (ITP, leukaemia). Unwell + petechiae/purpura anywhere → 999; emergency assessment for meningococcal disease, sepsis, DIC and other serious pathology.
Other lesion clues
Perifollicular purpura with poor diet → consider scurvy. Petechiae/purpura with pallor, lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly → consider leukaemia or marrow disease.
Palpable purpura (purpuric lesions that can be felt — slightly raised, may have urticarial component) is characteristic of small-vessel vasculitis — the palpability reflects perivascular inflammation and oedema in addition to the haemorrhage. In the context of petechiae and purpura assessment, palpating the rash takes 5 seconds and provides crucial diagnostic information: flat purpura points to coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia; palpable purpura points to vasculitis. The commonest cause of palpable purpura in children is IgA vasculitis (HSP) — the classic distribution is buttocks and extensor surfaces of lower limbs (gravity-dependent). The pathophysiology is IgA immune complex deposition in small vessel walls causing leucocytoclastic vasculitis. Adult palpable purpura may need targeted tests where clinically indicated (urinalysis, renal function, FBC, CRP/ESR, liver profile, ANA, ANCA, complement, cryoglobulins, hepatitis and HIV testing) as the underlying causes are more diverse and include connective tissue disease, haematological malignancy, and drug-induced vasculitis. The TTP pentad (thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, renal failure, fever, neurological symptoms) is rarely complete at presentation — any two of the first three should raise TTP as a diagnostic possibility and prompt emergency hospital assessment with direct haematology contact. Plasma exchange is life-saving in TTP and must be commenced within hours.

Differential Diagnosis

Meningococcal / bacterial septicaemia
Non-blanching petechiae/purpura + fever + unwell. Neisseria meningitidis (MC B most common in UK after MenC vaccination). Rapidly evolving. 999 — do not delay transfer; parenteral antibiotic if it does not delay transfer. Can occur without a rash; a non-blanching rash can have non-infectious causes; absence of fever does not exclude serious infection in an infant or immunocompromised patient.
ITP (immune thrombocytopenic purpura)
Well child, isolated thrombocytopenia, petechiae / bruising / mucosal bleeding, normal haemoglobin and white-cell count, blood film unremarkable apart from thrombocytopenia. Most common thrombocytopenic disorder in children. Post-viral (1–3 weeks after URTI). Acute (resolves <3 months) in 80% of childhood ITP. Before labelling ITP exclude platelet clumping, leukaemia/marrow disease, drug- or infection-associated thrombocytopenia, systemic autoimmune disease, TTP/HUS and congenital thrombocytopenia where suggested. Haematology-led: a well child may not need admission solely because platelets are <100 × 10⁹/L — same-day specialist discussion for severe thrombocytopenia, mucosal bleeding or diagnostic uncertainty.
IgA vasculitis (HSP)
Age 3–15 yrs. Palpable purpura on buttocks + lower limbs + arthritis + abdominal pain + renal involvement (haematuria + proteinuria). Post-streptococcal / post-viral. BP + urinalysis + dipstick at every presentation. Renal involvement in 30–50% — proteinuria / haematuria → nephrology if significant.
Acute leukaemia (ALL/AML)
Petechiae + pallor + fatigue + lymphadenopathy + hepatosplenomegaly + bone pain. Bone marrow failure → thrombocytopenia + anaemia + neutropenia. FBC: pancytopenia, blast cells on film. Same-day haematology. Most common cancer in children (ALL peak 2–5 yrs).
Viral petechiae
Non-specific viral URTI petechiae on soft palate or above clavicles. Well child, mild fever resolving. EBV/CMV: petechiae on soft palate (palatal petechiae may occur in EBV or streptococcal pharyngitis and are non-specific). Thrombocytopenia may accompany acute EBV. FBC + monospot if EBV suspected.
Drug-induced thrombocytopenia / purpura
Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — 5–10 days after heparin exposure, paradoxical thrombosis. Quinine, vancomycin, rifampicin, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs, antiplatelets, thiazides and other new medicines. Drug history essential. Do not stop essential medicines without assessing risk, except where an emergency reaction such as HIT is suspected (stop heparin immediately, urgent specialist assessment).
Senile purpura (actinic purpura)
Older adults — recurrent purple ecchymoses on sun-exposed extensor forearms / hands from trivial trauma. Fragile, atrophic skin from ageing / UV damage. Benign. Normal bleeding history and FBC. Reassure, skin protection, medication review. Vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) — similar distribution with perifollicular purpura + corkscrew hairs.
ANCA vasculitis (GPA/MPA)
Adults — palpable purpura + upper/lower respiratory symptoms (epistaxis, haemoptysis, haematuria). ANCA (cANCA/pANCA) positive. Renal involvement. Palpable purpura with renal, respiratory, neurological or systemic features → urgent assessment.
IgA vasculitis (HSP) — renal monitoring is the most important long-term clinical task for GPs. Renal involvement (IgA nephropathy in the setting of HSP) occurs in 30–50% of cases and is the primary determinant of long-term prognosis. Blood pressure and urine dipstick for protein and blood should be performed at every consultation during the acute illness and repeated over several months per the local paediatric/nephrology pathway. Nephrotic or nephritic syndrome (heavy proteinuria, oedema, or haematuria with renal impairment) requires urgent nephrology referral. Most childhood HSP nephritis resolves without treatment — but approximately 1–2% develop chronic kidney disease or end-stage renal disease over years. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a critical drug-related thrombocytopenia — it is paradoxically associated with both thrombocytopenia (purpura, bleeding) and severe arterial and venous thrombosis. The mechanism is heparin-dependent antibodies activating platelets (HIT antibody — anti-PF4/heparin). Any patient on heparin whose platelet count falls by >50% from baseline between days 5–14 of heparin exposure must have HIT considered, heparin stopped immediately, and switched to a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, fondaparinux, or DOAC). This is a haematological emergency — continuing heparin in HIT causes catastrophic arterial/venous thrombosis (limb amputation, stroke, PE).

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

History
Onset and progression · fever, rigors, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia or altered behaviour · recent cough, vomiting, crying or straining · recent viral illness · bruising, mucosal bleeding, haematuria, melaena or heavy menstrual bleeding · abdominal pain, vomiting, joint pain or swelling · urine colour and output · medication changes (heparin, quinine, rifampicin, vancomycin, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs, antiplatelets) · personal/family bleeding history · renal or autoimmune disease, HIV risk, malignancy · travel, TB exposure, animal contact, recent vaccination · in children: developmental mobility, mechanism, witnesses, delay in presentation, safeguarding concerns.
Vital signs (first)
Temperature, HR, BP, RR, SpO2, capillary refill, peripheral temperature, mental state — haemodynamic compromise → 999 before completing examination. A well child with petechiae and normal observations → controlled assessment. An unwell child with petechiae and any vital sign abnormality → 999; do not wait for blood results.
Rash characterisation
Glass test (blanching vs non-blanching). Inspect conjunctivae and all skin surfaces, including nappy areas (harder to see on brown, black or tanned skin). Location (face/neck after Valsalva — but distribution alone does not rule out serious disease). Distribution (lower limb/buttock = vasculitis; generalised = sepsis/ITP; periorbital = amyloid; sun-exposed forearms/hands = actinic purpura; perifollicular = scurvy). Evolution (spreading?). Mucosal bleeding / wet purpura. Palpability (raised = vasculitis). Colour/age (fresh dark red vs old yellow-brown). Size (petechiae vs purpura vs ecchymosis).
Systemic examination
Lymphadenopathy (leukaemia, EBV, lymphoma) · Hepatosplenomegaly (leukaemia, EBV, storage disorders) · Joints (IgA vasculitis, reactive) · Abdomen (IgA vasculitis — pain; leukaemia — mass) · Mucous membranes (thrombocytopenia — bleeding gums, blood blisters) · Fundoscopy (retinal haemorrhage in severe thrombocytopenia / hypertensive emergency) · Neurological signs
Investigations
Well patient (primary care): FBC + differential · Blood film (blasts, schistocytes = TTP/HUS) · Clotting screen if bleeding is significant, recurrent or unexplained · Renal function + urinalysis if vasculitis or renal disease possible · CRP/ESR if inflammatory or systemic disease suspected. Do not routinely order ANCA, ANA, anti-GBM, LDH, uric acid, EBV serology or broad infection panels in every well patient with a few petechiae — target tests to history and examination.
Unwell / suspected meningococcal disease
Investigations must not delay transfer or antibiotics. Hospital assessment may include blood culture, FBC + differential, CRP or procalcitonin, lactate, clotting screen + fibrinogen, meningococcal/pneumococcal PCR, renal and liver function.
Suspected TTP/HUS
Thrombocytopenia with anaemia, renal impairment, neurological symptoms or suspected haemolysis → emergency hospital assessment. FBC + film, renal function, bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes only where this does not delay transfer. Do not wait for ADAMTS13 results before contacting haematology.
Safeguarding assessment
Children: document all bruises/petechiae with body map. Consider maltreatment with: bruising/purpura in a pre-mobile infant; absent, implausible or developmentally inconsistent history; patterned, linear, clustered or protected-area lesions (ears, neck, cheeks, buttocks); injuries of different ages; delay in seeking help; associated fractures, burns or internal injuries. Treat as a safeguarding concern requiring urgent paediatric/safeguarding assessment in parallel with medical assessment — bleeding disorders and abuse can coexist. Assess immediate safety, fully expose and examine, document history, developmental stage and lesion sites/size/morphology, discuss with the safeguarding lead and paediatric team, and follow the local pathway. Haematological testing when indicated by bleeding history, examination or the safeguarding team — no panel excludes every bleeding disorder (BSH 2022). Do not discharge without an appropriate safety plan where there is significant concern.
Schistocytes on the blood film are pathognomonic of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) — red blood cell fragments produced when red cells are sheared by fibrin strands in small vessels. MAHA is the defining feature of TTP, HUS, and DIC. Identifying schistocytes on a blood film is the single most important investigation in a patient with thrombocytopenia + renal impairment + anaemia — it directs the diagnosis to a thrombotic microangiopathy and mandates emergency haematology input. GPs requesting FBC in suspected TTP/HUS must specifically ask for a blood film (many labs do not automatically perform a film unless requested or unless the automated analyser detects abnormalities). Retinal haemorrhage assessment in children with unexplained petechiae and no clear benign cause is important both clinically (severe thrombocytopenia or hypertension can cause retinal haemorrhage) and in the safeguarding context (retinal haemorrhage is a significant finding in suspected shaken baby syndrome). Ophthalmology referral for fundoscopy should be arranged if the cause of petechiae/purpura is unclear in a young child.

Referral Pathways

999 / emergency transfer
Suspected meningococcal disease or bacterial meningitis · rapidly progressive or widespread non-blanching petechiae/purpura with systemic illness · shock, altered consciousness, meningism, severe headache or seizures · purpura with haemodynamic compromise · severe thrombocytopenia with significant bleeding · suspected TTP/HUS · major GI, intracranial or concealed bleeding · febrile neutropenia or severe pancytopenia. Do not wait for blood results. Pre-alert; give ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin only if it does not delay transfer.
Same-day haematology / paediatrics
New thrombocytopenia with severe thrombocytopenia, mucosal bleeding or diagnostic uncertainty (same-day specialist discussion) · Blast cells on FBC film (leukaemia) · Pancytopenia · Platelet count <20 × 10⁹/L (urgent-discussion trigger) · Active bleeding with thrombocytopenia
Paediatric safeguarding
Bruising/purpura in a pre-mobile infant or unexplained/implausible bruising → urgent paediatric/safeguarding assessment, considering bleeding disorders and other medical causes in parallel. Discuss with safeguarding lead. Body map documentation. Child protection medical examination. Do not discharge without an appropriate safety plan where there is significant concern.
Paediatrics (IgA vasculitis)
Many clinically well children can be managed through a structured paediatric/GP follow-up pathway: BP, urinalysis, hydration, abdominal/joint assessment, renal function, weight and baseline renal findings. Same-day paediatric assessment for severe or persistent abdominal pain, GI bleeding, significant joint involvement or inability to mobilise, hypertension, significant proteinuria or haematuria, renal impairment, dehydration or systemic illness.
Rheumatology (urgent/routine)
Palpable purpura with renal, respiratory, neurological or systemic features → urgent assessment (ANCA vasculitis — GPA/MPA/EGPA); do not start high-dose immunosuppression before infection and mimics are considered unless directed by a specialist · Adults with cryoglobulinaemia features · Unexplained purpura + systemic autoimmune features (SLE, RA)
Haematology (suspected cancer pathway)
Unexplained thrombocytopenia not clearly ITP · Lymphoma features (lymphadenopathy + purpura + B symptoms) · Myeloma suspected (purpura + back pain + elevated protein)
The management of childhood ITP is increasingly non-interventional — current UK paediatric haematology practice (BSH, formerly BCSH, and the international ITP consensus, Provan et al. 2019 — international) recommends observation without treatment for most children with acute ITP who are clinically well, regardless of platelet count. The rationale is that 80% of childhood ITP resolves spontaneously within 3–6 months without treatment, and the risks of treatment (IVIG infusion reactions, steroid side effects) outweigh the benefits in an otherwise well child without significant bleeding. Treatment is only indicated for: active significant bleeding (oral mucosa bleeds, heavy epistaxis, haematuria, GI bleeding), planned invasive procedure, or specialist judgement in severe thrombocytopenia — decisions are haematology-led and not based on platelet count alone. Platelet transfusion in ITP is generally reserved for life-threatening or critical-site bleeding. GPs who see a child with petechiae and a low platelet count should refer to paediatric haematology and resist the urge to immediately request hospital admission "for safety" — many of these children can be managed safely as outpatients with individualised activity advice and avoidance of NSAIDs/aspirin while platelet count recovers. This approach reflects current UK paediatric haematology practice.

GP-Initiated Management

Suspected meningococcal sepsis
Ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin IV/IM — never delay transfer
Call 999 and pre-alert the receiving service. Do not delay transfer to give antibiotics. Give when meningococcal disease is strongly suspected or transfer is likely to be significantly delayed — NICE NG240 prefers ceftriaxone when available; benzylpenicillin is an acceptable pre-hospital option. Check doses against the current BNF/BNFC and local emergency medicines protocol (benzylpenicillin: <1 yr 300 mg · 1–9 yrs 600 mg · ≥10 yrs 1200 mg). Do not give an antibiotic to a patient with a severe immediate allergy to that drug.
Valsalva petechiae (benign)
Reassurance + safety-net
Well child + petechiae confined to face/neck + clear precipitant (coughing/vomiting/crying) + normal temperature and observations + whole child examined + rash not spreading + infection, medication, thrombocytopenia and safeguarding considered → diagnosis of exclusion; reassure. Review or contact the family within an agreed short interval. Written safety-net: return immediately if fever develops, the rash spreads, lesions become larger or purpuric, the child becomes lethargic, pale or unwell, or bleeding, abdominal pain, joint pain or reduced urine output develops. No treatment needed.
IgA vasculitis (HSP) — clinically well
Analgesia + hydration + monitoring
Ibuprofen 5–10 mg/kg TDS may help joint pain — avoid or discuss with paediatrics if renal impairment, dehydration, GI bleeding or significant proteinuria. Maintain oral hydration. BP + urinalysis at every review. Rest during acute phase. Avoid contact sports while purpura active. Steroids do not prevent long-term renal complications — do not start routinely in primary care without paediatric discussion. Documented urine/BP monitoring plan per local pathway.
Actinic (senile) purpuraReassure — benign, cosmetically distressing but harmless. Protective long sleeves. High-factor sunscreen to dorsal forearms. Routine vitamin C is not indicated unless deficiency is clinically suspected. Review medications: anticoagulants, NSAIDs, steroids, SSRIs — all worsen bruising tendency. Check FBC and clotting if unexpectedly severe.
Drug-induced thrombocytopeniaIdentify the implicated drug; do not stop essential medicines without assessing risk (except suspected HIT). Most drug-induced thrombocytopenia resolves within 1–2 weeks of stopping the causative drug. Same-day haematology if platelet count <20 × 10⁹/L or active bleeding. HIT: stop heparin immediately + switch to non-heparin anticoagulant + same-day haematology (HIT antibody test).
Pre-hospital antibiotics for suspected meningococcal disease must never delay transfer; doses should be checked against the current BNF/BNFC and local emergency medicines protocol, and NICE NG240 gives ceftriaxone preference when available. The 1.2 g adult dose can be remembered as "one vial" (benzylpenicillin typically comes in 600 mg vials — two vials for an adult). Having these vials stocked and accessible in every GP emergency bag is a professional responsibility. The Meningitis Now charity provides a recommended GP emergency bag contents list that includes benzylpenicillin. For IgA vasculitis (HSP), the steroid decision requires nuance — the European SHARE initiative recommendations for IgA vasculitis (international, Ozen et al. 2019) indicate that prednisolone reduces the duration of abdominal pain (by approximately 24 hours) but does not prevent or treat renal involvement. Therefore, steroids are only indicated when abdominal pain is severe or incapacitating, not as a routine treatment. The key GP action for HSP is setting up the urine and BP monitoring schedule — monitoring for renal involvement over several months per the local paediatric/nephrology pathway is the most important long-term management step.

ITP & Vasculitis — Specialist Treatments (GP Knowledge)

ITP — IVIG
IV immunoglobulin 0.8–1 g/kg — raises platelet count within 24–48 hours. Used for: active significant bleeding + low platelets, pre-operative platelet boost, very severe thrombocytopenia (<10 × 10⁹/L). Effect lasts 2–4 weeks. Hospital administration. Not curative — platelets fall again after IVIG effect wanes. Platelet transfusion in ITP is specialist-led and generally reserved for life-threatening or critical-site bleeding, not determined by a single platelet number.
ITP — oral prednisolone
Prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day × 14 days (children) or 1 mg/kg OD (adult). Increases platelet count in 50–75% within 7 days. Quicker response than watchful waiting but same long-term outcome. Side effects (immunosuppression, mood, glucose) limit chronic use.
Chronic ITP — thrombopoietin receptor agonists
Eltrombopag (oral) or romiplostim (SC injection) — stimulate platelet production. For chronic ITP (>12 months) not responding to first-line treatment. Specialist (haematology) initiation. Significant increase in platelet count in 70–80%. GP continues shared care prescription.
ANCA vasculitis
Induction: rituximab (B-cell depletion) or cyclophosphamide + high-dose prednisolone. Maintenance: azathioprine or rituximab. Plasma exchange for severe renal involvement. GP role: monitor infection risk, DMARD monitoring (FBC + LFTs on azathioprine), vaccination (avoid live vaccines). Do not start high-dose immunosuppression before infection and other mimics have been considered, unless directed by a specialist in an emergency.
Post-meningococcal care
Long-term complications: hearing loss (audiological follow-up), skin scarring / limb amputation (reconstructive surgery, prosthetics), psychological trauma (PTSD — child and family). Contact prophylaxis is organised through the UKHSA health protection team — do not independently prescribe ciprofloxacin or rifampicin to household contacts without public-health advice.
Ciprofloxacin prophylaxis for household contacts of meningococcal disease is organised by the local Health Protection Team (UKHSA) — the GP's role is to notify the HPT immediately after a case is confirmed, and the HPT will arrange contact assessment and prophylaxis. GPs should not prescribe prophylaxis independently without public-health advice — the HPT system handles this. However, knowing that ciprofloxacin 500 mg single dose is the first-line prophylaxis (preferred over rifampicin as it is single dose, single drug, available in pregnancy, and has high eradication efficacy for nasopharyngeal carriage) is important. The post-meningococcal care pathway is frequently neglected — patients who survive meningococcal disease (especially children who develop purpura fulminans and digital/limb ischaemia requiring amputation) have complex long-term physical, psychological, and social rehabilitation needs. The Meningitis Now and Meningitis Research Foundation charities provide support and advocacy. GPs should ensure these patients are referred to hearing services (sensorineural hearing loss from labyrinthitis affects 10% of survivors), clinical psychology (PTSD affects 25% of survivors and 50% of parents), and reconstructive surgical teams where needed.

Lifestyle

Prevention, Vaccination & Safety

Meningitis vaccination The schedule changes over time — check the current UKHSA complete routine immunisation schedule and the UKHSA Green Book (meningococcal and pneumococcal chapters). Students entering university: offer MenACWY if not vaccinated. Check immunisation status opportunistically.
Glass test teaching for parents Teach parents the glass test at every baby check and safety-net. Written information (Meningitis Now leaflet) given to parents at baby checks. "If your child develops a rash that does not fade under a glass, call 999 immediately. A rash that fades does not rule out serious illness — seek urgent help if your child seems unwell." Rashes can be harder to see on brown or black skin — check paler areas and inside the eyelids. This is one of the most impactful safety messages in paediatric primary care.
ITP activity advice Individualised by the haematology team according to bleeding, platelet trend and activity rather than a single platelet threshold; generally avoid activities with a high head-injury risk while thrombocytopenic. School attendance is usually fine. Bath/shower daily — no blood blisters on teeth brushing (use soft toothbrush). Avoid NSAIDs and aspirin (worsen platelet function) while thrombocytopenic. Return to normal activities as advised by haematology.
Protecting fragile skin (actinic purpura) Long-sleeved clothing to protect dorsal forearms. High-factor sunscreen (SPF50) — reduces further UV-induced dermal atrophy. Vitamin C only if deficiency is clinically suspected (then confirm and treat appropriately). Avoid unnecessary trauma. Review anticoagulants / antiplatelet medication — can these be reduced?
IgA vasculitis monitoring Inform parents: monitor urine colour at home (pink/red urine = haematuria → GP same-day). Home BP monitoring if recurrent episodes. Documented urine/BP monitoring plan per local paediatric/nephrology pathway — repeated urinalysis + BP over several months (a fixed 1/3/6-month schedule may be insufficient). Most resolve fully — 1–2% develop chronic kidney disease. School can resume once purpura resolving and child well.
Meningococcal survivor support Meningitis Now (meningitisnow.org): helpline, support groups, rehabilitation grants, peer support. Meningitis Research Foundation: research funding, family support. Provide leaflets at discharge from hospital and at first GP post-hospital consultation. Many families experience significant ongoing psychological trauma.
The MenACWY vaccination programme for university freshers was introduced specifically because university students (particularly those living in halls of residence for the first time) have historically been at disproportionately high risk of meningococcal W disease — contact with many new individuals from different geographical backgrounds in a shared living environment creates ideal transmission conditions. The MenACWY vaccine (Menveo or Nimenrix) provides protection against serogroups A, C, W, and Y. Students who did not receive MenACWY during the Year 9 school programme (age 13–14) should be offered catch-up vaccination via GP before going to university. Checking vaccination status at university pre-registration is a high-yield opportunistic intervention. The glass test is one of the highest-impact patient safety messages in the entire RCGP curriculum — it has been directly credited with saving lives by enabling parents to identify the non-blanching rash and call 999 before the child becomes deeply shocked. It should be taught at the 8-week baby check (when MenB is first given), reinforced at subsequent baby checks, and included in written safety-netting information for any febrile illness.
9
Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Valsalva petechiae — short interval
Well + confined to face/neck + clear Valsalva trigger → review or contact the family within an agreed short interval. Has rash resolved? Any fever or spread? If worsening or fever → same-day assessment. Full resolution expected within 3–7 days.
ITP — haematology-led
Follow a haematology-led plan: repeat FBC frequency reflects platelet count, bleeding and trend (weekly is not universally required). Individualised activity advice. Chronic ITP (>12 months) → haematology for second-line therapy (TPO agonists, rituximab, splenectomy). Stable chronic ITP: follow the haematology-led monitoring plan. Vaccination: pre-splenectomy vaccines (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib) if splenectomy planned.
IgA vasculitis — several months
Documented urine/BP monitoring plan with clear escalation advice, per local paediatric/nephrology pathway (a fixed 1/3/6-month schedule may be insufficient). Refer nephrology for persistent proteinuria, hypertension, renal impairment or nephrotic/nephritic features. Most resolve fully. Document in records for future hypertension assessment context.
Post-meningococcal
Audiological assessment within 4 weeks of being well enough to test, preferably before discharge (NICE NG240). Developmental review at 6 and 12 months. Psychological support (patient + family). Notify HPT for contact tracing. Review vaccination status (survivors need completing meningococcal vaccine series if immunocompromised during acute illness). Meningitis charity referral.
999 safety-net
New non-blanching rash + fever at any time. New fever or deterioration in a patient previously labelled with ITP, viral petechiae or vasculitis must trigger reassessment for sepsis — the original diagnosis does not protect against meningococcal disease. New neurological symptoms in purpura patient (meningitis, TTP). Active significant bleeding in thrombocytopenic patient.
Same-day GP / hospital
Rash spreading or lesions becoming larger or purpuric in a child who was initially reassured · Child becoming lethargic, pale or unwell · New bleeding, abdominal pain, joint pain or reduced urine output · New fever developing in any child with petechiae managed conservatively · Haematuria developing in IgA vasculitis patient · Platelet count falling in ITP patient on repeat FBC · Dark urine / anuria in any patient with purpura (HUS)
Renal monitoring for IgA vasculitis (HSP) is one of the most frequently overlooked follow-up requirements in paediatric GP practice. The risk of late-presenting renal complications (hypertension, proteinuria, progressive CKD) is real — approximately 1–2% of children with HSP develop significant long-term renal disease, and some do not develop proteinuria until months after the acute episode. The GP's role is to ensure urinalysis and blood pressure are checked repeatedly over several months after the acute illness, per the local paediatric/nephrology pathway — these checks are often delegated to Health Visitors or community nurses and never actually performed. Documenting the monitoring plan in the clinical record and setting review reminders is the standard of care. For ITP patients who subsequently undergo splenectomy (for chronic refractory ITP), the pre-splenectomy and post-splenectomy vaccination programme is a critical GP responsibility — these patients are functionally asplenic and require lifelong prophylactic penicillin V (250 mg BD) + vaccination against encapsulated organisms (pneumococcal, Hib, meningococcal, influenza annually). Failure to prescribe post-splenectomy penicillin is associated with overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) — a fulminant bacteraemia with pneumococcus or meningococcus carrying 50–70% mortality.
