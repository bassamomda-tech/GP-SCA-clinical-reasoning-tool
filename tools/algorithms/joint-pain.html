Safety

Red Flags — Septic Arthritis, Malignancy & Systemic Disease

Acute monoarthritis with a hot, swollen, painful joint should be treated as septic arthritis until infection has been excluded, particularly if the patient is systemically unwell, immunosuppressed, has a prosthetic joint or has bacteraemia risk. Fever may be absent, especially in older adults, immunosuppressed patients or those taking antipyretics.

Hot swollen joint ± fever Possible septic arthritis → same-day hospital assessment. Aspiration for cell count, Gram stain/culture and crystal analysis by an appropriately trained clinician — but do not delay antibiotics in a septic or unstable patient while waiting for aspiration. Take blood cultures promptly where feasible. Empirical antibiotics depend on the joint, local resistance, allergies, recent antibiotics, prosthetic material and gonococcal risk (local protocol). Prosthetic joint → local orthopaedic/microbiology pathway. Exclude a source (skin, urinary, dental). Joint aspiration should be performed only by clinicians with appropriate training, equipment, aseptic facilities and a reliable specimen pathway — it should not delay emergency transfer or antibiotic treatment.
Joint pain + weight loss + night sweats Malignancy (primary bone tumour, metastases, paraneoplastic), leukaemia, lymphoma. Age >50: metastatic disease (prostate, breast, lung, kidney). Urgent X-ray; suspected cancer pathway referral if it suggests malignancy. Elevated inflammatory markers disproportionate to joint findings.
Arthritis / tenosynovitis + pustular rash ± urethral/cervical symptoms Consider disseminated gonococcal infection — migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, pustular or vesiculopustular rash, acute monoarthritis, recent sexual exposure or urethral/cervical symptoms (S. aureus remains a major cause of septic arthritis). Urgent sexual-health or hospital assessment; NAAT and cultures as appropriate; manage per the current BASHH/local sexual-health pathway — do not rely on a routine primary-care oral antibiotic regimen.
Symmetrical small joint arthritis + systemic features RA, SLE, vasculitis — early specialist treatment reduces joint damage. Urgent rheumatology referral for persistent synovitis (NICE NG100; NICE QS33 — check local referral routes and targets). Do not wait for RF/anti-CCP, inflammatory markers or X-rays — normal results should not delay referral.
Joint pain + urinary symptoms + conjunctivitis Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome) — post-infectious (chlamydia, enteric organisms). HLA-B27 associated. Sexual-health assessment (NAAT, BASHH-guided treatment, partner notification, HIV/STI screening); eye assessment; rheumatology if persistent, severe or axial disease.
Joint pain + haemophilia / anticoagulant Haemarthrosis (spontaneous joint bleeding) — swollen, warm, stiff — no fever. Haemophilia → haemophilia treatment centre, with factor replacement where indicated. Management depends on severity and haemodynamic impact, anticoagulant type and timing, bleeding elsewhere, prosthetic joint or trauma. Do not routinely reverse a DOAC or warfarin for a stable haemarthrosis — discuss significant bleeding urgently with haematology or the anticoagulation service.
Septic arthritis is a rheumatological emergency — proteolytic enzymes from neutrophils and bacteria damage cartilage, so delay worsens outcome. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common organism; streptococci, gonococci and gram-negative organisms also occur. A high synovial fluid white cell count supports infection but is not a diagnostic threshold (crystal arthritis can produce similar counts) — Gram stain and culture matter. Aspiration before antibiotics helps guide therapy where it can be done promptly, but antibiotics must not be delayed in a septic or unstable patient. Risk factors: prosthetic joint (a serious infection — local orthopaedic/microbiology pathway), IV drug use, immunosuppression, diabetes, skin disease (psoriasis creates skin barrier breaks), recent joint injection. Any prosthetic joint with pain + fever = surgical emergency.

Inflammatory vs Mechanical — The Key Distinction

Inflammatory features
Morning stiffness >60 min (synovial inflammation — prolonged gel phenomenon) · Joint warmth and erythema · Systemic features (fatigue, fever, weight loss) · Often raised CRP/ESR (normal results do not exclude) · Multiple joints (polyarthritis) · Worse after rest, improves with activity · RF/anti-CCP may be positive (supportive only — negative serology does not exclude)
Mechanical features
Morning stiffness <30 min (OA) · Pain worsens with activity, improves with rest · No systemic features · Normal or mildly elevated CRP · Single or asymmetric joint involvement · Age >50, associated obesity/overuse · Crepitus, bony enlargement
Acute onset (hours)
Septic arthritis (hot single joint; fever may be absent) · Gout / pseudogout (crystal arthritis — urate crystals) · Haemarthrosis (trauma, anticoagulant) · Reactive arthritis (post-infectious, 1–4 weeks after infection)
Subacute (days to weeks)
RA (early — symmetrical small joints + morning stiffness) · Psoriatic arthritis (DIP joints + nail changes + psoriasis) · Reactive arthritis · Lyme disease (endemic area, tick bite history) · Viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, HIV, hepatitis)
Chronic (>3 months)
OA (mechanical, asymmetric, age-related) · RA (established) · Psoriatic · Ankylosing spondylitis (sacroiliac + axial) · Reactive (chronic) · Gout with tophi
The duration of morning stiffness is the single most diagnostically discriminating clinical feature for distinguishing inflammatory from mechanical joint disease. Morning stiffness >60 minutes strongly predicts inflammatory pathology (RA, psoriatic, reactive) — it reflects the time required for synovial gel phenomenon to resolve as the joint warms with activity. OA morning stiffness typically lasts <30 minutes. This 60-minute threshold is incorporated into the ACR/EULAR 2010 RA classification criteria. The second key distinction is the relationship of pain to activity: inflammatory arthritis characteristically worsens with inactivity and improves with movement (joints feel worse first thing in the morning and after sitting), while OA worsens with use (pain builds with sustained activity and relieves with rest). Patients often describe OA as "the more I do, the more it hurts" and inflammatory arthritis as "the more I rest, the stiffer I get."

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Septic arthritis
Hot swollen joint; fever may be absent. S. aureus most common; streptococci, gonococci, gram-negative organisms. Synovial fluid cell count, Gram stain and culture. X-ray: normal early, joint destruction late. Same-day hospital — antibiotics per local protocol ± washout. Emergency.
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)
Symmetrical small joint polyarthritis (MCPs, PIPs, wrists), morning stiffness >60 min, systemic fatigue. RF and anti-CCP are supportive, not diagnostic gatekeepers — negative serology does not exclude RA. X-ray: periarticular osteopenia + erosions (late) — imaging should not delay referral. Early specialist-led DMARDs. NICE NG100 (2018): refer urgently if small joints of hands/feet affected, more than one joint, or a delay of 3 months or longer since symptom onset; also persistent monoarthritis where inflammatory disease is suspected. Normal inflammatory markers, negative serology or normal X-rays should not delay referral.
Gout (crystal arthritis)
Acute: severe monarthritis (first MTP = podagra, ankle, knee), peaks 12–24 hrs. Tophi (chronic). Serum urate (may be normal during a flare — repeat after it settles, or aspirate if diagnostic uncertainty is important). Synovial fluid: MSU crystals (needle-shaped, negatively birefringent). Discuss urate-lowering therapy (NICE NG219) — can start during a flare with anti-inflammatory cover, or after it settles.
Pseudogout (CPPD)
Calcium pyrophosphate deposition — knee most common. Acute: similar to gout. Middle-aged/elderly, associated with hyperparathyroidism, haemochromatosis — in younger patients or recurrent/atypical disease consider calcium, phosphate, magnesium, ALP, ferritin/iron studies and PTH. X-ray: chondrocalcinosis (calcification of cartilage). Crystal microscopy: CPPD crystals (rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent).
Osteoarthritis (OA)
Age >45, activity-related pain, <30 min stiffness, no systemic features. Bony swelling (Heberden's nodes at DIP, Bouchard's at PIP). Usually a clinical diagnosis — X-ray (JSN, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis) only if diagnosis uncertain, atypical, rapidly progressive or for surgical planning. No blood test changes. Tailored exercise + weight-management support + topical NSAID.
Psoriatic arthritis
Occurs in a proportion of people with psoriasis. Asymmetric — can involve DIP joints (distinguishes from RA), dactylitis ("sausage digit"), enthesitis (tendon insertion pain), axial disease. Seronegative (RF negative). Nail changes (pitting, onycholysis). DMARDs + biologics.
Reactive arthritis
Post-enteric (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia) or post-urogenital (Chlamydia). Asymmetric large joint oligoarthritis 1–4 weeks post-infection. HLA-B27 associated. Course is variable — many settle, but some develop persistent or recurrent disease. NSAIDs (after risk assessment) first-line.
The anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) antibody is a highly specific test for RA, but it is supportive rather than diagnostic and a negative result does not exclude RA. Positive anti-CCP in early inflammatory arthritis is highly predictive of RA development and is associated with more erosive, aggressive disease. A patient with anti-CCP positive symmetrical small joint polyarthritis and elevated CRP should be referred to rheumatology as a matter of urgency (NICE NG100: refer urgently; NICE QS33 — check local referral routes and targets). NICE NG100 asks for referral of any adult with suspected persistent synovitis of undetermined cause, and urgently even with a normal acute-phase response or negative RF/anti-CCP — so all patients with persistent joint swelling of unclear cause should be referred to rheumatology regardless of serology. Distinguishing RA from psoriatic arthritis is clinically important as they have different DMARD pathways — psoriatic arthritis often responds well to TNF inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors, while methotrexate is the anchor DMARD for RA.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Joint examination
Swelling: effusion (soft, fluctuant) vs synovial thickening (boggy, doughy) vs bony enlargement (OA). Warmth (inflammatory/septic — compare to contralateral). Erythema (gout, septic arthritis — RA rarely causes erythema). ROM. Deformity. Crepitus. Tenderness on palpation vs movement.
Systemic examination
Skin: psoriatic plaques (psoriatic arthritis), butterfly rash (SLE), tophi on ears/hands/Achilles (gout), rheumatoid nodules. Eyes: uveitis (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, JIA). Nail pitting (psoriatic). Urethral discharge (gonococcal/reactive arthritis). Lymphadenopathy (viral, malignancy).
Bloods (targeted)
Suspected inflammatory arthritis: FBC · CRP + ESR · Renal + liver function · RF + anti-CCP (where RA suspected — supportive, not gatekeepers; avoid extensive autoantibody panels before referring clear synovitis). Suspected CTD only (photosensitive rash, oral ulcers, Raynaud's, serositis, cytopenias, proteinuria/haematuria, sicca with systemic features): ANA, dsDNA, ENA, complement, urine testing — ANA is not a general screen for joint pain. Suspected gout: Urate (may be normal during a flare — repeat after it settles, or aspirate if uncertainty is important). Suspected CPPD (younger, recurrent or atypical): calcium, phosphate, magnesium, ALP, ferritin/iron studies, PTH. HLA-B27 (suspected axial spondyloarthritis) · STI NAAT (gonorrhoea, chlamydia — if sexually acquired suspected)
X-ray
Not routine for all affected joints — order when it will clarify diagnosis, assess suspected fracture or malignancy, evaluate persistent inflammatory disease, establish baseline damage in specialist-led RA or assist surgical planning; it should not delay referral. RA early: periarticular osteopenia, soft tissue swelling (erosions are late). OA: JSN, osteophytes, sclerosis. Gout: punched-out erosions with overhanging edges (Martel's sign — late). Chondrocalcinosis (CPPD). Lytic lesion (malignancy).
Synovial fluid aspiration
Cell count, Gram stain/culture and crystal microscopy (MSU vs CPPD). Key in acute monoarthritis. Performed only by clinicians with appropriate training, equipment, aseptic facilities and a reliable specimen pathway (usually hospital/rheumatology) — must not delay emergency transfer or antibiotics.
DAS28 (Disease Activity Score in 28 joints) is the validated clinical tool used by rheumatologists to monitor RA disease activity and guide DMARD dose escalation and treat-to-target decisions. It incorporates: 28-joint tender count, 28-joint swollen count, patient VAS (visual analogue scale for global health), and CRP (or ESR). DAS28 <2.6 = remission; 2.6–3.2 = low activity; 3.2–5.1 = moderate; >5.1 = high. NICE NG100 mandates treat-to-target (target DAS28 <2.6 = remission) — DMARDs are escalated every 3 months until remission is achieved. GPs who see patients on DMARDs should be familiar with DAS28 from rheumatology letters to understand disease activity status and treatment appropriateness.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day hospital
Possible septic arthritis (hot swollen joint; fever may be absent) · Suspected prosthetic joint infection (local orthopaedic/microbiology pathway) · Acute haemarthrosis in haemophilia (haemophilia treatment centre) · Significant haemarthrosis on anticoagulation (discuss urgently with haematology/anticoagulation service) · Suspected disseminated gonococcal infection (urgent sexual-health or hospital assessment)
Rheumatology (urgent)
Persistent synovitis — small joints of hands/feet, more than one joint, delay of 3 months or more from onset, or persistent monoarthritis where inflammatory disease is suspected (NICE NG100; QS33 — use local referral routes and targets) · Do not wait for RF/anti-CCP, ESR/CRP or X-ray · Document joint distribution, swelling, function and duration · New inflammatory polyarthritis · Suspected psoriatic arthritis (dactylitis, enthesitis) · Suspected ankylosing spondylitis (young adult + back pain + morning stiffness >3 months)
Rheumatology (routine)
Established inflammatory arthritis with disease flare · gout refractory to ULT · CPPD/pseudogout recurrent attacks · SLE or connective tissue disease suspected
Orthopaedics
OA with severe functional impairment (joint replacement consideration) · Loose bodies · Meniscal tear · Suspected bone malignancy (X-ray lesion → suspected cancer pathway referral)
GUM
Reactive arthritis (post-STI): NAAT, BASHH-guided treatment, HIV/STI screening · Suspected disseminated gonococcal infection (BASHH/local pathway) · Partner notification and treatment
The urgency of RA referral (NICE NG100: refer urgently; NICE QS33 sets referral standards — check local routes and targets) is based on evidence that treatment delay significantly worsens long-term joint damage outcomes — the window of opportunity for suppressing the immune cascade before erosive joint destruction occurs is narrow. Patients with RA who start DMARDs within 3 months of symptom onset have significantly better 5-year and 10-year functional outcomes than those starting later. The GP should not wait for repeat bloods or X-ray reports before referring — clinical suspicion of persistent synovitis is sufficient to refer urgently — normal inflammatory markers, negative serology or normal X-rays should not delay referral. The rheumatologist will arrange the further investigations. The most common cause of delayed RA diagnosis in primary care audit data is repeat GP consultations without progression to referral.

Treat

GP-Initiated Treatment

Acute gout
NSAID, colchicine or short corticosteroid
Choose by renal function, hypertension, heart failure, ulcer risk, anticoagulants and age; doses per NICE NG219/local formulary. NSAID (e.g. naproxen) + PPI if suitable. Colchicine: caution in renal or hepatic impairment, older adults, macrolides or strong CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein inhibitors, cytopenias or neuromuscular disease. Corticosteroid regimen per local protocol. Urate-lowering therapy can be started during a flare if necessary with anti-inflammatory cover, or after it settles. Ice pack. Elevate affected joint. Review in 4–6 weeks to discuss ULT.
OA (mild–moderate)
Topical NSAID + exercise
Topical NSAID (e.g. diclofenac gel, dosed per product information) — first-line for knee OA, consider for other joints (NICE NG226). Paracetamol and weak opioids: do not routinely offer (NICE NG226) — only infrequent short-term use if other options are unsuitable. Oral NSAID + PPI if topical inadequate, only after assessing GI, renal, hepatic and cardiovascular risk. Tailored therapeutic exercise for everyone (physiotherapy referral where needed). Weight-management support where relevant. Avoid opioids (NICE NG226, 2022: do not offer strong opioids; do not routinely offer weak opioids).
Reactive arthritis
NSAID (after risk assessment)
NSAIDs are first-line for reactive arthritis — the course is variable and some develop persistent or recurrent disease. Chlamydia-associated: do not prescribe doxycycline solely for arthritis — sexual-health assessment with NAAT, treatment per the current BASHH STI guideline, partner notification and treatment, HIV/STI screening where appropriate. Antibiotics for a triggering enteric infection do not necessarily improve established reactive arthritis. Joint and eye assessment. Physiotherapy for joint function. Rheumatology if persistent, severe or axial disease (e.g. sulfasalazine). HLA-B27 positive patients have more persistent disease.
RA — bridgingWhile awaiting rheumatology: NSAIDs (naproxen + PPI) for symptom control. Steroid bridge only on rheumatology advice. DMARD initiation should normally be rheumatology-led or undertaken within an agreed shared-care pathway. Do not delay urgent referral while awaiting blood tests or imaging. Do not initiate immunosuppression in an undiagnosed inflammatory arthritis without specialist advice.
Gout ULTDiscuss ULT after a first flare; offer for recurrent or troublesome flares, tophi, chronic gouty arthritis, CKD stages 3–5 or diuretic treatment (NICE NG219). Start according to indication, patient preference and clinical context — it can be initiated during a flare with anti-inflammatory cover, or after the flare has settled. Allopurinol: start at a low dose, particularly in CKD (check eGFR) — not a fixed dose for everyone; titrate to the serum urate target (NICE NG219: <360 μmol/L), continuing prophylaxis during dose escalation. Counsel about rash and allopurinol hypersensitivity; consider HLA-B*58:01 testing only according to local/national recommendations and the relevant ethnicity/risk pathway. Febuxostat is an alternative (NICE NG219) — requires a cardiovascular-risk discussion; not automatically preferred or excluded in cardiovascular disease. Annual urate monitoring once stable.
Starting DMARDs (methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine) in primary care before a rheumatological diagnosis is confirmed is inappropriate and potentially harmful. DMARDs require: tuberculosis screening (IGRA test — methotrexate reactivates latent TB), hepatitis B screening (methotrexate and biologics can reactivate HBV), DEXA scan baseline (steroid-sparing), full blood count and LFT baseline, and patient counselling about teratogenicity and monitoring requirements. These are rheumatologist-led investigations. The GP's role is symptomatic relief (NSAIDs after risk assessment; steroid only on specialist advice) while ensuring urgent rheumatology referral is made; DMARD initiation is normally rheumatology-led or within an agreed shared-care pathway. The risk of delaying DMARD initiation is real (joint erosion) but the risk of starting immunosuppression without proper screening is also significant.

RA Monitoring & DMARD Shared Care (Local Protocol)

Responsibility
The specialist team remains responsible for diagnosis, initiation, dose changes and specialist review; primary-care monitoring and prescribing occur only under a current written shared-care agreement. If no current shared-care agreement exists, the GP should not assume responsibility for monitoring or adjusting DMARDs.
Methotrexate monitoring (shared care)
FBC, ALT/AST, creatinine/eGFR and albumin at the frequency set by the current local shared-care protocol (BSR csDMARD guideline 2025) — more frequent at initiation, after dose changes or abnormal results, and adjusted for renal/hepatic function, age, interacting drugs and comorbidity. Use the approved local shared-care document and laboratory alert system; the thresholds below do not override the local protocol. Withhold and contact rheumatology if (BSR thresholds): WBC <3.5 × 10⁹/L, neutrophils <1.6, platelets <140, ALT/AST >100 U/L. New breathlessness, dry cough or fever → urgent clinical assessment, not simply routine withholding (methotrexate pneumonitis — important but rare). Document monitoring in EMIS/SystmOne protocol.
Folic acid
Schedules vary — follow the rheumatology shared-care protocol (common regimens: 5 mg once weekly on a different day from methotrexate, or daily folic acid excluding the methotrexate day) — reduces methotrexate toxicity (mucositis, nausea, hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression) without reducing efficacy. Ensure prescribed whenever methotrexate is prescribed.
Sick day rules
Follow the patient's specific shared-care instructions. Withholding methotrexate during severe infection, an infection requiring antibiotics or acute kidney injury may be appropriate; do not automatically stop every DMARD in every minor infection. Resume when recovered, per the shared-care plan. Document in patient records. Patient education card essential.
Biologic therapies (hospital-initiated)
TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept), IL-6 inhibitors (tocilizumab), JAK inhibitors (baricitinib, upadacitinib). Increased infection risk — TB screening is required before treatment (annual IGRA is not universally required). VZV reactivation — Shingrix according to UKHSA eligibility, not automatically for every patient. Vaccination plans depend on treatment, risk and UKHSA guidance — follow the Green Book and specialist pathway; hepatitis B vaccination is risk-based.
Pregnancy and DMARDs
Methotrexate: contraindicated in pregnancy — pre-conception washout and advice per the current BSR pregnancy guideline and specialist advice; paternal exposure is not universally risk-free without qualification — follow BSR/specialist advice. Hydroxychloroquine: generally compatible with pregnancy. Sulfasalazine: generally compatible; requires folic acid supplementation, with dose and timing per the pregnancy/rheumatology protocol. Biologics: pregnancy management is drug-specific (e.g. certolizumab has different placental-transfer characteristics from monoclonal antibodies) — decide with rheumatology and obstetric input. Pre-conception planning for all women of childbearing potential on DMARDs.
Methotrexate pneumonitis (MTX-induced lung toxicity) is a rare but life-threatening complication — it presents as progressive dyspnoea, dry cough, and fever, typically within the first 12 months of treatment. Any patient on methotrexate who develops new breathlessness, dry cough or fever needs urgent clinical assessment, with methotrexate withheld and specialist advice sought. Methotrexate pneumonitis is rare but can be serious. New dyspnoea in an RA patient on methotrexate must never be attributed to infection or heart failure without specifically considering MTX lung toxicity. The GP's monitoring protocol is set by the BSR csDMARD guideline (2025, replacing BSR/BHPR 2017) and local shared-care agreements — failure to monitor and resulting MTX toxicity is a documented cause of GP negligence claims.

Lifestyle

Exercise, Diet & Self-Management

Exercise for OA The most effective OA treatment is regular aerobic + strengthening exercise — stronger muscles absorb joint load and reduce pain. NICE NG226 (2022): offer tailored therapeutic exercise to all people with OA regardless of age, weight, or severity. Aerobic activity (walking, swimming, cycling) and strengthening, tailored to the person rather than a fixed prescription. Physiotherapy where a supervised programme is needed.
Weight loss (OA / gout) Weight-management support where relevant — weight loss reduces knee joint loading and can improve OA pain. Gout: obesity drives insulin resistance → reduced urate excretion; weight loss may help. Low-purine diet alone insufficient — ULT is required for most.
Gout diet Reduce: red meat, organ meat, shellfish (high purines), beer and spirits (raise urate + reduce excretion), fructose drinks. Increase: low-fat dairy (uricosuric), cherries (reduces inflammation + urate), water (promotes renal urate excretion). Dietary modification has a modest effect on urate — ULT is needed for most people with recurrent gout.
RA — fatigue management Fatigue is one of the most disabling RA symptoms — often under-recognised. Pacing strategies (activity-rest cycles), cognitive behavioural strategies (via NHS Talking Therapies), occupational therapy (joint protection, adaptive equipment). Treat anaemia (often contributory — haematinics or rheumatology review).
Arthritis charities Arthritis UK (arthritis-uk.org) — excellent patient information, helpline, local support groups, exercise classes. NRAS (National Rheumatoid Arthritis Society) — RA-specific support. Refer all new RA patients to these resources. Social prescribing referral via GP.
Vaccinations (inflammatory arthritis) All patients on immunosuppressive DMARDs: annual influenza (inactivated only — not live), pneumococcal (per UKHSA Green Book chapter 25), COVID booster (enhanced schedule). Pre-biologic: Shingrix and hepatitis B vaccination according to eligibility and risk (UKHSA Green Book and specialist pathway) — not mandatory for every patient. Live vaccines generally contraindicated on biologics (yellow fever, live flu nasal spray) — check the Green Book.
Exercise is the most evidence-based treatment for OA — multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrate that land-based exercise reduces OA pain and improves function, with no ceiling effect across ages and severities. The key barrier is patient belief that exercise "wears out" the joint — this is scientifically incorrect and must be actively dispelled. Cartilage has no blood supply; it is nourished by the compressive-release pumping action of joint movement. Regular moderate exercise maintains cartilage health. Paracetamol has minimal evidence for OA pain — a 2016 Lancet meta-analysis of 74 trials found paracetamol had clinically insignificant effects on OA pain compared to placebo. NICE NG226 (2022) therefore advises against routinely offering paracetamol for OA. NSAIDs have better evidence but worse side effect profiles — use lowest effective dose for shortest duration.

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Suspected RA — tracking
Rheumatology assessment per NICE QS33 and local referral targets. Patient to phone if no appointment within the expected timeframe. If deteriorating rapidly (joints multiplying, functional decline) → phone rheumatology for expedited appointment. Document urgency of referral clearly.
Gout — 4–6 weeks
Acute attack resolved? Urate recheck. Eligible for ULT? (NICE NG219: multiple or troublesome flares, CKD stages 3–5, diuretic therapy, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis; discuss after a first flare). ULT started at a low dose with prophylaxis during titration, and counselled about rash/hypersensitivity? Titrating to urate target? Annual urate monitoring once stable. Medication review (diuretics, aspirin).
DMARD monitoring (RA)
Only under a current written shared-care agreement — tests and frequency per the local protocol. Document on GP monitoring template. Alert for: new infection risk, MTX sick day rules, methotrexate pneumonitis signs. Shared care protocol with rheumatology.
OA — review at 6 weeks
Exercise programme commenced? Pain improving? Adequate analgesia? If inadequate: step up to oral NSAID. Consider referral to orthopaedics if severe functional impairment (joint replacement criteria). Occupational therapy if functional disability.
Same-day hospital / 999 if unstable

The risk of septic arthritis in patients on biologic DMARDs (TNF inhibitors, JAK inhibitors) is higher than in the general population — these drugs suppress the innate and adaptive immune response that normally prevents haematogenous seeding. Any patient on a biologic who develops a hot, swollen, acutely painful joint should be considered to have septic arthritis until joint aspiration proves otherwise. This is clinically challenging because biologics also suppress the inflammatory features of gout and RA flares — leading to presentations where crystal arthritis or RA flare mimics septic arthritis. The answer in all cases is the same: urgent hospital assessment for joint aspiration and culture. GPs should have a low threshold to call rheumatology directly when managing joint flares in biologic-treated patients.
