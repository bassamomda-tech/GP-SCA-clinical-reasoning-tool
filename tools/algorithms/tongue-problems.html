Safety

Red Flags — Tongue Cancer, Airway Compromise & Neurological Emergencies

Unexplained oral ulceration lasting more than 3 weeks requires suspected cancer referral, regardless of site — not only the lateral tongue. For a lump on the lip/oral cavity or a red or red-white patch consistent with erythroplakia/erythroleukoplakia, arrange urgent dental/oral assessment; a dentist may then refer through the suspected cancer pathway if clinically suspicious. Tongue swelling causing airway compromise = 999.

Unexplained oral ulcer >3 weeks, lump, or red/red-white patch Oral SCC — the lateral and ventrolateral tongue are important sites to examine. Indurated, firm, non-healing; often initially painless. Smoking and alcohol are major risk factors. NICE NG12: suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained ulceration >3 weeks at any site; a lump or red/red-white patch → urgent dental/oral assessment. Persistent ulceration, induration, unexplained lump, red/red-white patch, unexplained neck node, persistent referred otalgia or cranial-nerve deficit requires prompt specialist assessment. Do not biopsy in primary care. Do not treat empirically beyond 3 weeks.
Acute tongue swelling + stridor or drooling + known allergy or ACE inhibitor use Angioedema — anaphylaxis (nut, drug, latex, venom) or ACE inhibitor-induced (bradykinin-mediated, no urticaria, can occur years after starting ACEi). Both can cause life-threatening airway obstruction within minutes. → 999 for tongue, laryngeal or airway involvement. ABCDE airway and breathing management; IM adrenaline if airway, breathing or circulation problems consistent with anaphylaxis (Resuscitation Council UK). Involve anaesthetics/ENT early. Icatibant or C1-inhibitor only according to specialist/hospital protocol — not routine GP treatment. Stop the ACEi permanently. Any switch to an ARB should be individualised and documented — ARBs are not completely risk-free.
Unilateral tongue wasting and fasciculations + deviation on protrusion towards the weak side Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) palsy — tongue deviates to the WEAK (affected) side on protrusion. Causes: posterior fossa tumour, skull base metastasis, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, carotid artery dissection, motor neurone disease, MS. → A new unexplained CN XII palsy requires urgent neurological assessment and imaging of the brain, skull base and neck/carotid region. Consider malignancy, vascular disease/dissection, inflammatory disease, motor neurone disease and other structural causes. Acute vascular features (e.g. neck pain, Horner's, other neurological signs) → same-day emergency assessment rather than a routine GP MRI request.
Rapidly progressive macroglossia + facial coarsening + dry skin + cold intolerance Hypothyroid macroglossia (primary hypothyroidism causing myxoedematous tongue enlargement) OR amyloidosis — systemic amyloid infiltrates tongue, causing firm, non-pitting enlargement, may show impressions of teeth on lateral borders. Targeted tests: TSH/free T4 if hypothyroid features; serum electrophoresis, immunoglobulins and serum free light chains where amyloidosis/plasma-cell disease is suspected (haematology referral). Stridor or other airway compromise → 999. Urgent airway assessment if dysphagia, sleep-related obstruction or rapidly progressive enlargement.
Tongue pain + burning + numbness following dental procedure, jaw surgery, or oral injection Lingual nerve injury (branch of CN V3 — carries taste from anterior 2/3 tongue + general sensation from ipsilateral floor of mouth). Causes: lower third molar (wisdom tooth) extraction, inferior alveolar nerve block injection. Results in: altered/lost taste, numbness of tongue and floor of mouth. Document new persistent numbness, dysaesthesia or taste disturbance and discuss promptly with the dental/OMFS service. Urgent referral if complete sensory loss, severe dysaesthesia, suspected nerve transection, progressive symptoms or significant functional impact. Post-dental paraesthesia may improve spontaneously; specialist assessment determines whether and when microsurgical intervention is appropriate.
White non-wipeable tongue lesion + heavy smoker or heavy alcohol user A white patch alone is not automatically leukoplakia or cancer. A persistent unexplained non-wipeable white lesion requires dental/oral-medicine assessment; urgency depends on appearance, site, symptoms, size, progression, associated red areas, induration, lymphadenopathy and risk factors. Hairy leukoplakia (characteristic corrugated/vertical appearance, usually lateral tongue, associated with immunosuppression) is not synonymous with conventional leukoplakia → offer HIV testing where clinically indicated. Red or red-white lesions (erythroplakia/erythroleukoplakia) → urgent dental assessment (NICE NG12).
ACE inhibitor-induced angioedema is distinct from allergic (IgE-mediated) angioedema in mechanism and treatment — it is caused by accumulation of bradykinin (which ACE normally degrades; ACE inhibition prevents degradation → bradykinin accumulates → increased vascular permeability → angioedema). Because the mechanism is bradykinin-mediated rather than histamine-mediated, antihistamines and corticosteroids are ineffective. Icatibant and C1-inhibitor concentrate are specialist/hospital treatments used only according to local protocol, and evidence for icatibant in ACEi angioedema is inconsistent. Primary care priorities are: 999 for tongue, laryngeal or airway involvement, ABCDE management, IM adrenaline if there are airway, breathing or circulation problems consistent with anaphylaxis (repeat after 5 minutes if problems persist — Resuscitation Council UK), and early anaesthetics/ENT involvement. ACEi-induced angioedema can occur at any time during treatment, including after years of use, and is more common in people of Black African or Caribbean family origin. Stop the ACEi permanently and never restart; any switch to an ARB should be individualised and documented, as ARBs are not completely risk-free. The CN XII (hypoglossal nerve) anatomy is important for understanding tongue deviation: the hypoglossal nerve innervates the intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscles. When CN XII is damaged on one side, the ipsilateral tongue muscles are weak. On protrusion, the unaffected contralateral muscles push the tongue toward the weak side — therefore the tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion. This is the opposite of CN VII palsy (facial nerve — the face deviates away from the lesion). Remembering "tongue goes to the trouble" helps: the tongue points toward the damaged nerve side. Any new unexplained lower cranial nerve palsy (IX, X, XI, XII) requires urgent neurological assessment and imaging of the brain, skull base and neck/carotid region (same-day emergency assessment if acute vascular features) to consider skull base pathology (jugular foramen tumour — glomus jugulare, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, meningioma, metastasis).

Classification — Common Tongue Conditions

Glossitis (smooth, red, sore tongue)
Atrophy of filiform papillae → smooth, beefy-red, painful tongue. Causes: iron deficiency (a common cause), B12 deficiency (Hunter's glossitis — associated with SACD), folate deficiency, zinc deficiency, riboflavin (B2) deficiency, coeliac disease, Plummer-Vinson syndrome (iron deficiency + dysphagia + post-cricoid web — squamous cell carcinoma risk). Investigate according to history and examination (see Step 4). Correct a confirmed deficiency and find its cause.
Geographic tongue (benign migratory glossitis)
Well-demarcated areas of filiform papilla loss (red, smooth patches with white/grey serpiginous borders) migrating over days. Affects 1–3% population. Usually asymptomatic — occasionally sensitive to spicy foods or citrus. No treatment required — reassurance. Can be associated with psoriasis (both share IL-36 pathway). No malignant potential. Appears alarming but is entirely benign.
Black hairy tongue
Elongated, stained filiform papillae with black/brown/green discolouration. Causes: antibiotic use (altered oral flora), bismuth preparations, smoking, excessive coffee/tea, poor oral hygiene, xerostomia. Usually asymptomatic, occasionally causes gagging or metallic taste. Benign. Treatment: stop causative factor, improve oral hygiene, tongue brushing, adequate hydration, stop smoking.
Fissured tongue (scrotal tongue)
Deep grooves/fissures in the dorsal tongue surface — very common (5% population), increases with age. Usually asymptomatic. Associated with geographic tongue and Melkersson-Rosenthal syndrome (fissured tongue + orofacial swelling + facial nerve palsy triad). No treatment required. Food debris can accumulate in deep fissures — advise gentle tongue brushing. Reassurance that it is a normal variant.
Burning mouth syndrome (BMS)
Chronic burning pain of the oral mucosa (tongue most commonly) without visible lesion or identifiable organic cause. Primarily postmenopausal women. Bilateral, constant or worsening throughout the day, relieved by eating/drinking (distinguishes from neuropathic pain that worsens with stimulation). Associated with anxiety, depression, dry mouth. Diagnosis of exclusion — confirm no visible mucosal lesion; review medicines, dentures, oral hygiene, xerostomia and contact irritants; exclude or treat candidiasis, iron/B12/folate deficiency, diabetes, thyroid disease. Management: reassurance, explanation and psychological/CBT-based support; oral medicine/dentistry referral if persistent. Topical clonazepam, tricyclics, gabapentinoids and other neuropathic-pain strategies are specialist-directed and individualised.
Macroglossia
Enlarged tongue. Congenital: Down syndrome, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome. Acquired: hypothyroidism (myxoedema — most common endocrine cause), acromegaly (tongue + lips + hands + facial features enlarged), amyloidosis (firm, non-pitting, lateral teeth impressions), angioedema (acute — see red flags). Distinguish true macroglossia (enlarged tongue) from relative macroglossia (small jaw — micrognathia, making normal-sized tongue appear large). Investigate per suspected cause.
Geographic tongue generates significant patient anxiety when first discovered or when a patient reads about it online — patients often fear cancer or infection and present alarmed. The GP's role is to provide unambiguous reassurance: geographic tongue is a benign, common, completely harmless condition with no malignant potential. The explanation that it is caused by cyclical changes in the pattern of filiform papillae loss and regrowth (hence "migratory") usually satisfies most patients. The psoriasis association is worth mentioning because patients with psoriasis have a significantly higher prevalence of geographic tongue — both share dysfunction of the same inflammatory pathway (IL-36 cytokine signalling), and geographic tongue can be considered an oral manifestation of the same underlying immune dysregulation as psoriasis. Plummer-Vinson syndrome (also called Patterson-Brown-Kelly syndrome in the UK) deserves specific attention — it is the combination of iron deficiency anaemia, dysphagia from a post-cricoid oesophageal web, glossitis (smooth red tongue), koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), and angular cheilitis. It predominantly affects middle-aged women. Critically, the post-cricoid oesophageal web in Plummer-Vinson syndrome carries a significantly elevated risk of hypopharyngeal or oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma — this is why any patient presenting with iron deficiency + dysphagia + glossitis needs gastroscopy to identify and treat the web, and surveillance thereafter. Iron replacement resolves the glossitis and may resolve the web in early cases. Burning mouth syndrome is one of the most challenging conditions to manage in primary care — it is a neuropathic pain condition of the oral mucosa with a complex pathophysiology (peripheral small fibre neuropathy + central sensitisation + psychological factors) and limited treatment response. The key is diagnosis of exclusion — every identifiable treatable cause (candidiasis, nutritional deficiency, dry mouth, denture allergy, contact stomatitis from toothpaste flavourings or preservatives) must be identified and corrected before BMS is diagnosed. Many patients diagnosed with BMS actually have one of these treatable conditions.

Diagnose

Targeted Examination & History

History key points
Duration and change over time (static = benign; growing = suspicious) · Site: dorsal (geographic, BMS, hairy tongue), lateral/ventral (important SCC sites, aphthous) · Character: ulcer vs lesion vs colour change vs swelling vs pain only · Symptoms: pain (burning = BMS/glossitis; sharp = ulcer; referred earache = tongue SCC invading lingual nerve) · Smoking + alcohol status · Dental procedures (lingual nerve) · Medications: ACEi (angioedema), antibiotics (black hairy tongue), methotrexate (mucositis), bisphosphonates, immunosuppressants · Dysphagia + weight loss (Plummer-Vinson, tongue SCC) · Systemic symptoms (hypothyroidism, amyloidosis)
Tongue examination technique
Ask patient to protrude tongue (CN XII assessment — lateral deviation indicates ipsilateral palsy). Inspect dorsal surface. Use gauze to grasp tongue tip and retract laterally to fully expose lateral borders bilaterally — the most important part of tongue examination. Examine ventral surface (ventrolateral tongue = high-risk SCC site). Palpate any lesion bimanually (index finger intraorally, thumb extraorally under chin). Feel for induration. Palpate submandibular, submental and other cervical nodes. Do not aggressively examine the posterior tongue/oropharynx if there is stridor, drooling, severe dysphagia or suspected deep-neck infection — prioritise airway management and emergency transfer.
Tongue movement and CN XII
Protrusion: deviation toward the affected side (ipsilateral CN XII palsy). Lateral movement (side to side): both sides equally — any restriction in lateral movement suggests fixation by tumour (tongue cancer invading deep muscles). Fasciculations at rest: MND, CN XII lower motor neurone lesion. Tongue wasting (unilateral hemiatrophy): asymmetry visible at rest. Increased tongue size, fissuring, and macroglossic features: examine thyroid, extremities (acromegaly), skin (amyloidosis — periorbital purpura, macroglossia, carpal tunnel).
Oral mucosa and associated findings
White patches (wipeable suggests candida; persistent non-wipeable needs dental/oral-medicine assessment — not automatically leukoplakia) · Red or red-white patches (possible erythroplakia/erythroleukoplakia — urgent dental/oral assessment) · Angular cheilitis (iron/B12/folate/zinc deficiency, candida, poor denture fit — indicates nutritional status) · Smooth gingiva (nutritional deficiency + gingivitis) · Petechiae on palate (thrombocytopenia — check FBC if unexplained) · Kaposi's sarcoma (purple lesion on hard palate = HIV/AIDS) · Fordyce spots (ectopic sebaceous glands on buccal mucosa — benign yellow-white dots)
The gauze retraction technique for lateral tongue examination is the most important skill in oral cancer detection and is systematically underpractised in primary care — without physically grasping the tongue tip with gauze and pulling it laterally, the lateral and ventrolateral borders (important sites for tongue SCC) are completely invisible. It is physically impossible to see the lateral tongue border simply by asking the patient to open their mouth and protrude the tongue. The technique takes 15 seconds: tear a small piece of gauze (or use a tissue), grasp the tongue tip with the non-dominant hand, retract laterally to the patient's left, inspect the right lateral border, then retract to the right and inspect the left lateral border. Any ulceration, induration, leukoplakia, erythroplakia, or unexplained colour change should be visible. This should be a routine part of any oral examination in patients over 40 with mouth symptoms. Referred otalgia (referred pain to the ear) from tongue or oropharyngeal cancer is one of the most important clinical features to recognise — the sensory innervation of the tongue and oropharynx overlaps with the auricular branches of CN V3 and CN IX, meaning that tumours in this region can cause referred pain perceived in the ipsilateral ear. Any adult with persistent unilateral ear pain without obvious otological cause (intact tympanic membrane, normal otoscopy) warrants oral and oropharyngeal examination to exclude tongue base or tonsillar cancer. The tongue base is part of the oropharynx, not the oral cavity — HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer should not be conflated with conventional lateral oral-tongue SCC. Referred otalgia is a known presentation pattern of HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC and is one of the reasons these cancers are frequently delayed in diagnosis. Periorbital purpura with macroglossia is a highly specific presentation of systemic amyloidosis — the purpura is caused by amyloid infiltration of dermal blood vessel walls, making them fragile, and occurs characteristically around the eyes after minor trauma (Valsalva, vomiting). This combination of periorbital purpura + macroglossia + carpal tunnel syndrome in an older patient should prompt serum electrophoresis, immunoglobulins and serum free light chains + haematology referral.

Diagnose

Investigations

Glossitis / smooth red tongue
Investigate according to history and examination: FBC + ferritin if iron deficiency is possible · B12 + folate if macrocytosis, dietary risk, neuropathy or malabsorption · TSH/free T4 if hypothyroidism clinically suspected · TTG IgA + total IgA if GI symptoms, iron/B12/folate deficiency or recurrent aphthae (while eating gluten) · Glucose/HbA1c if diabetes suspected or clinically relevant. Routine zinc testing is not required for most patients.
Macroglossia screen
Targeted: TSH + free T4 if hypothyroid features · IGF-1 if clinical features of acromegaly · SPEP + immunoglobulins + serum free light chains where amyloidosis/plasma-cell disease is suspected (urine Bence Jones not routinely needed where the local pathway uses serum free light chains) · Karyotype (Down syndrome — if not already diagnosed) · Urgent airway assessment if dysphagia, sleep-related obstruction or rapidly progressive enlargement · 999 if stridor or other airway compromise
Burning mouth syndrome exclusion screen
First confirm no visible mucosal lesion and review medicines, dentures, oral hygiene, xerostomia and contact irritants · FBC + ferritin + B12 + folate (nutritional; zinc only if deficiency suspected) · HbA1c (diabetic neuropathy — small fibre) · TSH (hypothyroid) · Oral candida swab (exclude low-grade candidiasis mimicking BMS) · Patch testing (contact stomatitis from toothpaste or denture material — dental referral) · Salivary flow test (xerostomia — prescribed or Sjögren's-related)
Neurological tongue problems
Urgent neurological assessment + imaging of brain, skull base and neck/carotid region (new unexplained CN XII palsy — malignancy, dissection, inflammatory disease, MND; same-day emergency assessment if acute vascular features) · Lyme serology (CN palsy in endemic area) · ANA + anti-dsDNA + anti-Ro/La (SLE, Sjögren's — neuropathy) · ACE + CXR (sarcoidosis — cranial neuropathy)
Do not investigate
Typical geographic tongue (no investigations required — clinical diagnosis, reassurance only). Black hairy tongue with clear cause (antibiotics, smoking — no investigations if clear precipitant identified and resolving). Fissured tongue — no investigations required if asymptomatic. Fordyce spots — benign, no investigations.
The IGF-1 test for acromegaly is the appropriate first-line screening test (not GH, which fluctuates throughout the day). Acromegaly causes macroglossia through GH-mediated tissue overgrowth affecting all soft tissues including the tongue, lips, and parotid glands. Associated features to look for: enlarged hands and feet (rings/shoes no longer fitting), frontal bossing, prognathism, interdental spacing, hypertension, carpal tunnel syndrome, type 2 diabetes, sleep apnoea. The prevalence of acromegaly is approximately 6 per 100,000 — it is rare, but delayed diagnosis (mean 7–10 years from symptom onset) is the norm, partly because macroglossia is attributed to other causes. A random GH is unhelpful; failure of GH to suppress on a glucose tolerance test (specialist test) confirms acromegaly; elevated IGF-1 = pituitary MRI + endocrinology referral. Oral candidiasis causing burning mouth syndrome is an important diagnosis to exclude — low-grade, erythematous candidiasis (the atrophic form) can cause exactly the burning pain pattern of BMS without the visible white plaques of pseudomembranous candidiasis. This form of candidiasis is extremely easy to miss — the mucosa appears reddened and slightly glazed but not specifically abnormal to the untrained eye. A therapeutic trial of fluconazole 50 mg OD × 7 days (or nystatin suspension QDS × 2 weeks) can be both diagnostic and therapeutic — complete resolution of burning with antifungal treatment confirms candidal aetiology and should change the diagnosis from BMS to oral candidiasis. This trial should be performed before making a definitive diagnosis of BMS.

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day
Tongue swelling causing stridor or airway compromise (angioedema — anaphylaxis or ACEi-induced) → 999, ABCDE, IM adrenaline if anaphylaxis features · SJS/TEN, neutropenic sepsis or deep-neck infection → immediate hospital referral · Lingual nerve injury with complete sensory loss, severe dysaesthesia, suspected transection, progressive symptoms or significant functional impact → prompt dental/OMFS discussion
Suspected cancer: oral / head & neck
Unexplained oral ulceration lasting >3 weeks, at any site (NICE NG12) · Lump on the lip/oral cavity or red/red-white patch consistent with erythroplakia/erythroleukoplakia → urgent dental/oral assessment (NG12); the dentist may refer through the suspected cancer pathway
Urgent referral (local pathway): OMFS / ENT
Not NG12 suspected cancer criteria: Any tongue lump or induration without clear cause (NG12 routes a lump in the mouth to urgent dental assessment) · Persistent unexplained non-wipeable white lesion → dental/oral-medicine assessment, urgency by appearance, site, symptoms, size, progression, red areas, induration, lymphadenopathy and risk factors · Unexplained tonsillar asymmetry or tongue base mass · Unexplained persistent referred otalgia (ipsilateral ear pain) + negative ear examination
Urgent neurology
New unexplained CN XII palsy (tongue wasting + deviation on protrusion) — urgent neurological assessment and imaging; same-day emergency assessment if acute vascular features · Combined lower cranial nerve palsy (IX/X/XI/XII) = jugular foramen lesion · Tongue fasciculations + widespread muscle weakness (MND)
Oral medicine / OMFS (routine or urgent)
Burning mouth syndrome not responding to GP-initiated treatment (candida excluded, nutritional deficiencies corrected) · Major macroglossia causing dysphagia or speech difficulty · Lingual nerve injury (OMFS for microsurgical assessment) · Recurrent major aphthous on tongue
Endocrinology
Suspected acromegaly (elevated IGF-1 + macroglossia + acral changes) · Hypothyroid macroglossia (start levothyroxine in primary care, endocrinology if complex)
Haematology
Suspected amyloidosis (macroglossia + abnormal serum free light chains/electrophoresis) · Plummer-Vinson syndrome (iron deficiency + dysphagia + glossitis → urgent dysphagia/upper-GI pathway + haematology)
Post-dental lingual nerve paraesthesia may improve spontaneously, and the timing and indication for microsurgical repair depend on the severity, mechanism, serial neurosensory assessment and specialist opinion. New persistent tongue/floor-of-mouth numbness, dysaesthesia or taste disturbance after dental treatment should be documented and discussed promptly with the dental/OMFS service; urgent referral is appropriate for complete sensory loss, severe dysaesthesia, suspected nerve transection, progressive symptoms or significant functional impact. The clinical consequences of permanent lingual nerve damage include: permanent numbness of the ipsilateral anterior two-thirds of tongue, floor of mouth, and lingual gingiva; loss of taste from the anterior two-thirds of tongue (chorda tympani component); and chronic neuropathic pain (dysaesthesia). These significantly affect quality of life, and the medicolegal implications of delayed or absent referral are considerable. Not every post-injection altered sensation requires surgical referral within 48 hours. A red or red-white patch consistent with erythroplakia/erythroleukoplakia warrants urgent dental/oral assessment at first presentation, regardless of duration (NICE NG12); a dentist may then refer through the suspected cancer pathway if clinically suspicious. Malignant potential is determined once the lesion has been assessed and classified by an oral specialist. The practical clinical implication is: when examining the tongue, if you find a red patch that does not rub off with gauze → arrange urgent dental/oral assessment at that consultation. Do not wait, do not treat empirically, do not "review in 2 weeks."

Treat

Treatment by Condition

Reserve systemic steroids, clonazepam, dapsone, colchicine and thalidomide for specialist-led management.

Glossitis / nutritional
Treat confirmed deficiency and its cause
Iron: follow the current iron-deficiency pathway (BSG 2021) and investigate the cause — ferrous sulphate 200 mg is a product dose, not an elemental-iron dose. B12: replacement depends on cause and neurological involvement — no single universal hydroxocobalamin schedule. Folate: do not give folic acid until B12 deficiency has been considered. Zinc: only if deficiency is demonstrated or strongly suspected (excess zinc can cause copper deficiency). Coeliac: keep eating gluten until testing is complete; no gluten-free diet before diagnostic confirmation unless on specialist advice. Avoiding SLS toothpaste is an optional trial, not standard treatment.
Black hairy tongue
Remove cause + oral hygiene
Stop antibiotics if possible (or complete course then reassess). Stop smoking. Reduce coffee/tea. Tongue brushing twice daily (soft-bristled toothbrush on dorsal tongue surface). Adequate hydration (2 L/day). Chlorhexidine mouthwash BD × 2 weeks. Resolves within 2–4 weeks with consistent oral hygiene.
Burning mouth syndrome
Exclude causes; reassure; specialist-directed drugs
1. Confirm no visible mucosal lesion. 2. Review medicines, dentures, oral hygiene, xerostomia and contact irritants. 3. Exclude or treat candidiasis, iron/B12/folate deficiency, diabetes, thyroid disease and other relevant causes. 4. Consider oral medicine/dentistry referral if persistent. 5. Reassurance, explanation and psychological/CBT-based support (NHS Talking Therapies). 6. Specialist-directed options may include topical clonazepam, tricyclics, gabapentinoids or other neuropathic-pain strategies — individualised; clonazepam is off-label and can cause sedation, dependence, falls and cognitive impairment, so it is not routine GP treatment.
Angioedema (acute)Anaphylaxis: adrenaline 0.5 mg IM (anterolateral thigh) → 999; repeat IM adrenaline after 5 minutes if no improvement. Antihistamines are third-line (skin symptoms only) and corticosteroids are no longer routine (Resuscitation Council UK 2021). ACEi-induced (no urticaria): 999 for tongue, laryngeal or airway involvement; ABCDE; IM adrenaline if airway, breathing or circulation problems consistent with anaphylaxis; involve anaesthetics/ENT early; icatibant or C1-inhibitor only according to specialist/hospital protocol. Stop ACEi permanently. An adrenaline auto-injector is not routinely prescribed after isolated ACEi (bradykinin-mediated) angio-oedema — it is generally indicated after anaphylaxis where future exposure risk exists, following specialist advice.
Hairy leukoplakiaHairy leukoplakia = oral EBV infection in immunocompromised state. Offer HIV testing where clinically indicated. If HIV-positive: start antiretroviral therapy (ART) — hairy leukoplakia resolves completely with ART. If HIV-negative: assess other immunosuppressive states (solid organ transplant, haematological malignancy, high-dose steroid). Aciclovir 200 mg QDS × 2 weeks reduces lesion temporarily but does not treat the underlying immunosuppression.
Topical clonazepam for burning mouth syndrome is off-label and can cause sedation, dependence, falls and cognitive impairment; it should not be presented as routine GP treatment. Topical clonazepam, tricyclics, gabapentinoids and other neuropathic-pain strategies are specialist-directed (oral medicine) and individualised, after treatable causes have been excluded. Hairy leukoplakia as a marker of immunosuppression is one of the most important oral findings a GP can make — it is caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) replication in the oral mucosal epithelium, which only occurs when cellular immunity is suppressed. In the pre-ART era, hairy leukoplakia was a reliable marker of HIV infection with CD4 count below 300 cells/µl. In the current era, the same principle applies: EBV-driven hairy leukoplakia indicates significant cellular immunosuppression from whatever cause. The primary cause to exclude is HIV — an HIV test must be offered and strongly recommended to any patient with hairy leukoplakia who does not have another identified cause of immunosuppression. The lesion itself is asymptomatic and does not require treatment in most cases (it will resolve with ART in HIV-positive patients), but its significance lies entirely in what it reveals about the patient's immune status.

Treat

Macroglossia & Systemic Causes

Hypothyroid macroglossia
Initiate levothyroxine replacement — see Hypothyroidism algorithm. Starting dose (NICE NG145): about 1.6 micrograms/kg/day in adults under 65 without cardiovascular disease; 25–50 micrograms/day at 65 or over or with cardiovascular disease, increasing in 25-microgram steps, titrating to TSH within normal range. Macroglossia responds gradually over months — not immediate. Do not expect rapid tongue size normalisation. Refer endocrinology if complex (pituitary hypothyroidism, cardiac disease, pregnancy).
Acromegaly
Endocrinology-led management: transsphenoidal pituitary surgery (first-line for GH-secreting adenoma), somatostatin analogues (octreotide, lanreotide — if surgery incomplete or not possible), dopamine agonists (cabergoline — second-line), GH receptor antagonist (pegvisomant — for persistent elevated IGF-1 after surgery). Macroglossia and soft tissue changes partially regress with normalisation of GH/IGF-1 levels. Surveillance for acromegaly complications: colonic polyps (colonoscopy), OSA (sleep study), cardiomyopathy (echo).
Amyloidosis
Haematology-led. Type-dependent: AL amyloidosis (light chain — plasma cell dyscrasia: specialist-directed treatment). AA amyloidosis (reactive — treat underlying inflammatory condition). ATTR amyloidosis (transthyretin — hereditary or wild-type/cardiac): tafamidis (stabilises TTR). Macroglossia in AL amyloidosis is not reversible with chemotherapy — it regresses only if treatment produces sustained complete haematological remission.
ACE inhibitor angioedema — prevention
Stop ACEi permanently. If an antihypertensive/cardioprotective effect is needed, any switch to an ARB (losartan, candesartan, valsartan) should be individualised and documented — ARBs rarely cause angioedema but are not completely risk-free. Adrenaline auto-injectors are not effective for bradykinin-mediated (ACEi) angioedema — prescribe one only if anaphylaxis is also a risk. Allergy clinic for confirmed hereditary angioedema (C4 low, C1-esterase inhibitor deficient/dysfunctional). Medic-alert bracelet for patients with hereditary angioedema.
The distinction between AL amyloidosis and ATTR amyloidosis is increasingly important clinically as specific treatments have become available for ATTR. Wild-type ATTR (transthyretin) amyloidosis is the most common form of cardiac amyloidosis in older adults — it causes a restrictive cardiomyopathy that is frequently misdiagnosed as hypertensive heart disease or HFpEF. Tafamidis (Vyndaqel) is a disease-modifying treatment that stabilises the TTR protein and reduces cardiac amyloid deposition — recommended by NICE TA984 (June 2024) for wild-type or hereditary ATTR-CM in adults. GPs who encounter macroglossia in an older patient with unexplained cardiac failure and thick walls on echocardiography should consider amyloidosis and ensure haematology/cardiology review. The periorbital purpura + macroglossia combination is almost diagnostic of AL amyloidosis. The ACEi-to-ARB switch principle is one of the most important medication management decisions following ACEi-induced angioedema — ARBs block angiotensin II at the AT1 receptor without affecting the bradykinin pathway, so they cause less bradykinin accumulation and rarely cause angioedema — but they are not completely risk-free, so the switch should be individualised and documented. The patient and their subsequent GP must be clearly informed that ACEi must never be restarted — this must be documented in the patient's allergy record. The switch from ramipril to candesartan, for example, may maintain cardiovascular and renal protective effects with a lower, but not absent, angioedema risk. Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto) contains an ARB, must never be combined with another ARB, and is contraindicated after angioedema related to previous ACE inhibitor or ARB therapy (SPC).

Lifestyle

Oral Hygiene, Diet & Cancer Prevention

Tongue brushing Daily gentle brushing of the dorsal tongue surface with a soft toothbrush or dedicated tongue scraper removes accumulated debris, bacteria, and dead cells. Essential for: black hairy tongue (speeds resolution), BMS (reduces candidal load), halitosis (90% of oral malodour originates from tongue dorsum bacteria), fissured tongue (prevents food debris accumulation in fissures). Technique: brush from posterior to anterior, 5–8 strokes. Do not brush vigorously — damages filiform papillae.
Smoking cessation Smoking is the primary preventable cause of tongue and oral cavity cancer. Smoking and alcohol combined markedly increase oral cavity cancer risk. Risk reduces after cessation. Smoking also contributes to black hairy tongue, delayed healing of oral ulcers, and candidal overgrowth. NHS Stop Smoking Service referral, nicotine replacement, varenicline (Champix) or bupropion (Zyban). Every consultation = cessation opportunity.
Alcohol reduction Heavy alcohol use is the second major risk factor for tongue SCC (multiplicative effect with smoking). Alcohol disrupts oral mucosal barrier, increases carcinogen penetration. Reduce to <14 units/week (men and women). Alcohol also aggravates burning mouth, worsens glossitis, and promotes candidal overgrowth. AUDIT-C screen at first presentation of tongue symptoms. Brief motivational intervention or community alcohol team referral.
HPV vaccination and awareness HPV is associated with oropharyngeal (including tongue base) SCC — the tongue base is part of the oropharynx, distinct from conventional oral-tongue SCC. HPV vaccination advice should follow the current NHS eligibility programme; vaccination is not treatment for established HPV-related cancer. HPV is common, often transient and may have been acquired many years previously — HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer does not need routine GUM referral or partner notification; cancer care is through the head-and-neck oncology pathway.
Adequate hydration and xerostomia prevention Dry mouth (xerostomia) worsens glossitis, burning mouth, candidal overgrowth, and fissured tongue. Common causes: anticholinergic drugs (antihistamines, TCAs, antimuscarinics, antipsychotics), Sjögren's syndrome, post-radiotherapy. Target 8 glasses water daily. Sugar-free chewing gum (stimulates salivary flow — xylitol-containing preferred as it also reduces Streptococcus mutans). Biotène products (alcohol-free mouthwash, gel, spray). Avoid caffeine and alcohol (dehydrating). Pilocarpine 5 mg TDS for severe Sjögren's-related xerostomia (muscarinic agonist — increases salivary flow, specialist-initiated).
Dietary B vitamin and mineral intake Maintain adequate iron (red meat, legumes, fortified cereals), B12 (animal products — vegans must supplement), folate (dark leafy greens), zinc (shellfish, seeds, nuts, meat), riboflavin/B2 (dairy, eggs, almonds). These are the nutrients most commonly deficient in glossitis. At-risk groups: vegans (B12), elderly (B12 + iron + zinc), IBD patients (all), bariatric surgery patients (all). Targeted bloods in these groups when symptoms or clinical risk suggest deficiency.
Regular dental review and oral cancer screening Annual dental check-up for oral mucosal examination (dentists examine oral mucosa as part of routine check — they see patients more frequently than GPs and can detect leukoplakia, erythroplakia, early tongue lesions earlier). Emphasise to patients: the dental check is not just about teeth — it is an oral cancer screening opportunity. Patients who have not seen a dentist for >2 years should be prioritised for registration. NHS dental check available at all NHS dental practices.
Tongue symptom diary (BMS) Patients with burning mouth syndrome benefit from a symptom diary: time of day, severity (0–10 NRS), associated factors (food, drink, stress, medications, menstrual cycle, sleep quality). BMS characteristically has a diurnal pattern (worse as the day progresses, best in the morning) — documenting this validates the pattern and distinguishes BMS from organic pain (which tends to be more constant). The diary also identifies potential triggers (certain foods, toothpaste flavourings, stress) that can be removed. Bring diary to follow-up appointments.
The tongue dorsum is the primary source of oral malodour (halitosis) — not, as commonly believed, the stomach or systemic disease. Approximately 90% of oral malodour in otherwise healthy individuals originates from the anaerobic bacteria that colonise the posterior tongue dorsum, particularly in the crypts between the circumvallate papillae. These bacteria degrade sulphur-containing proteins (from food debris, desquamated epithelial cells, and saliva) to produce volatile sulphur compounds (VSCs: hydrogen sulphide, methyl mercaptan, dimethyl sulphide). Tongue brushing or scraping reduces VSC production by removing the bacterial substrate from the tongue surface. A tongue scraper is more effective than a toothbrush for VSC reduction (Haraszthy et al. 2007) and reduces malodour by approximately 75%. This is highly relevant to clinical practice — many patients attending for halitosis or recurrent sore tongue will benefit from this simple advice. HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC (which includes tongue base and tonsillar SCC) is managed through the head-and-neck oncology pathway; prognostic discussions belong with the specialist team. GPs should avoid giving the impression that HPV SCC is "not serious" — it still requires intensive treatment and carries significant treatment morbidity.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Geographic / fissured / black hairy tongue
No follow-up if: typical features, clear cause identified, patient reassured. Written safety-net: "Return if any new ulcer develops, if you notice a red or white patch that won't rub off, or if any area becomes persistently painful or changes in appearance." Document reassurance given.
Glossitis on treatment
Review at 8–12 weeks after commencing nutritional replacement: tongue normalising? Haematological values correcting (recheck FBC + ferritin/B12/folate)? If not responding as expected → re-examine tongue, reconsider coeliac (TTG IgA if not already done), consider oral medicine referral if atypical features emerge.
BMS ongoing management
Monthly reviews initially (BMS is chronic, fluctuating, and impacts quality of life). Assess pain NRS. Tolerability of any specialist-directed treatment. Psychological component: NHS Talking Therapies referral for CBT. Oral medicine/dentistry review if persistent. HRT is not a standard BMS treatment — evidence is insufficient.
Macroglossia monitoring
Hypothyroid: TSH every 3 months until stable (NICE NG145), then annually. Acromegaly: endocrinology-led monitoring (IGF-1, glucose, colonoscopy, echo). Amyloidosis: haematology-led (organ function monitoring, treatment response). All macroglossia: monitor swallowing, speech, and airway — any progression of dysphagia or snoring/apnoea → urgent reassessment.
Return immediately safety-net
Any tongue swelling causing difficulty breathing or swallowing → 999 · A new ulcer not yet at 3 weeks → document and review; unexplained ulceration lasting >3 weeks → suspected cancer pathway referral at the same visit — do not repeatedly treat a persistent ulcer with topical steroids while referral is delayed · Any new red/red-white patch → urgent dental/oral assessment; persistent non-wipeable white patch → dental/oral-medicine assessment · Tongue deviation without a clear cause → urgent neurological assessment (not every case needs an MRI ordered directly by the GP) · Worsening dysphagia → urgent dysphagia/upper-GI or head-and-neck pathway based on the clinical findings
Same-week GP
ACEi angioedema fully resolved but patient still on ACEi (stop permanently; individualise and document any ARB switch; record allergy) · BMS worsening despite treatment at 4 weeks (oral medicine referral) · Hairy leukoplakia identified in a patient not yet HIV-tested (arrange same week)
BMS predominantly affects postmenopausal women, but HRT should not routinely be suggested for BMS — evidence is insufficient and it is not a standard treatment. HRT decisions follow the standard menopause assessment (NICE NG23) for its usual indications. The swallowing and airway monitoring in macroglossia is a clinical safety principle — macroglossia from any cause can progress to cause obstructive sleep apnoea (tongue falling back during sleep → airway obstruction), dysphagia (difficulty manipulating food bolus), and in severe cases, at-rest airway compromise. The STOP-BANG questionnaire or Epworth Sleepiness Scale should be applied to all patients with macroglossia to screen for OSA. Any patient with macroglossia + excessive snoring + daytime somnolence warrants overnight oximetry or formal sleep study referral. Stridor or other airway compromise → 999. Progressive dysphagia or rapidly progressive macroglossia → urgent airway assessment + speech and language therapy referral for dysphagia assessment.
