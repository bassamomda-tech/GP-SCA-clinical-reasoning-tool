Safety

Red Flags — Malignancy, Symptomatic Mass & Urgent Features

Incidental renal mass + haematuria (frank or microscopic) + flank pain + palpable renal mass + weight loss Classic Triad of renal cell carcinoma — most patients now present incidentally but classic presentation carries higher stage. → suspected cancer pathway if visible haematuria meets NG12 criteria; otherwise prompt urology/uro-oncology referral. Imaging and staging are specialist-directed.
Incidental renal mass + new hypertension + hypokalaemia + elevated cortisol/aldosterone Adrenal mass mimicking or adjacent to renal mass — adrenal adenoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma. → Endocrinology via the local adrenal incidentaloma pathway: plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines where phaeochromocytoma is not excluded; aldosterone-renin ratio if hypertension with hypokalaemia; 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test for autonomous cortisol. Test and urgency depend on imaging appearance and biochemical syndrome.
Incidental renal mass + rapid growth on surveillance + size >4 cm + heterogeneous enhancement + necrosis Possible high-risk renal cell carcinoma → urgent urology/uro-oncology MDT review.
Renal mass + bone pain + hypercalcaemia Possible metastatic RCC → urgent urology/uro-oncology. Check corrected calcium, PTH and renal function. If myeloma is suspected, use the local haematology pathway (FBC, calcium, renal function, serum protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains, appropriate imaging).
Child + abdominal mass + haematuria + hypertension Wilms' tumour (nephroblastoma) — most common childhood abdominal tumour. → Same-day paediatric oncology. Do NOT palpate repeatedly (risk of tumour rupture and seeding). Urgent USS.
Bilateral renal cysts + family history + early-onset renal failure + extra-renal features (retinal haemangiomas, CNS tumours) Hereditary RCC syndrome: VHL disease, HLRCC (hereditary leiomyomatosis RCC), Birt-Hogg-Dubé. → Hereditary RCC assessment (NICE NG256) via the National Genomic Test Directory/local clinical-genetics pathway + urology. HLRCC (FH-deficient) lesions are aggressive — expedite specialist management; do not biopsy.
The VHL (von Hippel-Lindau) disease is the most clinically significant hereditary renal mass syndrome and carries a major GP recognition responsibility — VHL is an autosomal dominant tumour suppressor gene mutation on chromosome 3p that predisposes to: bilateral, multifocal clear cell RCC (often presenting early, age 20s-30s); cerebellar and spinal cord haemangioblastomas; retinal haemangiomas (presenting as visual symptoms); phaeochromocytomas; endolymphatic sac tumours (hearing loss); pancreatic cysts and neuroendocrine tumours. The typical presentation: a young adult with bilateral or multifocal renal cysts/masses, with or without family history. The critical management implication: VHL RCCs require nephron-sparing surgery wherever possible (partial nephrectomy) to preserve renal function given the bilateral and recurrent nature of the disease. NICE NG256 recommends hereditary assessment for people aged 46 or younger, with multiple renal tumours, a first- or second-degree relative with renal cancer, features of a hereditary syndrome or associated histology — refer via the local clinical-genetics pathway and to specialist RCC centres for surgical planning.

Renal Mass Classification — Bosniak System

Bosniak classification for renal cysts
Use Bosniak v2019 (state the version). Bosniak I: simple benign cyst → no follow-up. Bosniak II: minimally complex, benign → no routine follow-up. Bosniak IIF: secondary-care active surveillance, not routine surgery; treat if it progresses to III/IV or causes clinically significant symptoms (NICE NG256). Bosniak III: indeterminate → uro-oncology MDT; active surveillance or surgery depending on size, solid component, comorbidity and preference. Bosniak IV: manage as a likely malignant lesion → MDT discussion and treatment planning. Published malignancy rates vary with imaging criteria and cohort selection and are not individual-patient probabilities.
Solid renal mass — characterisation
Small solid mass <2 cm: consider active surveillance after imaging, staging and uro-oncology MDT discussion (NICE NG256). Solid mass 1-4 cm (T1a): renal cell carcinoma vs angiomyolipoma vs oncocytoma. CT multiphasic + contrast enhancement pattern distinguishes. Angiomyolipoma (AML): contains fat (CT Hounsfield units <-20 HU) — benign; most sporadic AMLs are managed by specialist surveillance. Solid mass >4 cm (T1b-T2): most are RCC — prompt urology/uro-oncology referral; active surveillance not generally recommended where treatment is feasible. Mass with invasion of Gerota's fascia, renal vein, lymph nodes (T3-T4): advanced RCC — urgent MDT referral.
RCC histological subtypes
Clear cell RCC: most common (approximately 70–85%) — VHL pathway, high vascularisation, best responds to antiangiogenic TKIs. Papillary RCC: second most common (10-15%) — type 1 (MET pathway) and type 2 (HLRCC pathway). Chromophobe RCC: approximately 4–5% — indolent, best prognosis. Collecting duct carcinoma: rare, aggressive. Angiomyolipoma: benign hamartoma (fat, muscle, blood vessels) — characteristic fat signal; TSC association.
The Bosniak classification is the universally used radiological risk stratification system for incidentally discovered renal cysts — it was developed by Dr Morton Bosniak (1986, updated 2019) as a CT-based scoring system. The 2019 update to the Bosniak classification (version 2019) refined the categories with more precise CT morphological criteria, particularly distinguishing Bosniak IIF from III based on the number of septa, calcification pattern, and contrast enhancement. For GPs: the practical application is that the radiology report from an incidental finding on CT or USS will often include a Bosniak category, and the GP's role is to act on that categorisation: Bosniak I-II = no action; Bosniak IIF = secondary-care active surveillance with a documented plan; Bosniak III-IV = prompt urology/uro-oncology referral for MDT review. A key practical pitfall: USS cannot reliably classify Bosniak category — any indeterminate cystic renal lesion on USS requires specialist-directed multiphasic CT or MRI for accurate Bosniak classification before clinical decision-making.

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
How discovered: incidental on USS/CT for another indication (most common today — approximately 60-70% of RCC are now detected incidentally), vs symptomatic presentation. Symptoms: haematuria (frank or microscopic — grade 2 or above on dipstick), flank pain (dull continuous = mass effect), weight loss, night sweats, fatigue (paraneoplastic). Paraneoplastic syndromes of RCC: polycythaemia (EPO secretion), hypercalcaemia (PTH-related peptide), hypertension (renin secretion), Stauffer syndrome (reversible hepatic dysfunction without hepatic metastases — elevated ALP/GGT with normal bilirubin). Family history: RCC, VHL, HLRCC, Birt-Hogg-Dubé. Smoking (associated with increased RCC risk). Previous nephrectomy (contralateral mass = must preserve). eGFR baseline.
Examination
Flank palpation: renal mass (bimanual ballottement — see abdominal mass algorithm). Unilateral limb oedema (renal vein or IVC thrombosis from tumour thrombus — RCC-specific emergency). Blood pressure (renovascular hypertension, renin secretion). Varicocoele (left-sided — left renal vein obstruction by tumour → impairs testicular venous drainage). Lymphadenopathy. Signs of metastatic disease: bone tenderness, hepatomegaly.
Investigations
Specialist radiology review then multiphasic CT or MRI per local protocol, usually via urology (phases vary; characterises enhancement, fat [AML], vascularity, local staging) · Urinalysis (haematuria); ACR if proteinuria, CKD or eGFR <60 — not a diagnostic test for RCC · FBC + U&Es + calcium; LFTs if clinically indicated · eGFR (baseline renal function) · BP · Staging (including chest CT) is specialist-determined by stage and symptoms — not necessarily needed for a small asymptomatic cT1a lesion · Bone imaging only if bone pain, pathological fracture, raised ALP or other suspicion — no routine bone scan · Split renal function (DMSA) only via urology/renal services for impaired function, solitary kidney, bilateral lesions or where renal preservation is critical · Do not routinely request urine cytology or renal biopsy in primary care
The paraneoplastic syndromes of renal cell carcinoma are important clinical red flags that GPs may encounter without realising the significance — RCC is one of the most prolific producers of paraneoplastic phenomena in oncology. The key syndromes to recognise: (1) Polycythaemia — elevated haemoglobin/haematocrit from ectopic EPO production; (2) Hypercalcaemia — PTHrP production causing symptomatic hypercalcaemia (confusion, polydipsia, constipation, weakness) without bone metastases; (3) Stauffer syndrome — reversible hepatic dysfunction (elevated ALP, GGT, ferritin) in the absence of liver metastases, caused by hepatocyte-affecting cytokines from the primary tumour; it resolves after nephrectomy and recurs with metastatic relapse (therefore an excellent follow-up marker); (4) Hypertension from ectopic renin; (5) Amyloid (secondary, AA-type). These associations are not specific enough to justify a routine "paraneoplastic screen" in every incidental mass; investigate unexplained polycythaemia or hypercalcaemia through standard pathways (corrected calcium, PTH, renal function and clinical context). Stauffer syndrome is a specialist diagnosis.

Surveillance Protocol & High-Risk Features

Active surveillance for small renal masses (T1a <4 cm)
Secondary-care-led (NICE NG256). Solid mass <2 cm: consider active surveillance after imaging, staging and uro-oncology MDT discussion. Solid 2–4 cm where surgery is unsuitable or declined: active surveillance or thermal ablation. Not generally recommended for solid masses >4 cm where treatment is feasible. Imaging by CT, MRI or ultrasound depending on renal function and lesion/patient factors — suggested at 3–6 and 12 months in year 1, then at least annually in years 2–5; after 5 years, discuss continuation or discharge (stability, fitness, age, comorbidity, preference). The plan should be documented and shared with the GP: modality and dates, named responsible team, symptoms requiring contact (e.g. haematuria, persistent abdominal pain) and triggers for MDT review. There is no universal growth-rate or size trigger. Hereditary syndromes (VHL, Birt–Hogg–Dubé, TSC) follow syndrome-specific specialist surveillance.
Renal mass biopsy
NICE NG256: offer image-guided core biopsy (not fine-needle aspiration alone) for suspected localised or locally advanced RCC ≤4 cm with a sufficiently large solid component when it could influence management. Consider for >4 cm if imaging suggests a benign lesion, before thermal ablation/SABR, or if the patient wishes to avoid surgery for a benign lesion. Do not offer if it will not change management, if there is renal vein/IVC invasion and surgery is indicated, or if a useful sample cannot be obtained. Predominantly cystic lesions: generally unhelpful. Not routinely needed in VHL; do not biopsy suspected HLRCC lesions. Tract seeding is very uncommon and of uncertain clinical significance. Not a primary-care investigation. Oncocytoma biopsy: characteristic histology (eosinophilic cytoplasm, round regular nuclei) — benign; however, hybrid oncocytic/chromophobe tumours exist.
Metastatic RCC — staging and systemic treatment
Specialist oncology-led (NICE NG256). Staging imaging is directed by stage, symptoms and MDT. First-line options depend on IMDC risk group, histology, fitness, comorbidity, immunotherapy contraindications, previous treatment and access criteria — e.g. nivolumab + ipilimumab (TA780), cabozantinib + nivolumab (TA964), lenvatinib + pembrolizumab and relevant TKIs. GPs should not select, initiate, interrupt or sequence systemic anticancer treatment; refer treatment-related complications to the treating oncology team or acute services according to severity.
The renal mass biopsy paradigm shift represents a significant change from the historical approach of treating all solid enhancing renal masses as malignant until proved otherwise — traditionally, a solid enhancing renal mass led directly to surgery (radical or partial nephrectomy) without histological confirmation. This approach carried a benign surgical rate of approximately 20-25% — meaning 1 in 4-5 nephrectomies were for benign conditions (oncocytoma, AML, lipid-poor AML). Image-guided renal mass biopsy (core biopsy, 18G needle, 2 cores minimum) has a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 90-95% and a very low complication rate. NICE NG256 (2026): offer biopsy for lesions ≤4 cm with a sufficient solid component where it could influence management, and before thermal ablation or SABR unless specialist circumstances dictate otherwise. GPs should understand that 'biopsy first, then treat' is increasingly standard practice at specialist RCC centres.

Referral Pathways

Prompt urology / uro-oncology
Solid enhancing or indeterminate renal mass · Bosniak III or IV cystic lesion · Suspected cancer pathway where NG12 criteria are met (visible haematuria aged 45+) or the local pathway specifies it for an incidental renal mass · Renal vein/IVC involvement, suspected metastases or severe symptoms → urgent specialist/hospital assessment · Age 46 or younger, multiple tumours or relevant family history → also hereditary assessment
Urgent urology (not the suspected cancer pathway, but within 2 weeks)
Bosniak IIF — secondary-care surveillance plan · Angiomyolipoma that is painful, bleeding, has high-risk vascular features or substantial growth (outside the cancer pathway) · Enhancing renal mass in a solitary kidney
GP management + planned surveillance
Simple renal cyst (Bosniak I-II) — no action, no follow-up required. Asymptomatic small AML — surveillance per a specialist plan; do not arrange repeated ultrasound without one.
Clinical genetics
NICE NG256 hereditary assessment: age 46 or younger · multiple renal tumours · first- or second-degree relative with renal cancer · features of a hereditary syndrome · associated tumour histology. Use the National Genomic Test Directory/local clinical-genetics pathway.
Benign lesions such as angiomyolipoma (AML) are generally managed outside the cancer pathway (NICE NG256). AML can bleed spontaneously (Wunderlich syndrome — acute flank pain, shock and falling haemoglobin), but current evidence does not support treating every AML over 4 cm. Most sporadic AMLs can be managed with specialist surveillance; active treatment — selective arterial embolisation, nephron-sparing surgery or, in selected tuberous-sclerosis-related disease, systemic therapy — is discussed for persistent pain, acute/recurrent bleeding, high-risk vascular features, substantial growth, very large tumour burden, pregnancy/childbearing considerations or other patient-specific risk. AML in tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC): often bilateral, multiple, large, and rapidly growing — requires specialist TSC centre review for mTOR inhibitor treatment (everolimus reduces AML size by approximately 50%). Any patient with TSC and renal lesions requires nephrology-urology co-management.

Treat

Surgical Options for Renal Cell Carcinoma

Partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing surgery)
Preferred where the lesion can be completely removed while preserving meaningful renal tissue and the benefit of renal preservation justifies the additional operative complexity. NICE NG256: surgery for suitable localised solid masses ≥2 cm; partial vs total nephrectomy depends on complete resectability, location, size and complexity, solitary kidney or bilateral tumours, pre-existing renal impairment, comorbidity and operative risk. 4 cm is not an absolute cut-off. Oncological outcomes equivalent to radical nephrectomy for T1a. Functional benefit: preserves renal parenchyma → better long-term eGFR → reduced cardiovascular mortality (CKD is a major cardiovascular risk factor). Laparoscopic or robotic-assisted: standard approach in most UK centres. Warm ischaemia time: <25 min target (reduced renal ischaemia during vascular clamping). Complications: haemorrhage, urinary fistula, temporary AKI.
Radical nephrectomy
Indication: large T1b-T3 tumours not amenable to partial resection; complex anatomy; surgeon/patient preference. Laparoscopic radical nephrectomy: gold standard (shorter hospital stay, less blood loss). Open radical nephrectomy: large complex tumours, IVC thrombus. Post-nephrectomy: eGFR typically falls by approximately 35-40% immediately post-operatively, then partially recovers to approximately 70-80% of pre-operative value. Monitor creatinine/eGFR after nephrectomy; if eGFR <60, offer ACR and manage per CKD guidance. ACEi/ARB where clinically indicated (particularly with albuminuria), not automatically because a kidney has been removed.
Ablative therapies
Thermal ablation (cryoablation or RFA) or SABR — biopsy first unless specialist circumstances dictate otherwise. For: small T1a masses in elderly/frail patients where surgery risk is high, single functional kidney, bilateral disease. Slightly higher local recurrence than surgery (approximately 5-10% vs 2-5% for resection). Nephron-sparing. Short hospital stay (day case possible). Post-ablation surveillance per the documented specialist plan.
The post-nephrectomy CKD surveillance responsibility in primary care is an important and frequently overlooked aspect of RCC aftercare — approximately 25-30% of patients who undergo radical nephrectomy develop CKD stage 3 or worse within 5 years. The eGFR decline after nephrectomy is not just a quality-of-life issue — it is a cardiovascular risk factor. Multiple studies have shown that post-nephrectomy CKD increases cardiovascular mortality more than the cancer itself in T1-T2 RCC patients. The GP's role in post-nephrectomy follow-up: (1) Annual eGFR monitoring; (2) Blood pressure and cardiovascular risk optimised per standard hypertension/CKD guidance (ACEi/ARB where clinically indicated, particularly with albuminuria); (3) ACR if eGFR is below 60 or there is proteinuria (NICE NG256); (4) Lifestyle: salt restriction, smoking cessation, weight management; (5) Avoid unnecessary or prolonged NSAID use, particularly in CKD, dehydration or intercurrent illness; contrast decisions follow radiology/renal guidance — an eGFR check should not delay necessary cancer imaging.

Systemic Therapy & Surveillance Post-Treatment

Post-operative surveillance for RCC (urology-led; local/EAU protocol)
Schedule set by the specialist team according to stage, histology and treatment; documented and shared with the GP (named responsible team, imaging dates). After treatment, check eGFR; ACR in primary care if eGFR <60 (NICE NG256). Signs of recurrence: rising creatinine (involvement of remaining kidney or contralateral), haematuria, new mass at surgical site, symptoms of metastasis.
Adjuvant therapy (post-nephrectomy)
Pembrolizumab (anti-PD1 immunotherapy): recommended by NICE TA830 (2022) as adjuvant treatment for high-risk clear cell RCC (pT2 grade 4 or pT3, or pT4 or pN+) after nephrectomy. Course: pembrolizumab 200 mg IV every 3 weeks for up to 17 cycles (approximately 12 months). Evidence: KEYNOTE-564 trial — approximately 32% reduction in disease recurrence. Eligibility and risk category are decided by the oncology/urology MDT. GP role: recognise possible immune-related adverse events and contact the oncology team urgently.
Systemic therapy — metastatic RCC
Specialist oncology-led per NICE NG256 (options include nivolumab + ipilimumab, TA780; cabozantinib + nivolumab, TA964; lenvatinib + pembrolizumab; relevant TKIs). Patients should have a written oncology monitoring plan. GPs treat hypertension using standard principles, check drug interactions, and contact oncology for severe, rapidly worsening or treatment-limiting hypertension or other toxicity. Do not alter or interrupt anticancer therapy without oncology advice unless directed by a shared-care protocol; check drug-specific issues against the SPC and local oncology protocol.
The immune checkpoint inhibitor adverse effects (immune-related adverse events — irAEs) require prompt GP recognition and escalation in patients receiving immunotherapy for RCC — nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and ipilimumab activate T-cell responses against tumour antigens but can also trigger autoimmune inflammation in any organ system. Urgently contact the oncology team or arrange same-day assessment for: new or worsening diarrhoea or abdominal pain; cough, dyspnoea or hypoxia; jaundice or markedly abnormal liver tests; severe headache, visual symptoms or confusion; profound fatigue, hypotension or suspected adrenal crisis; polyuria, polydipsia or suspected immune-mediated diabetes; chest pain, palpitations or new neurological symptoms; severe rash or mucosal involvement. Do not start corticosteroids or stop immunotherapy without specialist input unless managing a medical emergency under the local protocol. GPs should be alert to any new symptoms in immunotherapy patients that could represent an irAE — these require urgent oncology team contact, not a 'wait and see' approach.

Lifestyle

Lifestyle Factors, Kidney Health & Patient Support

Smoking cessation — RCC specific risk factor Advise and support smoking cessation because smoking is associated with kidney cancer risk, cardiovascular disease, CKD progression and postoperative complications. Refer to the NHS Stop Smoking Service.
Weight management and RCC prevention Use a patient-centred weight-management approach (NICE NG246), without causal or cancer-recurrence claims.
Post-nephrectomy renal protection After radical nephrectomy, the remaining kidney undergoes compensatory hyperfiltration (increased GFR in remaining nephrons) — this protects renal function initially but over decades causes glomerular hypertension and progressive nephron loss. Monitor creatinine/eGFR; if eGFR <60, offer ACR and manage per CKD guidance. Optimise BP and cardiovascular risk per standard hypertension/CKD guidance; ACEi/ARB where clinically indicated (particularly albuminuria). Avoid unnecessary or prolonged NSAID use, especially in CKD, dehydration or intercurrent illness. Contrast decisions per radiology/renal guidance — an eGFR check should not delay necessary cancer imaging.
Blood pressure monitoring on TKI therapy TKIs (sunitinib, pazopanib, axitinib, cabozantinib) commonly cause hypertension (VEGF pathway inhibition). Follow the written oncology monitoring plan; treat hypertension using standard principles, considering renal function, potassium and drug interactions, and contact the oncology team for severe, rapidly worsening or treatment-limiting hypertension. Do not alter or interrupt anticancer therapy without oncology advice.
Hereditary RCC syndromes — family cascade testing VHL disease: autosomal dominant — 50% of first-degree relatives are carriers. Arrange genetic counselling and VHL gene testing for all first-degree relatives of a patient with VHL-associated RCC. HLRCC (hereditary leiomyomatosis RCC): associated with uterine and skin leiomyomas — any woman with uterine fibroids at a young age + RCC family history requires HLRCC assessment. Hereditary assessment per NICE NG256 (age 46 or younger, multiple tumours, affected first/second-degree relative, syndromic features or associated histology) via the local clinical-genetics pathway.
Psychological support after RCC diagnosis Cancer diagnosis anxiety and depression: PHQ-9 + GAD-7 at diagnosis and at each follow-up visit. Macmillan Cancer Support: information on living with kidney cancer (macmillan.org.uk). Kidney Cancer UK (kcuk.co.uk): specialist support organisation, helpline, forum. Surveillance anxiety: many patients experience severe anxiety at each follow-up CT appointment ("scanxiety") — acknowledge this, provide clear safety-netting instructions, and consider CBT referral for significant anxiety.
Fertility and sexual function after nephrectomy Nephrectomy does not directly affect fertility or sexual function — both kidneys together are not required for hormonal function, and the operative site is remote from the reproductive tract in most patients. However: retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (performed for some high-risk cases) can damage sympathetic plexus → retrograde ejaculation in men. Discuss this risk pre-operatively. Testosterone levels: not directly affected by nephrectomy. Immunotherapy side effects: pembrolizumab and nivolumab can cause fatigue and libido reduction — acknowledge at follow-up.
Exercise and activity after nephrectomy Post-operative activity, contact-sport and driving advice depends on surgical approach, complications, occupation, pain and mobility — follow the surgeon's advice and insurer/DVLA guidance. Ongoing: regular moderate exercise (150 min/week) for cardiovascular health.
The 'scanxiety' phenomenon (anxiety surrounding surveillance imaging appointments) is a significant quality-of-life impairment for RCC survivors that is commonly underacknowledged — studies show that approximately 50-60% of cancer survivors experience clinically significant anxiety in the days and weeks before surveillance CT appointments. For RCC patients, who typically undergo CT surveillance every 6-12 months for 5 years, this creates a recurring cycle of anxiety that can profoundly affect daily functioning. GPs can help by: (1) proactively asking about anxiety at surveillance appointment times; (2) providing clear written information about what the follow-up CT is checking for and what the procedure involves; (3) confirming that results will be communicated promptly after the scan (delayed results amplify anxiety); (4) offering CBT referral for clinically significant surveillance anxiety. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) has specific evidence for cancer-related anxiety and scanxiety.

Follow-Up, Surveillance & Paraneoplastic Monitoring

GP responsibilities post-treatment (specialist-led follow-up)
Creatinine/eGFR after treatment; ACR if eGFR <60 (NICE NG256). BP and cardiovascular risk per standard hypertension/CKD guidance (ACEi/ARB where clinically indicated). Avoid unnecessary or prolonged NSAIDs; avoid aminoglycosides where possible. Surveillance imaging coordination — confirm patient received CT scan dates; track imaging results. Recurrence symptoms: unexplained weight loss, haematuria, bone pain, new neurological symptoms → contact urology/oncology same-week.
Immunotherapy (oncology-led)
Monitoring blood tests per the oncology plan. Urgently contact oncology or arrange same-day assessment for new/worsening diarrhoea or abdominal pain, cough/dyspnoea/hypoxia, jaundice or markedly abnormal LFTs, severe headache/visual symptoms/confusion, profound fatigue/hypotension/suspected adrenal crisis, polyuria/polydipsia, chest pain/palpitations/new neurological symptoms, or severe rash/mucosal involvement. Do not start corticosteroids or stop immunotherapy without specialist input unless managing an emergency under local protocol.
TKI therapy (oncology-led)
Follow the written oncology monitoring plan. Treat hypertension by standard principles and check interactions; contact oncology for severe, worsening or treatment-limiting toxicity. Do not change or interrupt anticancer therapy without oncology advice.

The annual eGFR + blood pressure monitoring after nephrectomy represents a simple but high-impact GP intervention — the evidence consistently shows that the largest driver of mortality in T1-T2 RCC survivors (who have excellent cancer-specific survival exceeding 90% at 5 years) is cardiovascular disease, not cancer recurrence. The post-nephrectomy CKD is an independent cardiovascular risk factor: eGFR below 45 mL/min is associated with approximately 2-fold increased risk of major cardiovascular events (MI, stroke, heart failure). GPs who monitor eGFR annually, treat hypertension to target, and address proteinuria in post-nephrectomy patients are directly preventing the leading cause of death in this population — which is more impactful, from a population health perspective, than monitoring for cancer recurrence.
