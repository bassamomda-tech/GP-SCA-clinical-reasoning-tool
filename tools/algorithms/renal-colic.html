Safety

Step 1 — Screen for Emergencies: Infected Obstructed Kidney & Mimics

Infected obstruction (stone + sepsis) is a urological emergency with high mortality. Always exclude before managing conservatively.

Suspected infected obstructed system Renal colic or obstruction with systemic illness, fever, rigors, hypotension, tachycardia, confusion, oliguria/anuria or AKI (fever may be absent) → Immediate emergency hospital assessment — IV antibiotics per local sepsis policy, urgent imaging and urological decompression; do not manage conservatively
Septic shock features HR >100, BP <90 systolic, altered consciousness, rigors → 999 Emergency — sepsis 6 protocol + urology
Bilateral obstruction / solitary kidney Suspected obstruction in a solitary functioning kidney, bilateral obstruction, anuria, AKI or deteriorating renal function → Same-day urgent urology — emergency transfer if unstable, septic, anuric or rapidly deteriorating
Uncontrolled pain Severe pain not responding to home analgesia → Same-day A&E — IV analgesia, imaging required
Pregnancy + renal colic Severe or persistent pain, obstruction, infection or renal impairment → Same-day obstetrics/urology discussion — ultrasound not CT (non-gadolinium MRI by specialists if inconclusive); no routine NSAIDs; tamsulosin only on specialist advice. Not automatically 999 if stable
AAA mimic Sudden severe abdominal/back/flank pain with collapse or syncope, pulsatile abdominal mass, known aneurysm or vascular disease, or haemodynamic instability → 999 Emergency — exclude aortic aneurysm. Older patients with vascular risk factors: low threshold for urgent assessment
Haematuria + clot retention Unable to pass urine + haematuria → Same-day urology — obstruction from clot
Immunocompromised / diabetes Renal colic + fever or systemic illness + DM or immunosuppression → Same-day hospital assessment (lower threshold; emphysematous pyelonephritis risk). DM/immunosuppression alone in a well patient without infection or obstruction does not mandate emergency urology
Infected obstructed kidney (pyonephrosis) carries a high mortality if not drained emergently — it does not respond to antibiotics alone because the infected urine cannot drain. Temperature alone can be misleading — some patients remain apyrexial initially. AAA classically presents with loin/flank pain in older men and can mimic renal colic exactly — pulsatile mass on examination, or known AAA, requires immediate 999. Pregnant women are a special population: radiation risk means CT-KUB is avoided; ultrasound is used (MRI by specialists if inconclusive), with specialist input according to severity, infection, obstruction and renal function.
Diagnose

Step 2 — Confirm Diagnosis: Typical History & Differential Diagnosis

Renal colic has a highly characteristic presentation — confirm it and exclude dangerous mimics.

Classic presentation
Sudden severe loin-to-groin pain (10/10 intensity). Colicky (waxing/waning). Cannot find comfortable position (distinguishes from peritonitis). Nausea/vomiting. Radiation to ipsilateral testis/labia
Haematuria
Dipstick blood supports but does not confirm a stone; its absence does not exclude one. Send MSU if infection is suspected (pyuria, nitrites, fever, systemic illness or atypical presentation). Persistent or unexplained visible haematuria → investigate separately per NICE NG12
Previous episodes
History of stones increases pre-test probability significantly. Ask about stone passage, previous lithotripsy
Key differentials
AAA (pulsatile mass, vascular RF, older age). Ectopic pregnancy (LMP, urine pregnancy test). Ovarian pathology (women: USS needed). Pyelonephritis (fever predominant, no severe colicky pain). Appendicitis (localised RIF, peritonism)
Urine pregnancy test
Mandatory in all women of childbearing age before imaging. Ectopic must be excluded
Stone risk factors
Low fluid intake, hot climate, family history, diet (high oxalate/protein/salt), gout, IBD, recurrent UTIs, anatomical abnormalities, medications (indinavir, triamterene, topiramate)
The inability to find a comfortable position is a key differentiating feature of renal colic from peritonitis (where patients lie still). The classic pain radiation pattern reflects the ureteric anatomy — upper ureter pain radiates to ipsilateral testis/labia via the genitofemoral nerve. Urine pregnancy test is a mandatory first step in women — ectopic pregnancy can present identically to renal colic and delay in diagnosis is a recognised cause of maternal death (MBRRACE UK). Haematuria may be absent in confirmed stone cases — its absence should not cause abandonment of the diagnosis if the clinical picture fits.
Diagnose

Step 3 — Stone Classification: Size, Position & Composition

Stone characteristics determine likelihood of spontaneous passage, urgency, and long-term prevention strategy.

Size — spontaneous passage
Smaller distal ureteric stones are more likely to pass; passage is substantially less likely as size increases and for proximal stones. Also depends on composition, degree of obstruction, duration and anatomy — use the CT report and local urology advice rather than a single percentage table
Position
Distal ureter (near bladder): highest passage rate. Proximal ureter / renal pelvis: lower passage rate, longer time. Kidney: renal stones may be incidental
Calcium oxalate
Most common. Radio-opaque. Risk factors: hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia, low fluid. Increases with oxalate-rich diet
Uric acid
Radio-lucent on plain X-ray (visible on CT). Gout, high protein diet, dehydration, DM, metabolic syndrome. Responds to alkalinisation — specialist-initiated, with monitoring
Struvite
Infection stones (Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas). Staghorn calculi. Require treatment of underlying infection. Associated with recurrent UTIs
Cystine (rare)
Genetic (cystinuria). Present in childhood/young adults. Radio-opaque (faintly). Specialist-managed only (fluids, alkalinisation, chelating agents)
Stone size is the single most important predictor of spontaneous passage — this drives the management decision of conservative vs intervention. EAU 2024 guidelines (international) recommend medical expulsive therapy for distal ureteric stones over 5 mm; NICE NG118 advises considering it for distal ureteric stones under 10 mm. Stone composition, determined from CT Hounsfield units or stone analysis after passage, determines secondary prevention — uric acid stones are uniquely responsive to urinary alkalinisation, making the distinction clinically important. Struvite/infection stones will recur unless the underlying urease-producing organism is eradicated — antibiotics alone without stone removal will fail. Offer stone analysis whenever a stone is retrieved.
Diagnose

Step 4 — Targeted Examination

Examination is brief but critical — exclude emergencies and assess severity.

Vital signs
Temperature → fever + tachycardia = infected obstruction (emergency). BP + HR → haemodynamic compromise (AAA, sepsis). SpO₂ for systemic unwell
Abdominal inspection
Distension (ileus), guarding, rigidity → peritonitis / AAA. Patient unable to lie still = characteristic colic
Aorta palpation
Pulsatile expansile mass >3cm → AAA → 999 immediately. Do not delay for USS
Loin/flank
Costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness → pyelonephritis / hydronephrosis. Renal colic often has flank pain radiating anteriorly
Abdominal palpation
Deep palpation for tenderness → peritonism (appendicitis, diverticulitis). Renal colic: often minimal guarding. Ureteric stone: tenderness medial to McBurney's
Genitalia
Testicular exam in men (exclude torsion). Hernia orifices. Radiation of pain to scrotum/labia
Urine dipstick
Haematuria supports (does not confirm) a stone. Nitrites/leucocytes → infection. Glucose → DM
Pregnancy test
Women of childbearing age — urine βhCG before any imaging decision
The examination in renal colic is as much about excluding dangerous mimics as confirming the diagnosis. The patient who cannot keep still is a positive sign — patients with peritoneal inflammation lie motionless. Palpating for an aortic aneurysm takes 10 seconds and can be lifesaving — AAA is an uncommon but important mimic — older patients with vascular risk factors warrant a low threshold for urgent assessment. CVA tenderness with fever raises the possibility of obstructed infected system even with normal BP/HR — these patients can deteriorate rapidly.

Step 5 — Investigations

Urine dipstick + MSU 1st line
Haematuria supports but does not confirm a stone. Nitrites/WBC → infection (changes urgency). MSU culture if infection suspected (pyuria, nitrites, fever, systemic illness, atypical presentation). Pregnancy test if female
Low-dose non-contrast CT Preferred · within 24h
Preferred test for adults with suspected renal colic, within 24 hours unless contraindicated or clinically inappropriate (NICE NG118). Identifies stone size, position, degree of obstruction. NOT in pregnancy. Does not necessarily require admission — stable patients with controlled pain, no sepsis, no AKI, able to drink and with reliable follow-up may use an urgent outpatient pathway if CT is available within 24 hours
Urine USS Alternative
First-line in pregnancy and children. Shows hydronephrosis (indirect obstruction marker). Lower sensitivity than CT for stones. Does not show ureter well. Not sufficient if CT-KUB indicated
Plain KUB X-ray
Detects radio-opaque stones (calcium, struvite, cystine). Cannot detect uric acid stones. Limited sensitivity — not recommended as sole investigation (EAU 2024, international). May have a role in follow-up of known radio-opaque stones per urology/radiology advice
U&Es + eGFR All patients
Check renal function — AKI? Single kidney? Baseline. Uric acid level if uric acid stone suspected. Serum calcium in all adults with renal/ureteric stones (NICE QS195)
FBC, CRP If infection suspected
WBC elevated → infection. Raised CRP supports infection. Blood cultures if septic
Stone analysis ± metabolic tests
Offer stone analysis when a stone is retrieved (especially if recurrent, unusual or high risk). Serum urate, bicarbonate, phosphate, PTH and 24h urine only for recurrent, bilateral, childhood, familial, staghorn, cystine, uric-acid or otherwise high-risk disease — via urology/renal stone services. Not mandatory for every first-time stone former
NOT needed acutely
Contrast CT, IVU, retrograde pyelography — secondary care. Do not delay emergency referral for additional imaging
Low-dose non-contrast CT is the preferred investigation, with high sensitivity and specificity, and can identify AAA and other pathologies — NICE NG118 advises it within 24 hours for non-pregnant adults. However, it involves ionising radiation — important for young recurrent stone formers who will have multiple scans over a lifetime. NICE NG118 recommends ultrasound as first-line imaging for children and pregnant women. In recurrent or high-risk stone formers, specialist metabolic assessment (including 24-hour urine) identifies correctable abnormalities (hypercalciuria, hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia) that allow targeted prevention.
Refer

Step 6 — Referral Criteria

999 Emergency
Suspected infected obstruction or sepsis. Haemodynamic instability. AAA suspected. Bilateral or solitary-kidney obstruction / anuria if unstable, septic or rapidly deteriorating
Same-day A&E
Uncontrolled pain at home (IV paracetamol ± opioid). Stable obstruction in a solitary kidney, bilateral obstruction or AKI (urgent urology). Renal colic + rigor without fever (early sepsis). Persistent vomiting / unable to tolerate oral fluids. Pregnancy with significant pain, obstruction, infection or renal impairment (obstetrics/urology)
Urgent urology (within days)
Ongoing intolerable pain or stone unlikely to pass (NICE NG118: surgical treatment within 48 hours). Stone not passed at planned reassessment. Worsening renal function. Failed medical expulsive therapy. Recurrent episodes same stone
Routine urology
Recurrent or high-risk stone disease for metabolic prevention: recurrent, bilateral/multiple, young-onset, family history, cystine/uric-acid/struvite/calcium-phosphate stones, solitary kidney, CKD, or stones needing intervention. Staghorn calculus. Cystinuria / uric acid stones requiring specialist management
Primary care manage
Uncomplicated first or recurrent episode. Small distal ureteric stone. Pain controlled. Able to drink. No AKI, anuria, bilateral or solitary-kidney obstruction. No fever/systemic illness. Imaging and follow-up arranged. Advise stone passage confirmation
The referral decision in renal colic is largely driven by whether there is obstruction + infection (the only true emergency) or whether the stone is likely to pass spontaneously (conservative management safe). Small distal stones usually pass without intervention — aggressive referral is not warranted. Larger and proximal stones are less likely to pass and need urology intervention planning (ESWL, ureteroscopy, or PCNL). Recurrent or high-risk stone formers benefit from systematic metabolic workup and prevention strategies, which are best coordinated through urology or specialist stone clinics.
Treat

Step 7 — Analgesia, Medical Expulsive Therapy & Secondary Prevention

Acute analgesia is the primary GP role. NICE NG118 advises considering an alpha-blocker (off-label) for distal ureteric stones <10 mm. No routine antispasmodics.

Analgesia — 1st line
NSAID by any route (e.g. diclofenac) NSAID
Lowest effective dose for the shortest duration; check dose against the product and local formulary (e.g. diclofenac max 150mg/day). More effective than opioids for renal colic. Avoid in significant renal impairment or AKI, dehydration or significant vomiting unless renal function assessed, active peptic ulcer or significant GI bleeding, heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, peripheral arterial disease, cerebrovascular disease (MHRA Drug Safety Update, June 2013), uncontrolled hypertension and pregnancy; consider age, anticoagulants and antiplatelets. Not routinely in a solitary kidney without checking renal function
Analgesia — 2nd line
IV paracetamol, then opioid
If NSAID contraindicated or inadequate: urgent-care/hospital assessment for IV paracetamol; opioid only if both are contraindicated or inadequate (NICE NG118). Oral paracetamol + codeine is not equivalent and is not the routine step in a severe acute presentation. Avoid NSAIDs in AKI/solitary kidney
Medical expulsive therapy
Tamsulosin 400mcg OD Off-label
Consider for a confirmed distal ureteric stone <10 mm where conservative management is appropriate, particularly when passage is less likely because of size (NICE NG118). Discuss uncertain benefit, postural hypotension and ejaculatory effects. Avoid with infection, obstruction with renal impairment, hypotension or another indication for urgent intervention. Duration per local urology pathway; must not delay intervention for uncontrolled pain, deteriorating renal function, infection or a stone unlikely to pass
Uric acid stonesSpecialist stone pathway: confirm composition or strong biochemical suspicion before dissolution therapy. Potassium citrate (caution in CKD, hyperkalaemia and interactions; over-alkalinisation may promote calcium phosphate stones) and allopurinol (depends on recurrence, persistent hyperuricosuria, gout and response) are specialist-initiated, with monitoring of urine pH, renal function and electrolytes
Calcium stones (1st line prevention)Adequate fluid intake after the acute episode: aim for ~2–2.5L urine/day. Maintain normal dietary calcium (with meals) — do NOT restrict. Reduce salt and avoid excessive animal protein. Tailor diet to stone composition and metabolic results, ideally via a stone-prevention service or renal dietitian
Calcium stones (2nd line)Specialist-directed after metabolic assessment: thiazide diuretics if hypercalciuria confirmed: Bendroflumethiazide 2.5mg OD. Potassium citrate if hypocitraturia. Allopurinol if hyperuricosuria with calcium oxalate stones
Anti-emeticsMetoclopramide 10mg PO/IM — short-term only (max 5 days; neurological adverse effects); avoid if obstruction/perforation suspected; caution in renal impairment. Or ondansetron 4–8mg — QT prolongation risk; check electrolytes and interacting medicines. If unable to retain fluids, arrange assessment rather than relying on antiemetics
NSAIDs are first-line analgesia for renal colic (NICE NG118) — multiple RCTs and meta-analyses confirm NSAIDs are superior to opioids for pain control and have fewer side effects. The mechanism is prostaglandin inhibition reducing ureteric spasm and renal pelvis pressure. Medical expulsive therapy (MET) with tamsulosin is supported by NICE NG118 (2019; distal ureteric stones <10 mm) and EAU 2024 (international) for distal ureteric stones — it relaxes the ureteric smooth muscle via alpha-1 blockade, increasing passage rate and reducing time to passage. The evidence for stones >10mm is weaker. Importantly, dietary calcium restriction for calcium stone formers is CONTRAINDICATED — it paradoxically increases urinary oxalate and worsens stone formation. This is a common misconception that must be explicitly corrected.
Lifestyle

Step 8 — Secondary Prevention: Lifestyle Modifications

Fluid intake After the acute episode: enough fluid to produce ~2–2.5L urine/day, adjusted for climate, activity, renal function and heart failure. Plain water best. Pale yellow urine target. During acute colic: normal hydration only — do not force large volumes
Dietary calcium — DO NOT RESTRICT Normal calcium intake, taken with meals. Low calcium diet paradoxically INCREASES oxalate absorption → more stones. Dairy is NOT the enemy
Dietary oxalate Tailor to stone composition — no blanket avoidance. If calcium oxalate stones: moderate very oxalate-rich foods (spinach, rhubarb, nuts, chocolate, beetroot) and take calcium with meals
Reduce salt intake High dietary sodium increases urinary calcium. Target <6g/day salt. Reduces calciuria → fewer calcium stones
Animal protein Avoid excessive red meat, fish and poultry (no fixed limit — may be inappropriate in older, frail or malnourished patients). Reduces urinary uric acid, calcium, oxalate and increases pH. Important for uric acid and calcium stone prevention
Urine pH monitoring For uric acid stones on specialist alkalinisation — monitor urine pH as directed by the stone service. Over-alkalinisation may promote calcium phosphate stones
Weight management Obesity increases stone risk (metabolic syndrome → hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia). BMI reduction targets reduce recurrence. Refer to NHS weight management programme
Stone passage confirmation Strain urine during acute episode (coffee filter or urine strainer). Collect stone and send for composition analysis — guides prevention strategy
Fluid intake is the single most impactful secondary prevention intervention — reducing recurrence in RCTs. After the acute episode, advise a urine output of around 2–2.5L/day, adjusted for climate, activity, renal function and heart failure. The calcium restriction myth is clinically important to correct actively — patients often self-restrict dairy based on a logical but incorrect assumption, which worsens their stone disease. The dietary calcium binds oxalate in the gut, reducing intestinal oxalate absorption. Straining urine to collect stones for composition analysis is free, non-invasive, and provides actionable information that guides targeted prevention — it should be recommended to every stone patient.
Safety

Step 9 — Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

48–72h review
Pain controlled? Able to tolerate oral fluids? MSU culture result back? If worsening → same-day A&E. Check U&Es if not done at first presentation
4-week review
Has stone passed? Confirm with patient (stone in strainer / UTI ruled out). If not passed, pain persists, renal function worsens or the stone is unlikely to pass → refer urology. Review tamsulosin — stop if stone passed
6-week review (post-A&E)
U&Es, BP. Imaging result reviewed. Urology follow-up arranged if stone not passed or intervention required
3–6 months (recurrent)
Recurrent/high-risk stone formers: metabolic assessment and prevention via urology/renal stone service; GP supports monitoring as directed. Diet diary review
Safety-net 999
Fever + loin pain returning (infected obstruction). Collapse/hypotension. Anuria. Unable to pass urine at all
Safety-net same-day
Vomiting preventing oral analgesia/fluids. Severe uncontrolled pain. Haematuria with clots. Fever without loin pain (early sepsis)
Haematuria follow-up
Macroscopic haematuria that persists after stone passage → MSU, then suspected cancer pathway referral if aged 45 or over with unexplained visible haematuria (NICE NG12, updated April 2026); otherwise urology (exclude bladder/renal cancer)
Annual review
Recurrent stone formers: U&Es, serum calcium, uric acid. Monitor eGFR — chronic stone disease can cause CKD. USS/imaging for silent stones per urology guidance
Stone recurrence is common without prevention — follow-up to implement prevention measures is as important as acute management. Persistent macroscopic haematuria after stone passage should not be automatically attributed to the stone — urothelial malignancy must be excluded, particularly in older patients and smokers (NICE NG12 haematuria pathway). Long-term stone disease can cause progressive CKD — eGFR monitoring detects this early. The 48–72h safety-net call is particularly important because infected obstruction can develop after initial presentation appears benign — a patient who was afebrile at 9am can be septic by evening.
