1
Safety

Red Flags — Systemic Disease, Dysmorphic Features & Malignancy

Most short stature is familial or constitutional delay. Act urgently when short stature is associated with dysmorphic features, systemic disease, or rapidly decelerating growth velocity.

Short stature + declining growth velocity (crossing centile lines downward on growth chart) Pathological growth failure — systemic disease (coeliac, IBD, renal failure, cardiac disease, chronic infection), endocrine cause (growth hormone deficiency, hypothyroidism, Cushing's syndrome), or malignancy. Paediatric referral — urgent if neurological symptoms, suspected intracranial/pituitary tumour, severe systemic disease or suspected malignancy. Growth velocity is more important than height alone.
Short stature + delayed puberty + anosmia Kallmann syndrome — hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism with olfactory bulb hypoplasia. Paediatric endocrinology referral (imaging arranged by secondary care). GnRH deficiency → sex hormone deficiency → delayed puberty + infertility.
Short stature + learning disability + cardiac defects + Down syndrome features Chromosomal disorder (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Noonan syndrome). Karyotype or genomic testing via paediatric endocrinology/genetics. Turner syndrome (45,X or mosaic — may have few classic features) in girls: short stature + primary amenorrhoea + broad chest + webbed neck + aortic coarctation — often diagnosed late.
Short stature + headache + visual symptoms + polyuria/polydipsia in a child Craniopharyngioma — suprasellar tumour causing GH deficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes insipidus, and optic nerve compression. Urgent paediatric/endocrine assessment — MRI arranged by secondary care (not a GP-initiated pathway unless explicitly available locally).
Disproportionate short stature (short limbs relative to trunk) + macrocephaly Skeletal dysplasia (achondroplasia, hypochondroplasia — FGFR3 mutations). Paediatric/genetics referral — skeletal imaging arranged by the specialist.
Short stature + hepatomegaly + splenomegaly + abnormal LFTs Metabolic/storage disorder (glycogen storage diseases, Gaucher, mucopolysaccharidoses — MPS). Specialist inherited metabolic disease (IMD) referral for enzyme/genetic testing.
Short stature + weight gain + hypertension + proximal weakness / bruising / violaceous striae Possible Cushing syndrome (consider glucocorticoid exposure) → paediatric endocrinology referral. Cortisol testing is specialist-directed — not an automatic GP panel. Severe hypertension or significant endocrine disturbance → urgent referral.
Turner syndrome is one of the most commonly delayed diagnoses in the UK — the average age of diagnosis is 15 years in girls identified during routine medical encounters, compared to approximately 9 years for girls identified incidentally (e.g., through chromosomal analysis for another reason). Turner syndrome (45,X or mosaic variants) affects approximately 1 in 2,000 girls and presents with: short stature (main presenting complaint in 70%), primary amenorrhoea, webbed neck, wide carrying angle (cubitus valgus), broad chest with widely spaced nipples, and cardiovascular abnormalities (aortic coarctation — present in ~30% — and bicuspid aortic valve). The diagnosis is frequently delayed because: (1) girls with Turner's may have relatively normal height in childhood (short stature may only become apparent in adolescence when the pubertal growth spurt fails to occur); (2) the distinctive physical features are subtle and not reliably identified without systematic examination; (3) physicians may wait for amenorrhoea before investigating, by which time the girl is in her mid-teens. Any girl who is consistently below the 2nd centile for height without a clear familial explanation, or who has any two of the characteristic features, should be referred for karyotype or appropriate genomic testing via paediatric endocrinology/genetics — mosaicism may give few classic features. Growth hormone therapy can improve adult height in Turner syndrome, but timing is a specialist decision based on age at diagnosis, height, growth velocity, ovarian function, bone age and psychosocial factors; not all girls with Turner syndrome require somatropin (NICE TA188).

Classification — Causes of Short Stature

Normal variants (most common — 80%)
Familial short stature (FSS): short parents → short child. Target range: mid-parental height (MPH) ±8.5 cm (locally accepted method — depends on growth chart): MPH girls = [(father's height − 13cm) + mother's height] ÷ 2; MPH boys = [(mother's height + 13cm) + father's height] ÷ 2. Growth velocity and puberty timing are normal. Do not label FSS solely because the parents are short — the child should also have an appropriate growth velocity and no red flags. No treatment needed — reassure. Constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP): delayed bone age + delayed puberty + normal growth velocity once tracking. Family history (father reaching puberty late — "late bloomer"). Eventually achieves normal adult height. Often the most difficult to distinguish from pathological causes.
Endocrine causes
Growth hormone deficiency (GHD): idiopathic or secondary to craniopharyngioma/pituitary pathology. Requires paediatric endocrinology assessment — diagnosed using auxology, growth pattern, bone age, biochemistry and, where appropriate, stimulation testing (cut-offs vary by assay, test and laboratory; a single GH level is not diagnostic). Do not request or interpret GH stimulation tests in primary care. IGF-1 may form part of specialist assessment but is influenced by nutrition, puberty, thyroid status and chronic disease. Specialist-initiated somatropin (NICE TA188). Hypothyroidism: short stature with delayed bone age, weight gain, constipation, cold intolerance, delayed relaxation of tendon reflexes. Confirm with TSH + free T4 when clinically indicated — treatable with levothyroxine. Cushing's syndrome: short stature + weight gain + hypertension + proximal weakness, bruising or violaceous striae → paediatric endocrinology referral. Testing (e.g. urinary cortisol, dexamethasone suppression) is specialist-directed according to age, medication exposure and pre-test probability — not an automatic GP panel.
Chronic systemic disease
Coeliac disease: most common systemic cause of short stature in the UK — may present without GI symptoms. Anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (IgA deficiency → false negative) while eating gluten. Positive → paediatric gastroenterology; do not start a gluten-free diet before specialist assessment. Growth may improve after confirmed coeliac disease is treated with a strict gluten-free diet, but response depends on age, severity, adherence, nutritional status and other disease. IBD (Crohn's — terminal ileum → malabsorption of zinc, calcium, vitamins). CKD (renal osteodystrophy + poor nutrition). Cystic fibrosis (malabsorption). Congenital heart disease.
Genetic / chromosomal
Turner syndrome (45,X or mosaic — may have few classic physical features) — girls: short stature + primary amenorrhoea + cardiac/renal abnormalities. Noonan syndrome (autosomal dominant — short stature + cardiac defects + similar features to Turner but in boys and girls, normal karyotype). Down syndrome (trisomy 21 — short stature is one of many features). Prader-Willi, Silver-Russell (undergrowth syndromes).
Skeletal dysplasias
Achondroplasia (FGFR3 — autosomal dominant): most common skeletal dysplasia. Disproportionate short stature (short limbs, macrocephaly, trident hands). Spinal stenosis risk. Vosoritide (C-natriuretic peptide analogue) now approved for children with achondroplasia.
Coeliac disease is the most common systemic cause of unexplained short stature in UK children — it is estimated that approximately 30–40% of coeliac children present without gastrointestinal symptoms (silent coeliac disease), with short stature, iron-deficiency anaemia, or fatigue as the sole presenting features. Anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA (anti-tTG IgA) combined with total serum IgA (to exclude IgA deficiency, which causes false-negative anti-tTG) is the recommended first-line screening test per NICE NG20. The test should be performed on a normal gluten-containing diet (the antibody disappears within weeks of starting a gluten-free diet, making the test falsely negative if gluten has been restricted). GPs encountering any child with unexplained short stature, growth faltering, or iron-deficiency anaemia should routinely screen for coeliac disease with anti-tTG IgA + total IgA. A positive test needs paediatric gastroenterology confirmation — duodenal biopsy, or a no-biopsy pathway for very high tTG levels (BSPGHAN/ESPGHAN criteria) — — a strongly positive result does not automatically mean biopsy is always required; paediatric gastroenterology applies the current ESPGHAN/NICE pathway. Do not start a gluten-free diet before specialist assessment. After diagnosis, growth may improve on a strict gluten-free diet, depending on age, severity, adherence, nutritional status and other disease.

Assessment — Growth Measurement & Investigation

Growth measurement (essential technique)
Standing height: calibrated wall-mounted stadiometer where possible (not tape measure) — shoes and bulky clothing off, feet together, heels against the stadiometer, head in the Frankfort plane, gentle upward traction on mastoid process. Obtain at least two measurements and record the better-quality result. Plot height, weight and BMI. Weight: without shoes/heavy clothing. BMI centile (not BMI alone in children). Sitting height + subischial leg length (proportionality — detects disproportionate short stature). Head circumference (infants).
Growth chart plotting (UK-WHO 0–4 years; UK90 4–18 years)
Plot all measurements accurately with correct calendar age. Height centile AND growth velocity (cm/year) — velocity is more informative than a single height measurement. Expected normal growth velocity: infants: 25 cm/year (age 0–1); toddlers: 12 cm/year (1–2); pre-school: 7–8 cm/year; school age: 5–6 cm/year; puberty: 8–10 cm/year peak velocity. A fall of more than about one centile space (BSPED) increases concern for pathology. Calculate growth velocity only from reliable measurements over an adequate interval — 3–6 months may be useful in an older child, but measurement error can be substantial, especially in younger children. Repeat a single short measurement with correct technique unless there are urgent red flags.
Mid-parental height (MPH) calculation
Girls: [(father's height − 13 cm) + mother's height] ÷ 2. Boys: [(mother's height + 13 cm) + father's height] ÷ 2. Target range approximately MPH ±8.5 cm (locally accepted method). Plot the child's height and predicted adult height in relation to the mid-parental height range. A substantial discrepancy between height and target height, or a downward crossing of height centiles, increases concern for pathology. Tracking within the range with normal growth velocity and no red flags → likely familial short stature.
Investigations (primary care, where indicated — targeted, not a universal panel)
FBC (anaemia — systemic disease) · ESR/CRP (chronic inflammation) · U&E + creatinine (CKD) · LFTs (liver, storage disorder) · TFTs (TSH + free T4) (hypothyroidism, considering clinical context) · Anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (coeliac — while eating gluten) · Urinalysis (renal) · HbA1c (only if diabetes suspected) · other targeted tests based on symptoms and examination. Via paediatric/endocrine/genetic pathways (unless a clearly agreed local arrangement): Bone age X-ray (supportive, not diagnostic) · IGF-1 · Karyotype / genomic testing (girls with unexplained short stature — Turner syndrome) · GH stimulation testing · specialist imaging.
Bone age X-ray (left hand and wrist radiograph assessed by a paediatric radiologist using the Greulich and Pyle atlas or TW2 method) is one of the most useful investigations in short stature assessment — it compares the child's skeletal maturation to their chronological age. The findings guide interpretation: (1) Bone age significantly delayed vs chronological age (e.g., 10-year-old child with bone age of 7–8 years) = consistent with constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP), but also seen with hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, chronic disease, malnutrition, glucocorticoid exposure and some genetic syndromes — in CDGP the child may still have significant growth potential and puberty will come late; (2) Bone age approximately equal to chronological age with short stature = the child's skeleton is aging at the expected rate but at a lower height — likely familial short stature, less growth potential remaining, or endocrine cause; (3) Advanced bone age (older than chronological age) in a short child = concerning for early puberty with premature epiphyseal fusion, or chronic glucocorticoid use. Bone age is supportive rather than diagnostic — interpret it with growth velocity, mid-parental height, puberty, examination and systemic features. It should generally be arranged through paediatrics/endocrinology when it will influence management, and is not usually required for every child with familial short stature.

Investigation result → interpretation
Bone age delayed + puberty delayed + family history + normal growth velocity + no red flags
Consistent with CDGP — but bone age is supportive, not diagnostic: delayed bone age also occurs with hypothyroidism, GH deficiency, chronic disease, malnutrition, glucocorticoid exposure and some genetic syndromes. Interpret with growth velocity, MPH, puberty and examination, with specialist input.
Bone age normal/advanced + well below MPH range
Familial short stature or pathological cause — investigate further.
TSH elevated + FT4 low
Acquired hypothyroidism — levothyroxine at an age- and weight-dependent dose per local paediatric formulary, with paediatric input. Growth may improve once euthyroid.
Anti-tTG IgA positive
Possible coeliac disease — paediatric gastroenterology referral (biopsy or no-biopsy pathway per current ESPGHAN/NICE). Do not start a gluten-free diet before specialist assessment.
Karyotype: 45,X or mosaic
Turner syndrome — specialist paediatric endocrine/genetics referral with coordinated cardiac, renal, hearing, thyroid, metabolic and reproductive surveillance; GH only where the specialist indicates.
Suspected GH deficiency
Paediatric endocrinology assessment — stimulation testing arranged and interpreted by specialists (cut-offs are assay- and test-specific); somatropin specialist-initiated and monitored (NICE TA188).
Disproportionate short limbs + macrocephaly + X-ray findings
Skeletal dysplasia — genetics + specialist orthopaedics.
Turner syndrome karyotyping in all girls with unexplained short stature is standard practice — the karyotype (chromosomal analysis from a blood sample) takes approximately 2–3 weeks and definitively identifies 45,X or mosaic Turner syndrome. Karyotype or appropriate genomic testing should be arranged through paediatric endocrinology/genetics, or a clearly agreed local arrangement; Turner syndrome can occur with mosaicism and few classic physical features. The clinical implication of the delayed Turner diagnosis is significant: growth hormone therapy (licensed for Turner syndrome in girls — NICE TA188) is specialist-initiated and monitored, and its timing depends on age at diagnosis, height and growth velocity, ovarian function, bone age and psychosocial factors; not all girls with Turner syndrome require somatropin. Confirmed or suspected Turner syndrome requires specialist paediatric endocrine/genetics referral and coordinated cardiac, renal, hearing, thyroid, metabolic and reproductive surveillance.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Urgent / same-week
Short stature with headache, visual symptoms and polyuria/polydipsia (suspected craniopharyngioma — MRI via secondary care) · Rapidly falling growth velocity with neurological symptoms · Suspected intracranial or pituitary tumour · Severe systemic disease or suspected malignancy · Severe hypertension or significant endocrine disturbance
Paediatric endocrinology (within 4 weeks)
Suspected or confirmed Turner syndrome · GH deficiency suspected (reduced growth velocity, growth pattern) · Delayed puberty · Hypothyroidism with significant growth failure · Cushing's suspected · Precocious puberty (early puberty with advanced bone age causing premature epiphyseal fusion)
Paediatric / general referral
Height ≤0.4th centile at any age · Height <2nd centile from age 3 years with reduced growth velocity, substantial discrepancy from MPH or other red flags · Fall of more than about one centile space or reduced/declining growth velocity · Height substantially below MPH range · At any height: dysmorphism, disproportion, systemic illness, delayed or precocious puberty (BSPED 2022) · First presentation of chronic disease (coeliac, IBD, CKD) causing growth failure
Genetics
Suspected chromosomal disorder (dysmorphic features + short stature) · Skeletal dysplasia
GP management
Familial short stature (within MPH range, normal growth velocity, no red flags, normal targeted investigations): reassure. CDGP (delayed bone age, family history, normal investigations, normal growth velocity): reassure + annual growth monitoring. Hypothyroidism identified: discuss with paediatrics; treat per local paediatric formulary.
NICE TA188 recommends somatropin for growth failure in children with GH deficiency; the diagnostic work-up (auxology, growth pattern, bone age, biochemistry and, where appropriate, stimulation testing with assay-specific cut-offs) is set by paediatric endocrinology rather than by TA188 itself; somatropin should be initiated and monitored by a paediatrician with specialist expertise in growth disorders. TA188 also covers (without GH deficiency): Turner syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome, SHOX gene deficiency, chronic renal insufficiency, and persistent growth failure after being born small for gestational age. GH is not automatically indicated for height below a specific centile alone, familial short stature, constitutional delay, or Noonan syndrome unless the relevant licence/specialist criteria are met. GP role in GH therapy: prescribing under an agreed shared-care protocol (use the current local protocol, e.g. Greater Manchester, rather than this general guide) — dose changes remain specialist-led and monitoring arrangements must be explicit; injection site rotation advice (lipoatrophy prevention); and recognising side effects for urgent specialist contact (benign intracranial hypertension — papilloedema, headache — and hip pain suggesting slipped capital femoral epiphysis — SCFE, which is more common with GH therapy).

Treat

Treatment by Cause

Hypothyroidism
Levothyroxine
Dose by age and weight per BNFC (the 10–15 micrograms/kg/day regimen is for neonatal congenital hypothyroidism, not acquired hypothyroidism in older children). Follow the local paediatric formulary. Repeat TFT timing depends on severity and treatment initiation. Congenital hypothyroidism detected by newborn screening is managed through the specialist pathway.
Coeliac disease
Strict gluten-free diet (after confirmed diagnosis)
Refer paediatric gastroenterology — diagnosis per current ESPGHAN/NICE pathway (a strongly positive result does not automatically mean biopsy). Do not start a gluten-free diet before specialist assessment. Growth may improve on a strict GFD, depending on age, severity, adherence, nutritional status and other disease. Anti-tTG monitoring per the paediatric gastroenterology/shared-care plan. Dietitian referral.
GH deficiency
GH injections (somatropin)
Daily SC injection. Initiated and monitored by a paediatrician with specialist expertise in growth disorders (NICE TA188). GP may prescribe under an agreed shared-care protocol (current local protocol); dose changes, monitoring and stopping decisions remain specialist-led. Do not stop or adjust in primary care unless there is an urgent suspected adverse effect and the specialist team has been contacted.
Turner syndrome
Specialist-led GH ± oestrogen
Timing of GH and puberty induction is individualised by the specialist team (age at diagnosis, height and growth velocity, ovarian function, bone age, psychosocial factors); not all girls require somatropin (NICE TA188). Cardiac, renal, hearing, thyroid, metabolic, bone and reproductive surveillance per current Turner syndrome specialist guidance and the individual plan.
CDGP — treatment options
Watchful waiting (preferred)
In most cases: reassure + annual growth monitoring. If severe psychological distress: short course low-dose testosterone (boys) or low-dose oestrogen (girls) can advance puberty without significantly compromising final adult height — specialist decision.
The psychological impact of constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP) in adolescent boys is frequently underestimated — a 14-year-old boy who is significantly shorter than all his peers and has not yet begun puberty experiences profound social difficulties: exclusion from sport teams, bullying, difficulties with romantic relationships, and severe self-esteem damage. Parents (particularly fathers who experienced CDGP themselves) are often dismissive: 'I was the same at his age and I turned out fine.' While CDGP has an excellent ultimate prognosis (normal adult height and fertility), the psychological harm during the years of delayed development is real. In current paediatric endocrine practice, treatment to advance puberty (testosterone esters in boys — Sustanon 50–100 mg IM monthly × 3–6 months) is appropriate when severe psychological distress is present, particularly when bone age is sufficiently delayed that a short course will not compromise final adult height. This treatment decision should be made in consultation with paediatric endocrinology, but GPs should raise the option with families rather than waiting passively.

Treat

GH Therapy — Specialist Monitoring & Transition

Transition to adult care
Transition from paediatric to adult endocrinology should be coordinated by the specialist team. Retesting for persistent GH deficiency is specialist-led, as not all childhood GH deficiency persists into adulthood. NICE TA64 relates to adult severe GH deficiency and is not a primary-care diagnostic pathway.
Monitoring GH therapy (all ages)
Children receiving somatropin require specialist endocrine monitoring, including growth velocity, height, weight, pubertal development, IGF-1 and adverse effects according to the specialist protocol. Possible complications: benign intracranial hypertension, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, progression of scoliosis, oedema, glucose intolerance. Urgent specialist review for severe headache, visual symptoms, limp or hip/knee pain — urgently contact the endocrine team and arrange appropriate assessment; do not stop treatment without specialist communication.
Height outcomes with treatment
Without treatment: GH deficiency → approximately −3 to −4 SD final height. With appropriate GH treatment: typically −1 to −1.5 SD (close to MPH range). Turner syndrome without treatment: approximately 20 cm below population mean. With GH: approximately 8–10 cm gain. Coeliac with GFD: near-normal final height if treatment started before pubertal growth spurt.
The IGF-1 monitoring principle for GH therapy reflects the pharmacological mechanism — GH does not act directly on the growth plate but stimulates hepatic production of IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1), which then acts on chondrocytes to promote longitudinal bone growth. IGF-1 levels therefore reflect GH action at the tissue level and are the preferred biomarker for GH therapy monitoring. The target: age- and sex-matched IGF-1 in the mid-normal range (approximately 0 to +2 SDS). Supranormal IGF-1 (>+2 SDS) suggests over-treatment and should trigger dose reduction (long-term supranormal IGF-1 is associated with increased cancer risk — this is the principal long-term safety concern with GH therapy). Sub-normal IGF-1 suggests under-dosing or poor compliance. IGF-1, growth and adverse-effect monitoring (including glucose intolerance) follow the specialist protocol; any GP responsibilities must be stated explicitly in the shared-care agreement.

Lifestyle

Psychosocial Support, Nutrition & Height Optimisation

Nutritional adequacy for growth Assess dietary adequacy (energy, protein and micronutrients such as zinc and iron) — undernourished children grow poorly. Refer to a paediatric dietitian when undernutrition or malabsorption is suspected. Calcium from dairy, fortified plant milks and leafy greens. Vitamin D: follow current UK national guidance, which varies by age and risk group.
Sleep Adequate sleep is important for general health. Children need adequate sleep: ages 6–12: 9–12 hours; teens: 8–10 hours. Sleep hygiene: consistent sleep/wake schedule, no screens 1 hour before bed, dark and cool bedroom. Screen for sleep-disordered breathing where relevant — particularly with obesity, craniofacial disorders, Down syndrome or adenotonsillar symptoms (snoring, witnessed apnoeas).
Psychosocial support for short stature Short stature can significantly impact self-esteem, peer relationships, and academic performance (teachers unconsciously treat short children as younger). Anti-bullying strategies at school. Sport and physical activity (non-height-dependent activities: swimming, martial arts, gymnastics, cycling). Growth Hormone Research Society patient resources. Little People of America (LPA) for skeletal dysplasia. CHILD GROWTH FOUNDATION (childgrowthfoundation.org) — excellent UK resource.
Turner syndrome — long-term health Confirmed or suspected Turner syndrome requires specialist paediatric endocrine/genetics referral and coordinated cardiac, renal, hearing, thyroid, metabolic, bone and reproductive surveillance — intervals follow current Turner syndrome specialist guidance and the individual's surveillance plan, not fixed GP rules. Ophthalmology (strabismus, ptosis) as needed.
Exercise and growth Regular physical activity does not stunt growth — this is a myth. Weight-bearing exercise actually promotes bone density. Extreme endurance training in pre-pubertal children (long-distance running, intense gymnastics) can suppress GnRH/gonadotrophin axis → delayed puberty → reduced peak bone mass. Moderate sport participation is beneficial and encouraged.
Genetic counselling For chromosomal conditions, skeletal dysplasias, or familial conditions: genetic counselling for parents and, when appropriate, the young person. Achondroplasia: autosomal dominant (50% inheritance risk if parent affected; most cases are de novo mutations). Turner syndrome: not inherited. Noonan syndrome: autosomal dominant (50% risk). RASopathy conditions (Noonan, CFC, Costello) now have specific genetic testing available.
Dietary supplements to avoid Anabolic steroids (internet-purchased) used by teenagers or parents to accelerate height are dangerous — cause premature epiphyseal fusion, permanently reducing final adult height, plus cardiovascular and psychiatric side effects. Growth-boosting products (amino acid supplements, homeopathic "height pills") have no evidence of benefit. Advise against all non-prescribed growth supplements.
The Child Growth Foundation (CGF) is a UK charity that provides excellent patient information on all aspects of growth disorders — their website (childgrowthfoundation.org) has condition-specific information sheets for parents and young people covering Turner syndrome, GH deficiency, CDGP, and skeletal dysplasias, written in accessible language. GPs should routinely signpost families dealing with growth concerns to the CGF as a trusted resource. The Turner Syndrome Support Society (tsss.org.uk) is a condition-specific charity providing peer support, information on current management standards, and advocacy for appropriate investigation and treatment timelines. GPs who identify Turner syndrome should provide the family with these resources at the same consultation as the diagnosis discussion.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

All short children — growth monitoring
Plot height + weight every 6 months (or 3-monthly if recently started treatment). Same calibrated stadiometer and consistent technique; plot height, weight and BMI. Document growth velocity (cm/year) — the most important parameter. Growth chart in medical record (printout or electronic).
On GH therapy
Specialist endocrine monitoring per protocol (growth velocity, height, weight, puberty, IGF-1, adverse effects). Severe headache or visual symptoms → urgent specialist review (benign intracranial hypertension). Limp or hip/knee pain → urgent specialist review and assessment (SCFE). Contact the endocrine team urgently — do not stop GH without specialist communication.
Coeliac on GFD
Monitoring (anti-tTG, nutritional bloods, growth, dietitian review) per the paediatric gastroenterology/shared-care plan rather than automatically by the GP. Plot height and weight at reviews.
Turner syndrome follow-up
Follow current Turner syndrome specialist guidance and the individual surveillance plan (cardiac, renal, hearing, thyroid, metabolic, bone and reproductive) — not fixed GP intervals. Support HRT adherence.
Urgent referral
Severe headache, visual symptoms or papilloedema on GH → urgent endocrine team contact + same-day assessment · Limp or new hip/knee pain on GH → urgent endocrine contact + same-day orthopaedic assessment (SCFE) · Polyuria/polydipsia + headache + visual disturbance in short child → urgent paediatric/endocrine assessment (MRI via secondary care)
Within 4 weeks
Height falling more than about one centile space or reduced growth velocity without explanation → paediatric referral · Positive anti-tTG in short child → paediatric gastroenterology (continue gluten until assessed) · Suspected or confirmed Turner syndrome → paediatric endocrinology/genetics
Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) is a specific complication of GH therapy that GPs in shared care must know — GH therapy increases growth rate, which temporarily increases the shear stress on the growth plate of the femoral head. In susceptible children (particularly obese, or those with GH deficiency who are growing rapidly with treatment), the femoral head can slip off the femoral neck at the growth plate. Clinical presentation: limp + hip pain (sometimes referred to the knee) in a child on GH therapy. The risk period is during the first 6–12 months of GH therapy when growth rate is fastest. Action: same-day orthopaedic assessment (urgent surgical pinning prevents further displacement and avascular necrosis) and urgent contact with the endocrine team, who direct any change to GH treatment. GPs managing children on GH therapy should document this safety net explicitly at each review and ensure the family knows to report any new hip or knee pain immediately.
