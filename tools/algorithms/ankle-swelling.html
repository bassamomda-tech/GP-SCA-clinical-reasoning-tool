1
Safety

Red Flags — Rule Out Life-Threatening Causes First

Act immediately if any red flag present — do not proceed to routine work-up.

Acute unilateral swelling + calf pain/warmth Tender, hot, asymmetric leg, recent travel/surgery/immobility → Same-day Wells score + D-dimer / proximal leg vein USS (NICE NG158)
Bilateral oedema + breathlessness PND, orthopnoea, raised JVP, bibasal crackles → Same-day Suspected heart failure → emergency assessment
Sudden severe leg swelling + collapse Haemodynamic instability, pale/mottled limb → 999 Phlegmasia cerulea dolens / ileofemoral DVT
Facial + ankle oedema + haematuria/proteinuria Periorbital puffiness, frothy urine → Same-day Nephrotic syndrome
Jaundice + ankle oedema + abdominal distension Stigmata of liver disease, ascites → Same-day Hepatic failure / decompensated cirrhosis
Oedema + severe hypertension in pregnancy BP ≥160/110, severe headache, visual disturbance, epigastric/RUQ pain, vomiting, reduced fetal movements → Immediate obstetric Pre-eclampsia / HELLP — 999 if seizure, collapse or unstable
Unexplained unilateral swelling or lymphadenopathy Examine abdomen, pelvis, groin and nodes → 2WW Consider suspected-cancer referral for unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, especially with fever, night sweats, breathlessness, pruritus or weight loss (NICE NG12); otherwise image or refer by findings — do not label it malignant lymphoedema without evidence
Swelling + fever + erythema tracking Spreading cellulitis, lymphangitis, systemic sepsis → Same-day Admit if septic / diabetic

Missing a DVT in unilateral leg swelling risks fatal pulmonary embolism. New heart-failure presentations can deteriorate rapidly without diuresis. Unrecognised pre-eclampsia endangers mother and baby. Nephrotic syndrome requires rapid urine dipstick — proteinuria ++ or +++ with oedema mandates same-day review. Always check both legs simultaneously to detect asymmetry.

2
Diagnose

Characterise the Oedema — History & Pattern Recognition

Determine whether oedema is pitting/non-pitting, unilateral/bilateral, and acute/chronic. Pattern drives diagnosis.

Onset & duration
Acute (<72h): DVT, cellulitis, trauma. Subacute (days–weeks): cardiac, renal. Chronic: venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, medications
Bilateral vs unilateral
Bilateral: systemic cause (cardiac, renal, hepatic, medication, hypoalbuminaemia). Unilateral: local cause (DVT, cellulitis, lymphoedema, chronic venous insufficiency)
Pitting vs non-pitting
Pitting: cardiac, renal, hepatic, venous, medications. Non-pitting: lymphoedema, myxoedema (hypothyroidism), lipoedema
Associated symptoms
Breathlessness (HF/PE), haematuria/frothy urine (renal), jaundice (hepatic), joint pain (inflammatory), skin changes (venous/lymphatic)
Medication review
Amlodipine/CCBs Common, NSAIDs, steroids, gabapentin/pregabalin, thiazolidinediones, oestrogen/HRT, minoxidil — all cause bilateral pitting oedema
Social history
Immobility, obesity, prolonged sitting (air travel, desk work), pregnancy, malnutrition (low albumin)
Risk factors & red-flag symptoms
Pain, erythema, warmth, fever; breathlessness, orthopnoea, PND, exercise tolerance; previous DVT, cancer, recent surgery, travel, immobility, pregnancy; rate of progression
Distribution
Ankle only: venous/gravity. Extends to thigh: systemic. Abdominal oedema + ascites: hepatic/cardiac. Face + ankles: nephrotic/allergic

Amlodipine is the commonest cause of bilateral ankle oedema in primary care — always check the drug chart before extensive investigation. The pitting/non-pitting distinction is the single most useful examination finding: non-pitting oedema essentially rules out cardiac/renal/hepatic causes. Bilateral oedema with normal cardiac/renal/hepatic function in an ambulant patient is idiopathic oedema until proven otherwise.

3
Diagnose

Classify the Underlying Cause

Cardiac
Bilateral pitting, worse with exertion, orthopnoea, PND, raised JVP. HF with preserved or reduced EF. Check BNP/NT-proBNP
Venous insufficiency
Bilateral, worsens through day, improves overnight, varicosities, haemosiderin staining, lipodermatosclerosis. Commonest cause in elderly
DVT
Unilateral, acute, calf tenderness/warmth/erythema. Two-level Wells ≥2 = DVT likely. Requires formal exclusion
Renal
Periorbital morning oedema, marked proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia and oedema together (albumin <25 g/L supports but does not diagnose), frothy urine. Check urine ACR/PCR, albumin, creatinine
Hepatic
Ascites + ankle oedema, low albumin, stigmata of liver disease, alcohol history. Check LFTs, albumin, coagulation
Lymphoedema
Dorsum of foot involved, non-pitting in later disease, skin thickening/hyperkeratosis, recurrent cellulitis, Stemmer sign supports (not absolute); history of malignancy/surgery/radiotherapy. Lipoedema spares the feet, bilateral and tender
Medication-induced
Bilateral pitting, onset after starting new drug (especially amlodipine), no systemic disease. Consider drug switch
Hypothyroidism
Non-pitting, generalised myxoedema, cold intolerance, weight gain, bradycardia. Check TSH (reflex FT4) if suspected (NG145)

Classification drives the treatment pathway completely — a diuretic that helps cardiac oedema can worsen lipoedema and does nothing for lymphoedema. Venous insufficiency is the most common cause of bilateral ankle swelling in the community, especially in older adults. A positive Stemmer sign supports lymphoedema but does not establish it alone. Hypothyroidism is often missed because oedema is non-pitting and TFTs are not routinely ordered.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Vital signs
BP (hypertension → renal/cardiac), HR (AF → cardiac), RR (tachypnoea → HF/PE), temp (fever → cellulitis/DVT), SpO₂
Weight & BMI
Recent gain >2 kg → fluid retention. Obesity → venous hypertension and dependency oedema. Compare to previous weights
Jugular venous pressure
Raised JVP (>4 cm above sternal angle) → right heart failure, constrictive pericarditis, SVC obstruction
Heart sounds
S3 gallop, murmurs, displaced apex → cardiac cause. Check for pericardial friction rub
Chest auscultation
Bibasal crackles → pulmonary oedema (LVF). Pleural effusion → dullness to percussion
Abdominal exam
Hepatomegaly (HF/hepatic), ascites (shifting dullness → hepatic/cardiac), splenomegaly
Leg examination
Symmetry, pitting test (press 5 sec over tibia), skin changes (haemosiderin, liposclerosis, ulcers, varicosities), calf circumference if DVT suspected (calf tenderness counts only within the Wells criteria — do not use Homan's sign), pulses, capillary refill, temperature, sacral oedema if immobile
Stemmer's sign
Attempt to pinch dorsal foot skin: impossible = positive → lymphoedema. Most reliable lymphoedema sign

Raised JVP with ankle oedema is a useful bedside pointer to heart failure. Serial weights help HF management — an individualised action plan can include a weight-gain trigger. Homan's sign has poor sensitivity/specificity for DVT and should not be used diagnostically.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — Targeted, Not Blanket

All presentations
Observations, urinalysis (protein, blood) and medication review. Bloods guided by the picture: U&Es (systemic or renal disease, diuretics), albumin + LFTs (hepatic, nephrotic), FBC (anaemia, infection, malignancy), TSH (if thyroid features), urine ACR/PCR (proteinuria)
Suspected cardiac
NT-proBNP — <400 ng/L untreated: HF less likely but not excluded if suspicion remains (falsely low with obesity and on diuretics, ACEI/ARNI, beta-blockers, MRAs; raised by AF, renal, lung or liver disease, sepsis). 400–2000 ng/L: specialist assessment and echo within 6 weeks. >2000 ng/L: urgent referral, specialist assessment and echo within 2 weeks (NICE NG106). ECG, CXR
Suspected DVT
Two-level Wells (NICE NG158). Likely (≥2): proximal leg-vein USS within 4 h; if not possible, D-dimer then interim therapeutic anticoagulation and USS within 24 h; USS negative but D-dimer positive → stop interim anticoagulation, repeat USS at 6–8 days. Unlikely (≤1): quantitative D-dimer within 4 h (consider age-adjusted threshold over 50); negative → DVT unlikely; positive → USS within 4 h, or interim anticoagulation and USS within 24 h. Take FBC, renal and hepatic function, PT, APTT when starting interim anticoagulation — do not delay treatment for results; document bleeding risk, surgery, pregnancy, weight and interacting drugs
Suspected renal
Urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR), eGFR, serum albumin. Suspect nephrotic syndrome with marked proteinuria + hypoalbuminaemia + oedema; urgent renal assessment if severe hypoalbuminaemia, nephrotic-range proteinuria, AKI, haematuria, thrombosis or systemic illness
Suspected hepatic
LFTs, albumin, bilirubin, INR/PT (marker of synthetic function), USS abdomen (ascites, liver texture)
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT order extensive tests for bilateral ankle oedema if CCB started recently — trial of drug switch first. Do NOT order BNP if clear drug-induced cause identified

NT-proBNP is the gatekeeper test for heart failure, but a low value does not exclude valvular disease, ischaemia or HF when suspicion remains. D-dimer is sensitive but non-specific, so it is most useful when DVT is unlikely on Wells. Test thyroid function when the history or examination suggests it.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When Primary Care Is Not Enough

999
Haemodynamic instability, suspected massive PE, phlegmasia cerulea dolens, acute pulmonary oedema, severe hypoxaemia, collapse or shock, acute limb ischaemia
Same-day
Suspected DVT per the NG158 pathway, new symptomatic heart failure, suspected nephrotic syndrome (marked proteinuria + hypoalbuminaemia), decompensated liver disease, jaundice or new ascites
2WW · NICE NG12
Unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly (especially with fever, night sweats, breathlessness, pruritus or weight loss) → consider suspected-cancer referral. Unexplained unilateral swelling: examine abdomen, pelvis, groin and nodes — a mass follows its NG12 site pathway. DVT: assess for additional cancer symptoms/signs and investigate or refer if present (no routine extensive screening)
Cardiology / HF clinic
NT-proBNP >400: echo within 6 weeks. NT-proBNP >2000: urgent echo within 2 weeks (NICE NG106). Preserved EF heart failure → specialist confirmation
Nephrology
eGFR <30 or rapidly declining, nephrotic range proteinuria (ACR >300 mg/mmol), haematuria + proteinuria combination
Lymphoedema clinic
Confirmed or suspected lymphoedema → specialist compression and management. Diuretics do not treat lymphatic dysfunction — not routinely, unless a separate cardiac, renal or hepatic indication
Vascular surgery
Chronic venous insufficiency with non-healing venous leg ulcer, severe varicosities affecting quality of life, arterial disease found before compression (refer per local leg-ulcer pathway)
Primary care manage
CCB-induced oedema (switch drug), mild bilateral venous oedema with normal investigations, idiopathic oedema of pregnancy (physiological)

The BNP/NT-proBNP pathway (NICE NG106) was designed specifically to streamline HF referral from primary care — using it correctly avoids both under-referral (missed HF) and over-referral (normal BNP). Diuretics do not treat lymphoedema and are used only for a separate indication. Assess arterial supply before moderate or strong compression — unrecognised PAD with compression can cause limb ischaemia; ABPI can be falsely high in diabetes or calcified arteries, where toe pressures or specialist assessment help.

7
Treat

Treatment Pathway — Cause-Specific Management

Cardiac oedema (HF)
Loop diuretic First-line
Loop diuretic for congestion — starting dose by congestion, renal function, previous diuretic use, frailty and local HF protocol; lowest effective dose. Diuretics are not disease-modifying: optimise therapy via the HF pathway once phenotype is confirmed
DVT confirmed
DOAC anticoagulation Start same day
Rivaroxaban 15 mg BD for 21 days, then 20 mg OD (with food), or apixaban 10 mg BD for 7 days then 5 mg BD; at least 3 months. Before prescribing: Cockcroft–Gault CrCl (not eGFR), weight, FBC, renal and hepatic function, bleeding risk, pregnancy/breastfeeding, CYP3A4/P-gp interactions (GM DOAC guidance). Specialist advice for CrCl <15, active cancer, antiphospholipid syndrome (triple-positive: DOACs unsuitable), significant hepatic disease, extreme weight, pregnancy (LMWH) or recurrence on anticoagulation
Medication-induced
Drug switch First step
Switch amlodipine to lercanidipine (lower oedema rate) or change CCB to ACEI/ARB/thiazide for BP management. Review all offending agents
Venous insufficiency
Compression hosiery First-line
Fitted graduated compression where arterial supply is adequate — check pulses and arterial risk; ABPI or toe pressure where indicated (diabetes, absent pulses, neuropathy, suspected PAD). Not in decompensated HF, acute DVT, acute ischaemia, severe PAD or severe cellulitis without assessment. Venous ulcers → leg-ulcer service (NWCSP)
Step 1Address reversible causes — stop/switch offending drugs, treat underlying infection, elevate limbs, reduce dietary salt if intake is high (no routine salt restriction in heart failure — NICE NG106)
Step 2Compression therapy — for venous oedema once arterial supply is assessed (ABPI >0.8 is a practical, not absolute, threshold for full compression). Bandaging for lymphoedema via specialist service
Step 3Diuretic therapy (cardiac/renal/hepatic congestion only) — loop diuretic, dose individualised per local protocol; titrate to clinical response at the lowest effective dose
Step 4Treat underlying cause — HF: confirm phenotype, then optimise via the HF pathway — for HFrEF, ACEI/ARNI/ARB + beta-blocker + MRA + SGLT2 inhibitor as tolerated (NICE NG106); SGLT2i ± MRA also in HFmrEF/HFpEF. Hypothyroid: levothyroxine (start 25–50 mcg OD if older or cardiac disease, otherwise about 1.6 mcg/kg/day; NICE NG145/BNF), titrated to TSH. Hepatic: specialist management
Step 5Refractory oedema — refer to the HF/renal specialist team; combination diuretics (e.g. metolazone) only under specialist guidance

Furosemide reduces hospitalisation in acute decompensated HF but does not improve mortality — disease-modifying therapy (ACEI/ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2 inhibitor) is what saves lives. Lercanidipine causes less oedema than amlodipine. Rivaroxaban and apixaban are first-line DOACs for DVT (NICE NG158) with no requirement for overlapping heparin. MRAs reduce mortality in HFrEF — part of foundational therapy, not a refractory-oedema add-on.

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions — Often More Effective Than Drugs

Leg elevation Elevate legs above hip level for 30 min, 3× daily. At night, raise foot of bed by 15 cm. A simple, useful measure for venous oedema.
Salt restriction Adults: no more than 6 g salt/day (2.4 g sodium). In heart failure, NICE NG106 does not advise routine salt restriction — ask about intake and advise reducing it if high. Read food labels: processed foods are the main source.
Fluid restriction Not routine in heart failure (NICE NG106): restrict fluids only for dilutional hyponatraemia or high intake, and keep the need under review. Avoid excess water loading. Established HF: an individualised action plan may include a weight-gain trigger.
Exercise & mobilisation Calf muscle pump activation: foot dorsiflexion exercises 10 reps/hour when seated. Regular walking supports the calf pump.
Weight loss Weight management where appropriate — obesity causes dependency oedema independently of cardiac/renal causes. Support smoking cessation.
Compression hosiery adherence Worn from rising to bedtime — compliance is the main barrier. Ensure correct fitting (measure in morning before swelling). Consider donning aids for elderly patients.
Skin care Moisturise daily to prevent skin breakdown. Avoid trauma. Early treatment of fungal infections (tinea pedis) to prevent cellulitis entry point.
Alcohol reduction Alcohol causes both direct hypoalbuminaemia and hepatic disease. Target <14 units/week; brief intervention in consultation.

Leg elevation lowers venous and capillary pressure in the legs. Evidence that salt restriction helps heart failure is limited, which is why NICE NG106 asks about intake rather than restricting it routinely. Tailor advice to mobility, frailty, cardiovascular status and cause. Compression non-adherence drives recurrent venous ulcers — education and practical aids (applicators, zip stockings) help. Skin breakdown in lymphoedema leads to recurrent cellulitis which further damages lymphatics — a vicious cycle broken by good skincare.

9
Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

1 week
Check U&Es after starting or changing a diuretic where higher risk (congestion, renal impairment, frailty, ACEI/ARB/ARNI/MRA co-therapy) per local protocol. Assess diuresis response. Review compliance with compression. Recheck BP if hypertensive
2–4 weeks
Reassess oedema severity, weight change. If drug-induced oedema: confirm resolution after switch. Review investigation results (BNP, echo, USS)
3 months
Full review: classification confirmed, treatment effective? Step-up if inadequate response. HF: formal echocardiography result should be back
Ongoing (chronic)
Annual review for chronic venous insufficiency / lymphoedema / HF. Weight monitoring, medication review, skin assessment, compression renewal
Safety-net 999
Sudden severe breathlessness, chest pain, SpO₂ drops, collapse, acute severe unilateral leg pain/pallor (arterial ischaemia)
Safety-net same-day
Rapidly progressive swelling, new unilateral swelling with pain/warmth (DVT), new or worsening breathlessness or chest pain, fever or spreading erythema, jaundice or new ascites, severe proteinuria or haematuria, pregnancy with severe hypertension or symptoms, weight gain beyond the HF action-plan trigger
Diuretic monitoring
ACEI/ARNI/ARB/MRA (NG106): renal function and electrolytes before starting, 1–2 weeks after starting and after each dose increase, then every 3–6 months, sooner if the clinical picture changes. Use laboratory ranges; follow local renal/HF protocols for a significant creatinine rise, hyperkalaemia, hypotension or suspected AKI
Anticoagulation (DVT)
At least 3 months, then reassess provoking factor, recurrence and bleeding risk, and preference: consider stopping if a transient provoking factor has resolved; consider extended treatment after unprovoked or recurrent events if recurrence risk outweighs bleeding (NG158). Active cancer: 3–6 months then review. Assess for additional cancer symptoms or signs and investigate or refer if present (NG12 2026) — no routine extensive occult-cancer screening

Loop diuretics commonly cause hypokalaemia and renal changes — check U&Es early in higher-risk patients, particularly those on digoxin. 3-month DVT anticoagulation review is a critical safety checkpoint — extending unnecessarily increases major bleeding risk, but stopping too soon risks recurrence — balance the two for each patient. NICE advises against routine extensive cancer investigation after unprovoked DVT, but the 2026 NG12 update asks clinicians to assess for additional cancer symptoms or signs and investigate or refer if present. HF diuretic adjustments for weight gain should follow an individualised plan.
