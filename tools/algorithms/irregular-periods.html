1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude urgent and malignant causes first

Most irregular bleeding in the reproductive years is anovulatory and benign, but the same complaint can hide an early pregnancy (including ectopic), a cervical or endometrial cancer, or a pituitary lesion. Run through the tiles below before you settle on a benign label, and document that you have done so.

Pregnancy not excluded Any change in bleeding pattern in someone who could be pregnant → Urine hCG first. Positive test + pain, shoulder-tip pain or collapse → suspect ectopic (NICE NG126, 2019) → Same-day EPAU / A&E
Postmenopausal bleeding, aged 55 or over Unexplained bleeding more than 12 months after the last period that cannot be attributed to HRT → Suspected cancer pathway — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), rec 1.5.12
Postmenopausal bleeding, under 55 Unexplained and not attributable to HRT → Consider suspected cancer pathway — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) for endometrial cancer
Cervix looks abnormal On speculum the appearance of the cervix is consistent with cervical cancer (friable, ulcerated, mass) → Consider suspected cancer pathway — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)
Persistent postcoital / intermenstrual bleeding Not a stand-alone NG12 criterion, but needs a speculum, STI tests and a clear plan. Normal cervix + persisting symptoms → Gynaecology referral (local PCB/IMB pathway)
Haemodynamic instability Heavy bleeding with dizziness, syncope, tachycardia or hypotension → 999 (haemorrhage or ruptured ectopic)
Age 45+ with new persistent irregular or intermenstrual bleeding Higher prior probability of endometrial hyperplasia or cancer, especially with obesity, PCOS, tamoxifen or diabetes → Urgent imaging ± gynaecology
Galactorrhoea + amenorrhoea Check prolactin first (repeat if mildly raised). Persistently raised without a drug cause → Endocrinology for pituitary MRI
Visual field loss or new headache + amenorrhoea Bitemporal field loss suggests a pituitary macroadenoma pressing on the chiasm → Same-day specialist discussion
Rapid virilisation Deepening voice, clitoromegaly, fast-progressing hirsutism over months → suspect an androgen-secreting tumour → Urgent endocrinology
Ovarian symptoms aged 40+ Persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic pain or urinary frequency → CA125 with the age-specific NG12 (updated April 2026) thresholds (e.g. ≥35 IU/mL at 40–49) → Urgent direct-access USS
Weight loss or low BMI + amenorrhoea Consider an eating disorder or a systemic illness (coeliac disease, thyrotoxicosis, malignancy). Weigh, check bloods and ask directly about restriction and purging
Cancer Research UK ranks uterine cancer as the fourth most common cancer in UK women, and bleeding is usually its first symptom, so the bleeding history is where it gets caught early. The April 2026 update to NICE NG12 kept the suspected cancer pathway for unexplained postmenopausal bleeding at 55 and over, but only where HRT does not explain it. For unscheduled bleeding on HRT the guideline points to British Menopause Society practice. Persistent postcoital bleeding is not an NG12 trigger by itself; a normal-looking cervix with ongoing bleeding still needs a gynaecology assessment through your local pathway. Ectopic pregnancy remains a cause of maternal death in the UK, so any woman who could be pregnant needs an hCG before anything else. A prolactinoma can grow silently until it threatens vision. In an SCA case, say out loud that you are excluding pregnancy and cancer first: examiners mark safety before cleverness.
2
Diagnose

History — Characterise the menstrual pattern

Pin down the pattern before you order tests. A bleeding diary (paper or app) kept over about 3 cycles turns "irregular" into dates, and often settles the question of whether the problem is infrequent periods, frequent periods, bleeding between periods or heavy periods.

Cycle characterisation
First day of each period, cycle length, days of bleeding, flooding or clots, pain. A normal adult cycle is roughly 21–35 days
Pattern of irregularity
Infrequent (cycles over 35 days, or fewer than 8 periods a year); frequent (under 21 days); absent (no period for 3 months with previously regular cycles, or 6 months with previously irregular ones — a working definition used in practice); intermenstrual (IMB) or postcoital (PCB) bleeding
Onset and triggers
Recent change of contraception, weight change, stress, travel, illness, intensive training, new medicines, recent pregnancy or breastfeeding
Reproductive history
Age at menarche; pregnancies and miscarriages; current contraception and whether pregnancy is wanted now or later — this shapes every treatment choice
Contraception / HRT
Unscheduled bleeding is common in the first 3 months of any hormonal method and with progestogen-only methods (implant, injection, POP). New or persistent bleeding after that deserves assessment (FSRH CEU guidance on problematic bleeding with hormonal contraception, 2015)
Sexual health
New or multiple partners, condom use, discharge, dyspareunia — chlamydia and gonorrhoea cervicitis commonly cause PCB and IMB
Systemic symptoms
Acne, hirsutism, scalp hair thinning, weight gain (PCOS); heat or cold intolerance, palpitations, fatigue (thyroid); galactorrhoea, headache, visual change (prolactin/pituitary); hot flushes, night sweats (perimenopause, POI)
Eating and exercise
Restriction, purging, rapid weight loss, very high training loads, low energy availability — functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea
Medications
Antipsychotics, metoclopramide and domperidone raise prolactin; anticoagulants and some SSRIs increase bleeding; tamoxifen affects the endometrium
Family history
PCOS, type 2 diabetes, thyroid disease, early menopause (POI), bleeding disorders such as von Willebrand disease, endometrial or bowel cancer (Lynch syndrome)
Ideas, concerns, expectations
Fear of cancer, worry about fertility, embarrassment about hair growth or weight. Ask directly — it changes what the patient wants from the consultation
A bleeding diary converts a vague complaint into data you can act on and gives you a baseline for judging whether treatment works. Contraception is the commonest reason for irregular bleeding in general practice and is easy to overlook if you do not ask about it first. The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline for PCOS (international; Monash/ESHRE) defines irregular cycles from 3 years after menarche as under 21 or over 35 days, or fewer than 8 cycles a year, and uses wider limits in the first years after menarche, so age since menarche matters. Ask about fertility plans early: someone trying to conceive needs a different plan from someone who wants cycle control and contraception. The sexual history is not optional, because an STI is a treatable cause of postcoital bleeding.

Diagnose

Classification — Identify the aetiology group

Sort the likely cause into one of the groups below. The feature in each row is the one that separates it from its neighbours.

PCOS
Adults need 2 of 3: irregular cycles or anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound or raised AMH. Don't use ultrasound or AMH within 8 years of menarche, where both criteria rest on cycles plus hyperandrogenism (International PCOS Guideline, 2023 — international)
Thyroid disease
Hypothyroidism: heavier or irregular periods; hyperthyroidism: lighter, infrequent or absent periods. TSH is cheap and treatable — check it
Hyperprolactinaemia
Galactorrhoea ± headache ± infrequent periods. Separate drug-induced, macroprolactin (a lab artefact) and pituitary adenoma
Premature ovarian insufficiency
Under 40 with infrequent or absent periods and menopausal symptoms, plus raised FSH. NICE NG23 (2015, updated 2024) asks for 2 FSH samples 4–6 weeks apart. ESHRE (2024, international) accepts one FSH >25 IU/L after at least 4 months of disordered cycles, repeated only if in doubt
Perimenopause
Aged 45 or over with a changing cycle ± vasomotor symptoms: diagnose clinically without FSH (NICE NG23)
Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea
Low weight, restriction, heavy exercise or stress; low or low-normal LH/FSH with low oestradiol. A diagnosis of exclusion
Structural (PALM: polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma, malignancy/hyperplasia)
More likely with IMB, PCB, heavy bleeding, an enlarged or irregular uterus or pelvic pain. Needs ultrasound ± hysteroscopy
Cervical / infective
Cervicitis (chlamydia, gonorrhoea), ectropion, cervical polyp — suggested by PCB and discharge
Physiological
First 1–3 years after menarche, postpartum, breastfeeding, perimenopause — often settles, but only after red flags are excluded
Iatrogenic
Hormonal contraception, antipsychotics, anticoagulants, tamoxifen, chemotherapy or pelvic radiotherapy
The 2023 International PCOS Guideline puts prevalence at about 10–13% of women of reproductive age, making PCOS the commonest endocrine cause of infrequent periods. The guideline now lets AMH stand in for ultrasound in adults, but not in adolescents, where multi-follicular ovaries are normal. Premature ovarian insufficiency is easy to mislabel as stress or PCOS. Missing it leaves a young woman without oestrogen, which harms bone and cardiovascular health, so FSH belongs in the work-up of anyone under 40 with prolonged absent periods. Classifying the cause tells you where the patient goes next: primary care for most PCOS, thyroid and drug causes; gynaecology for structural disease; endocrinology for pituitary or androgen-secreting lesions; and a specialist service for POI.
4
Diagnose

Examination — Focused clinical assessment

Offer a chaperone and explain why each part matters. Examine before you investigate — NICE NG88 (heavy menstrual bleeding, 2018; since updated) asks for a physical examination before investigations or LNG-IUS fitting.

BMI, waist and BP
Low BMI (<18.5): hypothalamic amenorrhoea, eating disorder. Raised BMI and waist: PCOS and insulin resistance. BP as part of cardiometabolic risk
Skin / hair
Hirsutism (a modified Ferriman–Gallwey score of 4–6 or more, depending on ethnicity, per the International PCOS Guideline 2023), acne, scalp hair loss, acanthosis nigricans (insulin resistance), purple striae and proximal weakness (Cushing's)
Thyroid
Goitre, tremor, tachycardia, lid lag (hyper); bradycardia, dry skin, slow-relaxing reflexes (hypo)
Breasts
Only if galactorrhoea is reported: bilateral milky discharge points to prolactin. Unilateral or blood-stained discharge is a breast-pathway question
Visual fields
Confrontation fields if prolactin is raised or there is headache — look for bitemporal loss
Abdomen
Palpable pelvic mass or enlarged uterus (fibroids); signs of virilisation (clitoromegaly) suggest a tumour
Speculum
Needed for any PCB, persistent IMB or discharge. Look at the cervix itself: an ectropion, polyp or cervicitis versus a suspicious, friable or ulcerated lesion
Bimanual
Uterine size and contour, adnexal masses, cervical excitation or adnexal tenderness (pelvic infection, ectopic)
Swabs
NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea (vulvovaginal or endocervical) when there is PCB, IMB or any STI risk
Cervical screening status
Check the history and make sure routine screening is up to date. Screening is not a diagnostic test for symptoms: a normal recent result does not explain PCB, and you don't need a screening sample before referring a symptomatic woman
BMI is often the single most informative finding: it points you towards PCOS at one end and hypothalamic amenorrhoea or an eating disorder at the other. Hypothalamic amenorrhoea is diagnosed clinically and confirmed by the pattern of low oestradiol without raised FSH. Virilisation (clitoromegaly, a deepening voice, rapidly worsening hirsutism) is uncommon but suggests an ovarian or adrenal androgen-secreting tumour and needs urgent endocrinology input. Cervical screening is designed for people without symptoms. A symptomatic cervix needs to be looked at, swabbed for infection and referred on its appearance or on persistent symptoms, not "smeared and reassured". A documented speculum examination is also the thing examiners and medicolegal reviewers look for.

Diagnose

Investigations — Hormone profile and imaging

Order tests to answer specific questions. A standard "hormone profile" sent on a random day is often uninterpretable.

First line for infrequent or absent periods
hCG TSH Prolactin FSH + LH Oestradiol — timed to days 2–5 if there is any bleeding, otherwise random
PCOS work-up
Total testosterone SHBG (free androgen index). Assess glycaemic status at diagnosis with HbA1c or fasting glucose (an OGTT is preferred in higher-risk women per the International PCOS Guideline 2023), plus Lipids. Pelvic ultrasound or AMH only in adults when the diagnosis is still unclear
Suspected POI
FSH repeated 4–6 weeks later (NICE NG23). Once confirmed, the specialist will usually arrange karyotype, fragile X premutation testing and adrenal/thyroid antibodies (ESHRE 2024 — international)
Androgen excess
Markedly raised testosterone (well above the lab's female reference range) or rapid virilisation → DHEAS and 17-OHP (non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia) and urgent endocrinology
Raised prolactin
Repeat, avoiding stress and recent breast stimulation; ask the lab to screen for macroprolactin; review drugs; check TSH (hypothyroidism raises prolactin)
Structural pathology
Pelvic ultrasound (TVUSS) for suspected fibroids, polyps or adenomyosis, a pelvic mass, or PCB/IMB with a normal cervix, per local pathway. NICE NG88 prefers outpatient hysteroscopy where the history suggests polyps, submucosal fibroids or endometrial pathology
Bleeding / anaemia
FBC for anyone with heavy or prolonged bleeding (NICE NG88). Consider a coagulation screen and von Willebrand testing if heavy since menarche or with a personal or family bleeding history
Ovulation (fertility)
Mid-luteal Progesterone — day 21 of a 28-day cycle, later for longer cycles, repeated weekly until the next period if cycles are irregular (NICE CG156 fertility, 2013, updated 2017)
Not routine in primary care
MRI pelvis; endometrial sampling (gynaecology-led); pituitary MRI before prolactin has been confirmed and macroprolactin excluded
TSH and prolactin are cheap, treatable and often missed, so check them in almost every work-up for infrequent or absent periods. Macroprolactin is a large, biologically inactive complex that raises the lab result without causing disease. Screening for it saves unnecessary MRI scans and referrals. POI is confirmed by persistently raised FSH, and the diagnosis has major implications for bone, heart and fertility, so it goes to a specialist once confirmed. Endometrial sampling is a gynaecology procedure, decided on after imaging and clinical assessment. Testosterone assays differ between laboratories, so the trigger for urgent endocrinology is a result markedly above your lab's female reference range or clinical virilisation, not a single universal number. In the SCA, explain in plain language why each blood test is being done and how the results will reach the patient.
6
Refer

Referral Criteria — Gynaecology, Endocrinology, and beyond

Suspected cancer pathway (NICE NG12, updated April 2026)
Refer: aged 55 or over with unexplained postmenopausal bleeding not attributable to HRT (rec 1.5.12). Consider: under 55 with unexplained postmenopausal bleeding not attributable to HRT; cervix whose appearance is consistent with cervical cancer. Ovarian: age-specific CA125 thresholds for urgent direct-access USS in those aged 40+
Same-day
Haemodynamic compromise from bleeding; suspected ectopic pregnancy (positive hCG + pain or bleeding, per NICE NG126); acute abdomen; visual field loss with suspected pituitary lesion
Urgent gynaecology
Persistent PCB or IMB with a normal-looking cervix and negative STI tests; age 45+ with new persistent IMB; a pelvic mass that is not clearly benign; ultrasound showing a thickened or irregular endometrium (per the local pathway)
Specialist POI / menopause service
Confirmed POI — hormone replacement, bone and cardiovascular protection, fertility and genetic counselling (NICE NG23)
Routine gynaecology
Fibroids or polyps on imaging, suspected adenomyosis or endometriosis, heavy bleeding not controlled by first-line treatment (NICE NG88)
Endocrinology
Confirmed hyperprolactinaemia with no drug or macroprolactin explanation; markedly raised androgens or virilisation (urgent); suspected Cushing's or non-classic CAH
Fertility services
Anovulation in someone trying to conceive — refer early rather than waiting a year, as a known cause is present (NICE CG156)
Eating disorder service
Suspected eating disorder — assess and refer per NICE NG69 (2017); don't rely on BMI alone to judge risk
Primary care manage
PCOS with no red flags; hypothyroidism; drug-induced hyperprolactinaemia (with prescriber input); perimenopause; contraception-related bleeding after assessment
NG12 recommendation 1.5.12, as amended in April 2026, is a "refer" for unexplained postmenopausal bleeding at 55 and over. The under-55 and cervix criteria are "consider", which leaves room for judgement but still means documenting your reasoning. POI needs specialist input because untreated oestrogen deficiency at a young age raises the risk of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease; NICE NG23 recommends hormone treatment, either HRT or a combined hormonal contraceptive, unless contraindicated. Confirm a raised prolactin before referring: a single high value after a difficult venepuncture, or one due to macroprolactin, generates avoidable scans. The endocrinologist decides whether pituitary imaging is needed and when.

Treat

Treatment — Cause-directed and fertility-aware management

PCOS (no pregnancy wish)
Combined hormonal contraception 1st line
Regulates cycles, protects the endometrium, improves hirsutism and acne (International PCOS Guideline 2023). Check UKMEC before prescribing, especially BMI, BP and migraine with aura. Dose and choice per BNF.
PCOS (trying to conceive)
Refer fertility services
The International PCOS Guideline (2023) names letrozole as first-line pharmacological ovulation induction. It is unlicensed for this use in the UK and specialist-initiated. Advise folic acid, and weight optimisation if BMI is raised.
Primary hypothyroidism
Levothyroxine
NICE NG145 (2019): under 65 with no cardiovascular disease, consider starting at about 1.6 micrograms/kg/day (to the nearest 25 micrograms). Aged 65+ or with CVD, 25–50 micrograms/day and titrate. Aim for TSH within the reference range.
Drug-induced hyperprolactinaemia
Medication review
Switch or reduce the culprit only with the prescriber (psychiatry for antipsychotics). Never stop an antipsychotic abruptly. If amenorrhoea persists, consider oestrogen replacement for bone health.
Perimenopause / POI
HRT or combined hormonal contraception
For POI, offer hormone treatment until at least the average age of natural menopause unless contraindicated (NICE NG23). In perimenopause, remember contraception is still needed.
Bleeding on hormonal contraception
Assess, then adjust
Once pregnancy, STI and cervical pathology have been excluded, options depend on the method (FSRH CEU guidance, 2015). Many settle after 3 months.
Endometrial protectionIf periods are infrequent and no hormonal contraception is used, induce a withdrawal bleed at least every 3–4 months with a progestogen course of at least 12 days, or use combined contraception or an LNG-IUS (RCOG Green-top Guideline 33, 2014). Dose per BNF
Metformin (PCOS)An option alongside lifestyle change in adults with PCOS and BMI ≥25 for weight and metabolic outcomes (International PCOS Guideline 2023). Off-label for PCOS in the UK; start low and titrate for GI tolerance; dose per BNF
HirsutismCombined contraception first, with cosmetic hair removal alongside; specialist-led anti-androgens need reliable contraception because of the risk to a male fetus
Months without a period mean months of oestrogen without progesterone on the endometrium. RCOG Green-top Guideline 33 advises a withdrawal bleed at least every 3–4 months for women with PCOS who are not using hormonal contraception. A meta-analysis (Barry et al., Hum Reprod Update 2014) found women with PCOS had about 2.8 times the odds of endometrial cancer. Doses are given only where a primary source states them (NICE NG145 for levothyroxine); otherwise follow the BNF. In the SCA, check the plan against the patient's goal (cycle control, contraception, fertility or hair) and state the risks you are protecting against.
8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions — The foundation of PCOS and hypothalamic care

Weight management (PCOS) With excess weight, a 5–10% loss within 6 months brings meaningful clinical improvements (International PCOS Guideline 2023). Any healthy eating pattern that is sustainable is acceptable; avoid weight stigma
Physical activity Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus muscle strengthening on 2 days (UK Chief Medical Officers' physical activity guidelines, 2019). Activity helps insulin resistance even without weight loss
Energy availability In hypothalamic amenorrhoea, periods usually return when energy intake matches expenditure: restore weight, reduce training load, address stress. Coaches and sports medicine can help athletes
Eating disorders Ask sensitively and early. Refer to the community eating disorder service; weight, bloods and ECG monitoring per NICE NG69 if restriction is severe
Bleeding diary Record each bleeding day on paper or an app for 3 months. Bring it to review to judge response
Bone health (POI, hypothalamic amenorrhoea) Weight-bearing exercise, adequate dietary calcium, and vitamin D per UK public health advice (10 micrograms daily). DXA as advised by the specialist
Alcohol and smoking Keep alcohol within the UK CMO low-risk limit of 14 units a week spread over 3 or more days; offer stop-smoking support — it improves cardiometabolic risk in PCOS
Emotional wellbeing PCOS is linked with higher rates of anxiety, depression and body-image distress; the International PCOS Guideline 2023 advises screening for them. Offer support or talking therapy
Lifestyle change is the foundation of PCOS care and is recommended for everyone with PCOS, whatever their weight, because insulin resistance drives much of the hyperandrogenism and anovulation. In hypothalamic amenorrhoea, adding hormones does not fix the underlying energy deficit: restoring energy availability does. Lifestyle discussions go better when you acknowledge the difficulty. In an SCA, a shared, specific and achievable goal scores better than a lecture.

Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Results (1–2 weeks)
Agree how results will be given. Act on hCG, prolactin, TSH and FSH; arrange the repeat FSH or prolactin if needed
3 months
Review the bleeding diary, response to treatment, side effects, weight and mood; confirm endometrial protection is in place
Annual (PCOS)
Weight, BP, glycaemic status and lipids as advised by the International PCOS Guideline 2023; review contraception and mood
POI
Shared care with the specialist: HRT review, bone health, cardiovascular risk
999 now
Heavy bleeding with fainting, chest pain, breathlessness or collapse
Same day
Pregnancy with pain or bleeding (ectopic); new visual loss or severe headache with absent periods
Re-consult promptly
Any bleeding after sex, between periods or after the menopause; bleeding that becomes heavier or continuous; new pelvic pain or bloating
Re-refer if
No improvement after about 6 months of treatment; wishing to conceive; imaging shows a new or thickened endometrium
Safety-net script"Most irregular periods are caused by hormone changes rather than anything serious. But if you bleed after sex, between periods, or at all once your periods have stopped for a year, please book to see us again — don't wait for your review. If you ever feel faint with heavy bleeding, or have strong tummy pain and could be pregnant, call 999 or go to A&E."
DocumentationRecord hCG result, speculum findings, the red flags you asked about, the plan for endometrial protection and the safety-net advice given
PCOS carries a lifelong raised risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiometabolic disease, so the International PCOS Guideline 2023 advises regular checks of weight, BP and glycaemic status. Endometrial protection is the step most often lost at follow-up when periods stay infrequent, so check it at every review. People with POI need long-term hormone treatment and review. Stopping at the average menopausal age is a shared decision with the specialist. A worded safety-net that names specific symptoms (bleeding after sex, between periods or after the menopause) is easier for patients to act on than "come back if worse".
