01
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude cancer, IBD complications, and dehydration emergencies first

Chronic diarrhoea is common; serious causes are uncommon but catastrophic if missed. Screen every new presentation systematically.
Rectal bleeding with diarrhoea Any age → do not attribute to haemorrhoids. FIT → colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE NG12), or same-day assessment if profuse or with symptomatic anaemia (999 if haemodynamically compromised). An anal/rectal mass is referred without waiting for FIT; a low FIT does not exclude IBD, anal pathology or upper-GI bleeding
Unintentional weight loss ≥5% Over 6 months with chronic diarrhoea → FIT (colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g); urgent direct-access upper GI endoscopy if 55 or over with upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia; aged 60 or over with weight loss and diarrhoea → consider urgent direct-access CT for pancreatic cancer (urgent ultrasound if CT unavailable) (NICE NG12). Targeted tests by symptom pattern — do not use CEA or CA-125 as general cancer screens (CA-125 only per the NG12 ovarian pathway at 40+). FIT is triage, not rule-out: a negative or unavailable FIT must not delay referral if concern remains. FIT does not assess upper-GI, pancreatic, ovarian or IBD pathology
Persistent change in bowel habit Looser, more frequent stools at any age → FIT → colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE NG12) regardless of other explanation
Iron-deficiency anaemia Any adult with diarrhoea → FIT → colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE NG12). Right-sided colon cancer classically presents with IDA + change in bowel habit
Nocturnal diarrhoea An alarm feature that increases the likelihood of organic disease (IBD, microscopic colitis, secretory causes) — do not dismiss as IBS without assessment
Severe dehydration / haemodynamic compromise In acute-on-chronic diarrhoea (e.g. IBD flare, C. diff) → 999 if shocked. Same-day if unable to maintain hydration
Fever + bloody diarrhoea Systemic sepsis picture with diarrhoea → infectious colitis (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, E. coli O157), IBD flare, C. difficile. One stool sample for culture/PCR urgently. Same-day assessment; 999 if signs of sepsis or shock. In suspected E. coli O157 do not give loperamide or an antibiotic — both increase the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome
Palpable abdominal mass Any new mass with diarrhoea → FIT, then a colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g (rectal mass: refer without FIT); imaging by site. Also examine for organomegaly and note cachexia — both shift the differential towards malignancy
Recent antibiotics / hospital admission Raises suspicion of C. difficile in new unexplained diarrhoea — send a diarrhoeal stool per the local two-step lab algorithm (do not test formed stool or asymptomatic patients)
Immunocompromised patient HIV, transplant, chemotherapy, biologics — opportunistic infections (CMV colitis, cryptosporidium, Giardia) require urgent specialist input
Most chronic diarrhoea in primary care is functional, but organic causes are common enough that identifying them is the main task. Colorectal cancer presenting with diarrhoea alone (without bleeding) is frequently right-sided and presents late — IDA may be the only clue. Nocturnal diarrhoea makes organic disease more likely and should not be attributed to IBS without assessment. C. difficile is significantly under-recognised in primary care — rates of community-acquired C. diff have increased since 2010. In immunocompromised patients, persistent diarrhoea requires urgent specialist assessment due to the broad range of opportunistic pathogens.
02
Diagnose

Define and characterise diarrhoea — confirm it is truly chronic diarrhoea

Chronic diarrhoea = persistent loose or watery stool for ≥4 weeks, judged on consistency, frequency, urgency, nocturnal symptoms and change from baseline — not a fixed ≥3/day. First confirm the definition is met — some patients report diarrhoea when they mean urgency or frequency of normal stools.
Definition
Bristol Stool Chart types 5–7 (soft blobs, fluffy, watery) · persistent change from baseline · Duration ≥4 weeks. Note: faecal urgency or incontinence with normal stool consistency ≠ diarrhoea
Onset and pattern
Sudden onset (weeks) → infection, IBD, drug-induced, bile acid malabsorption · Gradual onset (years) → IBS, coeliac, microscopic colitis · Continuous vs intermittent · Nocturnal (organic disease more likely — an alarm feature, not diagnostic) vs diurnal (functional more likely)
Stool characteristics
Watery = secretory (bile acid, VIPoma, Giardia) · Fatty / oily / offensive / difficult to flush = malabsorptive (coeliac, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, small bowel disease) · Bloody / mucoid = IBD, infection, cancer · Small volume + urgency + tenesmus = rectal / distal disease
Dietary triggers
Lactose (dairy) = lactose intolerance · Wheat = coeliac / non-coeliac gluten sensitivity · Sorbitol (diet drinks / gum) = osmotic diarrhoea · High-FODMAP foods = IBS · Fat = bile acid malabsorption / pancreatic disease
Associated symptoms
Abdominal pain relieved by defaecation = IBS · Bloating / flatulence = malabsorption, SIBO, IBS · Perianal symptoms (soreness, prolapse, skin tags) = IBD · Joint pain / skin rash / eye symptoms = IBD (extraintestinal manifestations)
Medications — full review
Metformin (common), PPIs, SSRIs, antibiotics, colchicine, magnesium-containing antacids, laxative overuse, orlistat, digoxin toxicity. Always take a full drug history including OTC and supplements
Surgical / medical history
Previous cholecystectomy (bile acid diarrhoea) · Bowel resection (short bowel) · Previous radiotherapy (radiation enteritis) · Thyroidectomy / diabetes (autonomic neuropathy) · HIV / immunosuppression
Many patients referred with "chronic diarrhoea" do not meet the definition — they have urgency, frequency of normal stools, or faecal incontinence, which require different management pathways. Drug-induced diarrhoea is underdiagnosed — metformin commonly causes diarrhoea and is often overlooked because it was started years before the symptom developed. Post-cholecystectomy bile acid diarrhoea is frequently misdiagnosed as IBS — test via SeHCAT or the local gastroenterology pathway where available. Stool characteristics (fatty vs watery vs bloody) point to completely different pathophysiological mechanisms and diagnostic pathways.
03
Diagnose

Classify by mechanism — the diagnostic framework that drives investigation

Osmotic
Diarrhoea stops with fasting. Causes: lactose intolerance, coeliac disease, sorbitol, lactulose, magnesium-containing products, malabsorption syndromes. Clue: improves when not eating
Secretory
Diarrhoea continues with fasting (secretory mechanism independent of food). Large volume, watery. Causes: bile acid malabsorption, VIPoma, carcinoid, microscopic colitis, Giardia, Cryptosporidium, collagenous colitis. Clue: nocturnal, persists despite fasting
Inflammatory
Blood + mucus + systemic features (fever, weight loss, CRP elevated). Causes: IBD (Crohn's / UC), infectious colitis, C. difficile, ischaemic colitis, radiation enteritis. Clue: systemic upset, elevated inflammatory markers
Malabsorptive / fat malabsorption
Steatorrhoea: fatty, pale, difficult to flush, offensive stools. Causes: coeliac disease, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (chronic pancreatitis, post-pancreatectomy), small bowel bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), Whipple's disease. Clue: weight loss + steatorrhoea
Dysmotility
Accelerated transit without structural disease. IBS-D, post-vagotomy, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, hyperthyroidism. Clue: often stress-related, no nocturnal symptoms, normal investigations
Functional (IBS-D)
A positive diagnosis (NICE CG61): typical symptoms with recurrent abdominal pain related to defaecation or stool change, no red flags, and reassuring baseline tests (FBC, CRP/ESR, coeliac serology) — not exhaustive exclusion. Persistent loose stools without recurrent pain = functional diarrhoea rather than IBS-D
Mechanistic classification directs investigation efficiently. Osmotic diarrhoea that stops with fasting does not need colonoscopy — dietary exclusion and coeliac screen are the first steps. Secretory diarrhoea that persists with fasting requires endoscopic investigation (microscopic colitis is invisible to the naked eye — biopsies are mandatory). Malabsorptive diarrhoea needs coeliac serology and faecal elastase (for pancreatic exocrine insufficiency) before colonoscopy. IBS-D is a positive clinical diagnosis made on a typical pattern, absent red flags and limited baseline tests — with a lower threshold for investigation in new symptoms over 50.
04
Diagnose

Targeted examination — look for systemic clues and extraintestinal signs

General / systemic
BMI (weight loss = organic until proven otherwise) · Pallor (anaemia = coeliac, IBD, cancer) · Lymphadenopathy · Muscle wasting · Peripheral oedema (hypoalbuminaemia = malabsorption / protein-losing enteropathy)
Skin
Dermatitis herpetiformis (intensely itchy blistering rash on extensor surfaces = coeliac) · Erythema nodosum / pyoderma gangrenosum (IBD extraintestinal) · Flushing (carcinoid) · Aphthous ulcers (Crohn's, coeliac)
Eyes
Uveitis / episcleritis / conjunctivitis = IBD extraintestinal manifestation. Ask about red / painful eye
Joints
Arthritis / arthralgia (large joints, migratory) = IBD / coeliac / reactive arthritis (Reiter's following infectious gastroenteritis)
Abdomen
RIF mass (Crohn's — ileal inflammation / abscess) · Hepatomegaly (IBD-related liver disease, PSC, metastatic cancer) · Tenderness distribution · Visible distension · Bowel sounds
PR examination
Perianal disease (fistulae, skin tags, fissures — common in Crohn's) · Rectal mucosa (proctitis — tender, contact bleeding) · Masses · Fresh blood / mucus on glove
Thyroid
Goitre / tremor / tachycardia / heat intolerance = hyperthyroidism causing dysmotility diarrhoea. Check TFTs
Dermatitis herpetiformis is pathognomonic for coeliac disease — if you see this on skin, the diagnosis is made. Perianal involvement is common in Crohn's disease (fistulae, abscesses, fissures, skin tags) — finding this on PR exam should dramatically raise suspicion for Crohn's and expedite referral. PSC (primary sclerosing cholangitis) is associated with UC and can present before bowel symptoms — abnormal LFTs with IBD should always trigger PSC investigation (ALP elevated, MRCP). Hypoalbuminaemia (<30 g/L) with diarrhoea indicates severe malabsorption or protein-losing enteropathy and requires urgent secondary care assessment.
05
Diagnose

Investigations — layered approach; targeted, not blanket

First-line (baseline)
FBC (anaemia, eosinophilia) CRP / ESR Coeliac serology (all) · if indicated: U&E + albumin (severity, dehydration, weight loss) LFTs (liver disease, malabsorption) TFTs (clinical features) Glucose / HbA1c (suspected diabetes) Coeliac: anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (total IgA to exclude IgA deficiency — false negative if low) FIT (if any NG12 colorectal feature — refer on ≥10 µg Hb/g; a negative FIT does not override a red flag)
Faecal tests
Faecal calprotectin — where IBD vs functional disease is the question and cancer is not suspected; interpret with the local (GM) pathway and assay cut-offs. Low → active colonic inflammation less likely (not cancer, coeliac, bile acid, pancreatic or microscopic colitis); raised → repeat or refer by magnitude (confounders: infection, NSAIDs, PPIs, cancer, diverticulitis). Targeted stool tests — one sample for culture/PCR when indicated; C. diff per lab algorithm; Giardia/parasites only with travel, exposure, immunosuppression or persistent unexplained diarrhoea
Faecal elastase
Faecal elastase-1 — if steatorrhoea suspected. <200 µg/g may suggest pancreatic exocrine insufficiency — watery stool can give a falsely low result; interpret with symptoms, nutrition and imaging. Order if post-pancreatitis, alcohol history, weight loss + steatorrhoea
Coeliac disease NG20
Anti-tTG IgA (positive = refer gastroenterology for duodenal biopsy to confirm — do NOT start gluten-free diet before biopsy). Must be eating gluten at time of testing; a negative test does not exclude coeliac disease if gluten was restricted or suspicion remains high. If IgA deficient: use anti-DGP IgG or anti-tTG IgG instead
If bile acid malabsorption suspected
Post-cholecystectomy, post-ileal resection, idiopathic BAM → SeHCAT or a validated alternative via gastroenterology; a monitored empirical trial if testing unavailable (response does not confirm the diagnosis). SeHCAT is a nuclear medicine test arranged via gastroenterology
Second-line (secondary care)
Colonoscopy + biopsies (IBD, microscopic colitis — invisible to naked eye) · OGD + duodenal biopsy (coeliac, Giardia, Whipple's) · CT abdomen (Crohn's complications, cancer, lymphoma) · Small bowel MRI (Crohn's extent) · Hydrogen breath test (SIBO, lactose intolerance)
Do NOT order
Routine CT for all diarrhoea without indication · Breath tests in primary care without specialist input · SeHCAT without gastroenterology referral · Colonoscopy as first-line without red flags / elevated calprotectin
Faecal calprotectin is endorsed by NICE (HTG320, formerly DG11) for distinguishing IBD from IBS in adults with diarrhoea. A low result makes active colonic inflammation less likely and can avoid colonoscopy in many, but thresholds are assay- and pathway-dependent. Coeliac disease affects 1% of the UK population — anti-tTG IgA can be falsely negative in IgA deficiency (always check total IgA). The patient must be eating gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing (NICE NG20). Microscopic colitis is the most underdiagnosed cause of chronic watery diarrhoea in women over 50 — the mucosa often looks normal, so colonoscopy with biopsies is needed; calprotectin may be only mildly elevated.
06
Refer

Referral criteria — structured pathway from emergency to routine

999 Emergency
Severe dehydration with haemodynamic shock · Profuse bloody diarrhoea with collapse · Toxic megacolon (IBD — distension + fever + peritonism) · Septic shock from enteric source
Same-day
Suspected C. difficile with systemic upset (elderly, post-antibiotic, care home) · Severe IBD flare (≥6 bloody stools/day plus any one of temperature >37.8°C, pulse >90, Hb <105 g/L or ESR >30 mm/h — Truelove & Witts severe criteria) · Unable to maintain oral hydration
Suspected cancer Colorectal
NICE NG12, offer FIT for: change in bowel habit or IDA at any age · rectal bleeding with abdominal pain or weight loss under 50 · rectal bleeding, abdominal pain or weight loss at 50+ · abdominal mass → referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g · rectal mass → referral without FIT · Pancreatic: aged 60+ with weight loss and diarrhoea → consider urgent direct-access CT (urgent ultrasound if CT unavailable)
Urgent gastroenterology
Raised faecal calprotectin per the local GM pathway · Positive coeliac serology (for duodenal biopsy) · Suspected new IBD without red flag urgency · Steatorrhoea / malabsorption · Suspected microscopic colitis (watery diarrhoea, female, age >50, on NSAIDs / PPIs)
Routine gastroenterology
IBS-D not responding to 6-month primary care treatment · Suspected bile acid malabsorption (for SeHCAT) · SIBO investigation · Positive Giardia / unusual parasites requiring specialist treatment oversight · Unexplained diarrhoea with normal standard workup
Other specialties
Dietitian: coeliac (gluten-free diet) · malabsorption · IBS-D (low-FODMAP) · Endocrinology: carcinoid / VIPoma (rare — diarrhoea + flushing + tachycardia) · Infectious diseases: immunocompromised with diarrhoea
Primary care manages
IBS-D (positive diagnosis, no red flags, baseline tests reassuring) · Lactose intolerance (dietary) · Drug-induced (switch medication) · Mild C. diff (community acquired, non-severe) · Dietary diarrhoea
Toxic megacolon is a surgical emergency and must be referred within hours. Severe UC (Truelove & Witts: ≥6 bloody stools/day + systemic upset) needs hospital IV steroids, not oral treatment at home. Carcinoid tumours and VIPomas are rare but important: diarrhoea + flushing + tachycardia → check 5-HIAA in urine (carcinoid) or VIP levels (VIPoma) — both require oncology input. Positive coeliac serology should always be referred for biopsy before starting gluten-free diet — biopsy is the gold standard and becomes negative (falsely normal) on a gluten-free diet.
07
Treat

Treatment ladder — cause-directed therapy with symptomatic support

Always treat the underlying cause first. Symptomatic antidiarrhoeals are an adjunct, not primary treatment for organic disease.
IBS-D
Antispasmodic + loperamide 1st line
Loperamide (start low, review) when no infective or inflammatory red flags + antispasmodic (e.g. mebeverine 135 mg TDS). Dietitian-led low-FODMAP if first-line advice fails
Coeliac disease
Strict gluten-free diet Lifelong
Refer to dietitian immediately after biopsy-confirmed diagnosis. No wheat, barley, rye. Check for nutritional deficiencies: Fe, B12, folate, vitamin D, Ca²⁺
C. difficile (first episode, any severity)
Oral vancomycin 1st line
NICE NG199 (2021): oral vancomycin 125mg QDS × 10 days for a first episode of any severity; fidaxomicin 200mg BD × 10 days if vancomycin is ineffective. Metronidazole is no longer recommended (least effective for sustained cure). Stop the causative antibiotic if safe; review PPIs and laxatives; no loperamide or other antimotility drugs; specialist/microbiology advice if older, frail, immunocompromised, severe or life-threatening
GiardiaTreat a confirmed infection per current GM antimicrobial guidance (pregnancy, immunosuppression, travel, treatment failure, reinfection matter). Assess household exposure and sexual practices where relevant (not STI-style contact tracing). Reassess if symptoms persist
Bile acid malabsorptionBile acid sequestrant (colesevelam or colestyramine) per local formulary and specialist advice — watch drug-binding interactions and timing. Response supports but does not confirm the diagnosis. Supplement fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) if prolonged use
Pancreatic exocrine insufficiencyPERT started and titrated by cause, meal size and nutrition per specialist/local guidance. Take with meals and snacks, spread through the meal. PPI co-prescription only where clinically indicated or specialist-advised
Lactose intoleranceLactose-free diet (trial 4–6 weeks). Most patients tolerate up to 12g lactose/day (240ml milk). Lactase supplements (Lactaid) with dairy. Ensure adequate calcium intake (fortified dairy-free products, calcium supplement 1000mg/day)
Microscopic colitisDiagnosis needs colonoscopy with biopsies. Budesonide induction and taper are gastroenterology-led. Review NSAIDs, PPIs and SSRIs with the patient (shared decision)
Symptomatic (adjunct)Loperamide only for likely functional diarrhoea/IBS-D without infective or inflammatory red flags — not with bloody diarrhoea, fever, suspected invasive infection or C. difficile, acute severe IBD, toxic megacolon, distension or obstruction. Start low and review. No routine codeine
Colesevelam is often better tolerated than colestyramine (tablet vs powder, fewer drug-binding interactions); the choice follows the local formulary and specialist advice. PERT dosing and any acid suppression follow specialist advice. Budesonide is the usual induction treatment for microscopic colitis, started via gastroenterology. Critically, loperamide is contraindicated in severe IBD flares (Truelove & Witts severe) and C. difficile — it can precipitate toxic megacolon by reducing gut motility and allowing toxin accumulation.
08
Lifestyle

Non-pharmacological — dietary and behavioural interventions with strong evidence

Low-FODMAP diet (IBS-D) Second-line after general dietary advice; must be supervised by a dietitian. Restriction phase 4–8 weeks then structured reintroduction. NHS dietitian referral available via GP
Gluten-free diet (coeliac) Strict lifelong adherence required — even trace amounts cause villous atrophy. Dietitian referral mandatory. Gluten-free prescribing per current local commissioning policy
Lactose exclusion trial Eliminate all dairy for 4–6 weeks — if improvement confirms lactose intolerance. Reintroduce gradually to determine threshold. Calcium supplementation essential if dairy eliminated long-term
Hydration 1.5–2L clear fluids/day. Avoid sorbitol (diet drinks, chewing gum), excess caffeine (>3 cups/day worsens diarrhoea via adenosine receptor), and alcohol (direct enterotoxic effect)
Meal regularity and pacing Regular meal times stabilise gastrocolic reflex. Eating slowly and mindfully reduces air swallowing and gut hypersensitivity. Avoid large meals — smaller, more frequent meals reduce load on digestive system
Stress and psychological factors IBS-specific CBT for persistent symptoms — via NHS Talking Therapies where available. Gut-directed hypnotherapy is included in NICE CG61 for refractory IBS. Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) effective adjunct
Probiotics (IBS-D) Optional: one product at the manufacturer's dose for at least 4 weeks (BSG: up to 12); stop if no benefit
Physical activity Regular moderate exercise (150 min/week) improves gut transit regulation, reduces psychological stress contributing to IBS, and improves IBD quality of life. Avoid intense exercise during IBD flare (increases gut permeability)
Medication review Review with the patient and decide together whether to stop or switch NSAIDs (worsen IBD, trigger microscopic colitis) · PPIs (associated with microscopic colitis, C. diff risk) · Metformin (consider modified-release) · Sorbitol-containing syrups
Low-FODMAP has good dietary evidence for IBS-D when first-line advice fails. It requires dietitian supervision because unsupervised restriction leads to nutritional deficiencies and an overly restrictive diet long-term. IBS-specific psychological therapy helps persistent symptoms. A metformin modified-release switch often reduces GI side effects and can be done in primary care. Probiotics for IBS: BSG 2021 guidelines recommend a trial of a multi-strain probiotic for at least 4 weeks as they are safe and have moderate evidence of benefit.
09
Safety

Follow-up & monitoring — structured review with vigilance for new red flags

2–4 weeks
Blood and stool results review. C. diff: review clinical response — no routine repeat stool test after resolution; persisting or recurrent symptoms → reassess (recurrence, alternative cause, post-infectious IBS, IBD) with microbiology advice. Dietary exclusion trial: assess response. Drug switch: assess GI symptom improvement
6–8 weeks
IBS-D: review FODMAP progress (dietitian letter). Bile acid malabsorption trial: assess response to colesevelam. Microscopic colitis on budesonide: symptom control. Coeliac: confirm dietitian referral accepted and gluten-free diet started
3 months
Full review. Have investigations been completed? Any outstanding referral? Coeliac: anti-tTG at 6–12 months interpreted with gluten exposure, adherence, baseline level, deficiencies and symptoms. IBD: specialist follow-up plan confirmed?
Annual (established diagnosis)
Coeliac: annual review with bloods guided by symptoms, adherence and comorbidity; DEXA by fracture risk, not routine · IBD: shared care monitoring per gastroenterology plan · Microscopic colitis / IBD: per specialist plan — primary care monitors hydration, anaemia, nutrition, steroid exposure, adverse effects and flares
C. diff recurrence monitoring
C. diff recurrence is common. If recurrence → Fidaxomicin 200mg BD × 10 days (NICE NG199, 2021: fidaxomicin for a further episode within 12 weeks of symptom resolution; vancomycin or fidaxomicin after 12 weeks) or faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) referral
999 Safety-net
Sudden severe bloody diarrhoea · Haemodynamic collapse · Abdominal rigidity (toxic megacolon) · Severe dehydration with confusion or reduced consciousness
Same-day GP Safety-net
Any new rectal bleeding · Significant weight loss · Unable to maintain oral hydration · Fever >38°C with diarrhoea · Symptoms worsening despite treatment · New abdominal mass
Re-screen red flags
At every follow-up — new weight loss, bleeding, or systemic symptoms in a patient with "established" IBS-D should prompt re-investigation. A changed symptom pattern, especially at 50+, needs re-evaluation even if IBS was previously diagnosed
Coeliac disease needs annual review — untreated or poorly adherent coeliac disease increases risk of enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL, rare but serious), osteoporosis, and nutritional deficiencies. Anti-tTG IgA should normalise within 12 months of strict GFD — failure to normalise usually reflects ongoing gluten exposure (intentional or inadvertent), interpreted with adherence, baseline antibody level, deficiencies and symptom response. C. difficile recurrence is common and each recurrence raises the risk of more. NICE NG199 (2021) recommends fidaxomicin for a further episode within 12 weeks of symptom resolution. FMT is a specialist option for multiply-recurrent C. diff. In patients over 50 with previously "diagnosed" IBS, any change in symptom pattern should trigger re-investigation — new colorectal cancer developing in patients with IBS is a documented pattern of delayed diagnosis.
