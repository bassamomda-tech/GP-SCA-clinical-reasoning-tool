1
Safety

Red Flags — Fracture, Achilles Rupture & Septic Arthritis

Ottawa Ankle Rules — X-ray indicated if: pain in the malleolar zone plus bony tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus (lower 6 cm of fibula or tibia) OR inability to take four weight-bearing steps both immediately after the injury and at assessment. Use in people over 5 years (NICE NG38) as a decision aid after clinical assessment. Highly sensitive when correctly applied but limited specificity — a positive rule indicates radiography, not a fracture.
Ottawa Foot Rules — X-ray if: pain in the midfoot zone plus bony tenderness at base of 5th metatarsal (peroneus brevis avulsion) OR navicular bone (medial foot) OR inability to take four weight-bearing steps both immediately after injury and at assessment. Always apply both ankle AND foot rules after ankle injury.
When Ottawa is unreliable Altered mental status, intoxication, distracting or multiple injuries, neuropathy, unreliable history/examination, pathological or suspected stress fracture → use clinical judgement; a negative rule does not avoid imaging
Deformity / neurovascular compromise / open injury Document skin integrity, pulses, sensation and deformity. Deformed ankle or neurovascular compromise → urgent reduction (ED). Palpate proximal fibula (Maisonneuve) and assess syndesmosis (pain above mortise, external-rotation pain, high-sprain mechanism)
Simmonds–Thompson test (Achilles rupture) Absent plantar flexion on calf squeeze (patient prone, compare with the other side) strongly suggests rupture. Do not rely on a palpable gap (may be absent with swelling or late presentation); inability to stand on tiptoe is supportive but not required. Same-day orthopaedics/ED — temporary immobilisation in plantar flexion; avoid dorsiflexion.
Hot, swollen ankle with severe pain or restricted movement Treat as septic arthritis until excluded → same-day hospital assessment. Synovial aspiration and blood cultures promptly where feasible, but do not delay antibiotics in a systemically unwell patient. Gout and septic arthritis may coexist — urate crystals do not exclude infection.
New unilateral calf swelling, pain, warmth or pitting oedema Two-level DVT Wells (NICE NG158): ≥2 (likely) → proximal leg-vein USS within 4 hours, or D-dimer + interim therapeutic anticoagulation + USS within 24 hours; ≤1 (unlikely) → D-dimer, USS if positive. After trauma consider haematoma, muscle injury, fracture or ruptured Baker's cyst; D-dimer may be raised by trauma, surgery, infection or inflammation. Chest pain, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, syncope or unexplained tachycardia → urgent PE assessment. Do not use a calf squeeze to diagnose or exclude DVT.
Stress fracture (insufficiency) Insidious onset pain in physically active patient or osteoporotic elderly person without clear injury. Point tenderness on fibula or calcaneus. Early X-ray may be normal → MRI generally preferred if suspicion remains (bone scan not routinely first). Management depends on site, high vs low risk, displacement, ability to walk, bone health and demands — urgent orthopaedics for high-risk sites (navicular, talus, anterior tibial cortex, base of 5th metatarsal, femoral neck, sesamoids).
The Ottawa Ankle Rules (OAR) were developed at the University of Ottawa in 1992 and are highly sensitive for ankle and midfoot fractures when correctly applied, with limited specificity, and reduce unnecessary X-rays. NICE NG38 recommends them for people over 5 years; they are less reliable with altered mental status, intoxication, distracting or multiple injuries, neuropathy, an unreliable examination, or suspected stress or pathological fracture. Critically, inability to take four weight-bearing steps both immediately after the injury and at assessment is as important a criterion as bony tenderness. Achilles tendon rupture is a commonly missed significant ankle injury — the Simmonds–Thompson test takes seconds and should be performed in every case of acute Achilles/posterior heel pain or sudden loss of push-off. A palpable gap may be absent, especially with swelling or delayed presentation.

Diagnose

History — Mechanism, Onset & Character

Mechanism of injury
Inversion injury (most common — rolls outward over lateral foot) → lateral ligament complex (ATFL, CFL, PTFL). Eversion injury → medial (deltoid ligament) damage or fibular avulsion. Forced dorsiflexion (fall from height) → Achilles, syndesmosis. No injury → inflammatory, degenerative, infection.
Onset and timeline
Acute (<48 hrs with trauma) → sprain, fracture, rupture. Subacute (days–weeks) → stress fracture, tendinopathy. Gradual onset without trauma → OA, inflammatory arthritis, gout (can be acute), posterior tibial tendon dysfunction.
Location of pain
Lateral (ATFL/CFL) → lateral ligament sprain. Medial (deltoid) → medial ligament / posterior tibial tendon. Posterior heel/Achilles → Achilles tendinopathy or rupture. Anteromedial → OA. Posterior tibial tendon (medial) → acquired flat foot. Diffuse → inflammatory / gout.
Functional impact
Weight-bearing possible? (Inability immediately post-injury → Ottawa X-ray criteria). Giving way (instability → chronic lateral ligament laxity). Morning stiffness >60 min → inflammatory arthritis. First-step pain improving with walking → plantar fasciitis (arising at inferior calcaneus).
Associated features
Warmth, erythema, swelling → inflammation, infection, gout, reactive arthritis. Bilateral swelling (pitting oedema) → systemic cause (heart failure, CKD, DVT). Previous ankle sprains → chronic instability. Psoriasis, IBD, sexually acquired infection → reactive / psoriatic arthritis.
The distinction between an acute lateral ligament sprain and a chronic ankle instability syndrome is clinically important. A single significant sprain that is inadequately rehabilitated leads to persistent proprioceptive deficit and mechanical laxity — the patient describes the ankle "giving way" on uneven ground. This is not a new injury each time but ongoing instability from the original inadequately healed ligament. The treatment is proprioceptive rehabilitation via physiotherapy, not repeated rest and RICE. Patients who present with recurrent "ankle sprains" without adequate rehabilitation should be referred to physiotherapy for a structured proprioceptive and strengthening programme. Persistent instability despite structured physiotherapy may indicate a ligament reconstruction referral to orthopaedics.
3
Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Lateral ligament sprain
ATFL (anterior talofibular ligament) most commonly injured. Grade I: stretch, no laxity; Grade II: partial tear, some laxity; Grade III: complete rupture, significant laxity. Anterior drawer test (ATFL laxity). Protection, relative rest, ice, compression, elevation + early progressive mobilisation; physiotherapy for persistent symptoms, recurrence or instability.
Achilles tendinopathy
Posterior ankle/heel pain, worse first thing in the morning and after exercise, improves with warming up. Tender thickened Achilles tendon. No rupture (Simmonds–Thompson negative). Progressive tendon-loading rehabilitation is first-line (isometric, heavy slow resistance or eccentric–concentric programmes, e.g. Alfredson). Imaging not routinely needed at presentation. Ask about fluoroquinolone exposure (tendon risk).
Achilles tendon rupture
Sudden severe posterior ankle pain — often described as "someone kicked me in the back of the leg." Simmonds–Thompson test positive (gap may be absent). Same-day orthopaedics — functional bracing or operative repair depending on age, activity, comorbidity, tendon gap, patient preference and local protocol. Ultrasound can help if uncertain but must not delay referral.
Gout
Acute: severe, hot, red, swollen ankle or first MTP joint (podagra). Peak at 12–24 hrs, resolves in 1–2 weeks. Serum urate (may be normal during acute attack — reduced by inflammation). If urate <360 μmol/L during a flare but gout strongly suspected, repeat at least 2 weeks after it settles. Consider joint aspiration if diagnosis uncertain, unusually hot/systemically unwell, or septic arthritis possible: MSU crystals (needle-shaped, negatively birefringent) — crystals do not exclude infection.
Ankle OA
Insidious onset pain, worse activity, better rest. Morning stiffness <30 min. Reduced range of motion (dorsiflexion). Diagnosed clinically — imaging not routinely needed; X-ray if diagnosis uncertain, atypical symptoms, marked deformity, trauma or surgical planning (joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis). Risk: previous fracture (post-traumatic OA most common cause of ankle OA).
Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD)
Acquired adult flat foot. Medial ankle pain, progressive flattening of medial arch. "Too many toes" sign (excess toes visible from behind). Assess single-leg heel raise, arch height, hindfoot alignment, forefoot abduction, tendon tenderness and function. Physiotherapy and appropriate footwear initially; orthoses selectively. Refer for progressive deformity, inability to single-leg heel raise, suspected rupture, severe pain or failed conservative care. Fluoroquinolones risk factor.
Peroneal tendon pathology
Lateral ankle pain persisting after sprain — often confused for ligament injury. Peroneal tendons (peroneus longus and brevis) run posterior to lateral malleolus. Subluxation: snap/clunk sensation. Tear: chronic lateral ankle pain. USS/MRI when the diagnosis is uncertain, symptoms persist or surgery is being considered.
Peroneal tendon pathology is a commonly missed diagnosis after an ankle sprain — some patients with "chronic lateral ankle pain after sprain" have a peroneal tendon tear rather than residual ligament injury. The peroneal tendons run posterior to the lateral malleolus in a shared fibrous tunnel — they stabilise the ankle in eversion and are stressed during inversion injuries. A peroneal tendon tear presents with persistent lateral ankle pain, swelling along the peroneal tendons (not at the ligament), and pain with resisted eversion. If lateral ankle pain persists beyond 6–8 weeks without improvement despite appropriate management, consider USS or MRI of the peroneal tendons, or refer to physiotherapy/MSK, where the diagnosis is uncertain or surgery is being considered. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin) are a significant risk factor for tendon rupture — both Achilles and peroneal tendons — via an unclear mechanism. The MHRA Drug Safety Update (March 2019) restricts fluoroquinolone use because of disabling, long-lasting effects including tendinopathy and tendon rupture, and the SPCs carry the warning (the US FDA boxed warning is the international equivalent). Avoid inappropriate prescribing, particularly with current tendinopathy; the MHRA advises stopping a fluoroquinolone at the first sign of tendon pain or inflammation, avoiding concurrent corticosteroids where possible, and recognising that tendon damage can occur during treatment or several months afterwards.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Ottawa Rules (apply first)
First document skin integrity, deformity, pulses, sensation and capillary refill. Age >5 with a reliable examination: palpate posterior edge/tip and lower 6 cm of fibula (lateral malleolus) and tibia (medial malleolus), base of 5th metatarsal, navicular; also proximal fibula (Maisonneuve) and syndesmosis. Four weight-bearing steps both immediately after injury and at assessment. If any criteria met → X-ray ankle and/or foot; X-ray also if clinical concern remains despite a negative rule.
Simmonds–Thompson test (posterior heel pain or loss of push-off)
Patient prone, feet over edge of couch. Squeeze mid-calf and compare with the other side. Normal: plantar flexion occurs. Absent plantar flexion strongly suggests rupture. Also assess: palpable gap (may be absent), inability to stand on tiptoes (supportive, not required), swelling/bruising over Achilles.
Ligament stress tests
Anterior drawer test (ATFL): stabilise tibia, draw foot forward — >5 mm laxity = Grade III rupture. Talar tilt test (CFL): invert foot — >10° difference = CFL laxity. Compare with contralateral ankle.
Gait assessment
Observe walking: antalgic (shortened stance phase), trendelenburg (hip weakness contributing to ankle stress), flat foot deformity (PTTD), "too many toes" sign (excessive heel valgus with forefoot abduction in PTTD).
Investigations
Targeted, not routine: X-ray ankle (AP + lateral + mortise) — if Ottawa criteria or another fracture indication. FBC, CRP, blood cultures + joint aspiration if infection suspected. Serum urate during or after a suspected gout flare (may be normal acutely). Renal function before NSAIDs, colchicine or ULT. Urinalysis/STI testing only if reactive arthritis suspected. USS for selected tendon pathology or guided aspiration. MRI for persistent suspected stress fracture or complex soft-tissue/intra-articular pathology (via orthopaedics/MSK specialist)
Serum urate during an acute gout attack may be paradoxically normal or low — the acute inflammatory response redistributes urate from serum into the joint and tissues. A normal urate during an acute attack does not exclude gout. If urate is normal during a flare, NICE NG219 (2022) advises repeating it at least 2 weeks after the flare has settled to establish the true level. The treat-to-target urate for ULT is <360 μmol/L, with a lower target of <300 μmol/L considered for tophi, chronic gouty arthritis or continuing flares (NICE NG219). The mortise view (15–20° internal rotation) is the most important X-ray for ankle assessment — it shows the tibiotalar joint space and the integrity of the ankle mortise. A widened lateral clear space (>5 mm) indicates syndesmotic disruption — a high ankle sprain that requires orthopaedic assessment and may need screw fixation.

Refer

Referral Pathways

A&E same-day
Deformity or suspected unstable fracture · Open fracture · Neurovascular compromise (urgent reduction) · Ottawa criteria met (X-ray via local pathway) · Suspected Achilles rupture (Simmonds–Thompson positive) · Hot, swollen, severely painful joint with possible infection · Suspected DVT/PE (two-level Wells pathway) · Rapidly progressive swelling with systemic illness
Orthopaedics / MSK urgent
Confirmed Achilles rupture (conservative vs surgical decision) · Osteochondral defect (OCD) on X-ray · Syndesmotic injury (widened mortise) · Peroneal tendon dislocation · Stress fracture at a high-risk site (navicular, talus, anterior tibial cortex, base of 5th metatarsal, femoral neck, sesamoids) · Ankle OA with persistent symptoms and functional limitation despite conservative management
Physiotherapy
Grade I–II ligament sprain (proprioceptive rehabilitation) · Achilles tendinopathy (tailored tendon-loading programme) · PTTD (strengthening + footwear; orthoses selectively) · Chronic ankle instability · Ankle OA (exercise programme)
Rheumatology
Gout refractory to ULT · Suspected inflammatory arthritis (RA, psoriatic, reactive) · Recurrent gout with tophi or renal stones · Gout with normal urate despite treatment
Podiatry
Orthotics or bracing considered selectively for PTTD, flat foot or ankle OA (with physiotherapy/orthotics) · Footwear advice · Callus/pressure point management in ankle OA · Diabetic foot assessment
The management of acute Achilles tendon rupture (conservative vs surgical) remains debated — the UKSTAR trial (Costa et al., Lancet 2020) showed that, for non-surgically treated ruptures, immediate weight-bearing in a functional brace (walking boot) was as good as a plaster cast, with better early patient-reported outcomes; non-operative treatment also avoids surgical complications such as wound infection and sural nerve injury, at the cost of a slightly higher re-rupture rate. Most UK orthopaedic centres now offer functional conservative management (immediate weight-bearing in equinus CAM boot) as the primary approach, reserving surgery for young athletes with high performance demands. The GP's role is to diagnose the rupture, arrange temporary immobilisation in plantar flexion (avoid dorsiflexion) or immediate orthopaedic referral, and arrange same-day review — not to decide on surgical vs conservative management.

Treat

GP Treatment by Diagnosis

Ankle sprain Grade I–II
PRICE + early mobilisation
Protection and relative rest; ice for comfort during the first 24–48 hours if helpful; compression if tolerated; elevation. Early, progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, range-of-motion exercises initially, then strengthening, balance and proprioceptive training. Lace-up brace for more significant sprains or sport. A short-term NSAID may be considered (not routinely) — caution with renal disease, ulcer disease, cardiovascular disease, anticoagulation, pregnancy, older age and interactions. Physiotherapy for persistent symptoms, recurrent sprain or instability.
Acute gout
NSAID, colchicine or oral corticosteroid
NICE NG219 (2022): offer an NSAID, colchicine or a short course of oral corticosteroid first-line, chosen by comorbidity, interactions and preference. Exclude septic arthritis before aspirating or injecting a joint. NSAID (e.g. naproxen, per local formulary): shortest effective duration, gastroprotection where indicated; avoid or seek advice in CKD, active or previous peptic ulcer disease, significant cardiovascular disease, anticoagulation and pregnancy; check interactions and renal function. Colchicine — dose per current product information and local formulary; do not prescribe without checking renal/hepatic function and interacting medicines (narrow therapeutic index, toxic in overdose). Cautions: renal or hepatic impairment, older age, clarithromycin, erythromycin and other potent CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein inhibitors, ciclosporin, statins (especially with renal impairment), blood dyscrasias, severe GI disease; stop and seek advice if significant nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain or diarrhoea. Short oral corticosteroid course (e.g. prednisolone) is the steroid option. Specific regimens depend on patient factors and local guidance. Do not stop ULT that is already established during a flare. Start new ULT 2–4 weeks after the flare settles (NG219 allows earlier starting if flares are frequent).
Achilles tendinopathy
Progressive tendon-loading rehabilitation
Isometric, heavy slow resistance or progressive eccentric–concentric loading (e.g. Alfredson eccentric heel drops) over about 12 weeks — supervised or tailored, particularly in older adults or with comorbidity. NSAIDs (short course only). Avoid corticosteroid injection into or around the Achilles tendon (rupture risk). Physiotherapy referral. A heel lift may temporarily reduce tendon load but is not universally required. Review fluoroquinolone exposure and avoid inappropriate prescribing.
Ankle OAExercise and weight management are first-line (NICE NG226, 2022). Consider a topical NSAID (e.g. diclofenac 1% gel TDS). Oral NSAID at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time if inadequate (with PPI). NG226: do not routinely offer paracetamol or weak opioids (unless infrequent short-term use when other options are unsuitable), do not offer strong opioids, and do not offer intra-articular hyaluronan. Avoid glucosamine. Orthotics and bracing selectively, preferably via physiotherapy, podiatry or orthotics — not routine first-line. Intra-articular corticosteroid may give short-term relief but is usually arranged through an MSK/orthopaedic service. Refer orthopaedics for persistent symptoms and functional limitation despite appropriate conservative management (ankle fusion or total ankle replacement).
Gout — ULTOffer ULT for multiple or troublesome flares, CKD stages 3–5, diuretic therapy, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis; discuss after a first flare (NICE NG219). Start 2–4 weeks after the flare settles (during a flare if flares are frequent); do not stop established ULT during a flare. Allopurinol first line (including major cardiovascular disease): start low, measure serum urate monthly and titrate to <360 μmol/L (consider <300 μmol/L for tophi, chronic gouty arthritis or continuing frequent flares). In CKD start lower and titrate cautiously, but do not automatically cap the dose below that needed to reach target; seek advice in complex renal impairment. Stop and seek urgent advice for rash or systemic hypersensitivity; consider severe cutaneous reaction risk (high-risk ethnic groups, renal impairment) and HLA-B*58:01 testing per local policy. Febuxostat is not interchangeable — consider cardiovascular disease and local restrictions. Colchicine (or NSAID) prophylaxis while reaching target — commonly 3–6 months, aligned with achieving and maintaining target urate. Annual urate once stable.
The Alfredson eccentric loading protocol for Achilles tendinopathy was developed in 1998 and is one of several evidence-based loading programmes — isometric and heavy slow resistance programmes are alternatives. Eccentric (lengthening) contractions specifically stimulate neovascularisation and collagen remodelling in the tendon. The programme requires compliance with twice daily exercises for 12 weeks — explaining the mechanism and time commitment to patients significantly improves adherence. Corticosteroid injection into or around the Achilles tendon should be avoided because of rupture risk. This is in contrast to plantar fasciitis (where steroid injection has a clear role). Starting allopurinol during an acute gout attack can prolong the attack — NICE NG219 (2022) advises starting ULT 2–4 weeks after the flare has settled (it can be started during a flare if flares are frequent), with prophylactic colchicine or NSAID cover for the first 3–6 months.

Treat

Immobilisation & Rehabilitation Principles

Confirmed fracture (non-displaced)
Below-knee plaster or CAM boot depending on fracture type and orthopaedic guidance. Non-weight-bearing crutches. Orthopaedic fracture clinic within 3–5 days. VTE and bleeding risk assessed via the local immobilisation pathway.
Ligament sprain — bracing
Lace-up ankle brace (superior to tubular bandage for Grade II–III). Allows protected weight-bearing while providing lateral stability. Preferable to plaster for Grade II sprains — early mobilisation reduces long-term stiffness. Continue for 6 weeks during sports.
Physiotherapy content
Phase 1 (0–72 hrs): PRICE, maintain ROM. Phase 2 (3 days–3 weeks): proprioception exercises, strengthening. Phase 3 (3–8 weeks): sport-specific training, return to running. Phase 4: return to sport when criteria are met (approximate phases, not fixed thresholds). Key: proprioceptive balance work reduces re-sprain risk.
VTE risk assessment
For patients whose treatment prevents normal weight-bearing or use of the limb, assess VTE and bleeding risk using the locally approved immobilisation pathway (a mobile patient in a removable boot who can weight-bear differs from a non-weight-bearing patient in a rigid cast). Risk factors: previous VTE, active cancer, pregnancy/postpartum, thrombophilia, major trauma or fracture, rigid cast and non-weight-bearing, obesity, significant immobility, oestrogen-containing contraception or HRT, age and comorbidity. Consider LMWH or fondaparinux only when VTE risk outweighs bleeding risk (NICE NG89); review if immobilisation continues beyond 42 days. The prescribing decision sits with the local orthopaedic/ED pathway.
VTE prophylaxis in lower limb immobilisation is a frequently neglected clinical duty in primary care. NICE NG89 (VTE prevention) advises that people whose lower-limb immobilisation prevents normal weight-bearing or use of the limb should have a VTE and bleeding risk assessment; it does not recommend automatic prophylaxis for every ankle injury, boot or plaster. NG89 advises considering LMWH or fondaparinux only when the VTE risk outweighs the bleeding risk, with review if immobilisation continues beyond 42 days. Whoever arranges immobilisation should document the VTE risk assessment. Prophylactic LMWH doses are fixed, not the weight-based treatment doses — dose per BNF (for example enoxaparin 40 mg OD subcutaneously).

Lifestyle

Rehabilitation, Prevention & Gout Lifestyle

Proprioceptive training Balance board/wobble board exercises after ankle sprain reduce re-sprain risk. Single-leg balance (eyes open then closed), progressively on unstable surface. Continue for 12 weeks post-sprain. Key to preventing chronic ankle instability.
Footwear advice Appropriate supportive footwear for ankle tendinopathy, OA, and PTTD. A heel lift may temporarily reduce Achilles tendon load in tendinopathy — not universally required. Avoid flat pumps (no heel support). Motion control shoes for flat foot. Orthotics selectively via podiatry/orthotics.
Gout — dietary modification Reduce: red meat, shellfish (high purine), alcohol (especially beer), fructose-sweetened drinks. Increase: low-fat dairy (uricosuric), cherries (reduce urate and inflammation), water (2+ litres/day promotes urate excretion). These modifications modestly reduce urate — insufficient alone for most patients but important alongside ULT.
Gout — medication review Review diuretics (thiazides, loop diuretics raise urate — switch if possible). Low-dose aspirin (raises urate — usually cannot stop but document). Ciclosporin, tacrolimus (post-transplant — raise urate). Losartan is the antihypertensive of choice in gout (mildly uricosuric).
Weight management Obesity worsens ankle OA (load), gout (elevated urate due to increased purine turnover), and tendinopathy (increased load). Weight management is recommended where appropriate in OA (NICE NG226).
Return to sport Criteria-based rather than fixed timescales: pain-free walking and hopping, near-full range of movement, strength comparable with the other side, no significant swelling, and ability to perform sport-specific tasks. Consider external ankle bracing when returning to sport.
Proprioceptive rehabilitation is the most important component of ankle sprain management for preventing recurrence — yet it is rarely prescribed in primary care, where PRICE (Protection, Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) is often the only advice given. Recurrence is common without proprioceptive rehabilitation and is reduced by a structured balance training programme. The mechanism is restoration of ankle proprioceptive afferent signalling — ligament injury disrupts the mechanoreceptors in the ligament, impairing balance and increasing the risk of re-injury. Balance board training retrains the central nervous system to compensate. This takes 12 weeks of consistent daily practice. Gout medication review is often the most impactful intervention — thiazide diuretics (bendroflumethiazide, hydrochlorothiazide) significantly raise serum urate and are a major treatable contributor to recurrent gout. Switching to an alternative antihypertensive (amlodipine, losartan — the latter is mildly uricosuric) can reduce gout frequency dramatically without ULT.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Sprain — 1–2 weeks / 4–6 weeks
Reassess if worsening, unable to weight-bear or not improving over 1–2 weeks. Review persistent pain or functional limitation at 4–6 weeks: re-examine, X-ray if not already done, and consider imaging for peroneal tendon or osteochondral injury where indicated. Refer MSK physiotherapy if not already referred.
Gout — 4 weeks post-attack
Recheck serum urate (after attack fully settled). Offer ULT (NICE NG219) for multiple or troublesome flares, CKD stages 3–5, diuretic therapy, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis; discuss it with everyone after a first flare. Titrate allopurinol with monthly serum urate to target. Annual urate monitoring once stable.
Achilles tendinopathy — 12 weeks
Eccentric programme completed? Symptoms improving? If no improvement after about 12 weeks of a compliant loading programme → orthopaedics/MSK specialist referral (imaging as directed) for further management options (PRP injection, ESWT — extracorporeal shockwave therapy).
Stress fracture
Pain improving? Reassess earlier if worsening or unable to weight-bear; MRI/referral according to site and risk (a normal repeat X-ray does not exclude a stress fracture). Low-trauma insufficiency fracture → assess for osteoporosis and secondary causes; DEXA is not automatic for every athletic stress injury.
999 / A&E safety-net
New unilateral calf swelling, pain, warmth or pitting oedema → DVT assessment using the two-level Wells score and the local DVT pathway; chest pain, breathlessness, coughing blood, collapse or a racing heart → urgent PE assessment. Sudden "snap" or loss of push-off at the back of the ankle (Achilles rupture). New inability to weight-bear after previous weight-bearing was possible (stress fracture completion)
Same-day GP

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) for chronic refractory Achilles tendinopathy delivers acoustic shockwaves through the skin to stimulate neovascularisation and collagen remodelling in the tendon, usually as an outpatient course once conservative treatment (including eccentric loading) has failed. NICE HTG426 (formerly IPG571, 2016) found no major safety concerns but inconsistent, limited efficacy evidence, so it should only be used with special arrangements for clinical governance, consent and audit or research. It is not available in all areas but can be accessed via physiotherapy MSK services or private. PRP (platelet-rich plasma) injection for tendinopathy shows promising early results in trials but is not currently funded by NHS for most indications — it may be available privately. The key principle: if around 12 weeks of a compliant loading programme fails, referral to a specialist MSK service is warranted rather than simply continuing the same management.
