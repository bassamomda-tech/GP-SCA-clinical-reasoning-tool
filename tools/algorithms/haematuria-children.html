Safety

Red Flags — AKI, Vasculitis, Malignancy & Trauma

Haematuria + oedema (periorbital or peripheral) + hypertension + proteinuria + oliguria Nephrotic-nephritic syndrome. Causes: post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy, MPGN, lupus nephritis. → Same-day hospital assessment for suspected AKI, oliguria, severe hypertension, pulmonary oedema, significant oedema, nephrotic-range proteinuria, systemic illness or rapidly worsening renal function. 999 only if clinically unstable, severe hypertension with neurological/cardiopulmonary symptoms, respiratory compromise or altered consciousness. Check BP, creatinine/eGFR, serum albumin, fluid status, uPCR, urine microscopy; complement and other tests when glomerulonephritis is suspected (specialist-directed).
Haematuria + purpuric rash (non-blanching) on buttocks and lower legs + arthritis + abdominal pain IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura, HSP). Microscopic urinary abnormalities are common; clinically significant nephritis is less frequent. → Urgent paediatric assessment. Renal function + uPCR + BP. Urgent nephrology assessment with hypertension, rising creatinine, significant or increasing proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome, persistent or recurrent macroscopic haematuria, reduced urine output, severe abdominal pain or GI bleeding, or testicular pain/swelling.
Haematuria in a child with recent sore throat (1-3 weeks ago) or impetigo Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN). Latent period: 1-3 weeks post-throat infection, 3-6 weeks post-skin infection. → Paediatric assessment: urinalysis/microscopy, uPCR, creatinine/eGFR and electrolytes, serum albumin, C3 (typically low; ± C4), ASOT and/or anti-DNase B (ASOT may be negative — not used in isolation), throat/skin assessment if infection active. Nephritic syndrome, hypertension, AKI, oedema or significant proteinuria → same-day paediatric assessment.
Abdominal mass, enlarged abdominal organ or unexplained visible haematuria Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) — most common renal tumour in children. → very urgent paediatric referral, usually within 48 hours — not automatically 999 unless the child is unstable, obstructed, in severe pain or acutely unwell; NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) asks for very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours) for a palpable abdominal mass, an unexplained enlarged abdominal organ or unexplained visible haematuria in a child. Renal USS urgently only if it does not delay paediatric referral. Do NOT palpate the abdomen repeatedly (tumour rupture risk). Further staging imaging is specialist-led.
Haematuria after significant trauma (RTA, fall from height, sports injury) Renal trauma — laceration, contusion, vascular injury. Significant trauma or suspected internal bleeding → emergency trauma assessment (999 if unstable). Management depends on haemodynamic stability, mechanism, abdominal/flank tenderness, associated injuries, degree of haematuria and suspected renal or urethral injury; imaging per the trauma pathway — CT is not automatic for every post-traumatic haematuria.
Persistent glomerular haematuria + family history of haematuria/renal failure + hearing loss Alport syndrome (X-linked or autosomal recessive — COL4A3/4/5 mutations affecting glomerular basement membrane type IV collagen). → Paediatric nephrology referral; genetic testing for COL4A3, COL4A4 and COL4A5 via specialist/genetic services; audiology and ophthalmology assessment; family assessment and genetic counselling. Renal biopsy only if genetic testing is unavailable or inconclusive, or an alternative diagnosis is suspected. Prognosis varies by genotype and phenotype.
Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) is the most important malignant cause of haematuria in children — it is the most common renal tumour in childhood, occurring predominantly between ages 3-4 years (range 1-8 years). The classic presentation is an abdominal mass discovered incidentally by a parent or during examination, often with haematuria. The cardinal rule: do not repeatedly palpate the abdomen if Wilms tumour is suspected — the tumour has a fibrous capsule that can rupture with vigorous palpation, causing intraperitoneal bleeding and tumour dissemination. After a single careful examination that identifies an abdominal mass, refer very urgently (usually within 48 hours, NICE NG12) without further abdominal palpation and document the finding. Prognosis for Wilms tumour is generally good with modern treatment (surgery + chemotherapy ± radiotherapy for high-stage disease). Early identification is the key GP contribution.

Classification of Haematuria in Children

Macroscopic vs microscopic haematuria
Macroscopic (gross) haematuria: visible red, pink, brown, or cola-coloured urine — should generally be investigated; urgency depends on associated findings (with AKI, hypertension, oedema or significant proteinuria → urgent hospital/nephrology; with abdominal mass → very urgent paediatric referral; unexplained without systemic features → urgent paediatric assessment, not automatically 999). Microscopic haematuria: dipstick is a screening test — confirm persistent haematuria with repeat urinalysis and, where clinically indicated, microscopy; interpret alongside BP, proteinuria, renal function, symptoms and family history. Use the local paediatric nephrology definition of persistent microscopic haematuria — a single positive dipstick is not persistent haematuria. Isolated asymptomatic microscopic haematuria in a child: less urgent but requires structured follow-up.
Dipstick interpretation pitfalls
False positive haematuria (red urine without RBCs): beetroot, blackberries, rifampicin, ibuprofen, phenytoin. Myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis) and haemoglobinuria (haemolysis) cause positive dipstick without true haematuria — confirm with urine microscopy (no RBCs on microscopy = false positive). Haemoglobin/myoglobin: both test positive on dipstick. False positives also occur with concentrated urine or contamination (including menstrual/vaginal). Confirm with repeat urinalysis and, where indicated, microscopy.
Glomerular vs non-glomerular pattern
Glomerular haematuria: dysmorphic RBCs on microscopy (acanthocytes — ring-shaped RBCs with membrane blebbing = highly specific), RBC casts (pathognomonic), proteinuria, brown/cola urine (haemoglobin oxidation in tubules), no clots (clots cannot form in tubular flow). Non-glomerular haematuria: normal-shaped RBCs, bright red/pink urine, clots possible, minimal proteinuria, pain or dysuria often present. This distinction guides the differential. Acanthocytes and red-cell casts support glomerular disease but are not always available or reliably interpreted in primary care — do not require microscopy before referring a child with obvious nephritic features.
The acanthocyte test is the most specific microscopic finding for glomerular haematuria — acanthocytes are red blood cells with irregular membrane protrusions ('blebs' or 'spicules') that form as the cells are squeezed through damaged glomerular basement membrane. They are best seen on phase-contrast microscopy; on standard light microscopy they appear as ring-shaped or crenated cells. A high proportion of acanthocytes is specific, but not sensitive, for glomerular disease. The presence of RBC casts is even more specific — these are red cells embedded in a Tamm-Horsfall protein matrix that forms in the renal tubules, confirming that the bleeding is intra-glomerular. Dipstick haematuria with visible acanthocytes or RBC casts on urine microscopy is a strong indication for urgent nephrology referral. GPs can request urine microscopy when glomerular haematuria is suspected, where available — but this must not delay referral of obvious nephritic features.

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

Initial GP assessment
1) Confirm true haematuria and exclude pigment/contamination. 2) Measure BP with an appropriate cuff; interpret by age, sex and height. 3) Dipstick for blood, protein, leucocytes and nitrites. 4) Send urine culture where UTI is possible. 5) Quantify proteinuria with uPCR when dipstick protein is positive or glomerular disease suspected. 6) Creatinine/eGFR and electrolytes if proteinuria, hypertension, oedema, macroscopic haematuria or systemic illness. 7) Review family history, recent infections, trauma, medicines and travel. 8) Refer according to the renal/urological pathway rather than ordering broad panels routinely.
History
Urine colour: red (fresh blood — lower tract or upper tract), brown/cola (glomerular — oxidised Hb), pink (dilute macroscopic haematuria). Pain: flank pain/renal colic (stone, UPJ obstruction, clot), suprapubic (cystitis), painless (glomerulonephritis, tumour). Frequency/dysuria: UTI. Timing: at start of stream (urethral), end of stream (bladder neck), throughout (glomerular or upper tract). Proteinuria: oedema, frothy urine. Recent throat or skin infection (PSGN — 1-3 week latency). Exercise (exercise-related haematuria — a diagnosis of exclusion; visible haematuria is not automatically benign). Trauma. Family history: haematuria, deafness, renal failure, kidney stones. Medications (rifampicin, NSAIDs, cyclophosphamide). Travel (schistosomiasis — endemic area + haematuria).
Examination
BP with an appropriate cuff, interpreted by age, sex and height (hypertension in GN, HSP nephritis, AKI). Oedema (periorbital on waking = nephrotic; ankle = older children). Skin: purpuric rash (HSP — buttocks/legs), pallor (haemolysis/anaemia in MPGN). Abdomen: mass (Wilms — do not repeatedly palpate), tenderness (UTI, stone), ascites (nephrotic). ENT: sensorineural hearing loss (Alport syndrome). Eyes: anterior lenticonus (Alport — characteristically anterior conical lens protrusion seen on ophthalmoscopy). Throat/skin (streptococcal infection source).
Investigations
Urine dipstick (blood, protein, leucocytes, nitrites) · Urine culture where UTI is possible (NICE NG224) · Microscopy where clinically indicated · Urine protein:creatinine ratio (uPCR) if dipstick protein positive or glomerular disease suspected — interpret with age-specific laboratory ranges and the local paediatric nephrology pathway · Renal function + electrolytes if proteinuria, hypertension, oedema, macroscopic haematuria or systemic illness · BP measurement (mandatory) · Renal USS not routine for isolated microscopic haematuria — use for persistent/recurrent macroscopic haematuria, abnormal renal function, proteinuria or hypertension, suspected stones, recurrent febrile UTI, structural urinary symptoms, family history of inherited renal disease, abnormal examination · FBC not routine for isolated microscopic haematuria · Spot urine calcium:creatinine ratio if hypercalciuria suspected · Specialist-directed by clinical pattern: ASOT/anti-DNase B, complement C3/C4, ANA, ANCA, anti-dsDNA, genetic testing (Alport).
The urine protein:creatinine ratio (uPCR) is the most clinically useful quantification of proteinuria in children — it correlates well with 24-hour urine protein excretion and is far more practical than a timed urine collection. Interpret uPCR using age-specific laboratory ranges and the local paediatric nephrology pathway: above approximately 20 mg/mmol is abnormal in many paediatric pathways; above 100 mg/mmol is significant and should prompt urgent clinical assessment, particularly with haematuria, hypertension or impaired renal function, but is not "nephrotic range"; nephrotic-range proteinuria is generally around 200 mg/mmol or more, or per the local definition. Do not use uPCR alone to determine urgency — check BP, creatinine/eGFR, serum albumin, fluid status and urine microscopy. Significant proteinuria with haematuria, hypertension, oedema or reduced renal function warrants urgent paediatric/nephrology advice. Isolated haematuria (without proteinuria, hypertension, or renal impairment) has a much broader differential and more benign prognosis — thin basement membrane disease, IgA nephropathy (mild), exercise-induced, or benign familial haematuria. The combination of haematuria + proteinuria is the most important investigation result in paediatric haematuria assessment.

Differential Diagnosis by Clinical Pattern

Glomerular
IgA vasculitis nephritis; post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis; IgA nephropathy; Alport syndrome/COL4A3–5 disease; thin basement membrane disease; lupus nephritis; complement-mediated glomerulopathy; ANCA-associated vasculitis; atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome. IgA nephropathy: a common glomerulonephritis in children. Episodic macroscopic haematuria 1-3 days after URTI (synpharyngitic haematuria — unlike PSGN, occurs simultaneously with the infection rather than 1-3 weeks later). Diagnosis: renal biopsy (IgA deposits in mesangium). PSGN: 1-3 weeks post-streptococcal throat, low C3, raised ASOT/anti-DNase B. Usually self-limiting. Alport syndrome: persistent microscopic haematuria + proteinuria + family history + deafness. Lupus nephritis: ANA/dsDNA positive + multisystem features.
Upper urinary tract / non-glomerular
UTI/pyelonephritis. Hypercalciuria — confirm with a spot urine calcium:creatinine ratio interpreted by age-specific reference ranges (24-hour collection rarely practical in young children; specialist-directed). Urinary calculi (calcium oxalate, cystinuria). Structural anomaly/UPJ obstruction (hydronephrosis + haematuria after exercise); renal cyst (ADPKD). Renal trauma. Renal tumour. Renal infarction (rare).
Lower urinary tract
Cystitis. Urethritis. Bladder or urethral trauma (catheterisation). Foreign body or local genital source. Bladder lesion (haemangioma, rhabdomyosarcoma — rare). Schistosomiasis (S. haematobium — return from Africa/Middle East + painless macroscopic haematuria).
Mimics
Beetroot and food pigments; rifampicin; haemoglobinuria; myoglobinuria; menstrual/vaginal contamination; porphyria (rare).
Benign / exclusion diagnoses
Exercise-related haematuria: a diagnosis of exclusion — repeat testing after rest only if the child is otherwise well and the history typical; persistent, recurrent or unexplained visible haematuria needs assessment. Benign familial haematuria / thin basement membrane disease: persistent microscopic haematuria + family history of isolated haematuria + normal renal function + no proteinuria — follow up annually.
Hypercalciuria is the most common cause of persistent isolated microscopic haematuria in children in the absence of proteinuria or renal impairment — it is a recognised finding in children with isolated microscopic haematuria when properly investigated. The mechanism: excess urinary calcium crystallises in the tubules and collecting system, causing microtrauma to the urothelium and red cell leak. The diagnosis: spot urine calcium:creatinine ratio interpreted by age-specific reference ranges. Confirmed hypercalciuria with no other cause of haematuria: do not restrict dietary calcium (risks bone density effects); reduce excess dietary sodium; normal age-appropriate fluids; thiazide diuretics only if directed by paediatric nephrology for recurrent calcium stones or symptomatic hypercalciuria. Paediatric nephrology referral for stone-forming hypercalciuria or persistent unexplained haematuria.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day paediatric hospital (999 only if unstable)
Haematuria with rising creatinine or oliguria · Haematuria with severe hypertension or neurological symptoms · Nephritic/nephrotic syndrome · Significant oedema or respiratory compromise · Suspected renal trauma with significant mechanism or instability · Haematuria with suspected acute abdomen · Testicular torsion symptoms · Severe HSP abdominal or scrotal complications
Very urgent (within 48 h, NICE NG12)
Palpable abdominal mass · Unexplained enlarged abdominal organ · Unexplained visible haematuria (possible Wilms tumour)
Urgent paediatric nephrology / paediatrics
Haematuria plus persistent proteinuria · Haematuria plus hypertension · Persistent or recurrent macroscopic haematuria · Suspected PSGN · Suspected IgA nephritis · Family history of haematuria, CKD or hearing loss · Suspected Alport syndrome · Recurrent stones or hypercalciuria with symptoms · Abnormal renal ultrasound
Paediatric urology
Renal mass on USS · Suspected stone disease with haematuria + renal colic · UPJ obstruction + hydronephrosis · Recurrent macroscopic haematuria + abnormal renal USS
GP-initiated investigations + follow-up
Isolated asymptomatic microscopic haematuria (persistent by the local definition) with normal BP, no proteinuria, normal renal function, no family history and no structural urinary symptoms: repeat urinalysis, BP and uPCR with clinical monitoring per the local paediatric nephrology pathway — renal USS and FBC are not routine. Refer if proteinuria, hypertension, renal impairment or other features develop.
Haematology / oncology
Sickle cell disease (trait or disease) causing haematuria → haematology. Suspected bladder/renal tumour → paediatric oncology.
The isolated asymptomatic microscopic haematuria management pathway in children is defined by the British Association of Paediatric Nephrology (BAPN) guidance — it requires structured investigation and long-term follow-up rather than immediate nephrology referral. Confirm persistence on repeat urine samples (to exclude transient causes — exercise, menstruation, minor UTI) using the local definition; if persistent, check uPCR and BP ± renal function, reserving ultrasound for specific indications; if all are normal, regular GP review per the local paediatric nephrology pathway is usually sufficient. The key safety-net: escalate to paediatric nephrology if at any annual review the child develops proteinuria (uPCR rising above 20 mg/mmol), hypertension, or renal impairment — these features suggest glomerular disease and need paediatric nephrology assessment.

Treat

Managing Specific Causes

UTI with haematuria (NICE NG224)
Obtain urine before antibiotics where possible, using clean-catch where possible. Send culture for suspected pyelonephritis, recurrent UTI, high/intermediate risk of serious illness, age under 3 months, positive dipstick where indicated, or treatment failure. Refer all babies under 3 months with suspected UTI to paediatric specialist care. Do not attribute haematuria to UTI without a properly collected urine sample and appropriate clinical features; visible haematuria that persists or recurs after successful treatment needs further assessment.
Post-streptococcal GN (PSGN)
Largely self-limiting in children. Children with nephritic syndrome, hypertension, AKI, oedema or significant proteinuria need paediatric assessment — fluid, salt and antihypertensive management is guided by paediatrics/nephrology, not a fixed GP regimen. Antibiotics (e.g. penicillin V) eradicate active streptococcal infection but do not reverse established immune-complex nephritis. Complement C3 normalises within 6-8 weeks (if still low at 8 weeks: consider MPGN — nephrology). Renal function: normalises in majority within weeks. Longer-term BP + uPCR follow-up per the paediatric/local pathway.
HSP (IgA vasculitis) nephritis
Mild nephritis (microscopic haematuria + low-level proteinuria by age-specific ranges, normal BP, normal creatinine): GP or community paediatrics follow-up with regular urinalysis and BP, frequency per the local paediatric pathway. If worsening: nephrology. Significant or increasing proteinuria, hypertension or rising creatinine → urgent paediatric nephrology advice; biopsy and immunosuppression are specialist decisions.
Hypercalciuria
Confirm with spot urine calcium:creatinine ratio (age-specific ranges). Reduce excess dietary sodium. Encourage normal age-appropriate fluids. Avoid excessive sugary drinks and cola, and excessive vitamin D supplementation. Dietary calcium: do NOT restrict (risks bone density). Thiazide or potassium citrate only if directed by paediatric nephrology/urology. Refer for recurrent stones, hypercalciuria with haematuria, nephrocalcinosis or impaired renal function.
Benign familial haematuria / thin basement membrane disease
Reassurance: no treatment required. Normal renal function and normal prognosis in most cases. Regular BP + uPCR + renal function per the local pathway (a minority develop proteinuria or renal impairment in adulthood — Alport spectrum). Family members should be assessed (first-degree relatives with haematuria or renal impairment). Genetic testing via specialist/genetic services if Alport syndrome suspected (hearing loss, proteinuria, family history of kidney failure).
IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease) management in children has evolved significantly with the TESTING trial (2022) and STOP-IgAN trial data — the key finding is that ACE inhibitors/ARBs (ramipril or losartan) are the cornerstone of treatment for IgA nephropathy with proteinuria, providing both antiproteinuric and nephroprotective effects independent of blood pressure reduction. In usual paediatric nephrology practice an ACE inhibitor is started for children with IgA nephropathy and persistent significant proteinuria (threshold set by the specialist team). Corticosteroid use in IgA nephropathy is based largely on adult trial data and is a specialist decision. For children with significant IgA nephropathy, ACE inhibitor ± corticosteroid treatment is initiated and monitored by paediatric nephrology — not by the GP.

IgA Vasculitis (HSP) — Acute Management

Acute HSP management in primary care
Non-nephritis HSP (skin rash + arthritis + abdominal pain, no or mild renal involvement): symptomatic management. Weight-based NSAID (e.g. ibuprofen per BNFC) for arthritis pain only if no contraindication — avoid with AKI, significant proteinuria or renal impairment, dehydration, GI bleeding, severe abdominal symptoms or other NSAID contraindications. Adequate hydration. Encourage activity according to symptoms (routine bed rest not needed). Prednisolone: not routinely recommended. Corticosteroids may be used by paediatric teams for severe abdominal pain, GI involvement, orchitis or significant renal disease — they do not replace assessment for intussusception and are not prophylaxis against it.
Renal monitoring in HSP
Arrange regular urinalysis and BP monitoring for at least 6 months after IgA vasculitis, with frequency set by the local paediatric pathway (often more frequent in the first weeks); extend follow-up where renal abnormalities persist or appear late. Most HSP nephritis is mild and self-limiting. Any haematuria or proteinuria developing after the rash → repeat uPCR, BP and renal function. Rising creatinine, hypertension or significant proteinuria → urgent nephrology advice.
Surgical emergencies in HSP
Intussusception (ileoileal — rare form, unlike ileocolic in other contexts): severe abdominal pain + bloody stool + vomiting. → same-day paediatric surgical assessment (999 if unstable). USS abdomen urgently. Surgical referral if USS confirms intussusception (ileoileal intussusception does not reduce with air enema — surgical). Testicular torsion: can be a rare complication of HSP vasculitis in boys — scrotal pain + swelling → immediate surgical assessment (time-critical; ultrasound must not delay referral; 999 if unable to arrange immediate transfer).
HSP recurrence
HSP can recur, usually more mildly. Renal involvement can appear late. GP should maintain urine and BP surveillance for at least 6 months after the initial episode, per the local paediatric pathway. Any new rash with worsening renal parameters: nephrology review.
The testicular involvement in HSP deserves explicit mention because testicular torsion can complicate HSP and presents a surgical emergency — the vasculitis of HSP can affect the small vessels of the testes, causing scrotal pain and swelling that can be confused with epididymo-orchitis. However, HSP-associated scrotal pain may also indicate true testicular torsion (which occurs at the same age group as HSP — school-age boys). Any HSP patient presenting with scrotal pain must have testicular torsion excluded urgently — an immediate surgical opinion (salvage of a torted testis is time-critical; ultrasound must not delay referral). A 'wait and see' approach to scrotal pain in any male of any age is never acceptable if torsion cannot be excluded clinically.

Lifestyle

Fluid Intake, Diet, Exercise & Long-Term Monitoring

Fluid intake for haematuria prevention Encourage normal age-appropriate fluids — requirements vary with age, body size, climate, diet, illness and renal status, so there is no universal target. Encourage water as the main drink; avoid excessive cola, energy drinks and sugary drinks. Pale urine is a practical guide.
Exercise and macroscopic haematuria Strenuous exercise can cause transient haematuria, but this is a diagnosis of exclusion — visible haematuria should not automatically be labelled benign. Repeat testing after rest only if the child is otherwise well and the history is typical; persistent, recurrent or unexplained visible haematuria requires assessment. Do not routinely restrict sport in isolated microscopic haematuria; restrictions in Alport syndrome, active nephritis, nephrotic syndrome or renal trauma are individualised by the renal team.
Stone prevention in hypercalciuric children Key dietary advice: reduce excess dietary sodium (processed foods, crisps, salt added at table). Normal age-appropriate fluids. Calcium: maintain dietary intake (do NOT restrict). Avoid excessive sugary drinks and cola. Vitamin D supplementation: avoid supra-physiological doses. Paediatric nephrology/urology review for recurrent stones, hypercalciuria with haematuria, nephrocalcinosis or impaired renal function.
Monitoring for Alport syndrome progression Some forms, particularly severe X-linked and autosomal-recessive disease, carry a high lifetime risk of progressive CKD; prognosis varies by genotype, phenotype and treatment and should be discussed by paediatric nephrology/genetics. ACE inhibitor initiation and timing are directed by paediatric nephrology (based on albuminuria/proteinuria, genotype, renal function, BP and local protocol), with monitoring of BP, creatinine/eGFR, potassium and uPCR or albumin:creatinine ratio. Shared care per specialist plan, including audiology (progressive SNHL) and genetic counselling for the family.
Diet in nephrotic syndrome / GN Fluid and sodium restriction are prescribed by paediatric nephrology according to oedema, serum sodium, renal function, urine output, blood pressure and nephrotic versus nephritic state — not as general primary-care advice. Do not restrict protein unless directed by specialist renal dietetics. Once proteinuria resolves: normal diet.
School management for children with renal conditions Children with chronic glomerulonephritis, Alport, or post-GN renal impairment may need: Individual Healthcare Plan (IHP) for medication administration at school (BP medications, immunosuppressants), awareness of signs of relapse (facial swelling, change in urine colour), sport/PE restrictions only if individualised by the renal team (e.g. Alport, active nephritis, nephrotic syndrome, renal trauma). Regular urine dipstick by school nurse if agreed. Liaison with hospital nephrology team and school SENCO.
Psychological support for children with renal diagnoses A diagnosis of significant glomerulonephritis or Alport syndrome is frightening for children and families — particularly when ESRD or transplant is discussed. Paediatric psychologist support via nephrology team. RCPCH and Kidney Care UK provide patient information resources. NKF (National Kidney Federation) youth network. Normalisation: "Most children with this condition can live normal active lives with treatment."
HSP — lifestyle during active disease Encourage activity according to symptoms (routine bed rest not needed). Keep well hydrated. Return to school when well enough. No specific dietary restrictions. Weight-based NSAIDs for arthritis only if no renal, GI or other contraindication. Sport restrictions with nephritis are individualised by the renal team. Return immediately if: severe abdominal pain, vomiting blood, inability to walk, significant scrotal pain.
Registry data (European Alport Registry, Gross et al, Kidney International 2012) suggest that earlier ACE inhibitor therapy delays kidney failure in Alport syndrome — but initiation, timing and dosing are directed by paediatric nephrology according to genotype, proteinuria, renal function and BP. The mechanism: GFR loss in Alport syndrome is driven by glomerular hypertension from the abnormal GBM, and ACE inhibitor-mediated reduction in intraglomerular pressure provides nephroprotection. GPs who identify persistent microscopic haematuria in a boy with a family history of hearing loss, haematuria, or renal failure should have a very low threshold for referral to paediatric nephrology — early specialist diagnosis allows timely treatment decisions.

Safety

Follow-Up Schedule & Safety-Netting

Isolated asymptomatic microscopic haematuria — annual review
Annual: BP (hypertension = refer urgently) + urine dipstick + uPCR + renal function (creatinine). If any abnormal result develops: paediatric nephrology referral. Frequency and duration of monitoring per the local paediatric nephrology pathway.
PSGN — follow-up
Per the paediatric/local pathway: urinalysis, BP and creatinine until resolved. C3 complement rechecked at 6-8 weeks (should normalise — if not, consider another glomerulonephritis such as MPGN — nephrology). Longer-term BP + uPCR follow-up (late hypertension in a small proportion).
HSP — renal surveillance
Regular urinalysis and BP for at least 6 months, frequency per the local paediatric pathway; extend if renal abnormalities persist or appear late. If nephritis develops: repeat uPCR, BP and renal function + urgent nephrology advice. If urine clear at the end of surveillance: discharge with advice to re-present if rash recurs.
Children on ACE inhibitors for renal disease
Initiated and directed by paediatric nephrology. Monitor BP, creatinine/eGFR, potassium and uPCR or albumin:creatinine ratio per the specialist plan. Growth monitoring (ACE inhibitors do not affect growth but renal disease itself may impair growth velocity).
Same-day hospital
Haematuria + AKI + oliguria · Haematuria + severe hypertension or neurological symptoms · Nephritic/nephrotic syndrome · HSP + severe abdominal pain + vomiting blood (intussusception) or scrotal pain — 999 only if unstable, respiratory compromise or altered consciousness. Abdominal mass or unexplained visible haematuria (Wilms) → very urgent referral, usually within 48 hours (NICE NG12)
Urgent advice
New or increasing proteinuria (age-specific ranges), hypertension or rising creatinine at any review → paediatric nephrology · IgA vasculitis with rising creatinine, hypertension or significant proteinuria → urgent nephrology advice · Persistent or recurrent visible haematuria without clear cause, or persisting after UTI treatment → paediatrics/nephrology/urology
The annual follow-up of isolated asymptomatic microscopic haematuria in children is a long-term commitment that requires proactive recall and documentation — many children with 'benign' isolated haematuria (thin basement membrane disease, mild IgA nephropathy, hypercalciuria) will never develop clinical problems, but a minority will develop proteinuria, hypertension, or renal impairment over the following years. The GP's role is to maintain a safety net: a child who was labelled 'isolated microscopic haematuria — benign' at age 8 and never reviewed again, who presents at age 22 with stage 3 CKD, represents a missed opportunity for specialist nephroprotective treatment. The recall system should be integrated into the practice's chronic disease register or coded appropriately (SNOMED CT haematuria code) to ensure the annual check is flagged.
