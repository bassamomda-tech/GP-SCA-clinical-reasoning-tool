1
Safety

Red Flags — Disseminated Gonorrhoea, Malignancy & Retention

Penile discharge requires a sexual history and STI screen before treatment. Never prescribe antibiotics without a history — you risk treatment failure and missed partner notification.

Discharge + fever + arthralgia + skin pustules on palms/soles Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI) — tenosynovitis + dermatitis + arthritis triad. 0.5–3% of untreated gonorrhoea. → 999 + IV ceftriaxone same day.
Penile mass or ulcer — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Consider a suspected cancer pathway referral (2WW) for penile cancer if there is a penile mass or ulcerated lesion when an STI has been excluded, a persistent penile lesion after STI treatment has been completed, or unexplained or persistent symptoms affecting the foreskin or glans. → 2WW urology
Discharge + sudden inability to void + previous gonorrhoea or instrumentation Urethral stricture → acute retention = 999. Progressive hesitancy + poor stream + dribbling → urgent urology.
Purulent discharge + suspected cephalosporin resistance (sex abroad, e.g. Asia-Pacific, or a contact of a resistant case) Possible extensively drug-resistant gonorrhoea. Same-day GUM so that cultures are taken before treatment and the regimen is guided by susceptibility.
Discharge + acute unilateral scrotal pain and swelling Epididymo-orchitis vs testicular torsion. If torsion is possible (sudden severe pain, especially under 25s, high-riding testis, absent cremasteric reflex) → immediate emergency urology referral — do not wait for a scan. Epididymo-orchitis with STI risk → same-day GUM.
Painless penile/oral ulcer + inguinal lymphadenopathy + sexual risk history Primary syphilis chancre. Syphilis serology (treponemal antibody, with RPR if reactive) and GUM same day for dark-ground/PCR testing of the ulcer. UK syphilis diagnoses are at their highest level since 1948 (UKHSA 2024).
How to use this step
Most men with discharge have an uncomplicated STI; these red flags pick out the few who need same-day hospital, same-day GUM or a suspected cancer pathway. Ask specifically about fever, joint or skin symptoms, scrotal pain, difficulty passing urine and any lump or ulcer on the penis.
Sexual assault
If the history suggests assault, offer referral to a sexual assault referral centre (SARC) — forensic samples, HIV post-exposure prophylaxis and emergency support are time-critical.
Cases of ceftriaxone-resistant gonorrhoea, several linked to travel, have been reported in England, which is why BASHH (gonorrhoea guideline 2025) keeps ceftriaxone 1 g IM as sole first-line therapy and asks for a gonococcal culture before treatment wherever possible, so that susceptibility can be tested. Ciprofloxacin is used only when the isolate is known to be susceptible. This is the practical reason suspected gonorrhoea is managed in GUM rather than with oral antibiotics in general practice. Epididymo-orchitis vs torsion: epididymo-orchitis is usually more gradual, with a tender, swollen epididymis behind the testis, often with discharge or dysuria; torsion is sudden and severe, often with nausea. No single clinical sign reliably excludes torsion and imaging must not delay surgical exploration, so any real doubt means emergency referral. Penile cancer is uncommon and usually affects older men, but a lesion that persists after STI treatment is exactly the scenario NG12 targets.

Diagnose

Classification — Causes of Penile Discharge

Urethral — STI
Gonorrhoea (profuse purulent, usually 2–7 days post-exposure) · NGU: Chlamydia, Mycoplasma genitalium (15–25% of NGU; macrolide resistance common in UK strains), Trichomonas, adenovirus and HSV (the latter two often with meatitis or conjunctivitis) · Around half of men with chlamydia have no symptoms
Non-STI urethral
Post-catheter/instrumentation urethritis · Chemical (soaps, lubricants) · Reactive arthritis (urethritis + arthritis + conjunctivitis) · Prostatitis (perineal pain + systemic features) · UTI (uncommon young men — investigate structural cause)
Preputial / glans
Candidal balanitis (white cheesy under foreskin, itch, erythema — check HbA1c) · Bacterial/anaerobic balanitis (phimosis, foul-smelling) · Zoon's plasma cell balanitis (bright red glazed patches — biopsy required) · Contact dermatitis
Chronic
Chronic prostatitis / CPPS · Urethral stricture (dribbling + poor stream) · Lichen sclerosus (BXO) / phimosis-related smegma accumulation
Discriminating features
Timing: discharge within a week of a new partner favours gonorrhoea; 1–3 weeks favours chlamydia/NGU. Character: copious yellow-green pus favours gonorrhoea; scant clear or mucoid discharge, often only first thing in the morning, favours NGU. Site: discharge from under the foreskin rather than the meatus points to balanoposthitis. Associated: conjunctivitis and joint pain suggest reactive arthritis; perineal pain and fever suggest prostatitis.
What is not discharge
Pre-ejaculate, post-micturition dribble and spermatorrhoea can be mistaken for discharge. A clear, non-staining fluid only with arousal needs reassurance, not antibiotics.
Mycoplasma genitalium accounts for roughly 15–25% of NGU and is common in persistent and recurrent urethritis. The BASHH NGU guideline (2015) replaced azithromycin 1 g stat with doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days as first line, because single-dose azithromycin was driving macrolide resistance in M. genitalium. Doxycycline treats chlamydia reliably but cures only a minority of M. genitalium infections, so persistent NGU after doxycycline → GUM for M. genitalium NAAT with resistance testing; GUM then chooses between an extended azithromycin course (macrolide-susceptible) and moxifloxacin (macrolide-resistant) per the BASHH M. genitalium guideline. Avoid repeated single-dose azithromycin in primary care.
3
Diagnose

Sexual History, Examination & Investigations

Sexual history (BASHH minimum)
Partners (number + gender, last 3 months) · Activity (oral/vaginal/anal, insertive/receptive) · Condom use · Last sexual contact · Previous STIs · HIV status (offered all) · Hep B/C · Chemsex enquiry (crystal meth, GHB/GBL, mephedrone — high STI transmission, PrEP/harm reduction needed)
Consultation set-up
Offer a chaperone for the genital examination; explain confidentiality (including for under-16s, applying Fraser guidance) and ask about safeguarding concerns — coercion, exploitation or partners much older than a young patient.
Examination
Inguinal nodes · Glans/prepuce: ulcers, erythema, warts · Foreskin retractile? Phimosis? · Meatus: express gently (purulent = GC; mucoid = NGU; white cheesy = Candida) · Scrotum: epididymal tenderness · PR if prostatitis (tender prostate — no vigorous massage)
Investigations
FVU 10–20 ml (first-void urine — NOT MSU) for chlamydia + gonorrhoea NAAT · Urethral swab for gonococcal culture (susceptibility) before antibiotics if gonorrhoea is suspected — usually taken in GUM, or send promptly if taken in the surgery · Syphilis serology TPHA+RPR · HIV 4th gen · Hep B/C if risk · HbA1c if candidal balanitis
First-void urine (FVU) is the standard NAAT specimen in men: the first 10–20 ml carries the urethral cells, whereas a midstream sample dilutes them and lowers sensitivity. Instruction: ideally avoid passing urine for an hour or two beforehand, then collect only the very first part of the stream. Write 'FIRST VOID URINE — CHLAMYDIA NAAT' on the request form. Chemsex: crystal meth + GHB/GBL + mephedrone during sex (predominantly MSM) — associated with MDR gonorrhoea, HIV, HCV. Non-judgmental enquiry: 'Some people use substances to enhance sex — does that apply to you?' Positive → GUM (multi-site testing, PrEP assessment, HCV screen, harm reduction: never mix GHB with alcohol, carry naloxone, 56 Dean Street/GMFA).

Diagnose

Investigation Framework

GP-feasible STI screen
Urethral NAAT swab + culture (GC sensitivity). FVU for Chlamydia NAAT. Syphilis serology + HIV. Hep B/C if risk. HbA1c if candidal balanitis.
GUM advantages
Multi-site testing for MSM (pharyngeal + rectal NAAT — urethral-only testing misses a large share of extragenital infection) · IM ceftriaxone · POCT same-day results · Partner notification officers · PrEP/PEP access · Chemsex support
GP management appropriate
Candidal balanitis (no STI features). Chemical/irritant urethritis. Confirmed uncomplicated Chlamydia: doxycycline + partner notification (local sexual health services can take over partner notification if the practice cannot).
Results you may meet
Chlamydia positive: treat, partner notification, retest after 3 months if under 25 (reinfection is common). Gonorrhoea positive on NAAT: refer to GUM same day for culture and ceftriaxone — do not treat orally. Both negative with ongoing symptoms: GUM for urethral microscopy, which confirms or refutes urethritis and guides NGU treatment.
GUM referral mandatory
Confirmed or probable GC · MSM (multi-site testing) · Persistent NGU after doxycycline · Complications (epididymo-orchitis) · Syphilis · HIV reactive · Chemsex
Surveillance data from UKHSA show that a large proportion of gonorrhoea and chlamydia in MSM is at the pharynx or rectum and is usually asymptomatic, so urethral-only testing misses it; pharyngeal testing is also advised for everyone with urogenital gonorrhoea and all contacts (BASHH 2025). MSM are advised to have at least an annual HIV/STI screen, and every 3 months if having condomless sex with new or casual partners. PrEP (tenofovir/emtricitabine, daily or event-based) is highly effective at preventing HIV and is available free through NHS sexual health clinics — GPs identifying higher-risk patients should direct them there. HIV post-exposure prophylaxis should be started as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and not beyond 72 hours (BHIVA/BASHH PEP guideline 2021) — refer to GUM or the emergency department immediately.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / Same-day
DGI (fever + arthritis + skin) · Acute urinary retention · Suspected gonorrhoeal septicaemia
Same-day GUM
Confirmed or probable GC (IM ceftriaxone required) · MSM (multi-site testing) · XDR gonorrhoea · Syphilis/HIV reactive · Chemsex · Sexual assault / PEP within 72h
GUM (1–5 days)
All penile discharge with STI possibility · Persistent NGU post-doxycycline · Epididymo-orchitis + STI screen · Partner notification support
2WW urology
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): penile mass or ulcerated lesion with STI excluded; persistent penile lesion after completed STI treatment; or unexplained or persistent symptoms affecting the foreskin or glans → suspected cancer pathway referral
Urology (urgent)
Blood-stained discharge without a clear infective cause · progressive stricture symptoms (poor stream, spraying, incomplete emptying)
Urology (routine)
Chronic balanitis unresponsive · Phimosis (potent topical steroid trial first) · Lichen sclerosus not settling on ultrapotent steroid · CPPS/chronic prostatitis
Dermatology / GUM
Persistent red patches of the glans not responding to treatment (possible Zoon balanitis or penile intraepithelial neoplasia — biopsy needed)
GP management
Candidal balanitis: clotrimazole 1% cream BD for 7–14 days, or fluconazole 150 mg single oral dose for more severe symptoms (BASHH balanoposthitis guideline 2026). Confirmed Chlamydia (uncomplicated): doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days + partner notification.
Test of cure (TOC) after gonorrhoea: the BASHH 2025 guideline no longer requires routine TOC for anogenital infection treated with ceftriaxone 1 g when the isolate is ceftriaxone-susceptible, because treatment failure has not been reported in that situation. TOC remains important where GUM advises it — for example pharyngeal infection, treatment with an alternative regimen, persisting symptoms or reduced susceptibility — and a NAAT TOC should not be taken sooner than 2 weeks after treatment, when dead organisms may still be detected. Culture is what reveals resistance, which is the fundamental reason gonorrhoea belongs in GUM. Partner notification look-back periods depend on the infection and symptoms (for example 4 weeks before symptom onset for men with symptomatic NGU or chlamydia, with longer periods for asymptomatic infection and for syphilis) — GUM health advisers set these case by case. SXT app (sxt.org.uk) enables anonymous partner notification by text/email — no direct contact details needed.

Treat

Treatment by Diagnosis

Gonorrhoea
Ceftriaxone 1 g IM stat (GUM clinic only)
Sole first-line regimen (BASHH 2025); culture before treatment. TOC where GUM advises (no longer routine for susceptible anogenital infection). Partner notification per GUM. Abstain until partners treated and 7 days after treatment.
NGU first-line
Doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days
First line (BASHH NGU 2015). Treats chlamydia reliably; cures only a minority of M. genitalium. NOT azithromycin 1 g stat (drives macrolide resistance). Partner notification last 4 weeks. Abstinence until both treated.
NGU recurrent/persistent
Refer to GUM: M. genitalium NAAT with macrolide-resistance testing, then regimen per BASHH M. genitalium guideline
Confirm urethritis on microscopy first. Avoid blind repeated courses in primary care; fluoroquinolones carry MHRA restrictions (January 2024).
Candidal balanitis
Clotrimazole 1% cream BD for 7–14 days, or fluconazole 150 mg oral single dose if more severe
BASHH balanoposthitis 2026. Add 1% hydrocortisone with the imidazole if very inflamed. HbA1c if recurrent. SGLT2i review. Symptomatic partners can be treated.
Bacterial balanitis
Guided by swab culture: anaerobic (foul-smelling) → metronidazole 400–500 mg BD × 7 days; streptococcal or staphylococcal → oral antibiotic per culture, dose per BNF
BASHH balanoposthitis 2026. Swab and STI tests first. Saline washes and hygiene advice; circumcision may be needed for recurrent cases with phimosis.
Treatment is organism-specific because the causes of balanoposthitis differ: candida, anaerobes, streptococci and non-infective dermatoses (lichen sclerosus, psoriasis, eczema, Zoon balanitis) all look similar. A swab, STI tests and a glucose/HbA1c check prevent blind treatment. Lichen sclerosus (white, scarred, sometimes tight foreskin) is treated first with an ultrapotent topical steroid such as clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment once daily, reducing over up to 3 months (BASHH balanoposthitis 2026); circumcision is for disease that does not respond or for phimosis, and long-term review is needed because of a small risk of penile cancer. Acute bacterial prostatitis is covered by NICE NG110 (2018): an oral antibiotic for 14 days then review (see step 7). Never massage the prostate in acute prostatitis (bacteraemia risk).

Treat

Balanitis, Phimosis & Prostatitis

Recurrent candidal balanitis + diabetes / SGLT2i
Optimise HbA1c. SGLT2 inhibitors cause glycosuria and increase genital candida; most episodes respond to standard treatment and genital hygiene. If episodes keep recurring, discuss with the patient whether to continue the SGLT2 inhibitor. Rare but serious: Fournier's gangrene (necrotising fasciitis of the perineum) — severe pain, swelling or fever with genital symptoms on an SGLT2 inhibitor needs same-day hospital assessment (MHRA Drug Safety Update, February 2019).
Phimosis — non-surgical
A trial of a potent topical steroid to the tight prepuce with gentle stretching often avoids surgery; urology for circumcision if it fails. Lichen sclerosus (BXO): ultrapotent topical steroid first (clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment); circumcision if unresponsive; histology if any area looks atypical.
Reactive arthritis (Reiter's triad)
Chlamydia: doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days. NSAIDs for arthritis (dose per BNF). Rheumatology if arthritis persists or is severe. Often HLA-B27 positive (not diagnostic). Full STI screen + GUM. Red eye with pain or photophobia may be uveitis rather than conjunctivitis — same-day eye assessment.
Prostatitis — acute bacterial
NICE NG110 (2018): send an MSU for culture, then start an oral antibiotic — ciprofloxacin 500 mg BD (or ofloxacin) for 14 days then review, continuing for a further 14 days if symptoms persist; trimethoprim 200 mg BD for 14 days if a fluoroquinolone is not appropriate. Fluoroquinolones now carry MHRA restrictions (January 2024) — use only when other options are unsuitable and warn about tendon, nerve and psychiatric effects. Hospital if systemically unwell or in retention. Chronic/CPPS: alpha-blocker, pelvic floor physiotherapy and psychological support; urology if persistent.
Antibiotic penetration into the prostate is limited, which is why acute prostatitis needs a longer course than cystitis. NICE NG110 (2018) sets 14 days followed by review rather than a fixed 28 days: stop if symptoms have fully resolved, or give a further 14 days if they persist, to reduce the risk of chronic bacterial prostatitis while avoiding unnecessary fluoroquinolone exposure. In a young man with dysuria and discharge, remember that urethritis from an STI is far more likely than prostatitis — test for chlamydia and gonorrhoea first.

Lifestyle

Prevention, Safer Sex & Wellbeing

Condom use Consistent use substantially reduces gonorrhoea and chlamydia transmission. Correct technique: leave space at tip, fully unroll, water-based lubricant. Also partially protects against HPV, herpes, syphilis.
STI screening Since June 2021 the National Chlamydia Screening Programme (UKHSA) proactively offers opportunistic screening in GP and pharmacy settings to young women and other people with a womb under 25; men can still test through sexual health services (including online kits) whenever at risk or symptomatic. MSM: at least annually, 3-monthly if condomless sex with new or casual partners.
HPV vaccination Gardasil 9 protects against HPV 6/11/16/18 and 5 other high-risk types. School programme with catch-up for eligible young people to age 25; MSM up to age 45 via sexual health services (UKHSA Green Book, chapter 18a). Reduces genital warts and HPV-related penile, anal and oropharyngeal cancer.
PrEP Anyone at higher HIV risk (e.g. MSM with condomless sex, partners of people with detectable HIV, recent rectal STI): GUM for PrEP assessment. Highly effective when taken as prescribed; free through NHS sexual health clinics.
Foreskin hygiene Daily retraction + warm water only (no soap under prepuce). Dry thoroughly. Emollient (Doublebase) instead of soap for recurrent balanitis. Cotton underwear.
Psychological support Shame and stigma are common after STI. "This is very common and treatable." PHQ-9 for depression. Psychosexual counselling for sexual dysfunction secondary to infection anxiety.
Partner notification Ethical responsibility. SXT app (sxt.org.uk) for anonymous notification by text/email. GUM contact tracing if patient unable. Look-back periods vary by infection and symptoms — GUM health advisers set them.
Hepatitis B vaccination Offer to MSM, people with multiple partners and other at-risk groups through sexual health services (UKHSA Green Book, chapter 18).
Chemsex harm reduction Never mix GHB/GBL with alcohol (fatal respiratory depression). Never take alone. Carry naloxone. Antidote (antidote.org.uk), 56 Dean Street, GMFA for specialist support.
Syphilis diagnoses in England are at their highest since 1948 (UKHSA, 2024) — maintain a high index of suspicion. Primary syphilis can present as a painless penile, oral, or perianal ulcer. Any indurated painless ulcer in a sexually active adult = syphilis serology urgently. Secondary syphilis (infectious rash, condylomata lata, lymphadenopathy) can mimic many conditions. Antidote (antidote.org.uk) is a specialist LGBTQ+ drug and alcohol service providing chemsex support — GPs should actively signpost rather than leaving patients without specialist support. In the SCA, a non-judgemental, routine framing works well: "I ask everyone with these symptoms some personal questions about sex so that I test for the right things — is that OK?"

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

After NGU treatment
Review 1–2 weeks: symptoms resolved? Persistent → repeat swab + GUM for M.gen NAAT. Ensure partner treated. Document STI, treatment, partner notification advice.
After gonorrhoea (GUM-managed)
TOC where GUM advises (NAAT no sooner than 2 weeks after treatment). Repeat HIV and syphilis tests after the window period if the exposure was recent. MSM: 3-monthly STI screen if multiple partners.
Recurrent candidal balanitis
3rd episode: HbA1c + culture + SGLT2i review. Phimosis: betamethasone trial or urology referral.
Return immediately
New fever + arthralgia + skin rash (DGI) · Urinary retention · Rapid expansion of penile lesion
Same-day GUM / urology
Symptoms not improving at 48h on correct antibiotics → culture + sensitivity · New scrotal pain developing (sudden severe pain = possible torsion → emergency urology, without waiting for a scan) · Partner confirmed drug-resistant infection
New scrotal pain in a man already being treated for urethritis is usually epididymo-orchitis, but torsion can coexist and testicular salvage falls sharply after about 6 hours from onset. Clinical signs (Prehn's sign, cremasteric reflex, testicular lie) cannot reliably exclude torsion, and a normal Doppler scan does not exclude it either — so when torsion is possible the correct step is emergency surgical referral, not an outpatient scan. Document the cremasteric reflex and testicular lie at every scrotal assessment. SCA safety-net wording: "If you get sudden severe pain in the testicle, or a fever with joint pains or a rash, go to A&E straight away. If the discharge hasn't settled a week after finishing treatment, come back or contact the clinic — you may need a different test."
