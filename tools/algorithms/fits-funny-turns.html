1
Safety

Red Flags — Must Not Miss

A first suspected seizure requires urgent referral for specialist assessment within 2 weeks. Send the patient to the emergency department or call 999 if there is persistent altered consciousness, ongoing/recurrent seizure activity, injury requiring treatment, new focal neurological deficit, meningism/fever, severe persistent headache, suspected intracranial pathology, significant metabolic disturbance, pregnancy/eclampsia, or a high-risk cardiac presentation. A patient who has fully recovered from a single suspected seizure, with no red flags and a normal initial assessment, may be referred urgently through the first-seizure pathway rather than automatically sent by ambulance.

Status epilepticus (seizure >5 min) Ongoing convulsion, post-ictal coma not resolving → 999 Life-threatening — buccal midazolam or IV lorazepam only per individual emergency plan/local protocol and training
First seizure with focal deficit or new headache Todd's paresis, persistent confusion, meningism → 999 Exclude intracranial lesion, encephalitis, SAH
Loss of consciousness + chest pain / palpitations Structural heart disease, exertional syncope → Same-day Cardiac arrhythmia / Stokes-Adams / long QT
Syncope in young patient with family history sudden death Long QT, HOCM, Brugada, WPW → Same-day ECG essential — ambulatory monitoring matched to episode frequency
High-risk syncope features Syncope while supine or during swimming, sudden unexplained death or inherited arrhythmia in a first-degree relative, known structural heart disease, severe anaemia or haemorrhage, recurrent unexplained episodes without prodrome → Same-day Urgent cardiology / acute assessment
Focal neurological symptoms >24h post-event Arm weakness, speech difficulty, visual loss → Same-day Suspect TIA/stroke — urgent neurovascular assessment. Transient loss of consciousness is not a typical TIA feature
Recurrent falls in elderly + loss of consciousness Carotid sinus hypersensitivity, heart block → Same-day ECG, lying/standing BP, cardiology
Seizure in known diabetic — severe hypoglycaemia Check capillary glucose immediately. Conscious and able to swallow: treat promptly with fast-acting carbohydrate per local diabetes protocol (treat at <4.0 mmol/L if diabetes-treated). Unconscious or unable to swallow: no oral treatment — 999 and glucagon or IV glucose if trained and appropriate. Review the diabetes regimen after any severe or recurrent episode
Seizure in pregnancy ≥20 weeks Hypertension, proteinuria, headache → 999 Eclampsia — call obstetric emergency team

The clinical challenge is that many patients presenting with "fits" do not have epilepsy — syncope and functional/dissociative seizures (PNES) can be misdiagnosed as epilepsy, leading to unnecessary antiseizure treatment. Conversely, cardiac syncope from arrhythmia is life-threatening if missed. A witness account is a key discriminator — always obtain collateral history and video footage where available; the diagnosis remains clinical and specialist.

2
Diagnose

Detailed History — The Most Important Diagnostic Tool

The history is central to the diagnosis. Always obtain a witness account — phone the witness if necessary — and ask for video footage where available (NICE NG217).

Pre-event
Prodrome: aura (epileptic), warning dizziness/visual greying/nausea (vasovagal), palpitations (cardiac). Posture (standing = vasovagal). Activity (exertional = cardiac). Trigger (cough, micturition = situational syncope)
During event (witness)
Colour change (pallor suggests vasovagal/cardiac; cyanosis is not reliable enough to diagnose epilepsy). Duration (syncope is usually brief, but convulsive movements may occur in syncope and seizures may be brief — not absolute). Movements: tonic-clonic, brief myoclonic jerks (can occur in vasovagal), head turning, prolonged limb-jerking, automatisms — supportive features, not diagnostic in isolation
Post-event
Recovery: rapid (<1 min = vasovagal/cardiac). Prolonged confusion >5 min = post-ictal (epileptic). Headache (post-ictal or migraine). Todd's paresis (unilateral weakness = focal epileptic)
Tongue biting
Lateral tongue bite = strongly supportive of epileptic seizure. Tip of tongue bite = non-specific, can occur in syncope
Incontinence
Urinary incontinence occurs in both epilepsy and syncope — NOT discriminatory. Faecal incontinence is not sufficiently specific to be a reliable discriminator
Medical history
Known epilepsy? Cardiac disease, structural heart disease? Diabetes? Drug history (epileptogenic drugs: tramadol, ciprofloxacin, tricyclics, antipsychotics)
DVLA question
Always ask about driving. First seizure: advise to stop driving and notify DVLA. Syncope: DVLA rules depend on whether it occurred while driving, whether there was avoidable provocation and whether episodes are recurrent — document the specific DVLA category and direct the patient to current DVLA guidance. Document this conversation clearly in notes

Lateral tongue biting is strongly supportive of epileptic seizure, and prolonged post-ictal confusion is also supportive — but no single feature is diagnostic in isolation. The Edmonton Syncope Score and LOSS Score help risk-stratify cardiac vs non-cardiac syncope. DVLA notification is a legal requirement — failure to advise patients about driving restrictions after first seizure is a significant medico-legal risk.

3
Diagnose

Classify the Event Type

Epileptic seizure
Supportive features: lateral tongue bite, prolonged post-ictal confusion, tonic-clonic movements, aura preceding event, no clear trigger — none diagnostic in isolation. Suspected → urgent first-seizure specialist referral (≤2 weeks)
Vasovagal syncope
Upright posture, prolonged standing, hot environment, emotional trigger, prodromal nausea/sweating/visual greying, rapid complete recovery (<1 min), pale during event, age <40 often
Cardiac syncope
Exertional, supine or during swimming, no prodrome, sudden onset without warning, palpitations, sudden unexplained death or inherited arrhythmia in a first-degree relative, structural heart disease, severe anaemia/haemorrhage, abnormal ECG, recurrent unexplained episodes. High risk: needs same-day assessment
Orthostatic hypotension
On standing, BP drop ≥20 mmHg systolic (or ≥10 diastolic) within 3 min — measure after at least 5 minutes supine, then after standing, recording symptoms. Consider inadequate heart-rate response, dehydration, autonomic failure and medication effects. Medications (antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers). Autonomic neuropathy (diabetes, Parkinson's)
TIA
Suspected TIA is sudden-onset focal neurological dysfunction, usually without loss of consciousness — transient LoC should prompt consideration of syncope, seizure or arrhythmia. Refer urgently for specialist assessment within 24 hours per the local TIA pathway; do not use ABCD2 to determine urgency (NICE NG128). Aspirin 300 mg immediately unless contraindicated, considering intracranial haemorrhage and alternative diagnoses where appropriate. See TIA algorithm
Hypoglycaemia
Known DM, recent insulin/sulphonylurea dose. Diaphoresis, tremor, confusion resolving with glucose. Check capillary glucose immediately; treat per local diabetes protocol (oral only if conscious and able to swallow). Review the diabetes regimen after any severe or recurrent episode
Functional / dissociative seizures (PNES)
Consider when the clinical pattern is atypical or variable, but do not diagnose from individual semiological features alone (prolonged duration, variable movements, emotional triggers and eye closure may occur but are not diagnostic; a normal routine EEG does not establish PNES; a psychiatric history is not itself evidence). Diagnosis usually requires specialist assessment and, where appropriate, video-EEG capturing a typical event. Continue to consider epilepsy, syncope and cardiac causes until adequately assessed. Refer neurology

Functional/dissociative seizures (PNES) are an important differential — misdiagnosis in either direction causes harm. Diagnosis is specialist-led, usually with video-EEG capturing a typical event where appropriate. Cardiac syncope is lethal if missed: Brugada syndrome, long QT, and HOCM cause sudden cardiac death in young, apparently healthy people — the ECG is the key screening test.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Vital signs
BP lying (after at least 5 minutes supine) and standing, with symptoms recorded (postural drop). HR (bradycardia → heart block, tachycardia → SVT/VT). Temperature (febrile → encephalitis, metabolic cause)
BM / blood glucose
Immediate capillary glucose. Interpret in context — in people treated for diabetes, hypoglycaemia is generally treated at <4.0 mmol/L; severe hypoglycaemia (needing third-party help) may occur at varying levels
Neurological exam
Full cranial nerves, power, tone, reflexes. Focal deficit = structural pathology (TIA/stroke, SOL). Tongue for laceration. Plantar responses
Cardiovascular exam
Auscultation: murmur (HOCM, aortic stenosis → exertional syncope). Carotid bruits. Irregular pulse (AF). Peripheral pulses
Carotid sinus massage
Only in controlled setting (specialist), NOT in primary care without resuscitation facilities. Can precipitate asystole
Eyes
Pupil response (unequal → intracranial pathology). Papilloedema (raised ICP). Nystagmus (posterior circulation)
Tongue & mouth
Fresh lateral laceration = strong evidence of seizure. Previous healed lateral bites also significant. Cheek biting (less specific)

Lying-to-standing BP is essential in any unexplained LOC — orthostatic hypotension is common in older people and is frequently missed. An innocent-sounding ejection systolic murmur in a young patient with exertional syncope may indicate HOCM — the murmur increases on Valsalva (unlike aortic stenosis). Papilloedema in a patient with seizures mandates emergency CT before LP — lumbar puncture with raised ICP causes tonsillar herniation.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — What to Order and When

Suspected seizure / unexplained collapse — consider
Capillary glucose, 12-lead ECG (cardiac disorders can mimic seizure), U&Es, calcium, magnesium where seizure, arrhythmia or metabolic disturbance is suspected; FBC, LFTs, CRP, toxicology, pregnancy test and other tests guided by clinical context. Routine LFTs, FBC and thyroid tests are not mandatory for every uncomplicated vasovagal faint
Suspected seizure
Refer urgently to a clinician with expertise in first seizures. EEG: consider when history and examination suggest an epileptic seizure; if the local pathway allows direct GP request, request only where the history supports an epileptic seizure, ideally within 72 hours, and keep the patient under specialist review. A normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy — do not use EEG to exclude epilepsy. Prolactin NOT routinely recommended
ECG — critical findings
Look for possible long QT, pre-excitation (delta waves + short PR), Brugada pattern, heart block or other abnormality. QTc interpretation depends on method, heart rate, sex and clinical context — do not make isolated GP diagnoses from borderline QT values or epsilon waves; an abnormal or suspicious ECG should prompt specialist interpretation
Cardiac monitoring
Match monitoring to episode frequency: 24–72-hour ambulatory ECG for frequent symptoms; longer external monitoring for less frequent episodes; implantable loop recorder for recurrent unexplained syncope where arrhythmia remains suspected — refer cardiology
Neuroimaging
Same-day emergency imaging/assessment for new focal deficit, persistent altered mental state, fever or meningism, persistent severe headache, significant head injury, anticoagulant use, immunosuppression, known malignancy, HIV, suspected stroke/haemorrhage, or other concern for an acute intracranial lesion. MRI with an epilepsy protocol is preferred after epilepsy is diagnosed or where a structural cause is suspected (not needed in idiopathic generalised epilepsy or other specified syndromes); CT is used acutely when MRI is unavailable, contraindicated or an urgent lesion must be excluded. A normal CT does not replace specialist review or epilepsy-protocol MRI
Blood tests — specific
Glucose + HbA1c (DM cause). Electrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg) — hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia cause seizures. U&Es (uraemic seizures). TFTs only if clinically indicated. Antiseizure drug levels only where the patient already takes a relevant medicine and the result will affect management
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT use EEG to exclude epilepsy, or diagnose epilepsy solely from a normal/abnormal EEG. Do NOT order prolactin (unreliable discriminator). Typical vasovagal in young person: ECG + lying-standing BP, no further imaging needed if clear diagnosis

ECG is the most important immediate investigation — inherited arrhythmia syndromes such as long QT cause sudden death in young people, many without prior symptoms. QTc measurement requires a calibrated measurement (automated ECG machines often miscalculate). NICE NG217 recommends MRI (epilepsy protocol) as the preferred imaging after epilepsy is diagnosed, unless idiopathic generalised epilepsy or another specified syndrome; CT is less sensitive for epileptogenic lesions. A normal EEG does NOT exclude epilepsy.

6
Refer

Referral — The 2-Week-of-Onset Rule for First Seizure

999
Status epilepticus, persistent altered consciousness or ongoing/recurrent seizure activity, seizure with new focal deficit, injury requiring treatment, meningism/fever, severe persistent headache, suspected intracranial pathology, significant metabolic disturbance, eclampsia, cardiac syncope with haemodynamic instability, exertional syncope in structurally abnormal heart
Same-day
First seizure not fully recovered, or with injury or other concern needing acute assessment → same-day ED/acute medicine (a first seizure that has fully recovered is referred urgently to be seen by a specialist within 2 weeks — NICE NG217). Cardiac syncope — ECG abnormality (long QT, Brugada, heart block). Any suspected TIA → aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated (consider intracranial haemorrhage and alternative diagnoses) and specialist assessment within 24 hours via the local TIA pathway (NICE NG128 — do not use ABCD2 scoring)
Neurology — urgent
All first seizures to be seen within 2 weeks by epilepsy specialist (NICE NG217). Do NOT start AEDs in primary care. After a first unprovoked seizure the specialist may consider treatment where there is a neurological deficit, unequivocal epileptic activity on EEG, a structural abnormality on imaging, high judged recurrence risk, or the patient finds the risk unacceptable — not dependent on MRI having been completed
Cardiology
Exertional, supine or sudden syncope without prodrome, syncope during swimming, abnormal ECG, structural heart disease, family history of sudden death or inherited arrhythmia, recurrent unexplained syncope. Ambulatory monitoring matched to frequency; loop recorder where arrhythmia remains suspected
DVLA notification advice
Advise stopping driving after a first suspected seizure and direct the patient to the current DVLA rules (DVLA Assessing fitness to drive — neurological disorders). Isolated first unprovoked seizure: Group 1 (car) — normally 6 months off driving, 12 months if there is an underlying factor that increases recurrence risk; Group 2 (HGV/bus) — normally 5 years, with additional requirements. Established epilepsy/multiple unprovoked seizures: Group 1 — generally 12 months seizure-free; Group 2 — generally 10 years seizure-free without antiseizure medication. Syncope: rules depend on whether it occurred while driving, avoidable provocation and recurrence — document the specific DVLA category rather than assuming no notification is needed. The patient contacts DVLA directly; the GP documents the advice and provides factual medical information when requested. Give patient written information
First suspected seizure — GP pathway
1. ABCDE, capillary glucose, injury assessment and recovery status. 2. Witness history/video if possible. 3. Neurological and cardiovascular examination. 4. 12-lead ECG. 5. Targeted blood tests. 6. ED/999 if red flags or incomplete recovery. 7. Otherwise urgent first-seizure specialist referral within 2 weeks. 8. Written seizure first-aid, safety and driving advice. 9. Do not diagnose epilepsy solely from one semiological feature or a normal/abnormal EEG
Suspected syncope — GP pathway
1. Establish posture, prodrome, trigger, exertion, palpitations and recovery. 2. Lying and standing BP measured appropriately. 3. ECG and cardiovascular examination. 4. Identify high-risk cardiac features. 5. Urgent/cardiology referral if exertional, supine, sudden without prodrome, abnormal ECG, structural heart disease or family history of sudden death. 6. Manage clear vasovagal or orthostatic syncope conservatively after excluding high-risk disease
Epilepsy specialist nurse
After epilepsy diagnosis confirmed — medication counselling, seizure diary, first aid advice, pregnancy planning (teratogenicity of AEDs)
Primary care manages
Established vasovagal syncope (typical features, normal ECG, normal exam). Orthostatic hypotension — medication review. Hypoglycaemia — DM management adjustment

NICE NG217 is explicit: all patients with a first seizure must be seen by an epilepsy specialist within 2 weeks. Starting AEDs in primary care before specialist review is inappropriate — the diagnosis must be confirmed because many "first seizures" are not epilepsy. DVLA rules for seizures are strict and legally binding — there is significant clinical and medico-legal risk in failing to advise patients. For syncope, the DVLA publishes specific criteria by category — direct patients to the current DVLA guidance on gov.uk.

Treat

Immediate Management & Primary Care Treatment

Active seizure in surgery
Midazolam buccal First-line
Convulsive seizure lasting 5 minutes or longer: call 999, protect the airway, recovery position where appropriate, time the seizure, do not restrain and do not put anything in the mouth. Give buccal midazolam (or rectal diazepam; IV lorazepam where IV access and resuscitation facilities are immediately available) only in accordance with the individual emergency plan/local protocol and training — check the prescribed product. A second benzodiazepine dose may be given per protocol if the seizure continues
Vasovagal syncope
Physical counterpressure Evidence-based
Leg crossing, hand gripping, squatting at onset of prodrome. Avoid triggers. Appropriate hydration and gradual postural change where safe; increase salt only after assessing BP, renal function and cardiac status. Compression stockings
Orthostatic hypotension
Medication review First step
Review causative medication (antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers) and comorbidities — changes individualised. Encourage appropriate hydration and gradual postural change where safe; increase salt only after assessing BP, renal function and cardiac status. Fludrocortisone is specialist-led (risks: hypertension, hypokalaemia, oedema, heart failure). Consider specialist referral if symptoms persist or autonomic failure is suspected
Epilepsy (specialist initiated)
AED choice by type Specialist only
Choice depends on seizure type, comorbidity, interactions, reproductive potential and specialist assessment — lamotrigine or levetiracetam are not always suitable alternatives. Valproate: MHRA safety measures apply to men and women — review by two specialists when initiating in patients under 55; women of childbearing potential need the pregnancy-prevention safeguards; men starting valproate need the updated specialist review requirements; never stop valproate abruptly. Topiramate also has pregnancy-prevention restrictions. See the current MHRA valproate safety material. DO NOT start in primary care
AcuteRecovery position + timing — protect head, do not restrain, call 999 if >5 min or not recovering. Record time, witness account, post-ictal state
ImmediateECG + BM — cardiac cause and hypoglycaemia are the two immediately treatable causes. Treat hypoglycaemia per local protocol — oral only if conscious and able to swallow; otherwise 999 and glucagon/IV glucose if trained
Short-termDVLA advice + driving restriction — document in notes. Provide patient information leaflet. Advise against operating heavy machinery, swimming alone, working at heights
DefinitiveSpecialist management — AED initiation by epilepsy specialist based on recurrence risk (MRI not an absolute prerequisite). Pacemaker for symptomatic heart block. ICD for malignant arrhythmia syndromes

Sodium valproate is contraindicated in women of childbearing potential unless the Pregnancy Prevention Programme is in place; MHRA measures require review by two specialists when initiating in patients under 55 (not required for men already taking valproate) — it causes major congenital malformations and neurodevelopmental disorders after in-utero exposure. Alternatives are a specialist choice based on seizure type and individual factors. Physical counterpressure manoeuvres for vasovagal syncope are backed by RCT evidence (PC Trial). Emergency benzodiazepines should be given according to the individual emergency plan/local protocol by trained staff.

8
Lifestyle

Seizure Safety, Triggers & Quality-of-Life Advice

Seizure triggers (epilepsy) Sleep deprivation, alcohol, missed medication, flashing lights (photosensitive epilepsy). Stress. Illness/fever. Menstrual cycle. Keep seizure diary.
Sleep hygiene Consistent sleep/wake times. Sleep deprivation lowers seizure threshold. Target 7–9 hours. Address insomnia — benzodiazepines inappropriate long-term.
Alcohol Alcohol can lower seizure threshold AND withdrawal seizures are dangerous. Advise avoiding binge drinking and alcohol withdrawal and discuss individual triggers — complete abstinence is not required for every person with epilepsy.
Bathing / swimming safety Epilepsy: shower rather than bath, never swim alone, no unsupervised water activities, avoid heights. Avoid locking the bathroom door. Drowning risk.
Vasovagal triggers Avoid prolonged standing, hot environments, dehydration. Encourage appropriate hydration and gradual postural change where safe. Increase salt only after assessing blood pressure, renal function and cardiac status (caution in hypertension, heart failure, CKD, liver disease). Compression stockings.
Driving & work DVLA rules apply — document advice. Some occupations prohibited during driving restriction (train driver, pilot, certain machinery). Appropriate occupational risk assessment / occupational health review if relevant.
SUDEP awareness Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy — risk varies substantially with seizure type, frequency, nocturnal seizures and treatment adherence. Good seizure control and adherence reduce risk; nocturnal supervision may be discussed but does not eliminate risk. Refer to epilepsy.org.uk for resources.
Medication adherence Never stop AEDs suddenly — withdrawal seizure risk. If dose missed: take as soon as remembered. Interactions: contraceptive pill (enzyme-inducing AEDs reduce efficacy — use additional contraception).

SUDEP is the leading cause of death in young people with epilepsy — epilepsy-related deaths number around 1,000 a year in the UK, many of them SUDEP. Good seizure control reduces SUDEP risk. Enzyme-inducing AEDs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, topiramate, oxcarbazepine) reduce contraceptive pill efficacy — unintended pregnancy with ongoing teratogenic AED exposure is a significant preventable harm. Vasovagal syncope patient education about physical counterpressure techniques can reduce further episodes.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Post-first episode
Review within 1 week. Confirm referral received. Check DVLA advice documented and written seizure first-aid and safety advice given. Assess for further episodes. Review any medication changes. Ensure not driving
Epilepsy established
GP review 3-monthly while establishing control. Annual epilepsy review once stable. Seizure diary review. Drug levels only where clinically indicated (not annually for all) — interpret with clinical response, adherence, pregnancy, toxicity, interacting medicines and formulation changes. AED side effect monitoring
AED monitoring
Valproate: baseline and clinically indicated FBC/LFTs; monitor symptoms and adverse effects — ammonia only if unexplained reduced consciousness, vomiting, ataxia or encephalopathy. Carbamazepine: FBC, LFTs and sodium per initiation and local monitoring protocol; alert for rash, marrow suppression, hyponatraemia. Lamotrigine: urgent review of any new rash, mucosal involvement, fever or systemic symptoms. Levetiracetam: mood, irritability, aggression, suicidality. Phenytoin: levels only where clinically indicated; monitor toxicity, interactions, gingival disease
Vasovagal
Review 4–6 weeks. Confirm trigger avoidance working. No further investigation if typical recurrent vasovagal. Consider tilt-table test referral if refractory
Safety-net 999
Any seizure lasting >5 minutes, cluster of seizures without recovery, new focal deficit after seizure, loss of consciousness with cardiac symptoms, suspected eclampsia in pregnancy
Safety-net same-day
Breakthrough seizure in established epileptic (may indicate infection, missed doses, drug interaction, non-compliance), exertional syncope, new neurological symptoms
Pregnancy planning (epilepsy)
Pre-conception counselling and folic acid 5 mg OD (not 400 mcg), coordinated between specialist and GP. AED review with neurology. Valproate: MHRA pregnancy-prevention safeguards; never stop abruptly. Topiramate: pregnancy-prevention restrictions. Register on Epilepsy and Pregnancy Register (UKEPPR)
DVLA driving review
Relicensing follows the current DVLA criteria (e.g. Group 1 generally 12 months seizure-free for epilepsy) — not necessarily a formal DVLA assessment if the criteria are met. Patients inform DVLA directly — GP documents advice and supports with factual medical information on request

Carbamazepine can cause hyponatraemia (SIADH) — elderly patients are at particular risk of falls from dilutional hyponatraemia. Valproate can cause hyperammonaemia presenting as encephalopathy — check ammonia if unexplained reduced consciousness, vomiting or ataxia. A structured review at least annually is standard practice for established epilepsy (NICE IND227 measures a structured review in the preceding 12 months) — seizure diaries should be reviewed, seizure frequency tracked, and medication step-up considered if breakthrough seizures occurring. Folic acid 5 mg (not the standard 400 mcg) is required in women with epilepsy on AEDs — enzyme-inducing AEDs deplete folate, and higher supplementation is needed to reduce neural tube defect risk.
