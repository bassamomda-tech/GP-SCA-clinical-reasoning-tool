1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude malignancy and serious infection

Night sweats have a broad differential. The clinical priority is excluding lymphoma, TB, HIV, and endocarditis before attributing to benign causes.

B-symptoms Drenching night sweats + unexplained weight loss (>10% over 6 months) + fever >38°C → urgent same-week bloods (FBC, film, LDH, ESR/CRP), chest X-ray and full node/spleen examination; if nodes or spleen are found see below. Unexplained weight loss without a site → consider a non-site-specific symptoms (rapid diagnostic) pathway where available
Persistent fever Fever >38°C for >2 weeks + night sweats → same-day review (TB, endocarditis, abscess, HIV)
HIV risk factors Unprotected sex, IDU, MSM, blood transfusion + night sweats → HIV test urgently
Cough >2–3 weeks + night sweats ± haemoptysis → TB until proven otherwise → urgent chest X-ray + 3 sputum samples (one early morning) for microscopy and culture (NICE NG33)
New heart murmur + fever Infective endocarditis (especially prosthetic valve, recent dental work, injecting drug use) → same-day medical admission; do not start antibiotics before blood cultures unless septic
Unexplained lymphadenopathy — NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) Adults: consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for Hodgkin lymphoma with unexplained lymphadenopathy, or for non-Hodgkin lymphoma with unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, taking into account associated symptoms — particularly fever, night sweats, shortness of breath, pruritus or weight loss (and alcohol-induced node pain for Hodgkin). Children and young people: very urgent referral (within 48 hours) for unexplained lymphadenopathy with those symptoms → 2WW haematology
Bone pain + night sweats + anaemia Myeloma or metastatic disease. NICE NG12: aged 60+ with persistent bone pain (especially back) or unexplained fracture → FBC, calcium and ESR/plasma viscosity; if these suggest myeloma → protein electrophoresis and urine Bence-Jones protein; if those suggest myeloma → 2WW haematology
Immunocompromised HIV+, transplant, biologics, steroids + night sweats → opportunistic infection or reactivated TB; same-day specialist review
Pallor, fatigue, bruising, recurrent infection, hepatosplenomegaly NICE NG12: consider a very urgent FBC (within 48 hours) to assess for leukaemia → same-day haematology if the film suggests acute leukaemia
Haemoptysis, aged 40+ NICE NG12: unexplained haemoptysis at 40 or over, or chest X-ray findings suggesting lung cancer → suspected cancer pathway referral for lung cancer → 2WW chest
Lymphoma (particularly Hodgkin's lymphoma) classically presents with night sweats, fever, and weight loss (B-symptoms) — the majority of patients are aged 15–35 or >65. Hodgkin lymphoma is highly curable, and outcomes are best when it is diagnosed before advanced-stage disease. Note that NG12 anchors lymphoma referral on unexplained lymphadenopathy (or splenomegaly for non-Hodgkin lymphoma), with night sweats as a weighting symptom — sweats alone, without nodes or spleen, call for investigation rather than an automatic 2WW. TB remains an important cause of night sweats in England, concentrated in large cities, in people born in higher-incidence countries, and in those with social risk factors such as homelessness, drug use or imprisonment (UKHSA TB surveillance). HIV can cause night sweats at any stage — always offer testing. Infective endocarditis is the must-not-miss cardiac cause.
2
Diagnose

History — Systematic characterisation of night sweats

True night sweats definition
Soaking through nightwear and bedding requiring a change — distinguish from normal warmth/hot bedroom. "Do you have to change your clothes?"
Duration and frequency
Acute (<2 weeks) vs chronic (>4 weeks); every night vs occasional; severity and distress to sleep
Associated fever
Subjective fever, rigors, chills? Documented temperature? Pattern (hectic, remitting, relapsing-remitting)?
Constitutional symptoms
Weight loss (how much, over what period?); fatigue; anorexia; malaise → B-symptoms (malignancy/TB)
Menopausal symptoms
Women 45–55: hot flushes, irregular periods, mood change, urogenital symptoms → menopause most likely cause
Medications
SSRIs/SNRIs (very common cause); tamoxifen; GnRH analogues; nifedipine; nitrates; steroids (withdrawal); antipyretics masking fever
Endocrine symptoms
Palpitations + sweating + weight loss → hyperthyroidism; episodic hypertension + headache + sweating → phaeochromocytoma (rare)
TB risk factors
TB contact; birth in or travel to higher-incidence countries; homelessness, prison, drug or alcohol misuse; prior TB; immunosuppression (HIV, biologics, steroids)
Sleep and breathing
Snoring, witnessed apnoeas, choking arousals, morning headache, daytime sleepiness → obstructive sleep apnoea
Alcohol and drugs
Units per week; withdrawal sweats; opioid use or withdrawal; recreational stimulants
Pattern clue
Sweats with flushes by day and night in a woman with changing periods → vasomotor; sweats with rigors and fever spikes → infection; sweats with itch after bathing → consider lymphoma or a myeloproliferative disorder
Ideas, concerns, expectations
Many patients fear cancer or HIV — ask directly, because naming the worry often shapes which tests are reassuring
The definition of "true night sweats" matters — many people who report night sweats are simply warm in bed, whereas drenching sweats that soak nightwear or bedding are more likely to have an organic cause. Antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs) are among the commonest drug causes of sweating in primary care, so a medication review before investigation can avoid unnecessary testing. Always check the timing of any new medicine against the onset of sweats. The menopausal history is the highest-yield question in women aged 45–55.
3
Diagnose

Classification — Identify the aetiology category

Physiological / environmental
Warm bedroom, heavy bedding, warm partner, alcohol before bed — history only; no investigation needed
Menopause / POI
Most common cause in women 45–55; declining oestrogen → altered thermoregulation. Also POI in women <40.
Drug-induced
SSRIs, SNRIs (most common), tamoxifen, GnRH agonists, antipsychotics, nitrates, calcium channel blockers
Infection
TB (classic), HIV, infective endocarditis, osteomyelitis, abscess, brucellosis, malaria (travel)
Malignancy
Lymphoma (Hodgkin's, NHL) — most important; leukaemia; solid tumours with systemic features
Endocrine
Hyperthyroidism; hypoglycaemia (nocturnal — check HbA1c in diabetics); phaeochromocytoma (rare but treatable)
Other systemic
Obstructive sleep apnoea (common, underdiagnosed); GORD; anxiety/panic disorder; autoimmune disease (SLE, RA)
Idiopathic
No cause found after thorough evaluation — a recognised outcome; reassure but monitor and re-examine if anything changes
Separating the common from the serious
Afebrile, weight-stable, normal nodes, clear vasomotor or drug explanation → manage in primary care. Fever, weight loss, nodes, splenomegaly, cough or abnormal FBC → investigate as in steps 4–6.
Menopause and drug side effects account for the majority of night sweats in primary care — the key diagnostic task is efficiently excluding the dangerous minority (lymphoma, TB, HIV). Obstructive sleep apnoea causes night sweats through arousal-induced sympathetic activation and is frequently overlooked — relevant questions include snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime sleepiness, and obesity. Nocturnal hypoglycaemia in diabetics on sulphonylureas or insulin is a serious and completely reversible cause that requires medication review. Phaeochromocytoma is rare but is a must-not-miss cause of episodic sweating with headache, palpitations and hypertension. Carcinoid syndrome (flushing with diarrhoea) and hyperthyroidism (weight loss with good appetite, tremor, heat intolerance) are other endocrine causes worth a single screening question.
4
Diagnose

Examination — Systematic physical assessment

Temperature
Document accurately. Fever + night sweats → infection / haematological malignancy. Afebrile → menopause, drugs, anxiety more likely.
Lymph nodes
Full lymph node examination (cervical, axillary, inguinal, epitrochlear). Hard, non-tender, rubbery nodes → lymphoma
Thyroid
Goitre, tremor, tachycardia, lid lag, exophthalmos → hyperthyroidism
Cardiovascular
New murmur (endocarditis); irregular pulse (AF associated with thyrotoxicosis); BP (phaeochromocytoma: labile hypertension)
Chest / respiratory
Dullness to percussion, bronchial breathing → TB; bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on CXR → sarcoidosis/lymphoma
Abdomen
Hepatosplenomegaly → haematological malignancy, infections (EBV, CMV, HIV); masses; liver disease stigmata
BMI and weight trend
Document weight. Compare with the last recorded weight in the notes — an objective fall is more reliable than recall.
Breast and testes
Examine where relevant: breast lumps in women; testicular mass in young men (can present with systemic symptoms)
Mouth and teeth
Dental abscess or poor dentition (endocarditis source); oral candida or hairy leukoplakia (HIV)
Skin
Pallor (anaemia), jaundice, rashes (HIV seroconversion, SLE), Kaposi's sarcoma (HIV), night sweat soaking visible
Lymph node examination is the highest-yield component of the physical examination for night sweats — rubbery, non-tender, matted nodes in a young person with weight loss and night sweats is Hodgkin's lymphoma until proven otherwise. Hepatosplenomegaly is a key examination finding that markedly increases the probability of haematological malignancy or systemic infection. Documenting and comparing serial weights is more sensitive than asking patients "have you lost weight?" — patients often underestimate significant weight loss.

Diagnose

Investigations — Risk-stratified blood panel and imaging

Baseline (all cases)
FBC + film; CRP / ESR; LFTs / U&Es; TFTs; Blood glucose / HbA1c; LDH (lymphoma marker)
Infection screen
HIV test (offer to all with unexplained night sweats — an HIV indicator condition; NICE NG60, 2016); Chest X-ray (TB, lymphoma, malignancy); blood cultures if febrile
TB workup
3 sputum samples (deep cough, one early morning) for microscopy and culture if respiratory symptoms/CXR changes (NICE NG33); IGRA/Mantoux tests show TB infection but do not on their own diagnose active TB; refer suspected active TB to the TB service
Endocrine workup
Menopause is a clinical diagnosis over 45 — do not use FSH to diagnose it in women aged 45+ with typical symptoms; FSH can be considered at 40–45 with symptoms and a change in periods, and in women under 40 with suspected premature ovarian insufficiency (two FSH levels 4–6 weeks apart) (NICE NG23); plasma free or urine metanephrines if phaeochromocytoma suspected
Haematology
Blood film (abnormal lymphocytes → lymphoma/leukaemia); protein electrophoresis if myeloma suspected (age >60 + bone pain)
Imaging
Chest X-ray — mandatory if B-symptoms or TB risk. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis — for lymphadenopathy / lymphoma staging (hospital-led)
Do NOT routinely
PET-CT (hospital specialist); bone marrow biopsy (haematology); CT without red flags in young patients — radiation risk vs yield
Other tests by picture
Blood cultures ×3 before antibiotics if endocarditis possible; malaria films if travel in the past year; EBV serology in young adults with sore throat and nodes; ferritin and calcium when sarcoidosis or malignancy is suspected; urinalysis
Reading the results
Lymphocytosis or blasts on the film → same-day haematology advice. Raised calcium → check PTH/myeloma screen. Raised ESR with anaemia in an older adult → myeloma screen. A normal first panel does not exclude lymphoma if nodes are present — the node, not the blood test, drives the referral.
LDH is inexpensive and non-specific, but a raised LDH alongside night sweats, weight loss and lymphadenopathy strengthens the case for urgent lymphoma assessment. HIV testing should be offered to anyone with unexplained night sweats, and NICE NG60 (2016) also recommends offering an HIV test to everyone registering with a practice, and to those having blood tests, in areas of high and extremely high HIV prevalence. The chest X-ray is the most useful single screening test for both TB and mediastinal lymphoma. Metanephrines have replaced catecholamines as the preferred screening test for phaeochromocytoma because they are more sensitive.
6
Refer

Referral Criteria — Urgent, 2WW, and routine pathways

Same-day / 999
Fever + sepsis signs; suspected endocarditis; haemodynamic compromise; TB with respiratory failure
2WW Haematology
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): adults with unexplained lymphadenopathy (Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma) or unexplained splenomegaly (non-Hodgkin lymphoma), weighing associated fever, night sweats, shortness of breath, pruritus or weight loss → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral; children and young people → very urgent referral within 48 hours; blood film or FBC suggesting leukaemia → very urgent (same-day) haematology
2WW Chest
NICE NG12: chest X-ray findings suggesting lung cancer, or unexplained haemoptysis at 40 or over → suspected cancer pathway referral for lung cancer
Urgent TB Service
Suspected active TB (e.g. cough, sweats and CXR changes) — urgent referral to the local TB service (NICE NG33); advise respiratory hygiene and avoiding contact with vulnerable people until assessed. TB is a notifiable disease — notify the UKHSA health protection team on clinical suspicion
Endocrinology
Suspected phaeochromocytoma (episodic hypertension + headache + sweating); confirmed hyperthyroidism not responding to treatment
Sleep Clinic
Suspected OSA (snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime sleepiness — use STOP-Bang and Epworth to support the history) → refer to a sleep service (NICE NG202, 2021); urgently if the person drives for work or has a safety-critical job; advise on DVLA rules for excessive sleepiness
Menopause specialist
HRT contraindicated (breast cancer, VTE history) + severe menopausal symptoms; premature ovarian insufficiency
Primary care manage
Drug-induced (review medications); menopause (HRT); idiopathic after negative workup; lifestyle modification
The 2WW haematology pathway is specifically designed for lymphoma — do not wait for specialist availability if B-symptoms are present. CT scanning and bone marrow biopsy for staging are time-critical. TB is a notifiable disease (Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010) — the doctor who suspects it notifies the UKHSA health protection team, which triggers contact tracing; do not wait for culture. Identifying OSA as a cause of night sweats is worthwhile because CPAP improves sleepiness and quality of life, and treated OSA is compatible with driving.
7
Treat

Treatment — Cause-directed management ladder

Menopausal night sweats
HRT 1st line
HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms (NICE NG23). Combined HRT (oestrogen + progestogen) if uterus intact. Transdermal oestrogen at standard doses does not raise VTE risk above baseline — prefer it where VTE risk is increased. Review at 3 months, then at least annually.
SSRI-induced
Medication review
Confirm the timing fits. Options: reduce the dose, or switch agent if the mood disorder allows — note venlafaxine commonly causes sweating, so it is not a switch for antidepressant-induced sweats. Discuss the balance with the patient rather than stopping abruptly.
HRT contraindicated (menopause)
Venlafaxine 2nd line
Non-hormonal options used off-label for vasomotor symptoms include venlafaxine, some SSRIs, gabapentin and clonidine — dose per BNF. They are less effective than HRT and are not first choice for vasomotor symptoms alone; avoid paroxetine and fluoxetine in women taking tamoxifen (they reduce its activation — see BNF interactions). Menopause-specific CBT is an option (NG23, November 2024). For women after breast cancer, agree the plan with the oncology team.
Nocturnal hypoglycaemia
Medication review
Reduce sulphonylurea dose; review insulin regimen; bedtime snack; CGM to confirm pattern; endocrinology if complex.
HyperthyroidismRefer to endocrinology; an antithyroid drug (usually carbimazole) is started by or with the specialist (NICE NG145, 2019). A beta-blocker such as propranolol can be started in primary care for symptoms — dose per BNF.
OSACPAP via the sleep service for moderate–severe OSA (NICE NG202, 2021). Weight loss if overweight. Reduce evening alcohol and sedatives.
TB / HIVSpecialist-led treatment. The GP role: prompt notification and referral, supporting adherence, checking for drug interactions and safeguarding contacts.
AlcoholBrief intervention; if dependent, plan assisted withdrawal rather than abrupt cessation (withdrawal itself causes sweating and can cause seizures).
HRT is the most effective treatment for menopausal vasomotor symptoms. NICE NG23 (updated November 2024) presents the risks of HRT in absolute terms so that women can weigh them for themselves; for most women under 60 the benefits for symptom control outweigh the risks. The risk of VTE with transdermal oestrogen at standard doses is no greater than baseline, which is why it is preferred for women with VTE risk factors. Non-hormonal drugs are less effective and are used off-label; menopause-specific CBT was added to NG23 in 2024 as an option alongside or instead of HRT. SSRIs and SNRIs are paradoxically both a cause of sweating and a treatment for menopausal flushes — a useful point to explain to patients.

Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Environmental and behavioural modifications

Cool sleeping environment Keep the bedroom cool. Fan, cooling mattress pad. Light, breathable cotton bedding. Moisture-wicking nightwear. Simple but highly effective.
Alcohol avoidance Alcohol is a potent vasodilator — reduces threshold for sweating. Avoid in the hours before bed; keep within 14 units a week (UK Chief Medical Officers' guidance, 2016).
Caffeine and triggers Some women find caffeine, alcohol and spicy food trigger flushes — a symptom diary helps identify personal triggers.
Weight management Obesity is the main risk factor for OSA and is associated with more troublesome vasomotor symptoms; modest weight loss can help both.
CBT for hot flushes CBT specifically designed for menopausal vasomotor symptoms — recommended as an option in NICE NG23 (November 2024), in addition to or instead of HRT. Available via some NHS Talking Therapies services or as guided self-help.
Layering bedclothes Use layers rather than single thick duvet — allows thermal self-regulation during sleep cycles without full arousal.
Smoking cessation Smoking is associated with more frequent and severe hot flushes and with earlier menopause. Refer to the stop-smoking service.
Sleep hygiene Regular sleep times, a spare set of nightwear by the bed, and a towel on the pillow reduce disruption while the cause is treated.
CBT for menopausal hot flushes (the MENOS trials) has demonstrated significant reduction in hot flush frequency and bother scores — comparable to pharmacological alternatives for women who cannot or choose not to use HRT. It should be offered alongside, not instead of, HRT discussion. Alcohol is a vasodilator and also causes sweating in withdrawal — an underemphasised, modifiable factor. Environmental cooling is inexpensive, safe, and immediately effective for any cause of night sweats while investigations are awaited.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

2 weeks
Review investigation results; confirm HIV/TB results reviewed; check drug-related cause identified and addressed
3 months
Review any menopause treatment for effect and tolerability (NICE NG23); symptom reassessment (e.g. Greene Climacteric Scale); dose adjustment if needed
3–6 months
If negative workup + no response to treatment → repeat FBC, CRP, CXR; consider CT if new features develop
Annual
HRT review at least annually: symptoms, bleeding pattern, BP, VTE and breast cancer risk, ongoing need; breast screening attendance; menopause specialist review if complex
Bleeding
Unscheduled bleeding on HRT that persists beyond the settling-in months needs assessment under the local bleeding-on-HRT pathway. Post-menopausal bleeding at 55 or over that is not attributable to HRT → suspected cancer pathway referral for endometrial cancer (NICE NG12, updated April 2026)
999 now
High fever + haemodynamic compromise + rigors; new neurological deficit; severe chest pain
Same-day GP
Weight loss accelerating; new lymphadenopathy; haemoptysis; new constitutional symptoms
SCA safety-net wording
"Your blood tests and chest X-ray haven't shown anything worrying, which is reassuring. If you notice a lump in your neck, armpit or groin, lose weight without trying, get a temperature or a cough that won't go away, please book to see me within a few days rather than waiting — and I'll see you in four weeks either way."
Re-investigate if
Night sweats persist >3 months with no cause found; B-symptoms develop at any point; new examination findings
Night sweats with a negative initial workup require a structured safety-net — lymphoma can present insidiously with a normal initial blood panel. Persistent unexplained night sweats at around 3 months should prompt re-examination, repeat bloods and a discussion with a specialist (haematology, infectious diseases or a rapid diagnostic service) about further imaging, even without new symptoms. HIV test results require active follow-up — some patients do not return for results. NICE NG23 recommends reviewing each menopause treatment at 3 months and then at least annually — reassessing benefit, risks and ongoing need.
