Safety

Red Flags — Subacute Combined Degeneration, Haematological Malignancy & Teratogenesis

Low folate + macrocytic anaemia + subacute neurological symptoms (tingling, proprioception loss, unsteady gait) WITHOUT CHECKING B12 FIRST Subacute combined degeneration (SACD) of the spinal cord from B12 deficiency masked by folate treatment. → Take diagnostic blood samples (FBC, B12/active B12, folate) immediately; start B12 treatment promptly if clinical suspicion is significant — do not wait for all confirmatory tests; do not rely on folic acid alone. Arrange urgent clinical review if gait disturbance, loss of proprioception, weakness, cognitive change or visual symptoms are present. Folate can correct the haematological features of B12 deficiency while neurological injury continues or becomes less apparent.
Pancytopenia (Hb + WBC + platelets all low) + macrocytosis + hypersegmented neutrophils + markedly reduced folate Severe megaloblastic anaemia or marrow disease. → Urgent same-day hospital/haematology assessment for severe or symptomatic anaemia, pancytopenia with infection or bleeding, suspected acute leukaemia/MDS, rapidly deteriorating counts or neurological symptoms. Use 999 if unstable, septic, actively bleeding, confused or unable to travel safely. Folate + B12 replacement. Check: is this a presentation of haematological malignancy (MDS, leukaemia) or severe nutritional deficiency?
Known pregnancy + low serum folate + high-risk pregnancy (previous NTD, anti-epileptic drugs, pre-existing diabetes) High NTD risk — inadequate folate supplementation. → Prescribe folic acid 5 mg OD immediately (high-dose) — do not delay while waiting for a folate result. Check B12, diet, malabsorption, adherence and relevant medication. A low serum folate alone does not require urgent obstetric referral; refer urgently if severe anaemia, significant vomiting/dehydration, neurological disease or another obstetric concern. BMI ≥25 alone is not an indication for 5 mg (NICE NG247).
Folate deficiency + alcohol misuse + poor dietary intake + weight loss + vomiting in first trimester Hyperemesis gravidarum with nutritional depletion — risk of Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine) and folate deficiency simultaneously. → Confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia or severe malnutrition requires urgent hospital assessment (999 if unstable or unable to travel safely). Give thiamine promptly when Wernicke encephalopathy is suspected; do not delay thiamine for glucose/carbohydrate administration. IV Pabrinex administration and dosing follow the local acute-care protocol. Not every patient with low folate, alcohol use or vomiting needs IV Pabrinex or 999.
Low folate + methotrexate use + mouth ulcers + macrocytosis + pancytopenia Methotrexate toxicity (folate antagonism). → Withhold methotrexate if significant cytopenia, severe mucositis, suspected sepsis, renal deterioration or significant LFT abnormality. Check FBC, LFTs, U&E/eGFR urgently. Contact the specialist/acute medical team. Folinic-acid rescue is specialist-led. Review prescribing — was folate supplementation prescribed?
Low folate + severe glossitis + angular cheilitis + profound fatigue + dysphagia in elderly malnourished patient Combined nutritional deficiency — risk of refeeding syndrome on correction. → Nutritional assessment. Thiamine before refeeding in severe cases (route and dose per NICE CG32 / local protocol). Dietitian referral.
The B12-folate interaction is one of the most important pharmacological interactions in clinical medicine — the 'folate trap' or 'methylfolate trap' means that B12 deficiency causes secondary intracellular folate deficiency. When folic acid is given to a patient with B12 deficiency, it partially corrects the haematological abnormalities (the blood count and MCV improve) but does nothing to address the neurological B12 deficiency. The neurological damage continues, and in fact appears to accelerate in some studies when folate is given without B12 — possibly because the increased DNA synthesis demand from haematopoietic recovery increases B12 utilisation, depleting the limited B12 stores further and worsening the neurological picture. The rule: before starting folic acid, obtain serum B12 or active B12 where clinically feasible, and never rely on folic acid alone. If both are deficient: treat B12 first, or treat both simultaneously. If there is neurological involvement or suspected megaloblastic anaemia with possible B12 deficiency, do not delay B12 replacement while awaiting results (NICE NG239). Neurological damage may become permanent if B12 treatment is delayed.

Causes of Low Folate — Classification

Inadequate dietary intake (most common)
Folate is found in: dark leafy vegetables (spinach, kale, broccoli, Brussels sprouts — highest concentrations), legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas), fortified cereals, citrus fruits, liver. Heat-labile: overcooking destroys up to 70-90% of dietary folate. At-risk populations: elderly (isolated, poor appetite, overcooking), food insecurity, eating disorders (anorexia nervosa), restricted diets (severe weight-loss diets), institutional care, alcohol dependence (poor diet + malabsorption). Folate stores are depleted within 3-4 months of inadequate intake (unlike B12 which takes 3-5 years to deplete from stores).
Increased demand — physiological and pathological
Pregnancy: folate requirement doubles (critical for cell division in rapidly dividing fetal cells — NTD risk). Lactation: increased requirement. Haemolytic anaemia (sickle cell, hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia): rapid RBC turnover increases folate requirement dramatically — long-term folic acid is often required, coordinated with haematology. Exfoliative skin disease (erythroderma, severe psoriasis): massive skin cell turnover depletes folate. Malignancy: rapidly dividing tumour cells consume folate. Dialysis: folate removed by haemodialysis (supplementation per renal-unit protocol).
Malabsorption and drug-related
Coeliac disease: folate absorption occurs in the proximal small intestine (jejunum) — coeliac disease damages this site specifically, causing folate malabsorption (and iron malabsorption — combined iron + folate deficiency is a classic coeliac presentation). IBD (Crohn's of small bowel): malabsorption + inflammatory consumption. Medications: methotrexate (inhibits dihydrofolate reductase — folate antagonist; folic acid 5 mg once weekly routine co-prescription), trimethoprim (folate antagonist — avoid in pregnancy, caution with MTX), phenytoin + carbamazepine + valproate (reduce folate absorption/metabolism), sulfasalazine (inhibits folate absorption), metformin (associated with B12 deficiency — not routine folate deficiency), alcohol (impairs folate absorption + increases urinary excretion).
Coeliac disease is the single most important treatable cause of folate deficiency to exclude in any patient presenting with low folate and macrocytic anaemia — approximately 10-15% of new coeliac diagnoses are made on the back of investigating folate or iron deficiency anaemia in primary care. The typical coeliac anaemia presentation: combined iron deficiency + folate deficiency (dual deficiency in the proximal small bowel — iron and folate both absorbed in the duodenum and proximal jejunum); this creates a 'dimorphic' blood picture (microcytic + macrocytic populations) where the MCV may appear normal despite significant dual deficiency. Anti-tTG IgA (with total IgA to exclude IgA deficiency) is the first-line coeliac test. IgA deficiency (affecting approximately 2-3% of coeliac patients) causes false-negative anti-tTG IgA — if total IgA is low, request anti-tTG IgG or anti-DGP IgG instead.

Diagnose

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Symptoms of deficiency: fatigue, dyspnoea, pallor (anaemia), sore mouth + tongue (glossitis, angular cheilitis), neurological (tingling, unsteady gait — B12 deficiency more likely but important to check). GI: diarrhoea, steatorrhoea, bloating, weight loss (malabsorption — coeliac, IBD, short bowel). Diet: is the patient eating vegetables and pulses? Cooking method. Alcohol intake (AUDIT-C). Medications: methotrexate (has folate supplementation been prescribed?), anti-epileptics (phenytoin, carbamazepine), trimethoprim course, sulfasalazine, metformin (duration). Reproductive: is the patient trying to conceive? Currently pregnant? Last menstrual period. PMH: haemolytic anaemia (sickle cell, thalassaemia, hereditary spherocytosis), malignancy, previous bowel surgery (short bowel), dialysis.
Examination
Pallor (conjunctiva + palmar creases). Mouth: glossitis (red smooth sore tongue — papillae atrophied), angular cheilitis (cracking at corners of mouth — iron + B12 + folate all cause this). Peripheral neuropathy screen (B12 deficiency — if neurological symptoms: vibration + proprioception + reflexes). Abdominal: hepatomegaly (haematological malignancy), lymphadenopathy. Skin: signs of malnutrition (muscle wasting, oedema), jaundice (haemolysis if folate depleted in haemolytic anaemia).
Investigations
FBC + MCV + reticulocyte count (macrocytosis MCV >100; hypersegmented neutrophils = megaloblastic) · Serum folate (interpret with dietary history and clinical context; red-cell folate not routinely required) · Total B12 or active B12 (before folic acid where feasible — see Step 1) · Blood film (if macrocytosis, cytopenias or diagnostic uncertainty) · Anti-tTG IgA + total IgA (where clinically indicated — while still consuming gluten; do not start a gluten-free diet before testing unless specialist advice; refer per NICE coeliac guidance if positive or suspicion remains) · LFTs + GGT (alcohol-related) · TFTs (if macrocytosis remains unexplained) · Ferritin/iron studies (where iron deficiency is possible) · CRP/ESR (if inflammatory disease suspected) · Renal function (where relevant). Homocysteine (non-specific — affected by renal disease, hypothyroidism and other factors) and methylmalonic acid are not routinely required for straightforward folate deficiency.
Red cell folate versus serum folate provide complementary information — serum folate (the standard test in most UK labs) reflects recent dietary intake over the preceding days, meaning a single good dietary intake before the blood test can give a falsely reassuring normal serum folate even in a patient with chronic deficiency. Red cell folate (RCF) reflects the folate status averaged over the lifespan of the red blood cell (approximately 120 days) and is therefore a more reliable indicator of chronic folate status. However, red cell folate can also be low in B12 deficiency (folate trap — secondary RCF depletion from B12 deficiency). In primary care, red-cell folate is not routinely required: serum folate is generally adequate for initial assessment, interpreted with dietary history and clinical context. Homocysteine is non-specific, and methylmalonic acid can support B12 deficiency but is not routinely required for straightforward folate deficiency. Most UK labs now report serum folate rather than red cell folate routinely — interpret with the clinical context.

Methotrexate Toxicity, Periconceptional Folate & Haemolytic Anaemia

Methotrexate and folate — routine co-prescription
Methotrexate (MTX) inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), blocking conversion of dietary folate to its active form (tetrahydrofolate). This causes relative intracellular folate deficiency, producing mucosal side effects (oral ulcers, GI symptoms) and haematological toxicity (macrocytosis, pancytopenia). Routine co-prescription is recommended from initiation: folic acid 5 mg once weekly on a different day from methotrexate, continued while methotrexate continues (SPS). Local rheumatology/dermatology protocols may use more frequent dosing. This supplementation does NOT reduce MTX efficacy against RA/psoriasis (the therapeutic antifolate effect on synovial cells is not affected by supplementation at these doses). It DOES significantly reduce: oral ulceration (by approximately 80%), GI side effects (nausea, diarrhoea), liver enzyme elevation, and haematological toxicity. GPs who prescribe MTX without co-prescribing folate are making a prescribing error documented in the BSR safety guidelines.
Periconceptional folic acid — NTD prevention
Standard dose: folic acid 400 mcg (0.4 mg) OD — advise anyone planning pregnancy or in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, ideally beginning before conception (NICE NG247). Available OTC or on NHS prescription. High dose 5 mg OD (prescription required): previous pregnancy affected by NTD; personal or family history of NTD or relevant congenital malformation; type 1 or type 2 diabetes; a haematological condition requiring folic acid supplementation (e.g. sickle cell disease, thalassaemia); medicines affecting folate absorption or metabolism (some anti-epileptic or HIV medicines) (NICE NG247). BMI ≥25 alone does not require more than 400 micrograms. Coeliac disease/malabsorption is not automatically an indication — consider dose by malabsorption, nutritional status and specialist advice. These women should receive 5 mg folic acid preconceptionally regardless of their folate blood level.
Folate in haemolytic anaemia
Sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia: chronic haemolysis dramatically increases RBC turnover (RBC lifespan reduced from 120 days to 10-20 days in severe haemolysis). Folate requirement increases proportionally — long-term folic acid is often required in chronic haemolysis; dose and frequency depend on the underlying condition and should follow the local haematology pathway. Folate status should not be the only monitoring parameter. A sudden fall in Hb, rising bilirubin/LDH, reticulocytopenia or macrocytosis requires urgent haematology assessment for aplastic crisis, marrow disease, B12 deficiency or iron deficiency.
The neural tube defect (NTD) high-dose folic acid (5 mg) prescription for high-risk groups is frequently under-prescribed in UK primary care — a Public Health England audit (2019) found that only approximately 30-40% of women with known risk factors received the recommended high-dose folic acid preconceptionally. The risk groups requiring 5 mg (not 400 mcg) are: previous NTD-affected pregnancy (approximately 5% recurrence risk; 5 mg reduces recurrence by approximately 70%), personal or family history of NTD or relevant congenital malformation, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, haematological conditions requiring folic acid (e.g. sickle cell disease, thalassaemia), and medicines affecting folate absorption or metabolism (some anti-epileptic or HIV medicines) (NICE NG247). BMI ≥25 alone is not an indication for 5 mg. The 5 mg dose is prescription-only — GPs prescribing preconception care for women with any of these risk factors must ensure the higher dose is prescribed and documented.

Refer

Referral Pathways

Haematology (urgent/same-day)
Severe or symptomatic anaemia · Pancytopenia with infection or bleeding · Suspected acute leukaemia/MDS · Rapidly deteriorating counts · Neurological symptoms. Use 999 if unstable, septic, actively bleeding, confused or unable to travel safely.
Gastroenterology
Confirmed coeliac disease (gluten-free diet initiation, small bowel assessment, associated complications). IBD with folate deficiency + malabsorption. Short bowel syndrome post-surgical.
Obstetrics / midwife
Low folate in pregnancy with severe anaemia, significant vomiting/dehydration, neurological disease or another obstetric concern (urgent). A low serum folate alone can be managed in primary care if clinically well. Hyperemesis gravidarum with nutritional compromise.
Dietitian
Severe nutritional deficiency (elderly, eating disorder, food insecurity). Post-bariatric surgery — complex nutritional supplementation planning. Any patient with multiple nutritional deficiencies.
GP management — the majority
Dietary folate deficiency (low intake, alcohol): folic acid 5 mg OD (often 4 months; individualise) + dietary advice + alcohol reduction. Methotrexate: add folic acid 5 mg once weekly on a different day to MTX (routine co-prescription; follow the shared-care protocol). Haemolytic anaemia: long-term folic acid per the haematology pathway. Coeliac disease: gluten-free diet + folic acid + iron + B12 supplementation as needed until nutritional absorption normalises. B12 + folate combined: treat B12 first (IM hydroxocobalamin) then folate (or simultaneously).
The folic acid duration of treatment depends critically on whether the cause has been corrected — for reversible causes of folate deficiency (recent dietary inadequacy, short-course trimethoprim, acute alcohol excess), folic acid 5 mg OD for 4 months is the standard course. This duration reflects the time needed to replete red cell folate stores (which have a 120-day lifespan) and correct the megaloblastic anaemia completely. However, for ongoing causes of increased demand or persistent malabsorption, long-term supplementation is required: lifelong for haemolytic anaemias (sickle cell, hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia), and until gluten-free diet normalises absorption for coeliac disease (typically 6-12 months minimum). The most common prescribing error is stopping folic acid at 4 months in a patient with coeliac disease who has not yet had mucosal recovery — documenting the reason for supplementation and the expected duration is essential.

Folic Acid Treatment Protocols

Dietary folate deficiency + no malabsorptionFolic acid 5 mg OD — 4 months is appropriate for many adults; individualise duration (longer for continuing malabsorption or chronic haemolysis). Check B12 before starting where feasible. Treat the underlying cause (alcohol excess, poor intake, malabsorption). Dietary review + education. Address alcohol intake if relevant. Recheck FBC at ~4–8 weeks to confirm response; repeating serum folate while taking folic acid is often unhelpful. If Hb/MCV fail to improve, reconsider B12 deficiency, iron deficiency, ongoing haemolysis, alcohol-related marrow toxicity, hypothyroidism, MDS or other marrow disease, non-adherence or malabsorption.
Methotrexate co-prescriptionFolic acid 5 mg once weekly — prescribed on a DIFFERENT day from MTX (conventionally the day after MTX). Routine co-prescription is recommended from initiation; continue while methotrexate continues. Dose and frequency follow the indication-specific shared-care protocol (some local protocols use more frequent dosing). It reduces mouth ulcers, GI symptoms, liver toxicity, and haematological toxicity without diminishing therapeutic efficacy. If symptoms or laboratory toxicity persist despite folic acid: check FBC, LFTs, U&E/eGFR; assess adherence, alcohol, interacting drugs and infection; withhold methotrexate if significant cytopenia, mucositis, renal impairment or severe infection; urgently contact the specialist, who may direct an increase in folic acid or folinic acid. Do not stop methotrexate for minor stable mouth soreness without assessment, but do not continue it in suspected serious toxicity.
Periconceptional supplementationStandard risk: folic acid 400 mcg OD — anyone planning pregnancy or in the first 12 weeks, ideally beginning before conception; continue to 12 weeks gestation. NICE high risk: folic acid 5 mg OD — ideally before conception (preconception counselling appointment), continue to 12 weeks gestation. If unplanned pregnancy detected within the first 12 weeks: start immediately. After 12 weeks: 400 mcg is not mandatory for the remainder of pregnancy — advise a balanced diet and follow antenatal supplementation guidance.
Haemolytic anaemia + dialysis — long-termLong-term folic acid per specialist protocol. Chronic haemolysis: dose and frequency depend on the underlying condition and the local haematology pathway; folate status should not be the only monitoring parameter. Dialysis patients (haemodialysis removes folate): supplementation may be appropriate, but dose, timing and monitoring follow the renal-unit protocol.
The methotrexate folate supplementation day-timing principle is clinically important and frequently misunderstood — folic acid should NOT be taken on the same day as methotrexate. The reason: while folic acid supplementation does not meaningfully reduce MTX's therapeutic antifolate effect on actively inflamed synovial tissue (at the doses used for RA/psoriasis), taking folic acid simultaneously with MTX theoretically maximises the competitive inhibition of DHFR by giving both at peak plasma concentrations simultaneously. The convention of taking folic acid the day AFTER MTX is therefore both pharmacologically logical and practically simpler for patients to remember ('MTX Monday, folic acid Tuesday'). Local shared-care protocols (e.g. Greater Manchester) support folic acid at least weekly and allow more frequent administration when needed, with specialist involvement.

Coeliac Disease Management & Nutritional Rehabilitation

Coeliac disease — gluten-free diet + nutritional correction
Strict gluten-free diet (GFD): removes all wheat, barley, and rye — mandatory and permanent. Rice, maize, potato, quinoa: safe alternatives. Oats: controversial — pure uncontaminated oats generally tolerated by most coeliacs, but introduce cautiously after remission. Specialist dietitian referral at diagnosis for GFD education. Replace deficiencies based on blood results. Annual review: symptom assessment, dietary adherence, nutritional status (FBC, folate, B12, iron, vitamin D, calcium, bone density — DEXA at diagnosis if risk factors).
Nutritional supplements at coeliac diagnosis
Iron: one oral iron tablet once daily (e.g. ferrous sulphate 200 mg), alternate days if not tolerated (BSG 2021); IV iron if oral iron is ineffective, not tolerated or malabsorption is severe. Folic acid 5 mg OD x 4-6 months (until mucosal recovery on GFD). B12: IM hydroxocobalamin loading if B12 deficient. Vitamin D: correct deficiency, then maintenance 800-2000 IU OD (coeliac = osteoporosis risk); check adjusted calcium about 1 month after any loading course; recheck 25-OH-D at about 3 months if malabsorption, symptoms or uncertain adherence — no routine annual 25-OH-D testing. Calcium: 1000 mg dietary or supplement. Zinc: supplementation if malnutrition significant. On successful GFD: absorption improves, supplements gradually weaned over 6-12 months if normal levels confirmed.
Refeeding syndrome — prevention
Risk: severely malnourished patients (NICE CG32 criteria, e.g. BMI <16, little or no intake for >10 days, or >15% unintentional weight loss in 3–6 months). Refeeding hypophosphataemia: insulin release on refeeding drives phosphate into cells → seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, respiratory failure, delirium. Prevention: oral thiamine 200–300 mg daily plus vitamin B compound strong, started before and continued for the first 10 days of refeeding (NICE CG32); start nutrition at 10 kcal/kg/day maximum and increase over 4-7 days; monitor phosphate, potassium, magnesium daily during initial refeeding phase. Hospital admission for severely malnourished patients (BMI <15).
Coeliac disease-related osteoporosis is one of the most important and frequently underappreciated long-term complications — the combination of calcium malabsorption (from small bowel damage), vitamin D malabsorption (fat-soluble vitamin), and ongoing systemic inflammation (before GFD) causes accelerated bone density loss. Approximately 30-40% of untreated coeliac patients have osteoporosis at diagnosis (T-score ≤-2.5) and a much higher proportion have osteopenia. DEXA scanning at diagnosis is recommended by the BSG for: all adults with coeliac disease diagnosed after the age of 55, any patient with documented nutritional deficiencies, and any patient with fracture risk factors. After starting GFD: bone density typically improves by approximately 5% per year in the first 2 years as calcium absorption normalises. DEXA is not automatically indicated at diagnosis; it depends on age, fracture risk, malabsorption severity and other risk factors.

Lifestyle

Diet, Alcohol & Preconception Education

Folate-rich food education Best dietary sources: dark leafy green vegetables (spinach, kale, rocket, watercress — raw or lightly steamed), broccoli and Brussels sprouts (steam, do not boil — preserves folate), legumes (lentils, chickpeas, edamame, black beans), fortified breakfast cereals (check label — 100 mcg per serving typical), liver (highest concentration but avoid in pregnancy — excess vitamin A). Citrus fruits (oranges, grapefruit). Asparagus. Avocado. Cooking destroys folate: raw or lightly cooked is best — microwaving or brief steaming is superior to boiling.
Fortification of food with folic acid USA and Canada mandate folic acid fortification of white flour since 1998 — NTD rates fell by approximately 35% in Canada post-fortification. UK: voluntary fortification of some cereals + bread. In 2021 the UK Government announced mandatory folic acid fortification of non-wholemeal wheat flour (similar to US/Canada) — implementation ongoing. GPs should know this is not yet fully implemented and supplementation advice remains essential for preconception women. Fortified foods supplement but do not replace pharmaceutical preconception supplementation.
Alcohol and folate — patient counselling Alcohol impairs folate absorption in the duodenum and jejunum (disrupts active transport mechanism), increases renal folate excretion, and interferes with folate metabolism in the liver (impairs conversion to active tetrahydrofolate). Result: even moderate-heavy drinkers may have low folate despite apparently adequate dietary intake. Folate supplementation alone (without addressing alcohol) will be less effective if drinking continues. AUDIT-C at every folate review in patients with any alcohol use. Brief intervention + NHS referral as appropriate.
Preconception counselling at every consultation For any woman of reproductive age not using contraception: confirm folic acid supplementation. Recommend 400 micrograms daily for anyone planning pregnancy or in the first 12 weeks, ideally beginning before conception (NICE NG247). Ask: "Are you planning a pregnancy in the next year?" — if yes: start folic acid immediately. High-risk groups (see Step 4): prescribe 5 mg OD. Document preconception folate advice in clinical record. The Healthy Start scheme: free vitamins (including folic acid) available for pregnant women and new mothers on Universal Credit — prescribe or signpost.
Eating disorders and folate Anorexia nervosa: severe caloric and micronutrient restriction causes multiple deficiencies including folate, B12, iron, zinc, thiamine, vitamin D. BMI <17.5 warrants assessment for refeeding syndrome risk before nutritional correction. MEED (Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders) guidance: thiamine before glucose, cautious refeeding, electrolyte monitoring. Serum folate in anorexia: often borderline or low — supplementation needed during nutritional rehabilitation. BEAT (beateatingdisorders.org.uk): referral for psychological eating disorder treatment.
Elderly nutrition and folate Isolated elderly patients are at highest risk of folate deficiency: limited food intake, difficulty shopping/cooking, heating food destroys folate, poor dentition limits vegetable consumption. Nutritional assessment at annual frailty review: BMI, MUST score (Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool), dietary recall. Prescribe folic acid 5 mg OD for any confirmed deficiency + social prescription (meal delivery service, Age UK lunch clubs, Meals on Wheels). Fortified drinks (Complan, Ensure, Fresubin) supplement oral intake.
Metformin and vitamin B12 Metformin is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency. Check B12 if symptoms or signs develop and consider periodic monitoring in people at risk, such as those on long-term or high-dose treatment, older people, those with malabsorption or anaemia (MHRA). Routine annual folate testing is not required solely because a patient takes metformin. Interpret B12 using local laboratory ranges and NICE NG239. If B12 deficiency is confirmed on metformin: IM hydroxocobalamin or oral cyanocobalamin 1 mg daily while on metformin (medicine-induced; NICE NG239). Do not stop metformin for B12 deficiency — supplement B12.
Anti-epileptic drugs and folate monitoring Enzyme-inducing AEDs (phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone, primidone, topiramate, oxcarbazepine): reduce folate by inducing CYP enzymes that catabolise folate + reduce GI absorption. Review folate status and supplementation in patients taking enzyme-inducing anti-epileptic medicines, particularly if pregnant, planning pregnancy, macrocytic, anaemic, malnourished or taking phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital or primidone. Coordinate pregnancy-related treatment with neurology. Do not stop anti-epileptic therapy abruptly. Valproate is a teratogenicity concern independent of folate status — folic acid does not remove this risk. Pregnancy prevention and medication review follow current MHRA requirements (Valproate Pregnancy Prevention Programme) and specialist advice.
The UK Valproate Pregnancy Prevention Programme (PPP) is a critical regulatory safety framework that every GP prescribing valproate must be enrolled in — sodium valproate causes NTDs in approximately 1-2% of exposed pregnancies and severe neurodevelopmental harm (autism, intellectual disability) in approximately 30-40% of children exposed in utero. The MHRA mandated the PPP in 2018: all women and girls of childbearing potential prescribed valproate must: enrol in the PPP; have an annual benefit-risk review with the specialist (neurologist or psychiatrist); be informed of the risks verbally and in writing at every prescription; use highly effective contraception; and have an Annual Risk Acknowledgement Form completed at each annual specialist review (MHRA January 2024 also restricts starting valproate in anyone under 55 to cases where two specialists independently agree). GPs prescribing repeat valproate in primary care should confirm: the Annual Risk Acknowledgement Form is current, effective contraception is confirmed, and annual specialist review is documented. GPs who prescribe valproate to women of childbearing age without PPP compliance are in breach of MHRA regulatory requirements.

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Folate deficiency treatment monitoring
Repeat FBC in approximately 4–8 weeks if there was anaemia or macrocytosis. Assess clinical response and the underlying cause. Do not routinely recheck serum folate at 4 weeks while taking folic acid. Continue long-term treatment where the cause persists (chronic haemolysis, ongoing malabsorption). Stop supplementation when the deficiency has corrected and the cause has been addressed, unless ongoing prophylaxis is required.
Coeliac disease monitoring
Clinical review, dietetic assessment, weight and nutritional blood tests (FBC, ferritin, B12, folate, vitamin D) are the priorities. Anti-tTG IgA can support follow-up but is not a universal annual compliance marker. DEXA if indicated by age, fracture risk, malabsorption severity and other risk factors. Refer if: anti-tTG remains elevated after 6-12 months on strict GFD (dietitian review), GI symptoms not improving (refractory coeliac — biopsy + specialist).
Methotrexate folate co-prescription audit
At every MTX prescription review: confirm folic acid 5 mg once weekly is co-prescribed. If not: add immediately. If MTX toxicity suspected: check FBC, LFTs, U&E/eGFR urgently; withhold MTX if significant cytopenia, severe mucositis, suspected sepsis, renal deterioration or significant LFT abnormality; contact the specialist/acute medical team (any folic/folinic acid change is specialist-led). Report MTX toxicity via MHRA Yellow Card.
Pregnancy — folate adequacy
Confirm 400 mcg/day (or 5 mg if high-risk) from first contact in pregnancy. Check serum folate only when deficiency, macrocytosis, malabsorption, poor intake or ongoing risk is present (not routinely at booking). If low: supplement, and check B12, diet, malabsorption (coeliac screen if indicated), adherence and medication. Do not delay folic acid advice while waiting for a result. If on AEDs: liaise with neurology re dosing + risk assessment.
999 / Same-day
Severe or symptomatic anaemia, pancytopenia with infection or bleeding, suspected acute leukaemia/MDS or rapidly deteriorating counts → same-day hospital/haematology (999 if unstable, septic, actively bleeding, confused or unable to travel safely) · SACD symptoms (gait disturbance, proprioceptive loss, weakness, cognitive or visual change) → urgent review and prompt B12 · Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia) → urgent hospital assessment + prompt thiamine
Within 1-2 weeks
Confirmed folate deficiency: take B12 bloods before starting folic acid where feasible; do not delay B12 treatment if neurological symptoms. Coeliac confirmed: dietitian + nutritional supplementation. Methotrexate without folate co-prescription: add immediately.
The SACD (subacute combined degeneration of the cord) risk from folate-before-B12 is the central safety message of this algorithm and deserves reinforcement at every folate deficiency consultation — the clinical scenario that kills: a patient presents with macrocytic anaemia and low folate is checked. B12 is not checked. Folic acid 5 mg is prescribed. The MCV normalises, the Hb rises, and the GP is satisfied. Three months later, the patient presents with progressive unsteady gait, tingling in hands and feet, and difficulty walking. B12 is now checked — it is profoundly deficient. The neurological damage from SACD, which would have been prevented by checking B12 at the outset and treating it, is now established and partially irreversible. This scenario is preventable by a single rule: NEVER prescribe folic acid for macrocytic anaemia or folate deficiency without checking B12 simultaneously.
