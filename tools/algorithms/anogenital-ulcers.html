Safety

Red Flags — Syphilis, Malignancy & Rapidly Spreading Infection

Painless well-defined genital ulcer with indurated (hard) base + unilateral non-tender inguinal lymphadenopathy + sexually active adult Primary syphilis (chancre) until proved otherwise. → Urgent GUM via the local sexual-health pathway. Laboratory syphilis serology (treponemal screening assay + quantitative RPR). Consider T. pallidum PCR from lesion material via sexual health/reference laboratory (dark-field microscopy is a specialist test). Staging and treatment (benzathine benzylpenicillin for early syphilis) by GUM per BASHH 2024.
Multiple painful genital ulcers + fever + tender inguinal lymphadenopathy + systemic illness + rapidly progressive Primary herpes simplex (HSV-1 or HSV-2) or chancroid (Haemophilus ducreyi — common in Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia). → Urgent GUM. HSV PCR swab (with typing). Aciclovir 400 mg TDS or valaciclovir 500 mg BD for 5–10 days. Same-day hospital if urinary retention, severe pain needing parenteral analgesia, neurological symptoms or pregnancy.
Painful anogenital or perianal lesions + fever or lymphadenopathy, proctitis, systemic illness, close skin-to-skin sexual exposure, or clustered/umbilicated lesions Mpox. → Lesion swabs via the sexual-health/infectious-disease pathway. Do not treat as routine HSV without appropriate assessment.
Unexplained anal mass or ulceration; unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding; penile mass or ulcerated lesion after STI excluded or persisting after STI treatment Anal, vulval or penile cancer (NICE NG12). → consider colorectal, gynaecology or urology suspected cancer pathway referral. Persistent, unexplained or suspicious ulcers need specialist assessment and biopsy where indicated (arranged by the specialist service); do not delay STI testing or treatment while awaiting biopsy.
Rapidly spreading painful ulceration + necrotic black eschar + systemic sepsis + perineal/perianal involvement Ecthyma gangrenosum or Fournier's gangrene (necrotising fasciitis). → 999. IV antibiotics. Immediate surgical debridement.
Severe systemic illness, urinary retention, severe pain needing parenteral analgesia, rapidly progressive necrosis, ocular or neurological symptoms, or pregnancy with suspected primary genital herpes or syphilis → Same-day hospital assessment (with obstetric input in pregnancy).
Recurrent oral and genital aphthae + ocular, skin, vascular, neurological or articular features Consider Behçet's disease (the triad is a clue, not a diagnostic rule). → Urgent ophthalmology for painful red eye, photophobia or visual disturbance; rheumatology/dermatology referral for recurrent or multisystem disease. HLA-B51 is not diagnostic; pathergy testing is specialist-led and not routinely required.
Anogenital ulcers + generalised lymphadenopathy + maculopapular rash on palms + soles + weight loss + immunosuppression or HIV risk Secondary syphilis or HIV primary infection (seroconversion illness). → GUM clinic same-day. HIV test + full STI screen urgently.
Primary syphilis is the most important single STI diagnosis not to miss in primary care — infectious syphilis diagnoses in England reached 9,513 in 2023 (UKHSA), the highest annual number since 1948, making it one of the fastest-rising STIs. The primary chancre is pathognomonic but frequently missed because: (1) it is painless (unlike HSV which is intensely painful), so patients may not notice it; (2) it can occur on the cervix, vagina, or rectum where it is not visible; (3) it resolves spontaneously in 3-6 weeks even without treatment, leaving the patient in the latent phase; and (4) it can be confused with other genital ulcers. The GP assessment standard: any painless, hard-based, well-defined genital ulcer in a sexually active adult, in any anatomical location, is syphilis until syphilis serology proves otherwise. Send laboratory syphilis serology with a treponemal screening assay and quantitative RPR; the laboratory performs confirmatory testing according to its validated algorithm (exact assays are laboratory-dependent). Treponemal tests usually remain positive for life and are not suitable for monitoring treatment response. A negative serology does not exclude very early primary syphilis — repeat testing (about 2 weeks after a negative lesion test, BASHH) if suspicion remains. Treatment and partner notification via GUM.

Classification of Anogenital Ulcers

STI-related ulcers
Genital herpes (HSV-2 most common genitally; HSV-1 increasing due to oral-genital sex): painful vesicles that rupture to form painful shallow ulcers with erythematous base, unilateral or bilateral, tender lymphadenopathy. Primary infection: systemic illness, high fever, multiple lesions, extremely painful, 2-3 weeks duration. Recurrence: shorter, less severe, prodromal tingling. Primary syphilis: single painless indurated ulcer, clean base, unilateral non-tender rubbery lymphadenopathy, heals in 3-6 weeks. Chancroid (Haemophilus ducreyi): rare in UK, common in Africa/Asia — painful ulcer with undermined edges, purulent base, tender fluctuant lymphadenopathy. LGV (Lymphogranuloma venereum — Chlamydia trachomatis serovars L1-L3): inguinal lymphadenopathy (buboes) + genital ulcer, proctitis in MSM. Mpox: painful anogenital/perianal lesions (may be clustered or umbilicated) ± fever, lymphadenopathy, proctitis.
Non-STI genital ulcers
Aphthous ulcers (idiopathic — same as oral aphthous): round, shallow, painful, female genital (vulva, vagina). Lipschütz ulcer (acute genital aphthosis): acute vulvar ulcers, which can occur in sexually active or inactive people — a diagnosis of exclusion; may follow EBV, CMV, influenza, COVID-19 or other systemic infection. Medication-related and other causes remain possible. Lichen planus (erosive): vulvar or penile erosions + white lacy striae. Lichen sclerosus: vulvar or penile white atrophic skin with fissures. Fixed drug eruption: recurrent ulcer or erythema at exact same site after drug exposure (NSAIDs, tetracyclines, co-trimoxazole). Crohn's disease: perianal fissures + fistulae + "knife-cut" ulcers (specialist management). Trauma / sexual assault.
Tumour and systemic
VIN (vulvar/vaginal/anal intraepithelial neoplasia) → SCC: warty or plaque-like lesion + central ulceration. Secondary syphilis: condyloma lata (moist flat papules in perianal/vulvar area + systemic features). HIV seroconversion: ulcers + generalised rash + lymphadenopathy + fever 2-4 weeks post-exposure. Behçet's disease: recurrent painful oral + genital ulcers ± ocular, skin, vascular, neurological or articular features.
The Lipschutz ulcer (ulcus vulvae acutum) is an important diagnosis of exclusion that avoids an incorrect STI label and patient distress — it presents as an acute painful vulvar ulcer, often in a girl or young woman, and can occur in sexually active people too. It may follow EBV, CMV, influenza, COVID-19 or other systemic infection. The clinical features: acute onset, intensely painful ulcers on the labia majora or labia minora, often multiple, shallow with grey or necrotic base, occurring within days of a viral prodrome. It is a diagnosis of exclusion — appropriate STI testing (including HSV PCR) is still needed. Paul-Bunnell testing is not required; use targeted EBV/CMV serology only when clinically indicated. Management: supportive (topical lidocaine gel, oral analgesia, saline bathing), with specialist advice for severe disease. Recognising it avoids an incorrect STI diagnosis and its psychological impact.

Assessment — History, Examination & Investigations

History
Pain (painless = syphilis; intensely painful = HSV, chancroid; moderately painful = aphthous, Lipschutz; painless = malignancy early). Onset: sudden + acute (HSV, syphilis) vs gradual (SCC, LS, lichen planus). Number: single (syphilis, SCC) vs multiple (HSV, aphthous). Duration: days-weeks (HSV, syphilis), months (SCC, lichen planus, Crohn's). Sexual history: new partners, unprotected sex, type of sexual activity (anal sex — LGV, HSV-2, syphilis), recent travel (chancroid — Africa, South Asia), close skin-to-skin sexual contact (mpox), HIV PEP window (exposure within 72h). Pregnancy and immunosuppression. Systemic: fever + lymphadenopathy (HSV primary, syphilis, HIV). Skin/joint/eye: Behcet's (uveitis, oral ulcers), reactive arthritis (circinate balanitis), lichen planus (elsewhere). Bowel symptoms (Crohn's perianal). Medications (fixed drug eruption). Previous episodes (HSV recurrence, aphthous).
Examination
Chaperone. Systematic genital examination with good lighting. Describe: location, number, shape, size, edges (regular vs irregular vs undermined), base (clean vs sloughing/purulent vs indurated), depth, overlying skin. Unilateral vs bilateral. Lymph nodes: inguinal (tender = HSV/chancroid/LGV; non-tender hard = syphilis/malignancy). Anal inspection (perianal ulcers, fissures, fistulae). Oral mucosa (Behcet's, secondary syphilis, HIV). Skin: palms/soles (secondary syphilis rash), rash distribution (HIV seroconversion). Male: urethra (discharge). Female: cervix (syphilitic chancre may be on cervix — missed clinically).
Investigations
Lesion swab: HSV-1/HSV-2 PCR/NAAT with typing (base of a fresh ulcer or unroofed vesicle; sensitivity falls as lesions heal — a negative from an old or crusted lesion does not exclude HSV) · T. pallidum PCR (where available, via sexual health) · VZV / mpox PCR (unilateral/atypical eruption; mpox features) · Laboratory syphilis serology + quantitative RPR · HIV test (4th generation laboratory Ag/Ab) · Hepatitis B/C (where indicated) · Gonorrhoea + chlamydia NAAT (site-specific, including rectal/pharyngeal by sexual history) · Chancroid testing (Haemophilus ducreyi) (if travel history or atypical appearance, via GUM) · EBV/CMV serology (only if systemic infection clinically suspected) · Targeted autoimmune testing only when the phenotype supports it (no routine ANA, anti-dsDNA or HLA-B51) · Biopsy (persistent, unexplained or suspicious ulcer — arranged by the specialist service)
The 4th generation HIV test (combined antigen-antibody test) is the current standard for HIV diagnosis in the UK — it detects both HIV antibodies and HIV p24 antigen (detectable before antibodies develop). This significantly reduces the window period compared to 3rd generation antibody-only tests. BHIVA/BASHH/BIA guidance uses a 45-day window period for fourth-generation laboratory testing (point-of-care antibody tests have a longer window): a negative result does not exclude infection until at least 45 days after exposure, so repeat testing is required if exposure was more recent or acute HIV is clinically suspected. Consider HIV RNA/specialist testing in suspected acute seroconversion with negative or indeterminate serology. If there was a high-risk exposure within 72 hours, do not delay PEP assessment — refer urgently to sexual health, A&E or HIV services.

Distinguishing Primary Syphilis from HSV

Clinical comparison table
Primary syphilis: single ulcer (usually); painless; indurated hard base; clean well-defined edges; heals spontaneously in 3-6 weeks; lymphadenopathy unilateral non-tender "rubbery" nodes; systemic illness absent; syphilis serology/RPR positive (may be negative very early — repeat if suspicion remains) or lesion T. pallidum PCR positive. Genital herpes: multiple vesicles/ulcers (usually); intensely painful; soft tender base with erythema; irregular ragged edges; heals in 2-3 weeks (primary); lymphadenopathy bilateral tender; systemic illness (primary): fever, malaise, myalgia; HSV PCR positive.
Co-infection
Syphilis and HSV co-infection occurs — particularly in MSM. A painful ulcer can be BOTH syphilitic AND herpetic. The principle: test for ALL STIs simultaneously (HSV PCR + syphilis serology + gonorrhoea + chlamydia + HIV ± mpox/VZV PCR) at the same visit rather than sequential testing based on clinical impression. A single clinical consultation at GUM can obtain all samples and initiate treatment if appropriate.
LGV (Lymphogranuloma venereum)
LGV (Chlamydia trachomatis L1-L3) is an important cause of proctitis in UK MSM. Presents as: (1) primary genital ulcer (painless, heals quickly, often missed); (2) inguinal syndrome (tender fluctuant lymph nodes, buboes that may rupture); (3) anorectal syndrome (proctitis — rectal pain, bleeding, tenesmus, discharge — common in MSM who practise receptive anal sex). Diagnosis: chlamydia NAAT positive on rectal or genital swab + LGV confirmatory test (genotype L1-L3). Treatment and partner notification via GUM (BASHH LGV guidance) — not a primary-care treatment pathway.
LGV (Lymphogranuloma venereum) anorectal syndrome in MSM is one of the most clinically significant STI syndromes to recognise in primary care — the anorectal presentation (proctitis) causes rectal pain, tenesmus, bloody or mucoid anal discharge, and constipation, and is frequently misdiagnosed as inflammatory bowel disease (particularly Crohn's proctitis) because the endoscopic appearance (rectal erythema, ulceration, friability) is similar. The key clinical discriminator: LGV proctitis occurs in MSM who practise receptive anal sex and may have concurrent STIs; IBD typically occurs in a different demographic and does not have concurrent STIs. Any MSM presenting with proctitis should have rectal NAAT swab for chlamydia + LGV genotyping + other STI screens before colorectal or gastroenterological investigation. Treatment is via GUM, which uses a longer doxycycline course than for standard chlamydia.

NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) — consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for: vulval cancer with an unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding; penile cancer with a penile mass or ulcerated lesion once an STI has been excluded as the cause, or a penile lesion persisting after STI treatment is completed; anal cancer with an unexplained anal mass or anal ulceration. Ophthalmology / rheumatology / dermatology
Suspected Behçet's: urgent ophthalmology for painful red eye, photophobia or visual disturbance; rheumatology/dermatology for recurrent or multisystem disease. Gastroenterology/colorectal: atypical fissures, fistulae, multiple perianal ulcers, tags, abscesses or persistent disease (possible Crohn's)
GP management
Recurrent HSV (known): episodic aciclovir (e.g. 800 mg TDS x 2 days). Acute genital aphthosis (Lipschütz) after STI testing: supportive analgesia, saline bathing, reassurance; specialist advice if severe. Treat empirically only according to clinical likelihood, local pathway and specialist advice. Fixed drug eruption: identify and withdraw causative drug.
The primary care HIV test offer in any patient presenting with an STI or anogenital ulcer is recommended in the BHIVA/BASHH/BIA adult HIV testing guidelines (2020) and NICE NG60 (2016) — HIV testing should be offered at every STI consultation as a routine, not a targeted, investigation. The rationale: HIV-positive individuals who are unaware of their status are the primary drivers of new HIV transmissions; HIV testing at all STI contacts maximises case detection; modern HIV treatment (ART) is highly effective and starts immediately after diagnosis. The GP should frame the HIV test as routine: 'We routinely test for HIV as part of our sexual health check — it is a simple blood test and I recommend it for everyone being assessed for any STI.' The opt-out approach (testing unless declined) significantly increases HIV testing rates compared to the opt-in approach.

Treat

Syphilis, HSV & Specialist STI Management

Primary syphilis treatment
Not a GP prescribing protocol. Suspected primary, secondary or early latent syphilis requires urgent GUM assessment, staging, baseline quantitative RPR and treatment according to the current BASHH guideline (2024). Benzathine benzylpenicillin is the standard treatment for early syphilis. Penicillin allergy, pregnancy, neurological/ocular symptoms and uncertain duration require specialist management. Pregnancy: penicillin remains the treatment of choice — penicillin-allergic pregnant patients need specialist desensitisation, not doxycycline. Doxycycline is an alternative for some non-pregnant, non-neurosyphilis cases, prescribed through GUM. Macrolides are not alternatives (resistance and treatment failure). Late latent: three weekly benzathine penicillin doses — interruptions and intervals need specialist interpretation. Neurosyphilis/ocular syphilis: specialist assessment and IV treatment. Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction: usually within 24 hours of treatment; not an allergic reaction or treatment failure — manage symptomatically; in pregnancy it may precipitate uterine contractions or fetal compromise and needs obstetric advice. Partner notification is a specialist sexual-health responsibility (BASHH 2024).
Genital herpes — treatment
First episode: aciclovir 400 mg TDS or valaciclovir 500 mg BD for 5–10 days (BASHH 2024); review and extend if new lesions continue to form or systemic/neurological complications are present. Start within 5 days of onset (or while new lesions forming). Analgesia (ibuprofen + paracetamol). Topical lidocaine gel (5%) for pain. For urinary retention from severe dysuria: voiding in a warm bath. Recurrence: aciclovir 800 mg TDS x 2 days (episodic treatment). Suppression: aciclovir 400 mg BD daily — consider when recurrences are painful, distressing, disruptive, or there is concern about transmission (not restricted to a fixed number of attacks); response varies; review need at least annually. Partner counselling: transmission risk from asymptomatic shedding; condoms reduce but do not eliminate transmission risk.
HIV PEP and PrEP
PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis): start as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours, and generally not beyond 72 hours, based on a documented exposure-risk assessment (including whether the source has an undetectable viral load). BASHH 2021 preferred regimen for most adults: tenofovir disoproxil/emtricitabine 245/200 mg once daily + raltegravir 1200 mg once daily for 28 days (raltegravir 400 mg BD in some circumstances, e.g. pregnancy or if once-daily formulation unavailable). Check renal function and drug interactions. Prescribed through sexual health, A&E or HIV services. PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis): tenofovir/emtricitabine daily via sexual health clinics; the on-demand 2+1+1 regimen is for anal sex in eligible MSM only — not suitable for vaginal exposure. Highly effective when taken as prescribed; efficacy depends on adherence and exposure type.
The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction after syphilis treatment is an important patient safety counselling point — many patients with primary or secondary syphilis develop the reaction, usually within 24 hours of their first treatment: fever, rigors, headache, myalgia, and worsening of existing lesions. It is caused by the rapid release of lipoproteins from dying Treponema pallidum spirochaetes, triggering a systemic inflammatory response. The reaction is expected, transient, and is not an allergic reaction or treatment failure — manage symptomatically. In pregnancy it may precipitate uterine contractions or fetal compromise, so obstetric advice is needed. Patients must be warned before treatment: 'You may feel quite unwell with fever and chills tonight — this is the infection dying off and it means the treatment is working. Take paracetamol, rest, and drink fluids. Contact us if you feel very unwell.' Failing to warn the patient often leads to unnecessary re-presentation to A&E with the self-described 'allergic reaction' to penicillin.

Treat

Non-STI Genital Ulcers & Recurrent Aphthous

Genital aphthous ulcers and Lipschutz ulcers
Genital aphthous (idiopathic): same pathogenesis as oral aphthous — painful, round, shallow ulcers with grey base and erythematous halo, vulva or vagina. Topical: triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% in orabase applied to ulcer, or topical lidocaine 5% gel for pain. Severe or major disease: specialist advice. Investigate recurrent attacks: check ferritin + B12 + folate + coeliac screen (anti-tTG IgA). Lipschütz ulcer (acute genital aphthosis — diagnosis of exclusion after STI testing): targeted EBV/CMV serology only if clinically indicated (Paul-Bunnell not required). Treatment: supportive (analgesia + saline bathing); specialist advice for severe disease.
Crohn's perianal disease
Perianal fissures (may be multiple + lateral — unlike common midline anal fissure of idiopathic origin), fistulae (track from perianal skin to anal canal), "knife-cut" ulcers (deep, well-defined, often with oedematous skin tags). Any young patient with atypical perianal fissures or fistulae that do not respond to standard conservative treatment should be referred to gastroenterology/colorectal services. Stool calprotectin and CRP may support assessment but do not exclude isolated perianal Crohn's. Antibiotics, imaging, seton placement and biologics require specialist management — not a primary-care treatment pathway.
Behcet's disease management
Consider with recurrent oral and genital aphthae (often more scarring than usual aphthous) plus ocular, skin (e.g. erythema nodosum), vascular, neurological or articular features. The oral + genital + uveitis triad is a useful clue but not a diagnostic rule; HLA-B51 is not diagnostic and pathergy testing is specialist-led. Urgent ophthalmology for painful red eye, photophobia or visual disturbance. Refer to rheumatology/dermatology for recurrent or multisystem disease — systemic treatment (colchicine, azathioprine, anti-TNF) is specialist-led.
The doxycycline 21-day course for LGV proctitis is a critical prescribing distinction from standard chlamydia treatment — Chlamydia trachomatis serotypes D-K (non-LGV) are treated first-line with doxycycline 100 mg BD x 7 days (BASHH chlamydia guideline 2018), with azithromycin 1 g then 500 mg daily for 2 days as the alternative. LGV serovars L1-L3 require a longer doxycycline course managed by GUM (BASHH LGV guidance). A patient treated for 'chlamydia' with azithromycin who actually has LGV will fail treatment, with ongoing proctitis and risk of rectal stricture formation from untreated LGV proctitis. The clinical lesson: any patient with chlamydia confirmed on rectal swab who has symptoms of proctitis (rectal pain, tenesmus, rectal discharge) must have LGV serogrouping requested before selecting antibiotic treatment.

Lifestyle

Sexual Health, Condom Use & Partner Notification

Consistent condom use Condoms reduce but do not eliminate genital herpes and syphilis transmission — transmission can occur from skin and lesion sites not covered by the condom. Polyurethane condoms (for latex allergy): comparable efficacy. Female condoms: equivalent STI protection for insertive partner. Dental dams: for oral-vulvar contact (reduces oral STI transmission). Correct use: correct size, correct orientation, throughout sexual activity (not just at ejaculation).
PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis) for HIV prevention PrEP (tenofovir/emtricitabine) is highly effective when taken as prescribed; efficacy depends on adherence and exposure type. NHS availability: via sexual health clinics. Eligible: HIV-negative people with significant ongoing HIV risk — most often MSM and trans women, but also, for example, heterosexual people whose partner has a detectable viral load (BHIVA/BASHH PrEP guidance). Daily PrEP for all eligible people; on-demand PrEP (2 + 1 + 1 dosing) is an anal-sex regimen for eligible MSM only — not suitable for vaginal exposure. Does not protect against other STIs — regular STI screening still required (3-monthly for MSM on PrEP). Side effects: mild GI disturbance initially, monitor renal function (the sexual health clinic arranges this).
HPV vaccination and anogenital cancer prevention Gardasil 9 (9-valent HPV vaccine): protects against HPV 6, 11 (warts) + 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 (high-risk cancer types). NHS school programme (Year 8, age 12-13; one dose). Catch-up: up to age 25 for those who missed school dose. GBMSM: up to age 45 through sexual health clinics (UKHSA HPV programme; Green Book chapter 18a). People living with HIV or who are immunosuppressed need the immunocompromised schedule (Green Book 18a). HPV vaccination does not replace cervical screening and does not treat existing HPV infection or lesions. There is no UK national anal cancer screening programme; the HIV clinic advises on anal cancer surveillance for people with HIV.
Regular STI screening for sexually active adults Offer an STI screen after any new partner or STI contact; BASHH advises at least annual testing for MSM and more frequent (3-monthly) testing for: MSM with multiple partners or on PrEP, HIV-positive individuals, anyone with recent new partner or STI diagnosis. Point-of-care testing (POCT): HIV + syphilis rapid tests at GUM clinics. Home STI testing kits (MHRA-approved) available via NHS: SH:24, Fettle.
Partner notification after STI diagnosis Partner notification (PN) is a public health and clinical obligation — refer confirmed syphilis, HIV, LGV, gonorrhoea and chlamydia diagnoses to the sexual-health service for formal partner notification. The GP should not attempt to independently manage partner notification for complex STIs. PN reduces onward transmission and prevents reinfection of the treated patient. Electronic partner notification (e-notification via text message): effective for chlamydia + gonorrhoea (reduces the need for patient to notify in person). For syphilis and HIV: patient referral + provider referral (GUM contact tracer) used in combination.
Recurrent HSV counselling and self-management Triggers for recurrence: stress, fatigue, UV light (labial HSV), illness, menstruation, local trauma. Identifying personal triggers allows avoidance and reduces recurrence frequency. Prodrome recognition: tingling, itching, burning at the outbreak site 12-48h before the ulcer — starting episodic treatment at the prodrome can reduce outbreak severity. Suppressive therapy: aciclovir 400 mg BD daily — consider when recurrences are painful, distressing, disruptive or there is concern about transmission; review at least annually. Psychological impact: HSV diagnosis causes significant distress — address at every consultation.
Sexual health and mental health STI diagnosis, particularly HSV or HIV, causes significant distress — shame, stigma, fear of disclosure to partners, impact on relationships and self-image. Acknowledge at every consultation. Assess mood and anxiety (e.g. PHQ-9/GAD-7 when clinically appropriate). NHS Talking Therapies for anxiety related to HSV. HIV: Terrence Higgins Trust (tht.org.uk), GMFA, National AIDS Trust — peer support and psychological services. HSV: Herpes Viruses Association (hva.org.uk).
Anogenital warts (HPV) and genital ulcer relationship Condylomata acuminata (genital warts from HPV 6/11) may become ulcerated — differential includes trauma, HSV, syphilis and secondary bacterial infection; ulceration alone does not imply malignant transformation. An ulcerated, indurated or irregular wart requires specialist assessment (VIN/AIN/SCC; biopsy where indicated). Secondary syphilis produces condyloma lata (flat moist broad papules in perianal/genital area, not pointed) which can be confused with condyloma acuminata. Lesion T. pallidum PCR and serology via sexual health confirm the diagnosis.
The psychological impact of genital herpes diagnosis is substantial and well-documented — many patients with newly diagnosed genital herpes experience significant anxiety, shame, and relationship distress. The prognosis, however, is usually better than patients initially fear: some people never have a recognised recurrence; most who do have recurrences find they become less frequent and less severe over time; and suppressive therapy reduces recurrences, although response varies. The GP's communication at the time of HSV diagnosis is critical for long-term psychological outcomes — stating clearly: 'Genital herpes is a very common condition. It is manageable, it does not define your sexual health, and there are effective treatments to reduce outbreaks.' This evidence-based reassurance, combined with written information and referral to specialist sexual health nursing, significantly reduces anxiety and stigma.

Safety

Follow-Up, Surveillance & Safety-Netting

After syphilis treatment
Follow quantitative RPR through the sexual-health service (same method and laboratory where possible), typically at 3, 6 and 12 months, with additional follow-up according to stage, HIV status and baseline titre. Treponemal tests are not repeated to assess cure. A fourfold fall is not guaranteed by 6 months (depends on stage, baseline titre, previous treatment, reinfection, HIV status). A fourfold rise suggests reinfection or treatment failure. Failure to decline appropriately should prompt GUM review rather than automatic retreatment or lumbar puncture. Partner notification and follow-up are sexual-health responsibilities — ensure the patient is linked to GUM.
After HSV diagnosis
Review at 2 weeks post-primary episode: psychological support, treatment response. Suppressive therapy discussion if recurrences are painful, distressing, disruptive or transmission is a concern. Pregnancy: primary or non-primary first-episode genital herpes in the third trimester, especially within 6 weeks of delivery → urgent obstetric/GUM management (planned Caesarean generally recommended; BASHH/RCOG 2024). Recurrent HSV alone does not routinely require planned Caesarean; lesions or prodromal symptoms at labour need obstetric assessment. Suppressive treatment timing follows BASHH/RCOG 2024. Neonatal HSV devastating — all women with HSV history should inform obstetric team.
After HIV diagnosis
Immediate BHIVA pathway: CD4 count + HIV viral load + resistance testing → ART initiation within 7 days (treat-all policy). Partner notification. Regular monitoring: HIV viral load 3-6 monthly, CD4 annually once stable. Opportunistic infection prophylaxis if CD4 <200 (co-trimoxazole for PCP; other prophylaxis as advised by the HIV clinic). Annual cervical screening for women with HIV (NHS Cervical Screening Programme); anal cancer surveillance per the HIV clinic.

Suspected cancer
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding; penile mass or ulcerated lesion once an STI is excluded (or persisting after STI treatment); unexplained anal mass or anal ulceration. Persistent, unexplained or suspicious ulcers (including suspicious VIN/AIN lesions) → specialist assessment and biopsy where indicated; do not delay STI testing or treatment while awaiting biopsy
The neonatal herpes transmission risk from a mother with genital HSV is one of the most important antenatal safety discussions in primary care — neonatal HSV carries a high mortality without treatment and causes devastating neurological sequelae in survivors. The highest-risk scenario: primary genital HSV infection in the third trimester (particularly at ≥36 weeks) because: (1) the mother has not yet developed protective antibodies; (2) viral shedding is maximal; and (3) the infant will not receive passive antibody protection. Management: primary or non-primary first-episode genital herpes in the third trimester needs urgent obstetric/GUM management — planned Caesarean section is generally recommended, especially within 6 weeks of delivery, because the risk of neonatal transmission during vaginal delivery is high. Recurrent HSV alone does not routinely require planned Caesarean. Suppressive aciclovir from 32 weeks (earlier where preterm delivery risk is high) reduces viral shedding and recurrence at delivery (BASHH/RCOG 2024). All women with a history of genital HSV should inform their midwife and obstetrician at booking.
