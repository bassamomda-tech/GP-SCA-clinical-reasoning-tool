1
Safety

Red flags — immediate life threats in acute confusion

Screen FIRST, before any cognitive testing. Delirium is the brain's signal that something else is wrong, and that something may be killing the patient. Check a capillary glucose and a full set of observations (NEWS2) at the first contact, including a home visit. Any red flag → 999 or same-day emergency assessment.
Hypoglycaemia Capillary glucose <4 mmol/L → treat now (oral glucose if the swallow is safe; glucagon/IV glucose if not) and call 999 if not responding or on a sulfonylurea/insulin with ongoing risk
Suspected sepsis New altered mental state is itself a high-risk criterion in NICE NG253 (2025), as are RR ≥25, HR >130, systolic BP ≤90, no urine for 18 h or a non-blanching rash → 999 with pre-alert
Meningism / purpuric rash Neck stiffness, photophobia, petechiae → 999. For strongly suspected meningococcal disease NICE NG240 (2024) advises IV/IM ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin (dose per BNF) before transfer only if it will not delay transfer
Reduced consciousness GCS ≤13, responds only to voice or pain, or drowsiness that is deepening → 999 (encephalitis, subdural, stroke, hypoglycaemia, toxidrome)
Focal neurology / sudden onset Hemiparesis, dysphasia, facial droop, visual field loss, new ataxia → 999 (stroke, subdural haematoma, space-occupying lesion)
Fall or head injury on an anticoagulant Any head injury in a confused patient taking a DOAC, warfarin or non-aspirin antiplatelet → same-day ED; NICE NG232 (2023) advises considering CT head within 8 hours of injury
Seizure Witnessed fit, tongue biting, incontinence, or unexplained prolonged drowsiness (possible non-convulsive status) → 999 if ongoing or not recovering
Severe hypertension Clinic BP ≥180/120 with new confusion, papilloedema or retinal haemorrhage → same-day specialist assessment (NICE NG136, 2019, updated 2023)
Toxidrome Pinpoint pupils + slow breathing (opioid: naloxone per BNF, 999); hot, dry, flushed, dilated pupils (anticholinergic); clonus, hyperthermia, agitation on serotonergic drugs (serotonin syndrome) → 999
Alcohol withdrawal / Wernicke's Tremor, sweating, hallucinations 6–72 h after the last drink, or confusion + ataxia + eye signs in a heavy drinker → same-day admission for parenteral thiamine and a benzodiazepine regimen (NICE CG100)
Delirium is a clinical syndrome, not a diagnosis: the precipitant has to be found. The causes that kill quickly (hypoglycaemia, sepsis, hypoxia, intracranial bleeding, meningitis, toxic states and alcohol withdrawal) can all be screened for in the first few minutes with a glucose, observations and a quick neurological look, which is why they come before any cognitive test. The sepsis guidance for people aged 16 or over (NICE NG253, November 2025) now replaces NG51 for adults; a new altered mental state still counts as a high-risk feature, so a confused older person with a suspected infection and any other high-risk sign needs emergency transfer rather than a community antibiotic. Subdural haematoma is the classic trap: the fall may have been days ago, may not have been witnessed, and the only sign may be fluctuating drowsiness in someone on an anticoagulant. Wernicke's encephalopathy rarely shows the full triad, so the threshold for parenteral thiamine in a heavy drinker who is confused should be low.
2
Diagnose

Confirm delirium — 4AT plus a collateral history

Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days and fluctuates. SIGN 157 (2019) recommends the 4AT to identify probable delirium and says it can be considered in community settings; it takes under two minutes and needs no training. NICE CG103 (2010, updated 2023) lists the indicators to look for: recent change (hours or days) in cognition, perception (hallucinations, illusions), physical function (reduced mobility, falls, poor appetite) or social behaviour (withdrawal, agitation, loss of co-operation).
4AT items
1. Alertness — normal = 0; clearly abnormal (drowsy or hyperalert) = 4. 2. AMT4 (age, date of birth, place, year) — no mistakes = 0; one = 1; two or more or untestable = 2. 3. Attention (months of the year backwards) — 7 or more correct = 0; starts but <7, or refuses = 1; untestable = 2. 4. Acute change or fluctuating course (from history or collateral) — no = 0; yes = 4
4AT score
≥4 = possible delirium ± cognitive impairment → act on it. 1–3 = possible cognitive impairment. 0 = delirium or severe cognitive impairment unlikely, but delirium remains possible if the acute-change information is incomplete. The 4AT is a screen: the diagnosis is clinical
Accuracy
Pooled sensitivity 0.88 and specificity 0.88 across 17 studies (Tieges et al., Age and Ageing, 2021)
Collateral history
ESSENTIAL. Ask a relative, carer or care-home staff: "What are they normally like? When did this start? Is it worse at some times of day? Any falls, new tablets, less eating or drinking, bowel or water problems?" A clear recent change from a known baseline is the single most useful fact
Time course
Delirium: hours to days, fluctuating, attention impaired. Dementia: months to years, attention relatively preserved until late. Depression: weeks, poor effort ("don't know" answers), attention usually intact. Primary psychosis: usually younger, clear consciousness, orientated
Subtypes
Hyperactive (restless, agitated, hallucinating), hypoactive (quiet, sleepy, withdrawn, eating less) and mixed. Hypoactive delirium is the form most often missed and is easily mistaken for depression or tiredness
Delirium on dementia
A sudden decline in someone with known dementia is delirium until proven otherwise; score the acute-change item from the carer's account, not from the patient's baseline test scores
The 4AT is weighted so that a patient who is too drowsy or agitated to be tested still scores 4 or more, which prevents the most unwell patients being falsely reassured. The acute-change item carries 4 points on its own, which is why the collateral history matters so much: without it you cannot score the test properly and you cannot distinguish a new delirium from a long-standing dementia. In the Tieges meta-analysis (Age and Ageing, 2021) the 4AT performed consistently across emergency, acute medical, surgical and care-home settings. Hypoactive delirium is underdiagnosed because the patient causes no trouble; a carer who says "she's just not herself, sleeping all day" is describing it. Always record the 4AT score in the notes so that the trajectory can be tracked at review.
3
Diagnose

Identify the underlying cause — PINCH ME

Delirium always has a cause, and in older people it is often several small ones added together. PINCH ME is a teaching mnemonic (clinical practice, not a guideline tool) that makes sure nothing is skipped. NICE CG103 risk factors that lower the threshold: age 65 or over, past or present cognitive impairment or dementia, current hip fracture, and severe illness.
P — Pain
Occult fracture after a fall, urinary retention, faecal impaction, pressure damage, dental pain. People with dementia may show pain only as agitation — use an observational pain scale such as the Abbey scale
I — Infection
Chest, urine, skin/cellulitis, biliary, cannula or wound sites, COVID-19 and influenza. Older people may be afebrile or even hypothermic
N — Nutrition
Poor intake, weight loss, thiamine deficiency (heavy alcohol use, prolonged vomiting, bariatric surgery), low B12
C — Constipation
Ask the date of the last bowel opening; consider rectal examination if impaction is suspected. Opioids and anticholinergics are common culprits
H — Hydration
Dehydration, AKI, hyponatraemia (diuretics, SSRIs, carbamazepine), hypercalcaemia, hypoxia and hypercapnia (COPD)
M — Medication
New drugs, dose changes and stopped drugs: opioids, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, anticholinergics (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, chlorphenamine), antiparkinsonian drugs, steroids, digoxin, lithium, antiepileptics. Withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines or SSRIs counts too. Include over-the-counter sleep aids and antihistamines
E — Environment
Recent move (care home, hospital, respite), missing glasses or hearing aids, sleep disruption, catheter, restraint, bereavement
A structured sweep stops premature closure on the first abnormality found — a positive urine or a low sodium may be one contributor among several. Medicines are among the most modifiable contributors, and total anticholinergic load (added up with an anticholinergic burden scale) matters more than any single drug. Wernicke's encephalopathy is easily missed outside obvious alcohol dependence; malnutrition from any cause can produce it. In older people, bacteria in the urine without urinary symptoms are common and are not by themselves an explanation for confusion (UKHSA diagnostic tool for suspected UTI in adults over 65, updated December 2024), so keep looking for another source if the picture does not fit.
4
Diagnose

Targeted examination

A focused head-to-toe examination finds most reversible causes. Do it even when the patient is hard to examine — a partial examination is far better than none.
Observations
NEWS2 set: RR, SpO₂, BP (lying and standing if safe), pulse (rate and rhythm), temperature, level of consciousness, capillary glucose. A new score of 5 or more, or 3 in any single parameter, triggers an urgent response (Royal College of Physicians NEWS2, 2017)
Neurological
GCS/ACVPU, pupils, focal weakness, speech, gait if safe, neck stiffness, nystagmus/ophthalmoplegia and ataxia (Wernicke's), tremor and asterixis (hepatic, uraemic or CO₂ retention), myoclonus (lithium or opioid toxicity, uraemia), clonus (serotonin syndrome)
Chest and heart
Crackles or bronchial breathing (pneumonia), wheeze, signs of heart failure, new AF
Abdomen
Palpable bladder (retention), distension, faecal loading, tenderness, jaundice, ascites
Skin
Cellulitis, pressure damage, non-blanching rash, bruising (falls, anticoagulation, safeguarding), dry mucosa, shingles
Hidden injury
Hip and wrist movement after a fall, scalp haematoma, rib tenderness
Medication check
Look at the actual boxes and blister packs at home, not only the repeat list; check doses taken, doses missed and anything bought over the counter
Urinary retention and faecal impaction are common, painless-looking causes in older men and people on opioids or anticholinergics, and they are found only by examining the abdomen. Low oxygen saturation in someone with COPD or pneumonia may explain the whole picture. Asterixis and myoclonus point towards a metabolic or toxic cause rather than a structural one, which changes the investigation plan. Unexplained bruising should also prompt thought about falls on anticoagulation and, where the story does not fit, about safeguarding. Checking the medicines physically in the home often reveals double-dosing from old and new boxes or a recently started sedative that is not on the repeat template.
5
Diagnose

Investigations

If the patient is being managed at home, bloods should be taken the same day and results chased the same or next day. If you are admitting, do not delay transfer for tests.
Bedside Now
Capillary glucose, SpO₂, temperature, ECG if available (new AF, ischaemia, QTc before any antipsychotic), bladder scan if available
Bloods Same day
FBC, U&E and creatinine, CRP, LFTs, bone profile (calcium), glucose, TFTs. Add B12 and folate if there is a background of cognitive decline, and magnesium/phosphate if malnourished or on a PPI or diuretic. Drug levels where relevant (digoxin, lithium, antiepileptics)
Urine Selective
Do not use a urine dipstick to diagnose UTI in people over 65; send a urine culture only when there are urinary symptoms or signs, or sepsis with no other source (UKHSA diagnostic tool, updated December 2024)
Chest X-ray
If chest signs, hypoxia or fever without an obvious source
CT head Hospital
Focal neurology, head injury (especially on anticoagulants — NICE NG232, 2023), reduced GCS, suspected raised pressure, or no cause found after a full screen. Not a routine test in delirium with an obvious medical precipitant
Hypercalcaemia NG12
Confusion with raised calcium in someone aged 60 or over with a picture consistent with myeloma → very urgent (within 48 hours) myeloma tests as set out in NICE NG12 (updated April 2026). Also consider bony metastases and hyperparathyroidism
Blood tests find the silent causes: hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, AKI, hepatic dysfunction and hypothyroidism rarely announce themselves on examination. Severity of hyponatraemia correlates loosely with symptoms, and a rapid fall is more dangerous than a chronic low level, so the trend matters as much as the number. Dipstick testing in older people is unreliable because asymptomatic bacteriuria becomes common with age; treating a positive dipstick often means the true cause is missed and an unnecessary antibiotic adds to the problem. CT head has a low yield when there is a clear medical cause and no focal signs, head injury, anticoagulation or reduced consciousness, but it becomes essential when any of those are present. Record a baseline QTc before haloperidol, because both the drug and the electrolyte disturbances of delirium prolong it.
6
Refer

Where should this patient be managed?

Delirium can be managed at home when the cause is clear and treatable, the person is safe, and someone can stay with them. Hospital itself can prolong delirium, so admission should buy something the home cannot provide — diagnostics, treatment or safety.
999 Emergency
Any Step 1 red flag: reduced consciousness, sepsis high-risk criteria, suspected meningitis/encephalitis, stroke, seizure, suspected Wernicke's, toxidrome
Same day Acute
No cause found despite a full primary-care screen; cause needs hospital treatment (IV fluids, oxygen, IV antibiotics); head injury on anticoagulant; alcohol withdrawal; severe agitation putting self or others at risk; nobody available to supervise; carers not coping
Urgent Community
Hospital-at-home or virtual ward, community frailty or rapid-response team, if available locally, for patients who can stay at home with extra support
Routine Specialist
Geriatric or old-age psychiatry review for recurrent or persistent delirium; memory service once delirium has settled if cognition has not returned to baseline (NICE NG97, 2018)
Home management
Clear reversible cause treated (for example constipation, a culprit drug, a simple infection with no high-risk features), safe home, a carer present, and daily review possible
Capacity and safety
Assess capacity for the specific decision (Mental Capacity Act 2005). If the person lacks capacity and objects to admission, act in their best interests, involve family or an attorney, and use the least restrictive option. Raise a safeguarding concern if neglect or abuse is suspected
Delirium is associated with poor outcomes whatever the setting: in a meta-analysis of older patients, delirium was linked to higher mortality (hazard ratio 1.96) and to more institutionalisation and dementia, independent of age and illness severity (Witlox et al., JAMA, 2010). That argues against under-triage. At the same time, unfamiliar surroundings, noise, broken sleep and immobility can prolong delirium, so the decision should weigh what admission adds. Capacity is decision-specific and fluctuates in delirium; if the person cannot understand, retain and weigh information about going to hospital, a best-interests decision with the family is lawful and appropriate, and should be documented clearly.
7
Treat

Management — treat the cause; non-drug care first

Treat each precipitant found. Non-drug care is for every patient. Sedation is a last resort, never a treatment for the delirium itself.
Treat cause
Antibiotic for a confirmed or clearly suspected infection per local formulary | encourage oral fluids and review diuretics for dehydration | catheter for retention | laxatives or enema for impaction | stop or reduce a culprit drug (do not stop benzodiazepines or antiepileptics abruptly) | oxygen and admission for hypoxia | parenteral thiamine in hospital for suspected Wernicke's, at the upper end of the BNF range (NICE CG100)
Non-drug care All
A familiar person present | calm, simple explanations repeated as often as needed | clock and calendar visible | glasses and working hearing aids | good daytime light, quiet dark nights | regular drinks and food, help with eating | get up and walk if safe | avoid moving rooms and changing carers | avoid catheters and restraint unless essential
Pain
Treat pain regularly with paracetamol first; if an opioid is needed, use the lowest dose and prescribe a laxative
Step 1De-escalate — verbal and non-verbal techniques, reduce stimulation, bring in a familiar person, address pain, thirst, a full bladder or bowel
Step 2Drug only if the person is distressed or a risk to themselves or others AND de-escalation has failed or is inappropriate (NICE CG103)
HaloperidolShort-term, usually 1 week or less, starting at the lowest clinically appropriate dose and titrating cautiously (NICE CG103); dose per BNF, ideally agreed with a geriatrician or old-age psychiatrist in primary care. Older people are at increased risk of cardiac and extrapyramidal effects — use the lowest dose for the shortest time and monitor (MHRA Drug Safety Update, December 2021). Check QTc and electrolytes first where possible
Parkinson's / DLBUse antipsychotics with caution or not at all (NICE CG103); people with Lewy body disease can have severe sensitivity reactions (NICE NG97, 2018). Seek specialist advice before any sedative
BenzodiazepinesNot a routine treatment for delirium — they can worsen confusion and cause falls and respiratory depression. Reserved for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal: in delirium tremens, oral lorazepam is first-line (NICE CG100)
AvoidSedating antihistamines, routine night sedation, physical restraint, and continuing an antipsychotic after the delirium has settled
No drug treats delirium; drugs only reduce dangerous distress while the cause is treated. The 2023 update of NICE CG103 narrowed the drug option to short-term haloperidol and added a reminder to take account of the MHRA's advice on its risks in older people. The MHRA's 2021 review found no new safety signal but noted variable practice and neurological adverse effects, so the lowest dose for the shortest time, with monitoring, is the standard. In dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson's disease, antipsychotics can cause severe sensitivity reactions and worsen motor function, so check the dementia subtype before prescribing. Benzodiazepines are the right choice only when the delirium is due to alcohol or sedative withdrawal. Any antipsychotic started during delirium should have a stop date written on the prescription and be reviewed at every contact.
8
Lifestyle

Prevention — reducing future delirium risk

A person who has had delirium once is at high risk of it again. Multicomponent prevention works: in hospital trials it cut incidence from 18.4% to 10.5% (Burton et al., Cochrane review, 2021). The same principles apply at home and in care homes.
Medication review Structured review at least annually and after every delirium episode. Reduce anticholinergic load, sedatives and opioids; check renal dosing
Sensory aids Up-to-date glasses, working hearing aids with spare batteries, wax removal when needed, and aids that travel with the person to hospital
Hydration and nutrition Regular drinks offered, not just made available; extra fluids in hot weather and during illness; weigh and screen for malnutrition
Sleep Regular routine, daytime activity and light, no caffeine late in the day; avoid starting hypnotics
Bowels and bladder Laxative with any regular opioid; ask about bowels at each review; treat retention causes
Mobility Keep moving; falls assessment; physiotherapy after illness
Care plan Record baseline cognition and function; agree an "if confused again" plan with the family and care home, including who to call and what to check first
Information Tell the person and family that delirium is common and usually temporary, what it feels like, and to report sudden changes early (NICE CG103)
The landmark trial of multicomponent prevention (Inouye et al., NEJM, 1999) targeted six risk factors — cognitive impairment, sleep deprivation, immobility, visual impairment, hearing impairment and dehydration — and reduced the number and duration of delirium episodes. The 2021 Cochrane review confirmed that multicomponent programmes probably reduce delirium incidence in hospital patients outside intensive care. These interventions are cheap and are largely things families and care homes can do if they know why they matter. NICE CG103 asks that people at risk and their families receive information about delirium; many relatives find episodes frightening and are reassured to hear that it is a recognised, usually temporary, response to illness.
9
Safety

Follow-up, monitoring and safety-netting

Delirium usually improves within days of treating the cause but can take weeks to clear fully, especially in dementia. Plan the reviews before you leave.
Daily
If managed at home: repeat obs and 4AT. Is attention improving? Is the cause responding? Are drinks and food going in? Is the carer coping?
48–72 hours
Not improving, or getting worse → reconsider the diagnosis and look for a second cause; low threshold for admission
1–2 weeks
Review results, stop any antipsychotic, confirm deprescribing has happened, check function and falls risk
After recovery
Reassess cognition once the delirium has fully resolved (usually weeks later). If cognition has not returned to baseline, investigate for dementia and refer to the memory service (NICE NG97)
999 safety-net
More drowsy or hard to wake, new weakness or speech problem, fit, non-blanching rash, breathing difficulty, or a fall with head injury on a blood thinner
Same-day GP
Confusion not improving after 48–72 hours, new fever, stops eating or drinking, agitation that cannot be settled, carer can no longer cope, or a possible side effect of a new medicine
Safety-net wording
"The confusion should start to lift over the next few days as we treat the cause. If they become sleepier, harder to wake, or develop weakness, a fit or a rash, ring 999. If they are not starting to improve in two or three days, or you are struggling, ring the surgery the same day."
Documentation
Record the episode, cause, 4AT scores, capacity decisions and cognitive outcome; add a delirium alert to the record and share it with the care home and any hospital team
Tracking the 4AT over time turns a subjective impression ("seems a bit better") into a documented trajectory, which makes escalation decisions clearer and helps out-of-hours colleagues. Delirium that persists or recurs is a warning sign: it can indicate an unresolved cause, a second precipitant, or underlying dementia that the episode has revealed. Cognitive testing during delirium is unreliable, so the formal assessment for dementia should wait until attention has recovered. For the SCA, examiners look for explicit safety-netting given to the carer in plain words, a clear plan for who reviews and when, and recognition of carer strain.
