Step 1

Safety — Red Flags, Emergency Exclusion & Safeguarding

Safety
Screen for medical emergencies, serious underlying disease, and — critically — child maltreatment before all other assessment.
⚠️ Safeguarding — Consider in every faltered growth presentation Consider safeguarding particularly if there is inadequate food provision, repeated non-attendance, failure to seek care, inconsistent history, unexplained injury, developmental regression, poor parent–child interaction, or concern about other children in the household. Lack of previous healthcare contact, parental anxiety or affect, social isolation, food insecurity or maternal postnatal depression need contextual assessment and do not alone establish neglect. Discuss concerns with the practice safeguarding lead or named professional and follow the local multi-agency pathway; gather collateral information where appropriate and arrange review if the referral threshold is not met. If maltreatment is suspected → Refer to Children's Social Care (this may lead to early help, a section 17 assessment or a section 47 enquiry). If the child is in immediate danger, contact Children's Social Care and/or the police.

Faltered growth (the preferred term replacing "failure to thrive") is defined in NICE NG75 by how far weight has fallen relative to where it started: a fall across 1 or more centile spaces if birthweight was below the 9th centile, 2 or more if birthweight was between the 9th and 91st, 3 or more if it was above the 91st — or a current weight below the 2nd centile whatever the birthweight. In the first weeks the triggers are different: a loss of more than 10% of birthweight, or failure to regain birthweight by 3 weeks. NICE NG75 and RCPCH growth chart guidance emphasise that faltered growth is multifactorial — most is related to intake rather than underlying organic disease, and most well children with isolated faltered growth do not need extensive investigation. Safeguarding should be considered in every case: faltered growth can be associated with neglect, but also with inadequate food provision, poverty, parental mental-health difficulties or feeding interaction problems; discuss concerns with the safeguarding lead and follow the local multi-agency pathway (NICE NG76, CG89). Medical red flags requiring urgent paediatric assessment include: severe undernutrition, developmental regression, organomegaly, dysmorphic features, recurrent infections, significant developmental delay, and parental concern disproportionate to examination findings. Two childhood cancers sit behind the organomegaly and pallor flags, which is why NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) is part of this step: an unexplained palpable abdominal mass or enlarged abdominal organ calls for a very urgent referral (within 48 hours) for neuroblastoma or Wilms' tumour, while unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly calls for immediate specialist assessment for leukaemia. The softer leukaemia features — pallor, fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, bone pain, bruising or bleeding — call for a very urgent full blood count within 48 hours in a well child, who is then managed on that result; an unwell child with a combination of features needs same-day telephone discussion with acute paediatrics without waiting for the FBC. An abdomen examined only for "distension" can miss the mass that matters.
Step 2

Diagnose — Accurate Growth Measurement & Plotting

Diagnose
Accurate measurement is the foundation. Errors in measurement or plotting are common and cause inappropriate referral or missed diagnosis.
Weight measurement
Use calibrated scales. Infants: naked. Children: minimal clothing, no shoes. Plot on WHO growth charts (UK-WHO 0–4yrs) or UK90 (4–18yrs). Record to nearest 10g (infants) or 100g (children). Time of day affects weight (plot same time for serial measurements). Correct for gestation in preterm infants according to local growth-chart guidance. Plot weight and length/height together.
Length / height
Length (lying, <2yrs): infantometer, two-person technique. Height (standing, ≥2yrs): stadiometer, Frankfurt plane (tragus–lower orbital margin horizontal). Convert between standing and lying (+0.7cm). Plot on RCPCH UK-WHO chart.
Head circumference
Measure and plot in infants and when there is concern about neurological development, microcephaly, macrocephaly or a change in head-growth trajectory — not mandatory in every faltered-growth consultation. Use non-stretchable tape. Largest occipitofrontal circumference. Small head → microcephaly (brain pathology). Large head → hydrocephalus, storage disorders.
Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC)
WHO criteria (6–59 months): MUAC <11.5 cm suggests severe acute malnutrition; 11.5–12.4 cm moderate acute malnutrition — alongside weight-for-height and bilateral pitting oedema. Not part of the NICE NG75 thresholds; may support assessment but should not be used in isolation or as a standalone 999/admission threshold.
Defining faltered growth (NICE NG75)
A fall across 1 or more weight centile spaces if birthweight was below the 9th centile; 2 or more if birthweight was 9th–91st; 3 or more if birthweight was above the 91st; or current weight below the 2nd centile whatever the birthweight. A centile space is the gap between two adjacent centile lines (for example 50th to 25th). Early days: loss of more than 10% of birthweight, or not back to birthweight by 3 weeks. A single low weight is not faltered growth — serial measurements needed
BMI and height clues (NICE NG75)
In children over 2 years with concern about weight or linear growth, calculate the BMI centile (length or height needed). BMI below the 2nd centile may mean undernutrition or simply a small build; BMI below the 0.4th centile suggests probable undernutrition needing assessment and intervention. A length/height centile more than 2 centile spaces below the mid-parental centile raises undernutrition or a primary growth disorder.
Mid-parental height (MPH)
Boys: (father's height + mother's height + 13cm) ÷ 2. Girls: (father's height + mother's height – 13cm) ÷ 2. Use biological parents' measured heights where there is concern about linear growth. A length/height more than two centile spaces below the mid-parental centile may indicate undernutrition or a primary growth disorder (NICE NG75) → discuss with paediatrics.
Growth velocity
Weight gain varies with age, gestation, feeding and illness — use centile trajectory and clinical context rather than a universal weekly weight-gain target. Deceleration across centiles is more significant than position alone.
Review Red Book
Review Personal Child Health Record (PCHR / Red Book) for all previous weights, lengths, immunisations, developmental checks. Essential context for trajectory assessment.
Measurement error is a common cause of apparent faltered growth in primary care — inconsistent clothing, uncalibrated scales and different measurement techniques all introduce error, so check technique and equipment before acting. WHO growth charts (UK-WHO) are used from birth to 4 years because they represent optimal growth for breastfed infants in a supportive environment. UK90 charts are used from 4 years onwards. A single weight below a centile means nothing without context — serial measurements demonstrating sustained downward crossing of centile lines is the diagnostic criterion. Mid-parental height calculation is essential for any child with short stature — familial short stature is the most common cause of small stature and requires no investigation if growth velocity is normal and MPH centile is concordant.
Step 3

Diagnose — Comprehensive History

Diagnose
A detailed multi-system history identifies the cause in the majority of cases. Cover feeding, systemic symptoms, social context, and family history.
Feeding history (infant)
Breastfeeding: frequency, duration, positioning, latch, maternal milk supply concerns, expressing (volume indicates supply). Breastfeeding effectiveness and milk transfer; wet and dirty nappies. Formula: preparation method (over-diluting formula is a common error), volume per feed, frequency. Weaning: timing, textures, variety. Appetite — good or poor?
Feeding history (child)
Dietary recall (24-hour / 3-day food diary): volume, variety, texture, frequency of meals. Mealtime behaviour: refusal, distress, gagging. ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder) — extreme selectivity, sensory aversion. Family meals together? Screen time during meals?
GI symptoms
Vomiting (posset vs projectile vs bilious). Diarrhoea (frequency, blood, consistency — loose / floating / offensive steatorrhoea → malabsorption). Constipation. Abdominal pain / bloating. Coeliac: diarrhoea + weight loss + abdominal distension after gluten introduction (~6 months).
Respiratory symptoms
Recurrent chest infections + faltered growth → cystic fibrosis. Chronic wheeze, cough. Snoring, apnoeas, sleep quality and daytime function → assess via the sleep-disordered-breathing pathway (large tonsils alone do not explain poor growth).
Systemic symptoms
Recurrent fever (immunodeficiency). Sweating (cardiac disease). Polyuria + polydipsia (diabetes, renal tubular acidosis). Pallor, bruising (haematological). Jaundice (liver disease).
Perinatal / birth history
Gestation, birth weight (SGA/IUGR), APGAR scores, NICU admission. Maternal health in pregnancy: infection (CMV, toxoplasma, rubella), substance use, nutrition. SGA may have constitutional small stature.
Social & environmental
Housing: overcrowding, cold, damp. Food security: benefit entitlement (Universal Credit, Healthy Start vouchers, Free School Meals). Parental mental health (PND, anxiety — affects feeding interactions). Domestic violence. Social isolation. Parental substance misuse.
Family history
Parental height and weight. Coeliac disease, IBD, cystic fibrosis, metabolic disorders. Constitutional delay of growth (father's puberty timing). Genetic syndromes.
Also ask (essential)
Gestation and corrected age · blood or mucus in stool · urinary symptoms · fever, lethargy, pallor, bruising · developmental progress and any regression · food avoidance, sensory aversion, gagging or choking · food security and ability to obtain appropriate food · parental mental health and practical support · engagement with services and safeguarding concerns.
Most faltered growth in the UK is related to intake — the history must focus primarily on feeding before investigating for organic disease. Coeliac disease is the most commonly missed organic cause — it affects ~1% of the population and presents with faltered growth, steatorrhoea, and abdominal distension after gluten introduction. Cystic fibrosis should be considered in any child with recurrent chest infections and faltered growth — the newborn bloodspot screen (heel prick) screens for CF but false negatives occur. ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder) is increasingly recognised as a cause of inadequate intake in children — it requires specialist CAMHS and dietetic input and does not respond to standard feeding advice. Social history is not optional — food insecurity is common and can drive faltered growth; it needs practical support and signposting alongside clinical care, and is not in itself evidence of neglect. Always ask about Healthy Start voucher entitlement.
Step 4

Diagnose — Targeted Physical Examination

Diagnose
A full top-to-toe examination is required. Most children with faltered growth are systemically well — a sick-looking child warrants urgent assessment.
General appearance
Well / alert / interactive vs unwell / lethargic. Dysmorphic features (Down syndrome, Turner's, Russell-Silver, Williams, Prader-Willi). Wasting (loss of fat / muscle), oedema (protein deficiency). Hygiene, clothing, parent-child interaction.
Skin & hair
Pallor (anaemia, malnutrition). Bruising / marks (safeguarding). Eczema (may contribute to poor intake if severe). Sparse, brittle hair (nutritional deficiency). Jaundice (liver disease). Mouth ulcers (IBD, nutritional deficiency).
Cardiovascular
Heart murmur (congenital heart disease — increased caloric expenditure). Cyanosis. Abnormal femoral pulses (coarctation). Tachycardia / diaphoresis with feeding.
Respiratory
Wheeze, crackles, chronic cough, clubbing → CF. Tachypnoea (cardiac, respiratory cause of FTT).
Abdomen
Distension (coeliac, malabsorption, organomegaly). Hepatomegaly (metabolic storage disease, liver disease, heart failure). Splenomegaly (haematological, infection, storage disease). Palpable kidneys (polycystic, Wilms tumour — also causes FTT).
Neurology
Tone (hypotonia → neuromuscular disease, Down syndrome, Prader-Willi — poor feeding), irritability (raised ICP, pain, neglect). Head circumference (micro/macrocephaly). Developmental assessment (see Step 5 algorithm for delay); regression → urgent paediatric assessment, not attributed solely to neglect.
Mouth & throat
Tonsils — assess with snoring, apnoeas, sleep quality and daytime function (sleep-disordered-breathing pathway). Dental caries (diet quality). Cleft palate (check for submucosal cleft). Tongue tie — relevant only if there is demonstrable functional feeding difficulty; do not assume it explains faltering growth.
Genitalia / pubertal staging
Precocious puberty causes short stature. Undescended testes are non-specific — interpret with hypotonia, developmental features, hyperphagia/obesity and dysmorphism. Turner's: webbed neck, wide carrying angle, absent secondary sexual characteristics.
The examination in faltered growth serves two purposes: to identify organic disease and to assess for signs of neglect or abuse. A child who is alert, interactive, and well-engaged with parents despite a low weight is very different from one who is withdrawn, apathetic, and showing poor parent-child interaction — the latter requires medical assessment with safeguarding considered alongside. Dysmorphic features, cardiac murmurs, and organomegaly all require secondary care assessment. Tongue tie (ankyloglossia) is relevant only where there is demonstrable functional feeding difficulty (latch, milk transfer) — assessment by a trained infant-feeding practitioner decides whether division helps. Large tonsils should be interpreted with snoring, apnoeas, sleep quality and daytime function and managed via the sleep-disordered-breathing pathway, not assumed to explain poor growth.
Step 5

Diagnose — Investigations

Diagnose
Most faltered growth does NOT require extensive investigation. Investigations should be targeted based on the history and examination.
Initial tests (NICE NG75)
Think about urinary tract infection (assess as in NICE NG224) and coeliac disease (only valid if the diet has contained gluten — NICE NG20). Consider observed feeding or mealtimes. Routine broad blood testing is not recommended for a well child with isolated faltered growth and a normal examination; go further only when the history or examination points somewhere.
Targeted bloods (only if organic cause suspected)
Select tests according to the clinical question and local paediatric advice. Possible tests include FBC, ferritin, U&E, bicarbonate, LFTs, inflammatory markers, TFTs, urine testing and coeliac serology. B12, folate, thyroid function, inflammatory markers or a metabolic screen are not routine in every well child.
Coeliac serology
Test only after gluten has been introduced: total IgA + IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-TTG) first line; if IgA deficient, use an IgG-based test. Child should eat gluten in more than one meal daily for at least 6 weeks before testing where possible. Do not start a gluten-free diet before specialist confirmation, even if serology is positive. Positive → refer to paediatric gastroenterology/paediatrics with gastroenterology expertise; specialist work-up may include EMA, HLA testing, endoscopy or a validated no-biopsy approach in selected cases (NICE NG20).
Urine
Urine testing if UTI is plausible (NICE NG224): MC&S. Dipstick: glucose (diabetes), protein (renal disease) when clinically indicated. Other urine tests (e.g. reducing substances) only on paediatric advice.
CF investigations
If CF is suspected: check the newborn bloodspot screen result (Red Book); assess respiratory and gastrointestinal features; discuss with/refer to the local CF team. Sweat testing is arranged through the specialist pathway
If dysmorphic features
Discuss with / refer to paediatrics or clinical genetics. Genetic testing (e.g. chromosomal microarray) is a paediatric/genetics decision, not a GP first-line test.
Bone age X-ray
Not a GP investigation for isolated faltered weight gain. Bone age is part of specialist assessment of short stature or abnormal linear growth — refer using the BSPED growth referral standards.
Do NOT routinely
Broad blood panels in a well child with isolated faltered growth. Extensive metabolic screen. Bone age X-ray for isolated faltered weight gain. Growth hormone testing (refer to paediatric endocrinology). MRI brain. Chromosomal microarray without a genetics/paediatric indication. Full immunology screen without significant infections. Vitamin D blood test solely because of faltered growth (test if deficiency or rickets clinically suspected).
NICE NG75 and paediatric consensus emphasise that investigation-driven approaches in faltered growth are ineffective — a focused history and examination identify the cause far more reliably than a "scatter-gun" blood panel. NICE NG75 recommends considering coeliac serology in faltered growth once gluten has been introduced — coeliac disease is significantly underdiagnosed. The child must be on a gluten-containing diet for at least 6 weeks before testing — parents sometimes withdraw gluten before the diagnosis is confirmed, which invalidates the test. Total IgA must be checked alongside anti-TTG to exclude IgA deficiency (which gives a false-negative IgA anti-TTG — use an IgG-based test if IgA deficient). Do not start a gluten-free diet before specialist confirmation. Sweat chloride testing for CF requires referral to specialist paediatric respiratory services — the test cannot be performed reliably in primary care. NICE NG75 frames the starting point narrowly: consider urine testing for UTI and coeliac serology, then investigate further only if the clinical picture points to a specific disorder. NICE NG20 asks that the child eats gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before serology — this is why parents are asked not to cut out bread and pasta while waiting.
Step 6

Refer — Referral Criteria, MDT & Safeguarding

Refer
Faltered growth is managed by a multidisciplinary team. Know when to refer urgently, involve paediatrics, or refer to social care.
999 / Same-day admission
Shock, severe dehydration or altered consciousness. Severe respiratory distress. Suspected sepsis. Hypoglycaemia or inability to maintain intake. Bilious vomiting with concern for obstruction. Severe acute malnutrition with clinical compromise. Immediate danger from safeguarding concerns.
Same-day paediatrics
Clinically unwell child with rapid weight loss or severe undernutrition. Significant dehydration or persistent vomiting. Suspected organic disease with a murmur, organomegaly, respiratory distress or neurological signs. Suspected immunodeficiency or serious infection. Developmental regression. Unwell child with a combination of possible cancer symptoms. Safeguarding concern requiring immediate protection or medical assessment. Young infant with significant weight concern who is unwell, dehydrated, losing weight rapidly or not responding to feeding support.
Suspected cancer · NICE NG12
Unexplained palpable abdominal mass or enlarged abdominal organ → very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours) for suspected neuroblastoma or Wilms' tumour. Unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment for leukaemia. Pallor, fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, bone pain, bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC, generally within 48 hours, in a well child with an isolated concerning feature. Unexplained visible haematuria with an abdominal mass or enlarged organ → very urgent assessment. Unwell child with a combination of cancer symptoms/signs → do not wait for the FBC; same-day telephone discussion with acute paediatrics. Follow local paediatric arrangements (not an adult-style 2-week-wait process); discuss with acute paediatrics where urgency is unclear. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026).
NICE NG75 referral triggers
Discuss with or refer to paediatric specialist services if there are symptoms or signs of an underlying disorder, no response to primary care interventions, slow linear growth or unexplained short stature, rapid weight loss or severe undernutrition, or features that raise safeguarding concern.
Safeguarding
Suspected neglect or non-accidental injury. Unexplained bruising. Repeated non-attendance or failure to seek care. Concern about other children. Parental substance misuse affecting the child's care. Discuss with the practice safeguarding lead or named professional; if maltreatment is suspected, refer to Children's Social Care and document. Follow the local safeguarding pathway — this may lead to early help, a section 17 assessment or a section 47 enquiry.
Routine paediatrics
Routine / discuss: symptoms or signs suggesting an underlying disorder. Failure to respond to appropriate primary-care intervention. Slow linear growth or unexplained short stature (BSPED referral standards). Persistent faltered growth with feeding difficulty. Suspected coeliac disease, CF, IBD or chronic disease. Dysmorphism or developmental concern. Safeguarding concerns that need specialist discussion.
Paediatric dietitian
Where dietary fortification, oral nutritional supplements or other nutritional support are needed. Dietary assessment; the dietitian calculates individual requirements. Essential MDT member.
Community / primary care MDT
Health visitor (primary support in infants — feeding observation, home visit). CAMHS (if ARFID, feeding aversion, maternal mental health). Speech and language therapy (swallowing difficulties, oral motor issues). Children's centre / family support worker (social support, food poverty).
Primary care manage
Mild faltered growth + adequate caloric intake + normal examination + no organic symptoms → dietary advice, health visitor support, agreed goals, re-weigh at an age- and risk-appropriate interval. Ensure Healthy Start vouchers, food bank referral if food insecurity.
Faltered growth is a quintessential MDT problem — no single professional can manage it effectively alone. The health visitor is the most important primary care professional in infant faltered growth — they have the frequency of contact, the home visiting capability, and the feeding expertise that GPs lack. The GP role is to exclude organic disease, identify safeguarding concerns, and coordinate the MDT. NICE NG75 asks for discussion with or referral to paediatric specialist services when there are symptoms or signs of an underlying disorder, when primary care interventions have not worked, when linear growth is slow or short stature is unexplained, when weight loss is rapid or undernutrition severe, or when there are safeguarding concerns. The primary care team should be able to call on an infant feeding specialist, a paediatric dietitian, a speech and language therapist with feeding expertise and a consultant paediatrician. The NICE NG12 row is separate from all of this: a mass or organomegaly is a cancer question first and a nutrition question second. Where maltreatment is suspected, refer to Children's Social Care via the local safeguarding pathway — the local authority, not the GP, decides whether early help, a section 17 assessment or a section 47 enquiry follows (Children Act 1989). Statutory guidance (Working Together to Safeguard Children 2026), NICE NG76/CG89 and GMC safeguarding guidance expect GPs to share concerns, discuss with the safeguarding lead and refer where indicated. Delay in safeguarding referral is a recurring finding in serious case reviews.
Step 7

Treat — Nutritional Intervention & Medical Management

Treat
The primary treatment for most faltered growth is increasing caloric intake. Treat the underlying cause where identified.
Breastfed infant — supply concern
Feeding support Immediate
Refer to IBCLC lactation consultant. Check latch, positioning, tongue tie. Assess milk transfer. Feed responsively according to the infant's cues and clinical needs — frequency varies; do not use a fixed number of feeds as a universal target. Supplementary expressed breast milk or formula if acute concern. Do not recommend formula without breastfeeding support first. If formula is added (NICE NG75, QS197): support continued breastfeeding, advise expressing to support supply, and give expressed breast milk before the formula. Explain that supplementation may reduce breast stimulation and milk supply, and give practical support to preserve breastfeeding if that is the family's goal.
Formula-fed infant — inadequate volume
Assess intake + preparation Dietitian-led
Check preparation carefully — make standard formula according to the manufacturer's instructions; carers must never concentrate or dilute formula without specialist advice. Assess actual intake and feeding effectiveness; avoid rigid volume targets and seek health-visitor, infant-feeding or paediatric-dietetic advice where intake is inadequate or uncertain. Do not routinely start energy-dense infant formula (e.g. Infatrini, SMA High Energy) in primary care — if required, prescribe and monitor through a paediatric dietetic/paediatric pathway, reviewing renal solute load, hydration, tolerance and growth.
Older child — poor intake
Caloric fortification Dietitian-led
Add fat/calorie-dense foods: butter, cream, cheese, peanut butter to existing meals. Regular meals and snacks. Oral nutritional supplements (e.g. PaediaSure, Fortini) only after feeding advice and food-first/fortification measures have been tried, preferably with paediatric dietetic or paediatric-team involvement and according to the Greater Manchester formulary and commissioning arrangements.
Organic cause identified
Treat underlying condition Specialist-led
Coeliac: strict gluten-free diet (dietitian). CF: CREON (pancreatic enzyme replacement), high-calorie diet, physiotherapy. Hypothyroidism: levothyroxine. IBD: specialist escalation. Cardiac: cardiological management.
Step 1Dietary assessment + food-first fortification: Where nutritional support is needed, a dietitian calculates individual requirements. Calorie-dense food additions to existing diet. Structured meal routine.
Step 2Oral nutritional supplements (ONS): Consider only after feeding advice and food-first measures, preferably with paediatric dietetic/paediatric involvement; prescribe per the Greater Manchester formulary and commissioning arrangements. Give between meals (they can suppress appetite for ordinary food). Review weight, linear growth, appetite, tolerance, adherence and effect on normal food intake; stop when no longer indicated.
Step 3Enteral (tube) feeding: Consider only when there are serious concerns about weight gain, specialist multidisciplinary assessment has been completed and other interventions have not worked. The route, location of initiation and monitoring plan are specialist decisions.
Step 4Treat organic cause: Coeliac → gluten-free diet + dietitian. CF → enzyme replacement (Creon) + high-energy diet + chest physiotherapy. Hypothyroidism → levothyroxine. Coordinate with specialist team.
Caloric enrichment (adding fat and protein to existing foods) is more effective and better tolerated than volume increases for most children with faltered growth — it avoids overwhelming small appetites. Where nutritional support is needed, a dietitian calculates the child's individual requirements — universal calorie targets can be unsafe with unrecognised metabolic, cardiac, renal or gastrointestinal disease. Oral nutritional supplements should be prescribed according to local formulary arrangements, preferably with dietetic involvement, given between meals and reassessed — they should supplement, not replace, normal foods. Pancreatic enzyme replacement (Creon) for CF must be given with every meal and snack — undertreatment causes malabsorption and persistent faltered growth despite adequate intake. For coeliac disease, strict gluten-free diet (not just low-gluten) is essential — even small amounts of gluten cause ongoing villous atrophy and malabsorption. NICE NG75 puts an oral liquid nutritional supplement after other measures: consider a trial when faltering continues despite feeding support and dietary change. Tube feeding is a specialist decision made after multidisciplinary assessment, never a primary care step. Product names and energy densities (Infatrini, SMA High Energy, PaediaSure, Fortini) and doses of Creon, levothyroxine and vitamin D should be checked in the BNF for Children before prescribing.
Step 8

Lifestyle — Family Support, Feeding Environment & Social Interventions

Lifestyle
Social, environmental, and behavioural interventions are as important as nutritional advice and must not be treated as afterthoughts.
Structured mealtimes3 meals + 2–3 snacks/day at regular times. No grazing. Sit at table together as a family. Meals <30 minutes — remove food without comment if not eaten. Reduces mealtime anxiety.
Reduce pressure at mealtimes Responsive feeding approach: let child lead hunger cues. Avoid force-feeding, bribing, or coaxing — increases food refusal. Division of responsibility: parent decides what/when/where; child decides how much.
Screen-free mealtimes Remove screens (TV, tablets, phones) during meals. Distractions impair hunger awareness and meal duration. Positive association with mealtimes reduces long-term feeding difficulties.
Food security support Assess eligibility: NHS Healthy Start (more than 10 weeks pregnant, or a child under 4, on a qualifying income-related benefit — from April 2026 £9.30/week for a baby under 1 and £4.65/week for a child aged 1–4, plus free vitamins). Free School Meals. Food banks. Universal Credit. Citizens Advice Bureau referral. Do not assume — always ask.
Maternal mental health PND affects feeding interaction and responsiveness. PHQ-9 / Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Refer for psychological support — NHS Talking Therapies/CAMHS. Improving maternal mental health improves infant growth.
Health visitor support Regular home visits for feeding observation and practical support. Can attend mealtimes. Key community support professional. Ensure referral made and follow-up agreed.
Cooking skills / food preparation support Refer to children's centre cooking classes. Sure Start / Family Support Worker for practical food preparation help. Avoid judgemental advice — focus on practical support.
Vitamin supplementation (NICE NG247) 0–6 months: breastfed — vitamin D 8.5–10 micrograms daily; formula-fed ≥500 mL/day — no additional vitamin D. 6–12 months: breastfed — vitamins A, C and D (vitamin D 8.5–10 micrograms daily); formula-fed ≥500 mL/day — no additional supplement. 1–4 years: vitamins A, C and D (vitamin D 10 micrograms daily); formula is not required from 1 year. Healthy Start vitamins are free if eligible, up to the child's fourth birthday. Routine vitamin D blood testing is not needed solely because of faltered growth — investigate if deficiency or rickets is clinically suspected.
The "division of responsibility" in feeding (Ellyn Satter model) is evidence-based — parents control what food is offered, when, and where; the child controls whether and how much they eat. Pressure-based feeding strategies (coaxing, bribing, force) reliably worsen food refusal and increase mealtime anxiety, causing a negative cycle. Reducing pressure, even when a child is failing to thrive, produces better long-term outcomes than pressure-based approaches. Maternal postnatal depression affects feeding responsiveness — mothers with depression are less likely to recognise infant hunger cues, feed responsively, or persist with breastfeeding. Treating maternal mental health is a direct intervention for infant growth. Food poverty is a structural issue affecting many UK children — practical social support (food banks, Healthy Start vouchers, benefit entitlement checks) is a clinical intervention, not a social nicety. NICE NG75 explicitly recommends addressing social determinants of growth faltering as part of clinical management.
Step 9

Safety — Monitoring, Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Safety
Growth monitoring must be structured with clear escalation criteria. Avoid excessive weighing, which increases parental anxiety without improving outcomes.
Weighing frequency (NICE NG75)
When faltering is a concern, weigh at intervals set by age and level of concern, but usually no more often than: daily under 1 month; weekly at 1–6 months; fortnightly at 6–12 months; monthly from 1 year. Measure length/height no more often than every 3 months. Over-frequent weighing increases parental anxiety and does not improve outcomes
Review plan
Agree the review interval according to age, severity, clinical condition, intervention and safeguarding risk. Document specific growth and feeding goals, who is responsible for review, and the escalation plan. Check health visitor and dietitian involvement, investigation results and family engagement.
Escalate
If the trajectory does not improve with appropriate primary-care intervention or new signs develop → discuss with or refer to paediatrics. Consider safeguarding if the family is not engaging. Reassess for organic cause if not yet excluded.
Catch-up growth criteria
Catch-up growth is individual — there is no universal timeframe. Review weight trajectory, weight-for-length/BMI, linear growth, feeding function, clinical condition, family circumstances and achievement of the agreed goals. Do not stop intervention when weight improves — maintain until growth trajectory stable
Safety-net 999
Acutely unwell, drowsy, or not responding. Signs of dehydration or shock. Severe acute malnutrition with clinical compromise. Bilious vomiting. Immediate danger to the child.
Safety-net same-day
Child stops feeding / refuses all food suddenly. Acute weight loss. New illness symptoms (fever, vomiting, diarrhoea). Parental concern unable to cope / risk of harm.
Consider safeguarding
Family repeatedly failing to attend follow-up appointments. No improvement despite apparent adequate support. New safeguarding concerns emerging. If uncertain — discuss with the Named GP for Safeguarding or the local children's social care front door (MASH) — the LADO handles allegations against people who work with children, not general concerns.
Document specifically
Record safeguarding concerns at the time — date, time, what was observed, who was present, what was said. Safety-netting advice must be specific (what to watch for, when to come back, who to call) and written in the notes, not a generic "return if worse".
What to say (SCA)
"One weight on its own doesn't tell us much — what matters is the pattern, so I'm going to plot what's in the Red Book and take a careful measurement today. In most children this is about calories getting in rather than anything wrong inside, so the work is practical: the health visitor watching a feed, and a dietitian helping make the food he already eats more calorie-dense — butter, cheese, cream — rather than trying to push more volume. I'll check a coeliac blood test, so please keep bread and pasta in his diet until it's done. And tell me if money for food is tight; that's something I can help with, not something to be embarrassed about."
SCA case
A 9-month-old was on the 50th centile at birth and is now just below the 9th (two centile spaces lost). He is alert and interactive. His mother is exhausted, has been watering down the formula to make the tin last, and has missed the last two health visitor appointments.
Examiner checkpoints
Confirms the fall on serial Red Book plots and considers measurement error; takes a feeding-first history and identifies over-diluted formula; asks about food insecurity directly and without judgement and checks Healthy Start; examines fully including the abdomen for masses or organomegaly and the mouth for tongue tie; sends coeliac serology with total IgA and advises keeping gluten in; re-engages the health visitor and refers to the dietitian; and — the step candidates miss — names the two missed appointments as a safeguarding consideration rather than an administrative detail, documenting the reasoning either way.
The frequency of monitoring should be tailored to severity and risk — NICE NG75 explicitly warns against over-frequent weighing, which creates parental anxiety and a medicalization of normal infant weight variation without improving outcomes. Catch-up growth is individual — NICE NG75 recommends a management plan with specific goals rather than a universal catch-up timeframe. Non-engagement with follow-up in the context of faltered growth should prompt safeguarding consideration and discussion with the safeguarding lead rather than simply being documented as "did not attend." Documenting safeguarding concerns contemporaneously (date, time, observations, who was present, what was said) is legally important and protects both the child and the clinician. The RCPCH Growth Chart guidelines recommend that growth monitoring should be part of a structured plan with clear escalation criteria documented at each review — "safety-netting" must be specific and recorded. In the SCA the mark usually turns on two things: whether the candidate explains why one number is not a diagnosis, and whether they treat missed appointments and food insecurity as clinical findings. Framing money worries as something the practice can help with, rather than a failing, keeps the family engaged — and engagement is what makes the review plan work.
