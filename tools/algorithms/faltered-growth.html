Step 1

Safety — Red Flags, Emergency Exclusion & Safeguarding

Safety
Screen for medical emergencies, serious underlying disease, and — critically — child maltreatment before all other assessment.
⚠️ Safeguarding FIRST — Consider in every faltered growth presentation Faltered growth is a recognised presentation of child neglect and non-accidental injury. Consider ALWAYS: Does this family engage with services? Are other children affected? Unexplained bruising or injuries? Inconsistent history? Inappropriate parental affect? If any concern → Refer to Children's Social Care (Section 17/47) and document RCPCH guidance followed.

Marked oedema + very low weight in infant Severe protein-energy malnutrition (rare in UK but occurs in neglect / restrictive diets) → Same-day paediatric admission

Faltered growth (the preferred term replacing "failure to thrive") is defined in NICE NG75 by how far weight has fallen relative to where it started: a fall across 1 or more centile spaces if birthweight was below the 9th centile, 2 or more if birthweight was between the 9th and 91st, 3 or more if it was above the 91st — or a current weight below the 2nd centile whatever the birthweight. In the first weeks the triggers are different: a loss of more than 10% of birthweight, or failure to regain birthweight by 3 weeks. NICE NG75 and RCPCH growth chart guidance emphasise that faltered growth is multifactorial — the majority (>80%) is due to inadequate caloric intake rather than underlying organic disease. However, the safeguarding dimension must be assessed in every case: faltered growth is one of the recognised presentations of child neglect, and the Working Together to Safeguard Children 2026 guidance requires GPs to apply threshold criteria for referral. Medical red flags requiring urgent paediatric assessment include: crossing 2+ centile lines, organomegaly, dysmorphic features, recurrent infections, significant developmental delay, and parental concern disproportionate to examination findings. Two childhood cancers sit behind the organomegaly and pallor flags, which is why NICE NG12 (updated April 2026) is part of this step: an unexplained palpable abdominal mass or enlarged abdominal organ calls for a very urgent referral (within 48 hours) for neuroblastoma or Wilms' tumour, while unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly calls for immediate specialist assessment for leukaemia. The softer leukaemia features — pallor, fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, bone pain, bruising or bleeding — call for a very urgent full blood count within 48 hours, and the child is then managed on that result. An abdomen examined only for "distension" can miss the mass that matters.
Step 2

Diagnose — Accurate Growth Measurement & Plotting

Diagnose
Accurate measurement is the foundation. Errors in measurement or plotting are common and cause inappropriate referral or missed diagnosis.
Weight measurement
Use calibrated scales. Infants: naked. Children: minimal clothing, no shoes. Plot on WHO growth charts (UK-WHO 0–4yrs) or UK90 (4–18yrs). Record to nearest 10g (infants) or 100g (children). Time of day affects weight (plot same time for serial measurements).
Length / height
Length (lying, <2yrs): infantometer, two-person technique. Height (standing, ≥2yrs): stadiometer, Frankfurt plane (tragus–lower orbital margin horizontal). Convert between standing and lying (+0.7cm). Plot on RCPCH UK-WHO chart.
Head circumference
Measure to age 2 (or beyond if neurological concern). Use non-stretchable tape. Largest occipitofrontal circumference. Small head → microcephaly (brain pathology). Large head → hydrocephalus, storage disorders.
Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC)
MUAC <11.5cm in 6–59 months → severe acute malnutrition. MUAC <12.5cm → moderate acute malnutrition. Simple, reliable in community. Does not require weight scales.
Defining faltered growth (NICE NG75)
A fall across 1 or more weight centile spaces if birthweight was below the 9th centile; 2 or more if birthweight was 9th–91st; 3 or more if birthweight was above the 91st; or current weight below the 2nd centile whatever the birthweight. A centile space is the gap between two adjacent centile lines (for example 50th to 25th). Early days: loss of more than 10% of birthweight, or not back to birthweight by 3 weeks. A single low weight is not faltered growth — serial measurements needed
BMI and height clues (NICE NG75)
If there is doubt about undernutrition, calculate BMI (length or height needed). BMI below the 2nd centile may mean undernutrition or simply a small build; BMI below the 0.4th centile suggests probable undernutrition needing assessment and intervention. A length/height centile more than 2 centile spaces below the mid-parental centile raises undernutrition or a primary growth disorder.
Mid-parental height (MPH)
Boys: (father's height + mother's height + 13cm) ÷ 2. Girls: (father's height + mother's height – 13cm) ÷ 2. ± 10cm target range. Child's height centile should approximate MPH centile. Significant discordance → investigate.
Growth velocity
Rate of growth over time. Weight gain norms: 0–3 months: ~200g/week; 3–6 months: ~150g/week; 6–12 months: ~70–90g/week; 1–2 years: ~50g/week. Deceleration across centiles is more significant than position alone.
Review Red Book
Review Personal Child Health Record (PCHR / Red Book) for all previous weights, lengths, immunisations, developmental checks. Essential context for trajectory assessment.
Measurement error is the most common cause of apparent faltered growth in primary care — up to 30% of weight measurements in primary care have significant errors due to inconsistent clothing, uncalibrated scales, or different measurement techniques. WHO growth charts (UK-WHO) are used from birth to 4 years because they represent optimal growth for breastfed infants in a supportive environment. UK90 charts are used from 4 years onwards. A single weight below a centile means nothing without context — serial measurements demonstrating sustained downward crossing of centile lines is the diagnostic criterion. Mid-parental height calculation is essential for any child with short stature — familial short stature is the most common cause of small stature and requires no investigation if growth velocity is normal and MPH centile is concordant.
Step 3

Diagnose — Comprehensive History

Diagnose
A detailed multi-system history identifies the cause in the majority of cases. Cover feeding, systemic symptoms, social context, and family history.
Feeding history (infant)
Breastfeeding: frequency, duration, positioning, latch, maternal milk supply concerns, expressing (volume indicates supply). Formula: preparation method (over-diluting formula is a common error), volume per feed, frequency. Weaning: timing, textures, variety. Appetite — good or poor?
Feeding history (child)
Dietary recall (24-hour / 3-day food diary): volume, variety, texture, frequency of meals. Mealtime behaviour: refusal, distress, gagging. ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder) — extreme selectivity, sensory aversion. Family meals together? Screen time during meals?
GI symptoms
Vomiting (posset vs projectile vs bilious). Diarrhoea (frequency, blood, consistency — loose / floating / offensive steatorrhoea → malabsorption). Constipation. Abdominal pain / bloating. Coeliac: diarrhoea + weight loss + abdominal distension after gluten introduction (~6 months).
Respiratory symptoms
Recurrent chest infections + faltered growth → cystic fibrosis. Chronic wheeze, cough. Obstructive sleep apnoea (large tonsils, snoring, restless sleep → increased caloric expenditure + reduced daytime intake).
Systemic symptoms
Recurrent fever (immunodeficiency). Sweating (cardiac disease). Polyuria + polydipsia (diabetes, renal tubular acidosis). Pallor, bruising (haematological). Jaundice (liver disease).
Perinatal / birth history
Gestation, birth weight (SGA/IUGR), APGAR scores, NICU admission. Maternal health in pregnancy: infection (CMV, toxoplasma, rubella), substance use, nutrition. SGA may have constitutional small stature.
Social & environmental
Housing: overcrowding, cold, damp. Food security: benefit entitlement (Universal Credit, Healthy Start vouchers, Free School Meals). Parental mental health (PND, anxiety — affects feeding interactions). Domestic violence. Social isolation. Parental substance misuse.
Family history
Parental height and weight. Coeliac disease, IBD, cystic fibrosis, metabolic disorders. Constitutional delay of growth (father's puberty timing). Genetic syndromes.
Over 80% of faltered growth cases in the UK are due to inadequate caloric intake — the history must focus primarily on feeding before investigating for organic disease. Coeliac disease is the most commonly missed organic cause — it affects ~1% of the population and presents with faltered growth, steatorrhoea, and abdominal distension after gluten introduction. Cystic fibrosis should be considered in any child with recurrent chest infections and faltered growth — the newborn bloodspot screen (heel prick) screens for CF but false negatives occur. ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder) is increasingly recognised as a cause of inadequate intake in children — it requires specialist CAMHS and dietetic input and does not respond to standard feeding advice. Social history is not optional — food poverty affects 15–20% of UK children and is a primary driver of faltered growth that requires social care intervention, not medical treatment. Always ask about Healthy Start voucher entitlement.
Step 4

Diagnose — Targeted Physical Examination

Diagnose
A full top-to-toe examination is required. Most children with faltered growth are systemically well — a sick-looking child warrants urgent assessment.
General appearance
Well / alert / interactive vs unwell / lethargic. Dysmorphic features (Down syndrome, Turner's, Russell-Silver, Williams, Prader-Willi). Wasting (loss of fat / muscle), oedema (protein deficiency). Hygiene, clothing, parent-child interaction.
Skin & hair
Pallor (anaemia, malnutrition). Bruising / marks (safeguarding). Eczema (may contribute to poor intake if severe). Sparse, brittle hair (nutritional deficiency). Jaundice (liver disease). Mouth ulcers (IBD, nutritional deficiency).
Cardiovascular
Heart murmur (congenital heart disease — increased caloric expenditure). Cyanosis. Abnormal femoral pulses (coarctation). Tachycardia / diaphoresis with feeding.
Respiratory
Wheeze, crackles, chronic cough, clubbing → CF. Harrison's sulcus (chronic air trapping). Tachypnoea (cardiac, respiratory cause of FTT).
Abdomen
Distension (coeliac, malabsorption, organomegaly). Hepatomegaly (metabolic storage disease, liver disease, heart failure). Splenomegaly (haematological, infection, storage disease). Palpable kidneys (polycystic, Wilms tumour — also causes FTT).
Neurology
Tone (hypotonia → neuromuscular disease, Down syndrome, Prader-Willi — poor feeding), irritability (raised ICP, pain, neglect). Head circumference (micro/macrocephaly). Developmental assessment (see Step 5 algorithm for delay).
Mouth & throat
Tonsil size (Grade 3–4 tonsils → OSA → increased caloric demand + poor sleep/appetite). Dental caries (diet quality). Cleft palate (check for submucosal cleft). Tongue tie (affects latch in breastfed infants).
Genitalia / pubertal staging
Precocious puberty causes short stature. Undescended testes (Prader-Willi). Turner's: webbed neck, wide carrying angle, absent secondary sexual characteristics.
The examination in faltered growth serves two purposes: to identify organic disease and to assess for signs of neglect or abuse. A child who is alert, interactive, and well-engaged with parents despite a low weight is very different from one who is withdrawn, apathetic, and showing poor parent-child interaction — the latter requires immediate safeguarding consideration. Dysmorphic features, cardiac murmurs, and organomegaly all require urgent secondary care assessment. Tongue tie (ankyloglossia) is a commonly missed cause of faltered growth in breastfed infants — it impairs latch and milk transfer, causing maternal nipple pain, poor infant satiety, and growth faltering. Assessment and division (frenotomy) by a trained practitioner resolves the problem. Tonsil size should always be assessed — Grade 3–4 tonsils can cause obstructive sleep apnoea, which dramatically increases basal metabolic rate and reduces appetite.
Step 5

Diagnose — Investigations

Diagnose
Most faltered growth does NOT require extensive investigation. Investigations should be targeted based on the history and examination.
Initial tests (NICE NG75)
Think about urinary tract infection (assess as in NICE NG224) and coeliac disease (only valid if the diet has contained gluten — NICE NG20). In a well child with no other clinical concern, further tests are unlikely to find a cause; go further only when the assessment points somewhere.
First-line bloods (if organic cause suspected)
FBC (anaemia, infection), U&E + creatinine (renal disease, renal tubular acidosis), LFTs + albumin (liver disease, nutritional status), TFTs (hypothyroidism), coeliac serology (IgA anti-TTG + total IgA — IgA deficiency can give false negative), ferritin + B12 + folate, glucose.
Coeliac serology
IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-TTG) + total IgA. Sensitivity 93%, specificity 99% on gluten-containing diet. Child must be eating gluten for 6 weeks before testing. If positive → refer paediatric gastroenterology for endoscopy + biopsy confirmation.
Urine
MC&S: UTI (recurrent UTI → renal scarring → growth faltering). Urine reducing substances (inborn errors of carbohydrate metabolism). Urine protein (nephrotic syndrome). Dipstick: glucose (diabetes).
CF investigations
Newborn bloodspot screen result (check Red Book). If not screened or result unavailable: sweat chloride test (gold standard, >60 mmol/L diagnostic). CFTR gene panel if clinical suspicion. Refer to paediatric respiratory for sweat test
If dysmorphic features
Chromosomal microarray (preferred over karyotype for dysmorphology). FISH for specific suspected syndrome. Refer to clinical genetics.
Bone age X-ray
Left wrist X-ray: Greulich-Pyle atlas. Used if short stature is the primary concern (not weight alone). Delayed bone age → constitutional delay / GH deficiency / hypothyroidism. Normal bone age → familial short stature.
Do NOT routinely
Extensive metabolic screen without clinical indication. Growth hormone testing in primary care (refer to paediatric endocrinology). Full immunology screen without recurrent infections. MRI brain without neurological signs.
NICE NG75 and paediatric consensus emphasise that investigation-driven approaches in faltered growth are ineffective — a focused history and examination identify the cause far more reliably than a "scatter-gun" blood panel. Coeliac serology is the one investigation that should be performed in almost all children with faltered growth and any GI symptoms — coeliac disease affects 1% of the population and is significantly underdiagnosed. The child must be on a gluten-containing diet for at least 6 weeks before testing — parents sometimes withdraw gluten before the diagnosis is confirmed, which invalidates the test. Total IgA must be checked alongside anti-TTG to exclude IgA deficiency (which gives false-negative anti-TTG in 2–3% of children). Sweat chloride testing for CF requires referral to specialist paediatric respiratory services — the test cannot be performed reliably in primary care. NICE NG75 frames the starting point narrowly: consider urine testing for UTI and coeliac serology, then investigate further only if the clinical picture points to a specific disorder. NICE NG20 asks that the child eats gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before serology — this is why parents are asked not to cut out bread and pasta while waiting.
Step 6

Refer — Referral Criteria, MDT & Safeguarding

Refer
Faltered growth is managed by a multidisciplinary team. Know when to refer urgently, involve paediatrics, or refer to social care.
999 / Same-day admission
Acutely unwell, dehydrated, or shocked child. Severe acute malnutrition (MUAC <11.5cm). Bilious vomiting. Suspected sepsis. Immediate safeguarding concern (at-risk child).
Same-day paediatrics
Rapid weight loss or severe undernutrition (NICE NG75 lists both as referral triggers; same-day when the child is unwell). Suspected organic cause: organomegaly, murmur + FTT, dysmorphic features. Severe parental anxiety with inability to feed. Child <6 months with significant weight concern.
Suspected cancer · NICE NG12
Unexplained palpable abdominal mass or enlarged abdominal organ → very urgent referral (appointment within 48 hours) for suspected neuroblastoma or Wilms' tumour. Unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment for leukaemia. Pallor, fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, bone pain, bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC within 48 hours. NICE NG12 (updated April 2026).
NICE NG75 referral triggers
Discuss with or refer to paediatric specialist services if there are symptoms or signs of an underlying disorder, no response to primary care interventions, slow linear growth or unexplained short stature, rapid weight loss or severe undernutrition, or features that raise safeguarding concern.
Safeguarding referral (Section 17/47)
Suspected neglect or non-accidental injury. Unexplained bruising. Family not engaging with health services. Multiple children affected. Parental substance misuse affecting child's care. Contact Children's Social Care and document. Follow RCPCH / local safeguarding pathway.
Routine paediatrics
Persistent faltered growth (≥3 months) despite dietary intervention + community support. Suspected coeliac, CF, IBD, or other organic cause. Dysmorphic features. Growth hormone deficiency suspected (short stature + poor velocity).
Paediatric dietitian
All children with confirmed faltered growth. Dietary assessment + caloric fortification plan. Feeding behaviour specialist. Essential MDT member.
Community / primary care MDT
Health visitor (primary support in infants — feeding observation, home visit). CAMHS (if ARFID, feeding aversion, maternal mental health). Speech and language therapy (swallowing difficulties, oral motor issues). Children's centre / family support worker (social support, food poverty).
Primary care manage
Mild faltered growth + adequate caloric intake + normal examination + no organic symptoms → dietary advice, health visitor support, re-weigh 2–4 weeks. Ensure Healthy Start vouchers, food bank referral if food insecurity.
Faltered growth is a quintessential MDT problem — no single professional can manage it effectively alone. The health visitor is the most important primary care professional in infant faltered growth — they have the frequency of contact, the home visiting capability, and the feeding expertise that GPs lack. The GP role is to exclude organic disease, identify safeguarding concerns, and coordinate the MDT. NICE NG75 asks for discussion with or referral to paediatric specialist services when there are symptoms or signs of an underlying disorder, when primary care interventions have not worked, when linear growth is slow or short stature is unexplained, when weight loss is rapid or undernutrition severe, or when there are safeguarding concerns. The primary care team should be able to call on an infant feeding specialist, a paediatric dietitian, a speech and language therapist with feeding expertise and a consultant paediatrician. The NICE NG12 row is separate from all of this: a mass or organomegaly is a cancer question first and a nutrition question second. Safeguarding referral must be made where neglect is suspected — Section 17 (child in need) for support, Section 47 (child protection) where significant harm is suspected. Statutory guidance (Working Together to Safeguard Children 2026) and GMC safeguarding guidance expect GPs to refer; the local authority's duties to assess and protect arise under sections 17 and 47 of the Children Act 1989. Delay in safeguarding referral is a recurring finding in serious case reviews.
Step 7

Treat — Nutritional Intervention & Medical Management

Treat
The primary treatment for most faltered growth is increasing caloric intake. Treat the underlying cause where identified.
Breastfed infant — supply concern
Feeding support Immediate
Refer to IBCLC lactation consultant. Check latch, positioning, tongue tie. Increase feeding frequency (8–12 feeds/day). Supplementary expressed breast milk or formula if acute concern. Do not recommend formula without breastfeeding support first. If formula is added (NICE NG75): support the mother to keep breastfeeding, advise expressing to build supply, and give any available breast milk before the formula — supplementation often ends breastfeeding otherwise.
Formula-fed infant — inadequate volume
Optimise volume + caloric density Dietitian-led
Check preparation (standard 1 scoop:30ml). Ensure adequate volume for weight (150–200ml/kg/day in infants). High-calorie formula (e.g. Infatrini, SMA High Energy) if poor volume tolerance — with dietitian guidance.
Older child — poor intake
Caloric fortification Dietitian-led
Add fat/calorie-dense foods: butter, cream, cheese, peanut butter to existing meals. Small frequent meals (6–8/day). Oral nutritional supplements (Paediasure, Fortini) if caloric enrichment alone inadequate — prescribe on FP10.
Organic cause identified
Treat underlying condition Specialist-led
Coeliac: strict gluten-free diet (dietitian). CF: CREON (pancreatic enzyme replacement), high-calorie diet, physiotherapy. Hypothyroidism: levothyroxine. IBD: specialist escalation. Cardiac: cardiological management.
Step 1Dietary assessment + caloric enrichment: Dietitian-calculated caloric target (120–150% estimated average requirement for age). Calorie-dense food additions to existing diet. Structured meal routine.
Step 2Oral nutritional supplements (ONS): Paediasure 1.0 kcal/ml (1–10 years) or Fortini (1–10 years, 1.5 kcal/ml). Prescribe on FP10 if dietitian confirms inadequate intake. Do not replace meals — supplement between meals.
Step 3Nasogastric / gastrostomy feeding: If oral feeding inadequate or child unable to meet nutritional requirements despite ONS. Inpatient initiation of NG feeds. Gastrostomy (PEG) for long-term requirements (neurodisability, CF, complex cardiac). Secondary care decision.
Step 4Treat organic cause: Coeliac → gluten-free diet + dietitian. CF → enzyme replacement (Creon) + high-energy diet + chest physiotherapy. Hypothyroidism → levothyroxine. Coordinate with specialist team.
Caloric enrichment (adding fat and protein to existing foods) is more effective and better tolerated than volume increases for most children with faltered growth — it avoids overwhelming small appetites. The standard dietary target for catch-up growth is 120–150% of the estimated average requirement for chronological age, though this is guided by the dietitian based on the child's specific needs. Oral nutritional supplements can be prescribed on FP10 (NHS prescription) when a dietitian has confirmed inadequate intake — they should supplement, not replace, normal foods, as children who rely on supplements can develop feeding aversion. Pancreatic enzyme replacement (Creon) for CF must be given with every meal and snack — undertreatment causes malabsorption and persistent faltered growth despite adequate intake. For coeliac disease, strict gluten-free diet (not just low-gluten) is essential — even small amounts of gluten cause ongoing villous atrophy and malabsorption. NICE NG75 puts an oral liquid nutritional supplement after other measures: consider a trial when faltering continues despite feeding support and dietary change. Tube feeding is a specialist decision made after multidisciplinary assessment, never a primary care step. Product names and energy densities (Infatrini, SMA High Energy, PaediaSure, Fortini) and doses of Creon, levothyroxine and vitamin D should be checked in the BNF for Children before prescribing.
Step 8

Lifestyle — Family Support, Feeding Environment & Social Interventions

Lifestyle
Social, environmental, and behavioural interventions are as important as nutritional advice and must not be treated as afterthoughts.
Structured mealtimes3 meals + 2–3 snacks/day at regular times. No grazing. Sit at table together as a family. Meals <30 minutes — remove food without comment if not eaten. Reduces mealtime anxiety.
Reduce pressure at mealtimes Responsive feeding approach: let child lead hunger cues. Avoid force-feeding, bribing, or coaxing — increases food refusal. Division of responsibility: parent decides what/when/where; child decides how much.
Screen-free mealtimes Remove screens (TV, tablets, phones) during meals. Distractions impair hunger awareness and meal duration. Positive association with mealtimes reduces long-term feeding difficulties.
Food security support Assess eligibility: NHS Healthy Start (more than 10 weeks pregnant, or a child under 4, on a qualifying income-related benefit — from April 2026 £9.30/week for a baby under 1 and £4.65/week for a child aged 1–4, plus free vitamins). Free School Meals. Food banks. Universal Credit. Citizens Advice Bureau referral. Do not assume — always ask.
Maternal mental health PND affects feeding interaction and responsiveness. PHQ-9 / Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Refer for psychological support — NHS Talking Therapies/CAMHS. Improving maternal mental health improves infant growth.
Health visitor support Regular home visits for feeding observation and practical support. Can attend mealtimes. Key community support professional. Ensure referral made and follow-up agreed.
Cooking skills / food preparation support Refer to children's centre cooking classes. Sure Start / Family Support Worker for practical food preparation help. Avoid judgemental advice — focus on practical support.
Vitamin supplementation All children 6 months–5 years: vitamin A, C, D (Healthy Start vitamins — free if eligible, cheap OTC otherwise). Vitamin D (NICE NG247): breastfed babies from birth to 1 year 8.5–10 micrograms (340–400 IU) daily; no supplement if having more than 500 mL infant formula a day; children aged 1–4 years 10 micrograms (400 IU) daily. Address rickets risk.
The "division of responsibility" in feeding (Ellyn Satter model) is evidence-based — parents control what food is offered, when, and where; the child controls whether and how much they eat. Pressure-based feeding strategies (coaxing, bribing, force) reliably worsen food refusal and increase mealtime anxiety, causing a negative cycle. Reducing pressure, even when a child is failing to thrive, produces better long-term outcomes than pressure-based approaches. Maternal postnatal depression affects feeding responsiveness — mothers with depression are less likely to recognise infant hunger cues, feed responsively, or persist with breastfeeding. Treating maternal mental health is a direct intervention for infant growth. Food poverty is a structural issue affecting 1 in 5 UK children — practical social support (food banks, Healthy Start vouchers, benefit entitlement checks) is a clinical intervention, not a social nicety. NICE NG75 explicitly recommends addressing social determinants of growth faltering as part of clinical management.
Step 9

Safety — Monitoring, Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Safety
Growth monitoring must be structured with clear escalation criteria. Avoid excessive weighing, which increases parental anxiety without improving outcomes.
Weighing frequency (NICE NG75)
When faltering is a concern, weigh at intervals set by age and level of concern, but usually no more often than: daily under 1 month; weekly at 1–6 months; fortnightly at 6–12 months; monthly from 1 year. Over-frequent weighing increases parental anxiety and does not improve outcomes
1–2 weeks
Ensure health visitor has made home visit. Feeding support in place. Dietitian referral made if caloric enrichment recommended. Check family engagement with services.
4 weeks
Reweigh: expected catch-up weight gain should be >2× expected weight gain for age. If no catch-up → reassess, escalate to paediatrics. Review any investigation results (coeliac serology, FBC).
3 months
If no improvement in growth trajectory despite adequate intervention → urgent paediatric referral. Review safeguarding concerns if family not engaging. Reassess for organic cause if not yet excluded.
Catch-up growth criteria
Successful catch-up: weight crosses centile lines upward, returns toward expected centile. Expected: 6–12 weeks of adequate nutrition before catch-up begins. Do not stop intervention when weight improves — maintain until growth trajectory stable
Safety-net 999
Acutely unwell, drowsy, or not responding. Signs of dehydration or shock. Severe nutritional compromise (MUAC <11.5cm). Suspicion of immediate physical harm.
Safety-net same-day
Child stops feeding / refuses all food suddenly. Acute weight loss. New illness symptoms (fever, vomiting, diarrhoea). Parental concern unable to cope / risk of harm.
Safeguarding trigger
Family repeatedly failing to attend follow-up appointments. No improvement despite apparent adequate support. New safeguarding concerns emerging. If uncertain — discuss with the Named GP for Safeguarding or the local children's social care front door (MASH) — the LADO handles allegations against people who work with children, not general concerns.
Document specifically
Record safeguarding concerns at the time — date, time, what was observed, who was present, what was said. Safety-netting advice must be specific (what to watch for, when to come back, who to call) and written in the notes, not a generic "return if worse".
What to say (SCA)
"One weight on its own doesn't tell us much — what matters is the pattern, so I'm going to plot what's in the Red Book and take a careful measurement today. In most children this is about calories getting in rather than anything wrong inside, so the work is practical: the health visitor watching a feed, and a dietitian helping make the food he already eats more calorie-dense — butter, cheese, cream — rather than trying to push more volume. I'll check a coeliac blood test, so please keep bread and pasta in his diet until it's done. And tell me if money for food is tight; that's something I can help with, not something to be embarrassed about."
SCA case
A 9-month-old was on the 50th centile at birth and is now just below the 9th (two centile spaces lost). He is alert and interactive. His mother is exhausted, has been watering down the formula to make the tin last, and has missed the last two health visitor appointments.
Examiner checkpoints
Confirms the fall on serial Red Book plots and considers measurement error; takes a feeding-first history and identifies over-diluted formula; asks about food insecurity directly and without judgement and checks Healthy Start; examines fully including the abdomen for masses or organomegaly and the mouth for tongue tie; sends coeliac serology with total IgA and advises keeping gluten in; re-engages the health visitor and refers to the dietitian; and — the step candidates miss — names the two missed appointments as a safeguarding consideration rather than an administrative detail, documenting the reasoning either way.
The frequency of monitoring should be tailored to severity and risk — NICE NG75 explicitly warns against over-frequent weighing, which creates parental anxiety and a medicalization of normal infant weight variation without improving outcomes. Catch-up growth (crossing centiles upward) typically begins 6–12 weeks after adequate nutritional intervention — do not expect immediate centile catches. Non-engagement with follow-up in the context of faltered growth is a safeguarding signal — it must be escalated rather than simply documented as "did not attend." Documenting safeguarding concerns contemporaneously (date, time, observations, who was present, what was said) is legally important and protects both the child and the clinician. The RCPCH Growth Chart guidelines recommend that growth monitoring should be part of a structured plan with clear escalation criteria documented at each review — "safety-netting" must be specific and recorded. In the SCA the mark usually turns on two things: whether the candidate explains why one number is not a diagnosis, and whether they treat missed appointments and food insecurity as clinical findings. Framing money worries as something the practice can help with, rather than a failing, keeps the family engaged — and engagement is what makes the review plan work.
