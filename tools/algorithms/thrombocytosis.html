1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Extreme Thrombocytosis and Acute Thrombosis

Screen for acute arterial or venous thrombosis, bleeding, very high counts with symptoms, and features suggesting a myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN). Most thrombocytosis is reactive.

Platelets ≥1000 × 10⁹/L Prompt clinical review and haematology discussion, particularly with bleeding, thrombosis, neurological or visual symptoms, splenomegaly, an abnormal film or suspected MPN; same-day specialist assessment if symptomatic or unstable. The count alone does not mandate emergency cytoreduction
Acute thrombosis Stroke/TIA with deficit, acute coronary syndrome, PE, DVT, splanchnic or cerebral venous thrombosis → 999 / relevant emergency pathway. Do not start aspirin solely because the platelet count is high; discuss antiplatelet and cytoreductive treatment urgently with haematology, especially if platelets >1000–1500 × 10⁹/L or bleeding
Splenomegaly New or rapidly enlarging splenomegaly + thrombocytosis → same-day haematology discussion; otherwise urgent referral (ET, PV, myelofibrosis, CML)
Erythromelalgia / digital ischaemia Burning pain, redness and warmth in hands/feet → urgent assessment if new or severe or with digital ischaemia (same-day haematology discussion if significant); haematology referral for persistent or unexplained symptoms. Don't advise self-starting aspirin without checking bleeding risk and platelet level
Neurological / visual symptoms Severe headache, visual disturbance, TIA symptoms, confusion or seizures → urgent assessment irrespective of the platelet count (999 if neurological deficit or suspected intracranial bleed). Consider thrombosis, bleeding, hyperviscosity and alternative neurological causes
Unexplained bleeding Epistaxis, GI bleeding or bruising with very high platelets → same-day haematology discussion (suspected acquired von Willebrand syndrome); major bleeding → 999

Very high platelet counts can be associated with bleeding (acquired von Willebrand syndrome, especially above 1000–1500 × 10⁹/L) as well as thrombosis, but the count correlates poorly with thrombotic risk and does not by itself mandate emergency cytoreduction (BSH). Urgency depends on bleeding, thrombosis, neurological or visual symptoms, suspected MPN, the rate of rise and any need for urgent surgery.

Acute thrombosis with thrombocytosis may be the presenting feature of an MPN, particularly at unusual sites (splanchnic or cerebral veins). Manage the thrombosis through the relevant emergency pathway — acute venous thrombosis is anticoagulated unless contraindicated, and aspirin is not automatically added — and involve haematology if an MPN is suspected.

Erythromelalgia (red, burning extremities) is an important clue to an MPN and may respond dramatically to aspirin, but it is not diagnostic and not automatically a same-day emergency. New or severe symptoms, or digital ischaemia, need urgent assessment.

Reactive thrombocytosis (infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, bleeding, malignancy) accounts for most cases, although proportions vary by setting. Reactive counts can exceed 700 or even 1000 × 10⁹/L, while ET may be only modestly raised — the platelet count alone cannot distinguish them.

2
Diagnose

Confirm Thrombocytosis — Repeat Count and Assess Persistence

Confirm persistent thrombocytosis and exclude transient reactive causes. A single elevated platelet count may be post-operative, post-bleeding, or post-infection.

Repeat FBC
Review previous FBCs and repeat at a clinically determined interval — sooner with symptoms, a rapidly rising count, thrombosis, bleeding, an abnormal film or suspected malignancy. Platelets ≥1000 × 10⁹/L: prompt clinical review and haematology discussion (same-day if symptomatic or unstable).
Reactive vs clonal
Platelet count alone cannot distinguish reactive from clonal thrombocytosis. Consider the full blood count pattern, blood film, clinical context, persistence, splenomegaly, thrombosis and molecular results.
Blood film
Request or review a blood film when thrombocytosis is persistent, unexplained or accompanied by other FBC abnormalities. Look for platelet clumping, giant platelets, left shift, basophilia, blasts, teardrop cells and leucoerythroblastic features.
Clinical context
Recent surgery or trauma? Active infection or inflammation (RA, IBD, pneumonia)? Iron deficiency (common cause)? Bleeding episode? Malignancy? Splenectomy or functional hyposplenism? Smoking and medications?
Thrombosis history
Prior DVT, PE, stroke, MI? Unusual site thrombosis (portal vein, splenic vein, cerebral vein)? Family history of blood clots? These suggest underlying myeloproliferative neoplasm.
Persistent thrombocytosis
Confirm the result and assess the trend. Persistent unexplained thrombocytosis is generally considered over at least 3 months (longer observation may suit mild or variable counts), although the urgency of assessment depends on the count, symptoms, blood film, associated abnormalities and clinical context. A single high count does not establish persistence.

Transient thrombocytosis is common post-infection (pneumonia, UTI), post-surgery, post-bleeding, or with active inflammation (RA flare, IBD). Recheck the FBC after the presumed cause has been treated; persistence is generally judged over at least 3 months, with longer observation for mild or variable counts (BSH 2025).

Iron deficiency is a common cause of reactive thrombocytosis. Ferritin may be falsely normal or raised in inflammation, so transferrin saturation and other iron indices help when ferritin is hard to interpret. Platelet normalisation after iron replacement is variable — recheck the FBC and iron studies rather than expecting a fixed timetable. Always check iron status in thrombocytosis.

Platelet count threshold: no single count separates reactive from clonal disease or establishes persistence. Reactive counts can exceed 700–1000 × 10⁹/L, and ET can present only modestly above 450. Persistent unexplained thrombocytosis needs evaluation even below 600, and some ET is negative for JAK2, CALR and MPL.

Blood film review can identify CML (left shift, basophilia), myelofibrosis (tear-drop cells, leucoerythroblastic picture), and ET (large abnormal platelets, megakaryocyte fragments). Essential for all persistent thrombocytosis.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Reactive vs Clonal Thrombocytosis

Classify thrombocytosis as reactive (secondary) or clonal (primary myeloproliferative neoplasm). This determines investigation pathway and treatment urgency.

Reactive thrombocytosis (most cases)
Causes: Infection, inflammation (RA, IBD, vasculitis), malignancy, iron deficiency, bleeding, post-operative, tissue damage, splenectomy or hyposplenism. Usually settles when the cause is treated; counts can be very high.
Clonal thrombocytosis
The count alone cannot distinguish clonal from reactive. Myeloproliferative neoplasms: Essential thrombocythaemia (ET), polycythaemia vera (PV), chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), myelofibrosis. JAK2/CALR/MPL mutations often present (some triple-negative); BCR-ABL1 in CML.
Essential thrombocythaemia (ET)
A specialist diagnosis based on persistent thrombocytosis, exclusion of reactive causes and other myeloid disorders (PV, prefibrotic/primary myelofibrosis, CML, MDS/MPN overlap, spurious thrombocytosis), molecular findings where present (JAK2, CALR, MPL; some triple-negative) and, often, bone-marrow morphology.
Polycythaemia vera (PV)
Raised Hb/haematocrit ± raised platelets and WCC; usually JAK2-positive. If erythrocytosis is present, use the separate polycythaemia pathway and refer to haematology; treatment is specialist-led.
CML (chronic phase)
Thrombocytosis + leucocytosis + splenomegaly. BCR-ABL fusion gene positive. Left shift on blood film (myelocytes, metamyelocytes, basophilia). Urgent haematology referral; treatment is specialist-led.
Myelofibrosis
Thrombocytosis early, thrombocytopenia late. Massive splenomegaly, tear-drop cells, leucoerythroblastic blood film. Bone marrow fibrosis on biopsy.

Reactive thrombocytosis is managed by treating the underlying cause; it is not usually treated with aspirin or cytoreduction, and thrombotic risk is assessed from the underlying condition rather than the platelet count. Antibiotics only for documented or strongly suspected bacterial infection; NSAIDs are not prescribed merely because the platelet count is raised.

Essential thrombocythaemia (ET) is a specialist diagnosis made after excluding reactive causes and other myeloid disorders. Thrombotic risk depends on previous thrombosis, age, JAK2 status, cardiovascular risk factors, microvascular symptoms and disease phenotype — platelet count correlates poorly with thrombotic risk (BSH) — and treatment is risk-adapted and haematology-led.

JAK2 V617F is found in most PV and in approximately 50–60% of ET; CALR and MPL frequencies vary between cohorts and diagnostic classifications. It activates JAK-STAT signalling. A positive result supports an MPN but does not by itself diagnose ET, and some ET is triple-negative.

Distinguishing ET from reactive: persistence despite treating the cause, splenomegaly, thrombosis, an abnormal film and molecular results point towards clonal disease. CRP/ESR may support an inflammatory cause, but normal inflammatory markers do not exclude reactive thrombocytosis or an MPN, and inflammation may be intermittent or occult. Iron deficiency can coexist with ET.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Splenomegaly and Vascular Symptoms

Examine for splenomegaly, signs of thrombosis, and microvascular symptoms suggesting myeloproliferative neoplasm.

Vital signs
BP elevated → PV (hyperviscosity increases peripheral resistance). Plethoric facies (ruddy complexion) → PV. Fever + thrombocytosis → infection, malignancy.
Spleen
Percuss and palpate left upper quadrant. Splenomegaly → MPN (ET, PV, myelofibrosis) or CML; often massive in CML and myelofibrosis. Usually absent in reactive thrombocytosis unless the cause itself enlarges the spleen. Check for lymphadenopathy.
Extremities
Inspect hands and feet. Erythromelalgia: Red, warm, burning extremities → ET/PV (platelet microvascular thrombosis). Acrocyanosis: Blue discoloration → hyperviscosity (PV). Gangrene/ulcers: Digital ischaemia → severe ET.
Neurological
Test for focal weakness, visual field defects, dysphasia (prior stroke/TIA). Assess for headache, dizziness, tinnitus (hyperviscosity symptoms in PV). Confusion, seizures → cerebral vein thrombosis.
Cardiovascular
Examine for DVT (calf swelling, tenderness), peripheral arterial disease (absent pulses). Auscultate for carotid bruit. Check for signs of MI (prior event).
Abdomen
Hepatomegaly → myeloproliferative neoplasm (extramedullary haematopoiesis). Ascites + splenomegaly → Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis, associated with JAK2+ disorders).
Skin
Plethora (ruddy face, red palms) → PV. Aquagenic pruritus (after a hot bath) → supportive clue for an MPN, not diagnostic. Bruising + petechiae → extreme thrombocytosis (acquired vWD).

Splenomegaly in thrombocytosis strongly suggests clonal disorder. Spleen sequestration paradoxically occurs in ET/PV despite high platelet count (extramedullary haematopoiesis). Reactive thrombocytosis does not cause splenomegaly unless the reactive cause itself does (e.g., lymphoma, infection).

Erythromelalgia (burning red extremities) is a clue to an MPN, not pathognomonic. It reflects platelet-mediated microvascular disturbance and may respond dramatically to aspirin — but aspirin is started on haematology advice after checking bleeding risk and the platelet level. New or severe symptoms, or digital ischaemia, need urgent assessment.

Plethora and aquagenic pruritus suggest PV. Plethora reflects a raised haematocrit; aquagenic pruritus is a supportive clue for an MPN but is not diagnostic and has other dermatological and systemic causes.

Thrombotic events (stroke, MI, DVT, Budd-Chiari) can be the presenting feature of ET/PV. Unusual site thrombosis (portal, splenic, cerebral veins) is highly suggestive of JAK2+ myeloproliferative neoplasm. Screen all thrombocytosis patients for prior thrombosis.

Investigations — JAK2 Mutation, Inflammatory Markers, and Iron Studies

Investigate to differentiate reactive from clonal thrombocytosis. Target investigations based on clinical context and platelet count.

Baseline bloods
FBC + blood film (film if persistent, unexplained or with other FBC abnormalities) CRP/ESR (may support an inflammatory cause; normal values don't exclude reactive disease or an MPN) Ferritin (iron deficiency common cause) Renal + liver function Review previous results; further tests targeted to symptoms.
Iron studies
Serum ferritin Transferrin saturation — Check ferritin and, where inflammation is present or ferritin interpretation is uncertain, transferrin saturation and other iron indices. Treat confirmed iron deficiency according to the underlying cause and local iron-replacement guidance, then repeat the FBC and iron studies.
Inflammatory markers
ESR CRP — Elevated suggests reactive cause (infection, RA, IBD, malignancy). Normal inflammatory markers do not exclude reactive thrombocytosis or an MPN.
JAK2 mutation
JAK2 V617F — If thrombocytosis is persistent and unexplained after reviewing the blood film, iron status and inflammatory/clinical causes, request JAK2 V617F according to the local haematology pathway or refer to haematology. If negative, CALR/MPL and broader testing are specialist-directed. A positive result supports an MPN but does not by itself diagnose ET.
If suspected ET/PV
JAK2 V617F Full blood count (Hct, WCC) LDH (cell turnover) Uric acid (gout risk). Haematology performs bone marrow biopsy if diagnosis unclear.
If suspected CML
BCR-ABL (Philadelphia chromosome), Blood film (left shift, basophilia). If suspected or positive, urgent haematology referral.
Infection/malignancy screen
If reactive cause suspected: Chest X-ray (pneumonia, malignancy), Urine culture (UTI), Adults aged 40 and over with thrombocytosis: consider an urgent direct-access Chest X-ray (NICE NG12). Other investigations targeted to symptoms and examination: FIT or upper GI investigation if GI symptoms or iron-deficiency anaemia indicate it; CT via the relevant specialist or local rapid-diagnostic pathway if malignancy is suspected — not routine broad imaging.
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT perform bone marrow biopsy in primary care. Do NOT restrict JAK2 testing to counts above 600 — follow the local pathway for persistent unexplained thrombocytosis. Do NOT delay treating iron deficiency to "see if platelets normalise" — treat and recheck. Do NOT start aspirin or cytoreduction for suspected ET in primary care.

JAK2 V617F testing supports an MPN when positive but does not by itself diagnose ET; diagnosis needs specialist correlation with marrow morphology and exclusion of reactive causes and other MPNs. Test persistent unexplained thrombocytosis (including below 600) via the local haematology pathway after reviewing the film, iron status and inflammatory/clinical causes.

Iron deficiency is a common cause of reactive thrombocytosis in primary care. Interpret ferritin with care in inflammation (transferrin saturation helps), treat according to the cause and local iron-replacement guidance, investigate the source of blood loss (gastrointestinal or menstrual) where appropriate, and recheck the FBC and iron studies — platelet normalisation is variable.

CRP/ESR may support an inflammatory cause, but normal inflammatory markers do not exclude reactive thrombocytosis or an MPN — inflammation may be intermittent or occult.

Bone marrow biopsy is gold standard for ET diagnosis (WHO criteria: megakaryocyte hyperplasia, no BCR-ABL, no reactive cause). Performed by haematology, not primary care. Often required, particularly if JAK2, CALR and MPL are all negative, or to exclude prefibrotic myelofibrosis (reticulin staining).

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to Involve Haematology

Refer on the whole clinical picture — persistence, explanation, symptoms, film, molecular results and the local pathway — not the platelet count alone. Clonal thrombocytosis requires specialist management.

999 Emergency
Stroke or TIA with neurological deficit; acute coronary syndrome; PE or haemodynamically significant DVT; acute splanchnic or cerebral venous thrombosis; major bleeding; digital ischaemia or threatened limb; severe headache with neurological/visual symptoms; collapse or suspected intracranial bleeding. Do not initiate aspirin/anticoagulation solely from the platelet count.
Same-day haematology
Same-day discussion for: symptomatic platelets ≥1000–1500 × 10⁹/L; active bleeding with extreme thrombocytosis; suspected acquired von Willebrand syndrome; significant erythromelalgia or digital ischaemia; a film suggesting acute leukaemia, CML or myelofibrosis; new or rapidly enlarging splenomegaly; recent thrombosis. An asymptomatic count >1000 with a clear reactive cause needs prompt reassessment and specialist advice, not automatic emergency admission.
Urgent haematology
Persistent unexplained thrombocytosis. Positive JAK2, CALR or MPL; BCR-ABL1 positive (CML). Abnormal blood film. Thrombocytosis with splenomegaly, B symptoms, leucocytosis or erythrocytosis. Unexplained thrombocytosis with thrombosis (especially at an unusual site). Platelets persistently >600–700 without an adequate secondary cause. Suspected ET, PV, CML or myelofibrosis. Significant bleeding or severe/progressive thrombocytosis.
Routine haematology
Timing per the local Bolton/Greater Manchester haematology pathway — there is no universal count-based rule such as "platelets >700 for >3 months". Persistent unexplained thrombocytosis at any age, including JAK2-negative cases with clinical suspicion.
Primary care management
Reactive thrombocytosis with a clear, adequate cause (infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, recent surgery) that is settling with treatment — recheck the FBC after treating the cause. Refer if it becomes unexplained, disproportionate, progressive or accompanied by other abnormalities or symptoms.
Specialist referrals
Respiratory: If malignancy suspected (CXR mass). Gastro: IBD with thrombocytosis. Rheumatology: RA, vasculitis causing reactive thrombocytosis.

Platelets ≥1000 × 10⁹/L need prompt review and haematology discussion, but not automatic emergency admission or cytoreduction: same-day specialist assessment is for symptomatic or unstable patients, bleeding, thrombosis, suspected acquired von Willebrand syndrome or suspected MPN. Platelet apheresis is a rare specialist bridge and is never indicated by the count alone.

JAK2 V617F positive supports a myeloproliferative neoplasm and warrants haematology referral. Refer urgently to haematology (NG12 has no specific platelet-count criterion for myeloproliferative neoplasms). Haematology confirms diagnosis, risk-stratifies (IPSET score for ET), and decides on aspirin ± cytoreduction.

Reactive thrombocytosis (iron deficiency, infection, inflammation) does not usually require haematology referral when a known cause adequately explains it and the count is settling with treatment. Recheck the FBC after treating the presumed cause; refer if it remains unexplained, disproportionate, progressive or accompanied by other abnormalities or symptoms.

Persistent unexplained thrombocytosis warrants haematology referral at any age, even if JAK2-negative and the count is below 700 — ET can present at any age and with only modestly raised platelets, and BSH 2025 guidance covers mutation-negative thrombocytosis.

7
Treat

Treatment — Treat the Cause; ET/PV Treatment is Haematology-Led

Treatment depends on whether thrombocytosis is reactive or clonal. Reactive: treat the cause; do not routinely prescribe aspirin. Suspected or confirmed ET/PV: do not initiate aspirin or cytoreduction in primary care — management is risk-adapted, haematology-led and under a formal shared-care protocol.

Reactive thrombocytosis
Treat underlying cause No aspirin
Treat infection (antibiotics only for documented or strongly suspected bacterial infection), inflammation and bleeding; treat confirmed iron deficiency per local guidance and investigate its cause (GI or menstrual loss). No cytoreduction; no NSAIDs merely because the count is raised; no aspirin unless an independent cardiovascular indication. Recheck the FBC after treating the cause.
Essential thrombocythaemia (lower-risk)
Aspirin if advised by haematology Specialist-led
Aspirin is commonly used but not automatic — considerations include platelets >1000–1500, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, active bleeding, intolerance, CALR-mutated or very low-risk disease, concomitant anticoagulation and GI bleeding risk. Monitoring per the haematology plan; cardiovascular risk management via standard pathways.
Essential thrombocythaemia (high-risk)
Cytoreduction Haematology-initiated
Cytoreduction is initiated and monitored by haematology. Hydroxycarbamide is commonly used in high-risk ET; interferon may be preferred in younger patients or pregnancy planning. Anagrelide is a specialist second-line option. Choice depends on age, prior thrombosis, JAK2 status, pregnancy plans, comorbidity and patient preference; there is no universal platelet target.

ET/PV treatment options (haematology-led; GP prescribing only under the Greater Manchester shared-care protocol):

Step 1Aspirin — risk-adapted and specialist-advised, not automatic (BSH lists platelets >1000 as a relative contraindication; caution with acquired von Willebrand syndrome, bleeding or anticoagulation). Consider a PPI if GI risk.
Step 2Hydroxycarbamide — commonly used in high-risk ET; initiated, dosed and targeted by haematology (dose is weight- and renal-function dependent). Monitoring per the current shared-care protocol.
Step 3Anagrelide — specialist-initiated second-line option. Adverse effects include tachycardia and palpitations, arrhythmia and heart-failure risk, fluid retention, headache and diarrhoea; check cardiac contraindications and interactions in the current SPC and with haematology.
Step 4Interferon — may be preferred in younger patients or when pregnancy is planned; specialist-led. Flu-like symptoms are common. Depression and other psychiatric illness need careful risk assessment and specialist monitoring, but are not always an absolute contraindication.
EmergencyPlatelet apheresis is a rare emergency bridge for life-threatening thrombosis, serious bleeding or urgent surgery in extreme thrombocytosis, under specialist haematology/transfusion guidance. The platelet count alone is not an indication.
Polycythaemia vera
If erythrocytosis is present, use the separate polycythaemia pathway and refer to haematology. Venesection, aspirin, cytoreduction, treatment targets and monitoring are specialist-led or under shared care.
Monitoring on treatment
Follow the current written shared-care protocol for FBC, renal and liver monitoring and for withholding treatment; do not alter or stop cytoreductive treatment without specialist advice unless the protocol specifically directs this. Annual review: thrombosis events, bleeding, symptom control, cardiovascular risk factors.
Pregnancy in ET/PV
Pregnancy planning or pregnancy in suspected or confirmed ET or PV requires early haematology and obstetric referral. Do not independently stop cytoreduction or start aspirin/LMWH; follow the specialist pregnancy plan.

Aspirin is commonly used in ET/PV to reduce arterial events, but its use is risk-adapted and specialist-led. It may be withheld or used cautiously with platelets >1000–1500, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, active bleeding, intolerance, CALR-mutated or very low-risk ET, concomitant anticoagulation or high GI bleeding risk.

Hydroxycarbamide (hydroxyurea) is commonly used for cytoreduction in high-risk ET. It is initiated, dosed and monitored by haematology, with formal transfer under the Greater Manchester shared-care protocol if GP prescribing is intended; the main toxicity is myelosuppression.

Risk stratification determines treatment and is done by haematology, considering age, previous thrombosis, JAK2 status, cardiovascular risk factors, microvascular symptoms, platelet level and acquired von Willebrand syndrome. A count above 1500 × 10⁹/L is mainly a bleeding (acquired von Willebrand) concern rather than simply a thrombotic-risk criterion.

Reactive thrombocytosis is managed by treating the underlying cause (iron, infection, inflammation); aspirin is not indicated for the platelet count itself, and any thrombotic risk is assessed according to the underlying condition rather than the count.

8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological — Cardiovascular Risk Reduction and Thrombosis Prevention

In ET/PV, address smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, obesity and physical inactivity through the patient's standard NICE/local cardiovascular-risk pathway; thrombocytosis does not change the usual targets.

Smoking cessation Smoking adds to arterial thrombotic risk. Offer stop-smoking support (behavioural support with varenicline or NRT) and referral to the stop-smoking service.
Blood pressure control Diagnose and manage hypertension per NICE NG136, using the usual targets and drug choice.
Diabetes management Manage diabetes per NICE NG28 and the local pathway, with individualised HbA1c targets and drug choice based on phenotype, renal function, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, obesity and hypoglycaemia risk. Annual retinopathy screening.
Lipid control Atorvastatin 20 mg OD if QRISK3 ≥10% (primary prevention) or 80 mg OD with established CVD (NICE NG238). Secondary-prevention target LDL-C ≤2.0 mmol/L or non-HDL-C ≤2.6 mmol/L (NICE NG238).
Hydration Avoid dehydration and maintain normal fluid intake, adjusted for cardiac, renal and hepatic comorbidity — particularly in hot weather, when flying or during illness.
Avoid immobility Keep mobile on long journeys and stay physically active. Travel thromboprophylaxis is decided on individual VTE risk (such as previous thrombosis) with specialist advice, not on the platelet count.
Alcohol moderation Keep within low-risk limits (no more than 14 units/week, spread over the week) and avoid binge drinking.
Surgery precautions Inform surgeons/anaesthetists of ET/PV before any procedure. Peri-operative management (aspirin, thromboprophylaxis, cytoreduction) is agreed between surgery, anaesthesia and haematology, considering bleeding and thrombotic risk, platelet count, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, the indication for aspirin, the type and urgency of surgery and current treatment.

Cardiovascular risk factors (smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, obesity, inactivity) add to thrombotic risk in ET/PV, so address them through the standard NICE/local cardiovascular-risk pathways alongside haematology-led treatment.

Smoking cessation is an important modifiable intervention because smoking adds to arterial thrombotic risk through endothelial dysfunction and platelet activation.

Hydration: dehydration raises haematocrit and blood viscosity, particularly in PV, so advise avoiding dehydration — with fluid intake adjusted for heart failure, renal impairment or prescribed fluid restriction rather than a fixed daily volume.

Immobility (long journeys, bed rest) increases venous stasis. Keep mobile; any travel or peri-operative thromboprophylaxis is based on individual VTE risk and specialist advice rather than the platelet count.

9
Safety

Follow-Up — Monitor Platelet Count and Safety-Net for Thrombosis

Monitor platelet trajectory, watch for thrombotic events, and re-refer if platelets rising or new symptoms. Chronic thrombocytosis requires long-term surveillance.

Reactive
Recheck the FBC after treatment of the presumed cause. Refer persistent thrombocytosis that is unexplained, disproportionate, progressive or accompanied by other abnormalities or symptoms — known ongoing inflammation, infection, iron deficiency or malignancy may explain it. If iron deficient, recheck the FBC and iron studies after treatment.
ET/PV, no cytoreduction
FBC at intervals set by haematology. Monitor for progression (rising platelets, new splenomegaly). Annual cardiovascular risk review (BP, lipids, HbA1c, smoking status).
On cytoreduction
Follow the current written Greater Manchester shared-care protocol for FBC, renal and liver monitoring and for withholding treatment. Discuss toxicity urgently with the specialist team; do not alter or stop cytoreductive treatment without specialist advice unless the protocol specifically directs this.
Annual haematology review
For ET/PV patients: Review thrombosis/bleeding events, symptom control (headache, pruritus, erythromelalgia), cardiovascular risk factors, medication adherence. Assess for disease progression (evolution to myelofibrosis: tear-drop cells, rising LDH).
Safety-net 999
Sudden severe headache, focal weakness, speech changes (stroke). Chest pain, breathlessness (MI, PE). Severe abdominal pain + ascites (Budd-Chiari syndrome). Unilateral leg swelling (DVT). Sudden vision loss (retinal vein occlusion). Major bleeding, a cold or threatened digit or limb, collapse.
Safety-net same-day
New or severe erythromelalgia or digital ischaemia. Unexplained bleeding with very high platelets (possible acquired von Willebrand syndrome). Severe headache, visual changes or confusion — urgent assessment irrespective of the platelet count. Very high or rapidly rising counts on monitoring → same-day haematology discussion. TIA symptoms (999 if ongoing deficit).
Re-referral criteria
Platelets rising despite cytoreduction. Thrombotic event on treatment (medication escalation needed). Intolerance to hydroxycarbamide (switch to anagrelide/interferon). Pregnancy planning or pregnancy — early haematology and obstetric referral; do not independently stop cytoreduction or start aspirin/LMWH.

Platelet trajectory in reactive thrombocytosis should settle once the cause is treated. Persistence that is unexplained, disproportionate or progressive raises the possibility of clonal disease — request JAK2 per the local pathway or refer to haematology — but ongoing inflammation, infection, iron deficiency or malignancy may still explain it.

Thrombotic events on treatment indicate inadequate control. If patient has stroke/MI/DVT while on aspirin + cytoreduction, haematology escalates therapy (higher hydroxycarbamide dose, switch to anagrelide, add anticoagulation). Primary care role is early recognition and urgent referral.

Disease progression: A minority of ET evolves to myelofibrosis over time. Signs: progressive splenomegaly, tear-drop cells on film, rising LDH, transition from thrombocytosis to thrombocytopenia. Myelofibrosis requires stem cell transplant evaluation (only curative therapy).

Cardiovascular review: manage smoking, BP, lipids, diabetes, weight and activity through the patient's standard NICE/local cardiovascular-risk pathway; thrombocytosis does not change the usual targets.
