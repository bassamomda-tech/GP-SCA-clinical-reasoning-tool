1
Safety

Red Flags — Ocular Emergencies Not to Miss

Acute visual loss is an ophthalmological emergency until proven otherwise. Sudden painless monocular loss or a curtain/shadow defect needs immediate assessment through the local eye emergency/stroke pathway — do not delay transfer for primary-care investigations or treatment. Most eye emergencies need same-day eye emergency assessment rather than an ambulance; use 999 for neurological features, systemic illness or unsafe transfer.

Sudden painless monocular visual loss Curtain/shadow defect, complete loss → Immediate eye emergency / stroke pathway Consider retinal artery occlusion, retinal vein occlusion, retinal detachment, vitreous haemorrhage, optic neuropathy. Do not delay for GP tests or treatment
Painful red eye + reduced vision + haloes Headache, nausea/vomiting, corneal haze, fixed/mid-dilated pupil → Immediate same-day eye casualty Acute angle closure — 999 if severe systemic illness, collapse, marked vomiting, confusion or cannot transfer safely. Keep upright; avoid pupil dilation
Visual loss + headache + scalp tenderness >50y Jaw claudication, temporal artery tenderness → Same-day Giant cell arteritis (also amaurosis fugax, diplopia, other cranial ischaemic symptoms) — treat immediately per local GCA pathway with same-day ophthalmology/rheumatology assessment; do not wait for ESR/CRP
Binocular visual loss + headache + vomiting Papilloedema, disc swelling → Same-day emergency Raised intracranial pressure — SOL, idiopathic intracranial hypertension; 999 if reduced consciousness or focal neurology
Floaters + flashing lights + curtain defect New onset, especially myopic patient → Same-day Retinal detachment until excluded — same-day eye emergency service; do not wait 24 h. A macula-sparing detachment remains urgent. Flashes/floaters without field loss → urgent optometry/ophthalmology per local pathway
Visual loss + facial weakness/slurred speech Sudden onset, other stroke features → 999 Posterior circulation stroke. FAST. Thrombolysis window
Chemical or penetrating eye injury Alkali (cement, bleach) far worse than acid → Emergency Begin immediate copious irrigation with clean water or saline; remove contact lenses if easy; continue irrigation during transfer; check pH where possible and irrigate until neutral (no fixed duration — alkali may need prolonged irrigation). No neutralising chemicals. Emergency ophthalmic assessment
Acute visual loss in diabetic patient Vitreous haemorrhage or tractional RD possible, but not automatic — consider vascular occlusion, GCA, stroke → Same-day Eye emergency assessment; risk-factor review must not delay referral

Central retinal artery occlusion is an ophthalmic and stroke emergency — there is no universally established routine treatment window; interventions such as thrombolysis depend on local specialist/stroke pathways and eligibility, so arrange immediate assessment. Giant cell arteritis is an important missed ocular emergency — start treatment immediately per the local GCA pathway, before temporal artery ultrasound/biopsy; delay risks bilateral blindness.

2
Diagnose

Characterise the Visual Problem

Onset & duration
Sudden (<24h): vascular, retinal detachment, acute glaucoma. Subacute (days–weeks): optic neuritis, uveitis. Gradual (months–years): cataract, AMD, glaucoma, refractive error
Monocular vs binocular
Monocular: eye or pre-chiasmal problem (retina, optic nerve, cornea). Binocular: post-chiasmal (brain — occipital cortex, visual pathways). Critical distinction
Painful vs painless
Painful: acute angle closure glaucoma, uveitis, corneal disease, optic neuritis (pain on eye movement). Painless: retinal vascular, AMD, cataract, ARMD
Type of blurring
Central: AMD, macular disease. Peripheral/field defect: glaucoma (arcuate), stroke (hemianopia). Floaters/flashing: vitreous/retinal. Halos/starbursts: cataract, refractive error
Diplopia
Monocular (persists with other eye covered): corneal or lens. Binocular (resolves with cover): cranial nerve palsy (III, IV, VI), thyroid eye disease, myasthenia. New binocular diplopia: same-day emergency if pupil-involving 3rd nerve palsy, severe headache, reduced consciousness, other neurological signs (ataxia, dysarthria, limb weakness, sensory loss), painful ophthalmoplegia, proptosis/orbital disease or suspected GCA; isolated stable diplopia → urgent ophthalmology, neurology or orthoptic assessment
Medical history
Diabetes (diabetic retinopathy), hypertension (hypertensive retinopathy, CRVO), AF (embolic CRAO), MS (optic neuritis), autoimmune (uveitis), corticosteroids (cataract, glaucoma)
Drug history
Hydroxychloroquine (retinal toxicity — RCOphth 2020 / GM shared care: annual monitoring after 5 years, or after 1 year if risk factors such as tamoxifen, eGFR <60 or dose >5 mg/kg/day; see Step 9), amiodarone (corneal deposits), ethambutol (optic neuritis), sildenafil (visual disturbance), tamoxifen (maculopathy)

The monocular vs binocular distinction is the most important early differentiator — covering each eye individually in consultation takes 10 seconds and separates ocular pathology from neurological pathology. New binocular diplopia in an elderly patient with hypertension strongly suggests microvascular cranial nerve palsy (III, IV, VI) — usually self-limiting but must exclude posterior communicating artery aneurysm (third nerve palsy with ptosis and dilated pupil = aneurysm until proven otherwise). Hydroxychloroquine retinopathy is detected through commissioned hospital retinal monitoring (OCT, fundus autofluorescence ± visual fields), not routine optometrist review — confirm the patient is enrolled.

3
Diagnose

Classify the Likely Diagnosis

Refractive error
Gradual bilateral blur (both near and far), improves with squinting, no pain, no other symptoms. Refer optometrist. Most common cause overall
Cataract
Gradual, worse in bright light, halo around lights, monocular diplopia, worse distance vision. Confirmed by ophthalmology. Surgical management
Age-related macular degeneration
Central vision loss (reading, faces), distorted straight lines (metamorphopsia), Wet AMD: new distortion, grey/blurred central patch or sudden central loss → urgent direct referral to the macula service, normally within 1 working day (NICE NG82)
Glaucoma
Peripheral field loss (arcuate scotoma), may be painless and asymptomatic until advanced. IOP may be normal (normal tension glaucoma). Early glaucoma is often asymptomatic and cannot be reliably diagnosed or excluded by confrontation fields or GP fundoscopy — detection is via optometry
Diabetic retinopathy
Background, pre-proliferative, proliferative stages. NHS Diabetic Eye Screening Programme — annual screening, or every 2 years since October 2023 for people with no retinopathy on their two most recent screens (UK NSC recommendation). Referral follows the grading and pathway recorded by the screening service — GPs should not re-grade retinal photographs. Sudden loss in diabetes is not automatically vitreous haemorrhage
Optic neuritis
Subacute monocular visual loss, pain on eye movement, reduced colour vision (especially red desaturation), RAPD present. Consider demyelinating disease, but also assess for ischaemic, inflammatory, infective, compressive and toxic causes
Dry eye / corneal
Fluctuating blur (improves with blinking/lubricants), gritty sensation, worse in dry/air-conditioned environments. Common — but check that a painful red eye with reduced vision is not being misclassified as dry eye

Dry eye disease is a common cause of "blurry vision" in primary care and most do not need specialist review. A simple test: ask if vision improves after blinking — yes = dry eye/surface disease. AMD is a leading cause of sight loss in older adults. Suspected wet AMD requires urgent referral to a macula service, normally within 1 working day; NICE NG82 advises anti-VEGF treatment as soon as possible and within 14 days of referral where indicated.

4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — What GPs Can Assess

Visual acuity
Snellen chart at 6 m (or near card). Record best corrected VA (with glasses). 6/6 normal. 6/60 or worse = significant impairment. Pinhole test: improves with pinhole = refractive error
Pupil reactions
Direct + consensual. RAPD (afferent pupillary defect = relative APD using swinging torch test) → optic nerve or retinal pathology (optic neuritis, CRVO, CRAO)
Visual fields
Confrontation fields are a screening assessment only — they cannot exclude field loss. Compare to examiner's. Bitemporal hemianopia (pituitary lesion), homonymous hemianopia (stroke/space-occupying lesion)
Extraocular movements
Check all 6 directions. Pain on movement → optic neuritis. Limitation → cranial nerve palsy (III = down-out, IV = vertical diplopia stairs, VI = lateral gaze failure)
External eye
Red eye (conjunctiva, cornea, ciliary injection). Proptosis (thyroid eye disease). Lid abnormalities. Corneal haziness (acute glaucoma)
Fundoscopy
Disc (papilloedema, cupping, pallor). Vessels (AV nipping, silver wiring). Macula (drusen = AMD, haemorrhage). Cotton wool spots, flame haemorrhages, neovascularisation. GP fundoscopy is a screening assessment only — it cannot exclude retinal or optic nerve disease
IOP (if tonometer available)
Do not diagnose chronic glaucoma or decide referral from a single non-contact tonometer reading. NICE NG81: refer via the local optometry/ophthalmology pathway for a suspicious optic disc, a repeatable visual-field defect consistent with glaucoma, or IOP ≥24 mmHg on Goldmann-type applanation tonometry (after repeat measures). Suspected acute angle closure is a clinical emergency — do not rely on tonometry

The pinhole test is the most underused tool in primary care — it can distinguish refractive error (improves with pinhole) from pathological visual loss (does not improve). RAPD is the key sign for optic nerve disease — it indicates asymmetric afferent input and is present in optic neuritis, CRVO, and CRAO. A complete third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil (surgical third nerve) must be differentiated from a pupil-sparing microvascular third nerve palsy — dilated pupil = aneurysm, emergency CT angiography required.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — When to Test What

Suspected GCA
Urgent FBC, ESR, CRP — do not delay treatment while awaiting results; normal results do not exclude GCA. Temporal artery ultrasound/biopsy via the local GCA pathway
Vascular visual loss
FBC, ESR/CRP, clotting, lipids, HbA1c, BP, ECG (AF as embolic source), carotid Doppler if TIA-equivalent — in hospital or via the eye emergency/stroke pathway; do not delay transfer to arrange these
Optic neuritis
Urgent ophthalmology/neuro-ophthalmology or neurology referral — MRI brain and orbits may be arranged by the specialist. Do not initiate corticosteroids routinely in primary care; treatment (usually IV methylprednisolone in selected cases) is specialist-led
Diabetic review
HbA1c, BP, lipids, urine ACR. Confirm National Diabetic Eye Screening appointment up to date (annual, or 2-yearly if low risk). Referral follows the grading and pathway recorded by the screening service — do not re-grade images. Risk-factor review must not delay eye referral
Thyroid eye disease
TFTs (TSH, T4, T3), TRAb antibodies (thyroid receptor antibodies). CT/MRI orbits (extraocular muscle enlargement). Refer endocrinology + ophthalmology
Amsler grid
Self-monitoring aid for patients with known AMD or where advised by the eye-care professional — not routinely for every over-50. Advise reporting new blurred/grey patches, distortion or objects appearing smaller (NICE NG82)
When NOT to investigate
Do NOT order CT head for gradual bilateral blur improving with pinhole — this is refractive error, refer optometrist. Do NOT delay GCA treatment for ESR/CRP. Do NOT delay transfer of acute visual loss for GP investigations

GCA bloods should be sent simultaneously with starting prednisolone — never delay treatment waiting for results. ESR can be normal in GCA, so CRP is sent alongside. In retinal artery occlusion a cardiac embolic source such as AF may be found — ECG and vascular risk assessment are arranged in hospital or through the stroke pathway. In optic neuritis, specialist MRI helps assess demyelinating disease, but ischaemic, inflammatory, infective, compressive and toxic causes must also be considered.

6
Refer

Referral — Urgency Stratification

999
Sudden major visual loss with neurological features; acute painful red eye with severe visual loss or systemic illness; chemical eye injury requiring ongoing irrigation; suspected acute angle closure with severe vomiting, collapse or inability to transfer safely; orbital cellulitis with visual loss, proptosis or systemic toxicity; visual symptoms with eclampsia or severe hypertension
Same-day eye emergency
Curtain/field defect or suspected retinal detachment; new flashes and floaters with reduced vision; vitreous haemorrhage; painful red eye with reduced vision (incl. suspected acute angle closure); suspected uveitis, endophthalmitis or corneal ulcer; suspected GCA with visual symptoms (treat per local GCA pathway); acute unexplained monocular visual loss incl. suspected CRAO (local eye emergency/stroke pathway); new binocular diplopia with red-flag features (pupil-involving 3rd nerve palsy, severe headache, neurological signs, painful ophthalmoplegia, proptosis, suspected GCA); new optic disc swelling
Urgent macula service / ophthalmology
Suspected wet AMD (new distortion, grey/blurred central patch, sudden central loss) → urgent direct referral to the local macula service, normally within 1 working day (NICE NG82), via direct optometry-to-macula pathway where available (document the local Bolton/Greater Manchester route); not 999 unless neurological or systemic emergency features. Urgent ophthalmology: central or branch retinal vein occlusion, new proptosis, isolated stable binocular diplopia (or neurology/orthoptics). 3rd nerve palsy with dilated pupil → same-day emergency
Suspected cancer
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026), rec 1.12.2: consider urgent referral (within 2 weeks) for ophthalmological assessment for retinoblastoma in a child with an absent red reflex. Outside NG12: painless progressive monocular visual loss with no clear cause (exclude intraocular malignancy such as uveal melanoma), unexplained optic atrophy or a new afferent pupillary defect → urgent ophthalmology via the local pathway
Routine ophthalmology
Suspected cataract (functional impairment), suspected chronic glaucoma (suspicious disc, repeatable field defect or Goldmann IOP ≥24 mmHg found by optometrist — NICE NG81), stable diabetic retinopathy above background, stable dry AMD
Optometrist (not hospital)
Gradual bilateral blur (refractive error first), mild dry eye, stable mild cataracts, routine glaucoma monitoring (shared care). Saves hospital capacity
Neurology
Optic neuritis (MRI + MS assessment), suspected pituitary lesion (bitemporal hemianopia), myasthenia gravis (fatigable ptosis + diplopia)

NICE NG82: refer suspected wet AMD urgently to a macula service (normally within 1 working day) and offer anti-VEGF as soon as possible, within 14 days of referral where indicated — delay risks permanent central vision loss. Optometrist-led triage pathways (primary eyecare acute referral services, PEARS) are available in most NHS areas and can see urgent eye problems promptly, freeing A&E and ophthalmology capacity for genuine emergencies.

7
Treat

Immediate & Primary Care Treatment

GCA (suspected)
Glucocorticoid per local GCA pathway Start immediately
Do not wait for ESR/CRP. The oral prednisolone dose (NICE material describes 40–60 mg initially) and whether IV methylprednisolone is needed follow the local GCA pathway, with same-day ophthalmology/rheumatology assessment. Consider glucose, BP, infection risk, bone protection by fracture risk and local steroid guidance, and gastric protection only when indicated. Tapering is specialist-guided
Dry eye
Hypromellose 0.3% drops First-line
4–6× daily, or Carbomer gel (Viscotears) for night-time. Preservative-free if frequent use, ocular-surface disease or preservative sensitivity. Omega-3 evidence is inconsistent — not routine. Recurrent or unexplained unilateral symptoms, photophobia, reduced vision or corneal staining → optometry/ophthalmology
Vascular CV risk reduction
Risk-factor management After confirmation
Assess and manage hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking and atrial fibrillation according to cardiovascular and stroke guidance. Do not initiate antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy solely on the basis of an unexplained visual disturbance without specialist or stroke-pathway assessment; anticoagulation only if AF or another indication is confirmed. Suspected retinal vein occlusion → prompt ophthalmology referral, then risk-factor management
Acute angle closure (pre-hospital)
Same-day eye casualty + keep upright Emergency
Immediate same-day ophthalmology/eye casualty; 999 if severe systemic illness, collapse, marked vomiting, confusion or cannot transfer safely. Keep the patient sitting upright and comfortable; avoid delay, exertion and pupil dilation. No routine GP pre-hospital acetazolamide, pilocarpine or topical pressure-lowering drops — these are ophthalmology-led (RCOphth acute angle closure pathway). Definitive: laser iridotomy
AcuteIdentify ophthalmic emergency — CRAO/acute angle closure/GCA: same-day eye emergency (999 only if criteria met). Chemical injury: irrigate immediately and continue during transfer. Time is vision
Primary careCardiovascular risk factor control — BP, HbA1c, lipids, smoking and AF per cardiovascular, stroke and diabetes guidance and individual targets; must not delay eye referral
ChronicMonitoring and specialist-initiated treatment — anti-VEGF (wet AMD), laser photocoagulation (retinopathy), trabeculectomy (glaucoma) — all specialist. GP ensures systemic risk factors controlled

In GCA, untreated patients risk visual loss in the fellow eye — start glucocorticoids per the local pathway before ultrasound/biopsy. In diabetic eye disease, optimise HbA1c and BP to individual targets alongside, never instead of, eye referral. Intravitreal anti-VEGF preserves vision in wet AMD — which is why anti-VEGF treatment for late AMD (wet active) is recommended in NICE NG82 (2018).

8
Lifestyle

Eye Health & Protective Interventions

Smoking cessation Smoking cessation reduces risk of AMD, cataract and vascular disease. Refer to stop-smoking service.
Glycaemic control (DM) Optimise diabetes control according to the patient's individualised diabetes targets. This must not delay eye referral.
Blood pressure control Optimise blood pressure according to the patient's diabetes/cardiovascular targets.
UV protection UV sunglasses and hat reduce cataract risk. UV-B most harmful. Polarised lenses more comfortable.
Diet — carotenoids A diet rich in leafy greens (kale, spinach) is reasonable general advice. AREDS2 supplements should only be considered following ophthalmology advice for the relevant stage of AMD.
Screen breaks / 20-20-20 rule Every 20 min, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. Reduces digital eye strain and accommodative spasm. Reduces dry eye from reduced blink rate.
Diabetic eye screening adherence At every diabetes consultation, confirm the screening appointment is booked. Unscreened patients are the highest risk for preventable blindness.
Home monitoring (AMD) For patients with known AMD (or where advised by their eye-care professional): self-monitor, e.g. with an Amsler grid, and report new blurred/grey patches, distortion or objects appearing smaller — urgent macula service referral, normally within 1 working day. Self-monitoring helps patients notice changes but does not replace regular eye-care review.

AREDS2-formula supplements are considered only on ophthalmology advice for the relevant stage of AMD. Diabetic eye screening is a key preventive intervention in diabetes — people who do not attend screening are at higher risk of preventable sight loss.

9
Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

GCA — steroid taper
Specialist-guided and individualised — follow the rheumatology/local GCA pathway plan rather than a fixed GP taper protocol. Report symptoms of relapse promptly to the specialist team
GCA — monitoring
BP, weight, glucose and infection risk; bone protection according to fracture risk and local steroid guidance; gastric protection only when clinically indicated. Ophthalmology review as directed by the specialist (not routinely annual)
Post-retinal detachment repair
Ophthalmology follow-up. Driving advice as given by the ophthalmologist (DVLA visual standards). Advise same-day eye emergency review for new flashes, floaters or field loss in either eye
AMD monitoring
Wet AMD: ophthalmology manages anti-VEGF frequency. Dry AMD: regular eye-care (optometry) review; discuss self-monitoring and report new distortion or central blur urgently. AREDS2 supplements only on ophthalmology advice
Diabetic retinopathy
NDESP (annual, or 2-yearly if low risk). HbA1c/BP/lipids at every GP review. Referral follows the grading and pathway recorded by the screening service. Pregnancy: follow the national diabetic eye screening and diabetes-in-pregnancy pathways
Hydroxychloroquine monitoring
Retinal monitoring is via the commissioned hospital protocol (OCT, fundus autofluorescence, visual fields as appropriate) — not routine annual optometrist review. RCOphth / Greater Manchester shared care: annual monitoring after 5 years, or after 1 year if risk factors (tamoxifen, eGFR <60, dose >5 mg/kg/day). Record at baseline: dose in mg/kg/day by actual body weight, renal function, tamoxifen use, macular disease, treatment duration and whether a baseline retinal assessment is done. If toxicity is detected, do not stop in primary care — the prescribing specialist and ophthalmologist discuss systemic benefit versus visual risk
Safety-net emergency
Same-day eye emergency care for any sudden visual loss in one or both eyes, a curtain/shadow, or an acute painful red eye with cloudy vision; call 999 for visual loss with stroke symptoms, confusion, collapse or severe illness, or sudden binocular field loss with neurological symptoms
Safety-net same-day
New floaters + flashes, new distortion or central blur in known AMD (urgent macula service, normally within 1 working day), any new RAPD, unexplained progressive visual loss over days
Low vision registration
Ask ophthalmology or the local sensory-impairment service about certification when sight loss is functionally significant — certification depends on acuity, field loss, functional impact and the better-seeing eye, and the CVI is usually completed by the ophthalmologist. Referral to low-vision rehabilitation should not wait until formal certification thresholds are reached. Access to benefits, RNIB, rehabilitation

GCA steroid tapers are prolonged, specialist-led and individualised. Tocilizumab (IL-6 inhibitor) is recommended in NICE TA518 (2018) for relapsing or refractory GCA (stopped after at most 1 year of treatment), allowing faster steroid taper and reducing cumulative steroid-related harm. AMD same-day emergency services (Macular Society) exist in most NHS regions — patients should have the service number. Pregnancy dramatically worsens diabetic retinopathy — women planning pregnancy should have pre-conception ophthalmology review and tightened glycaemic control.
