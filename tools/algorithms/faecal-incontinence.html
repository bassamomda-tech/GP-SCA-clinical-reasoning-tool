1
Safety

Red Flags — Malignancy, Spinal Cord & Acute Causes

New faecal incontinence with neurological symptoms, rectal bleeding, weight loss, anaemia, change in bowel habit or a rectal mass needs prompt assessment for spinal, colorectal or anal disease. Cauda equina syndrome causes faecal and urinary incontinence as a surgical emergency. FI alone, without compatible neurological signs, is not diagnostic of cauda equina and does not mean every new episode needs emergency MRI; FI alone is not a NICE indication for FIT.

Bilateral leg weakness + faecal/urinary incontinence + saddle anaesthesia Cauda equina syndrome — prolapsed lumbar disc or epidural mass compressing sacral nerve roots → emergency spinal assessment and urgent MRI — do not delay transfer for primary-care tests. Urgent emergency assessment for new urinary retention or loss of urinary sensation, saddle sensory disturbance, reduced anal tone, bilateral or progressive neurological deficit, or severe back/radicular pain with new bladder or bowel dysfunction — major red flags (not pathognomonic); surgical timing follows the spinal pathway. FI alone, without compatible neurological signs, is not diagnostic.
Rectal bleeding + change in bowel habit + weight loss + FI Colorectal cancer — rectal tumour can destroy the internal anal sphincter (IAS) or cause false urgency from rectal filling. Offer FIT for NG12 symptom groups — change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, abdominal mass, unexplained rectal bleeding, abdominal pain or weight loss in the relevant age groups, and anaemia at 60+ even without iron deficiency — and refer on a suspected colorectal cancer pathway if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026); NICE HTG690). FI alone is not a NICE indication for FIT. An unexplained rectal mass bypasses FIT (colorectal pathway); an unexplained anal mass or ulceration → suspected anal cancer referral without FIT. FIT is a triage tool and not an exclusion test: if symptoms persist or clinical concern remains high, refer despite a FIT below threshold or no returned sample.
New FI after pelvic radiotherapy New bleeding, mucus, urgency or FI needs assessment for radiation proctopathy, recurrent malignancy, strictures or other structural disease, and IBD or alternative causes — urgency, bleeding, mucus, tenesmus. Refer via gastroenterology/oncology or the specialist continence pathway; specialist treatments are not GP-initiated.
New FI + progressive neurological symptoms Spinal cord disease (MS, spinal tumour, transverse myelitis, syringomyelia), peripheral neuropathy (diabetic autonomic neuropathy — pudendal neuropathy causing sphincter denervation). Neurology referral. MRI spine. FI as a presenting symptom of MS is common and often delayed in diagnosis.
Acute onset FI in elderly patient after stroke Post-stroke faecal incontinence — sphincter control disrupted at cortical/subcortical level. Common and often overlooked — assess mobility, cognition, toilet access, constipation/loading and medicines. Bowel retraining programme via stroke rehabilitation team. Do not attribute to "just old age."
FI + perianal sepsis / fistula High anal fistula, rectovaginal fistula, or perianal abscess can destroy sphincter integrity. A painful fluctuant abscess needs urgent surgical review and drainage — antibiotics alone are inadequate for most drainable abscesses; 999 only for sepsis, shock, rapidly progressive infection or Fournier's gangrene. Suspected fistula → colorectal referral; do not probe a suspected fistula in primary care. Crohn's disease causes complex perianal fistulas — gastroenterology + colorectal assessment jointly.
Same-day / urgent colorectal assessment Rectal or anal mass · perianal abscess with systemic illness · Fournier's gangrene · significant rectal bleeding with instability · suspected complete bowel obstruction · severe abdominal pain, vomiting and distension with suspected impaction or obstruction.
Cauda equina syndrome is the most time-critical diagnosis in faecal incontinence — the sacral nerve roots (S2–S4) control both the external anal sphincter (EAS) and the external urethral sphincter, and compression by a prolapsed lumbar disc (most commonly L4/L5 or L5/S1) causes simultaneous faecal and urinary incontinence with perineal sensory loss. Delay risks permanent sphincter dysfunction, so assessment and MRI are emergencies. GPs must recognise that the classic "saddle anaesthesia" description (numbness in the area that would contact a bicycle saddle) is a late sign — earlier features include bilateral sciatica, urinary hesitancy, reduced urinary stream, and constipation. Any patient with low back pain and new bowel or bladder symptoms must have cauda equina excluded urgently. Faecal incontinence is one of the most under-reported and under-treated conditions in primary care — it is common, particularly in care home residents. The majority of sufferers never discuss it with their GP due to shame, embarrassment, and the misconception that "nothing can be done." NICE CG49 (Faecal Incontinence, 2007) emphasises that GPs should proactively ask about FI in high-risk populations (multiparous women, elderly, IBD patients, post-pelvic radiotherapy patients, diabetics) rather than waiting for patients to volunteer the symptom.

Diagnose

Characterise the Incontinence

Describe incontinence as passive vs urge and look for contributing mechanisms — these are symptom descriptions, not reliable indicators of one isolated sphincter lesion, and FI is commonly multifactorial. Ask specifically and sensitively — most patients will not volunteer details without direct questioning.

Baseline history (NICE CG49)
Frequency and duration of leakage · solid, liquid or flatus leakage · passive vs urge symptoms · nocturnal leakage · ability to defer defecation · incomplete evacuation · post-defecation smearing · stool frequency and Bristol type · obstetric history (OASI, forceps or vacuum delivery, birthweight) · previous anal, colorectal or pelvic surgery · pelvic radiotherapy · IBD, IBS, diarrhoea, constipation, neurological disease · medication, diet, fluid and toilet access · functional mobility and cognition (cognitive assessment when appropriate) · effect on daily life, work, sex, travel and psychological wellbeing
Passive FI
Leakage without awareness — patient does not feel the urge before leaking. Often suggests internal anal sphincter (IAS) dysfunction, neuropathy or prolapse — but FI is commonly multifactorial. IAS maintains resting tone and provides the "unconscious" continence mechanism. Passive FI occurs at rest, with position changes, or on bending. Common in obstetric injury (3rd/4th degree tear damaging IAS), post-haemorrhoidectomy, ageing.
Urge FI
Leakage despite urgent desire to defecate — patient feels the urge but cannot reach the toilet in time. Often suggests external anal sphincter (EAS) weakness, rectal hypersensitivity or reduced compliance — avoid single-cause attribution (CG49). EAS provides the voluntary "squeeze" continence. Common in obstetric injury (EAS damage), post-sphincterectomy, IBD (rectal inflammation reduces compliance), IBS-D, radiation proctitis.
Overflow / pseudo-incontinence
Liquid stool bypassing a faecal impaction and leaking passively — "overflow incontinence." Common in elderly, constipated, or cognitively impaired patients. Rectum full of hard stool on PR examination — but a normal DRE does not fully exclude more proximal loading. Use the term cautiously: liquid leakage also occurs with diarrhoea, sphincter dysfunction or incomplete evacuation. Treat the underlying loading — rectal treatment first (glycerol or bisacodyl suppositories, or an appropriate enema, per tolerance and local protocol); oral macrogol if rectal measures fail or are inappropriate and obstruction is excluded. Do NOT treat with antidiarrhoeal agents (worsens impaction).
Bowel diary
NICE CG49 recommends a bowel diary before first assessment and at review. Record: frequency of defecation, stool consistency (Bristol Stool Scale), urgency, incontinence episodes, diet and fluid intake, leakage context (urge/passive/with flatus). Consider a 3–7-day diary tailored to the patient. Identifies patterns, quantifies severity, and monitors treatment response.
Validated scoring — St Mark's score
St Mark's (Vaizey) Incontinence Score: scores 0–24 across frequency of solid, liquid, and gas leakage; pad wearing; lifestyle alteration; urgency; and need for antidiarrhoeals. Use where locally adopted to quantify severity and track response — referral depends on persistence, impact, cause, red flags and failed initial management, not a score threshold. Widely used in UK colorectal practice.
The passive/urge distinction can suggest — but not prove — the mechanism, and FI is commonly multifactorial: passive FI (IAS dysfunction) responds to bulking agents (to increase stool consistency and reduce liquid leakage), rectal irrigation, and in severe cases sphincter augmentation (sacral nerve stimulation or injectable bulking agents around the IAS). Urge FI (EAS weakness or rectal hypersensitivity) responds to pelvic floor exercises, biofeedback, antidiarrhoeal agents (loperamide), and in severe cases sphincter repair or sacral nerve stimulation. Many patients have mixed passive and urge FI, particularly after obstetric sphincter injury where both the IAS and EAS are damaged. The Bristol Stool Scale is the universal stool consistency tool used in the UK — Types 1–2 (hard lumps/sausage) = constipation risk; Types 3–4 (normal formed) = target; Types 5–7 (loose/liquid) = diarrhoea risk/FI trigger. Aim for a stool consistency that is predictable and manageable for the individual; Bristol types 3–4 are often useful targets but are not mandatory. Overflow incontinence is the single most important and most commonly missed diagnosis in FI in elderly patients — examine (including DRE where tolerated) any elderly or constipated patient with "diarrhoea and incontinence" to exclude faecal impaction. Treating apparent diarrhoea with loperamide in a patient who actually has faecal loading can worsen impaction and risk obstruction.

Diagnose

Causes & Risk Factor Assessment

Obstetric sphincter injury
Previous obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) is an important risk factor. 3rd degree (partial or complete EAS tear) and 4th degree (EAS + IAS + rectal mucosa) tears — more likely after instrumental delivery. A previous tear does not prove that current FI is caused by an ongoing sphincter defect; symptoms may occur years later and be multifactorial. Full obstetric history: mode of delivery (forceps, vacuum), number of vaginal deliveries, birthweight, any 3rd/4th degree repair. Symptomatic FI after OASI → specialist continence/colorectal pathway; endoanal ultrasound and anorectal physiology are specialist tests; sphincteroplasty is not automatically indicated and long-term benefit can be variable.
Pelvic floor / rectal prolapse
Full thickness rectal prolapse (rectum prolapses through anus — visible reduction of anorectal mucosa) — causes passive FI from gaping sphincter. Anterior rectocele (posterior vaginal wall weakness — faeces trapped in hernia, incomplete evacuation, soiling). Internal rectal intussusception. Mucosal prolapse. Third-degree haemorrhoids. These are not interchangeable diagnoses and may need different pathways. Refer colorectal surgery for symptomatic rectal prolapse or significant rectocele.
IBD and diarrhoeal conditions
Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's colitis cause urgency FI from rectal inflammation, reduced compliance, and urgency. IBS-D: liquid stool + urgency. Chronic diarrhoea of any cause (post-infectious, bile acid malabsorption, microscopic colitis — especially in women on NSAIDs/PPIs/SSRIs) can overwhelm a marginally competent sphincter.
Neurological causes
Diabetic autonomic/pudendal neuropathy. MS (sphincter dyssynergia). Spinal cord injury. Stroke. Parkinson's disease (autonomic dysfunction + constipation → overflow). Multiple system atrophy. Dementia (reduced cortical inhibition of defecation).
Iatrogenic / surgical
Post-haemorrhoidectomy (IAS damage). Post-lateral internal sphincterotomy (for anal fissure — IAS divided, can impair resting tone). Post-anterior resection (partial rectal excision — reduced rectal reservoir, urgency, clustering of defecation — anterior resection syndrome). Post-anal stretch (now abandoned — caused widespread IAS damage).
Drug-induced / dietary
Drugs causing diarrhoea → FI: laxatives (excess), metformin, SSRIs, NSAIDs (microscopic colitis), antibiotics (C. diff, post-antibiotic diarrhoea), magnesium-containing antacids. Dietary: excess caffeine, alcohol, artificial sweeteners (sorbitol, xylitol), spicy food. Review medicines early.
Obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) is an important risk factor for faecal incontinence, and the temporal gap between the injury and symptom onset is one reason it is missed — women who sustained a 3rd or 4th degree tear at age 25 may not develop symptomatic FI until their 40s or 50s as the ageing pelvic floor loses the remaining sphincter reserve. GPs should specifically ask about obstetric history and 3rd/4th degree tear documentation in any woman presenting with FI, regardless of how long ago the deliveries were. Many women were never told they sustained a significant sphincter injury. Metformin-induced loose stools can tip a marginal sphincter into FI; if metformin is contributing, a modified-release switch can be considered individually. The lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS) for anal fissure carries a well-documented risk of passive FI — this is part of informed consent before LIS. Post-LIS patients presenting with FI should be referred to colorectal surgery for physiological assessment (anal manometry) and consideration of sphincter repair.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Abdominal examination
Faecal loading (hard mass in LIF — descending colon loaded — overflow risk). Distension. Organomegaly. Any abdominal mass → FIT (NG12), then a colorectal suspected cancer pathway referral if ≥10 µg Hb/g — or despite a lower or unreturned FIT if concern persists.
Perianal inspection
Skin excoriation / dermatitis (chronic FI). Soiling/moisture (passive leakage). Gaping anus at rest (IAS weakness — should be closed at rest). Rectal prolapse (ask patient to strain/bear down — full thickness prolapse protrudes). Perianal scarring (obstetric injury, previous surgery). Skin tags. External haemorrhoids. Fistula openings.
Digital rectal examination (DRE)
Where tolerated and appropriate, with explanation, consent and chaperone — defer in severe fissure pain, suspected abscess or significant trauma (arrange specialist examination if essential). Inspect the anus in all where possible. Resting tone (IAS): normal = firm ring of resistance. Reduced resting tone = IAS weakness (obstetric, post-haemorrhoidectomy, autonomic neuropathy). Squeeze pressure (EAS): ask patient to squeeze — reduced squeeze = EAS weakness. Faecal loading (hard impaction — overflow). Rectal mass or tenderness. Anteroposterior scar (obstetric injury — anterior sphincter defect, 12 o'clock position). A normal DRE does not fully exclude more proximal faecal loading.
General assessment
General examination; cognition, mobility and toilet access when relevant (NICE CG49).
Investigations
Directed by the history and examination — there is no routine laboratory panel for uncomplicated FI. Consider: FBC (bleeding, anaemia, weight loss or suspected inflammatory disease) · CRP (suspected inflammatory or infective disease) · HbA1c (diabetes suspected or review overdue) · TSH (only if thyroid disease clinically suspected) · Coeliac serology (chronic diarrhoea, malabsorption, IDA) · Faecal calprotectin (chronic diarrhoea raising concern about IBD, particularly in younger adults) · Stool microbiology / C. difficile (history suggests infection or antibiotic-associated diarrhoea) · FIT per NG12 symptoms — no routine colonoscopy for every patient with FI · Anorectal physiology (anal manometry + endoanal USS — specialist: measures sphincter pressure and identifies sphincter defects)
Safeguarding
FI in a patient who cannot report symptoms (severe dementia, learning disability) — ensure adequate continence care is in place. Unexplained FI in a child or vulnerable adult raises the possibility of anal abuse — document carefully, discuss with safeguarding per local policy, document objectively, avoid leading questions, and refer to paediatric, adult-safeguarding or sexual-assault services as appropriate. Unexplained anal injury, bleeding, pain, bruising or FI in a child, dependent adult or vulnerable person should prompt consideration of safeguarding, particularly where the history is inconsistent, injuries are unexplained or there are additional concerns — follow local safeguarding procedures. FI in severe dementia or learning disability should also prompt assessment of constipation and overflow, mobility and toilet access, medication, cognitive and behavioural factors, and the adequacy of care support.
Anorectal physiology studies (anal manometry + endoanal ultrasound) are the specialist investigations that define sphincter anatomy and function — they are performed by colorectal physiologists in tertiary colorectal centres and are the prerequisite for surgical intervention (sphincter repair, sacral nerve stimulation). Anal manometry measures resting pressure (IAS function) and squeeze pressure (EAS function) — normal resting pressure 40–70 mmHg, squeeze pressure 80–180 mmHg. Reduced resting pressure = IAS damage; reduced squeeze = EAS damage. Endoanal ultrasound (360° sonography of the anal canal) visualises the sphincter ring and identifies defects — an anterior defect in a multiparous woman strongly suggests unrepaired obstetric injury. Faecal calprotectin helps separate IBD from IBS in diarrhoea-predominant FI — interpret with the local pathway cut-offs. It avoids unnecessary colonoscopy in the many patients whose FI is driven by IBS-D rather than inflammatory bowel disease. Check TSH when thyroid disease is clinically suspected.

Rectal irrigation or anal inserts only after specialist continence assessment (cognition, dexterity, anatomy, prolapse, obstruction risk, preference). Community-based conservative management coordination.
Sacral nerve stimulation (SNS — also called sacral neuromodulation) is the most effective interventional treatment for FI in patients with intact but dysfunctional sphincters — it works by modulating the sacral nerve roots (S3) with a permanent implanted electrode (similar to a cardiac pacemaker), improving sphincter tone, rectal sensation, and rectal compliance simultaneously. NICE HTG61 (Sacral nerve stimulation for faecal incontinence — this guidance migrated IPG99; the recommendations remain unchanged) supports SNS for FI with normal arrangements for consent, audit and clinical governance, performed in specialist units, and NICE CG49 (2007) places it after failed conservative management. It helps many selected patients. It is also effective for urge FI where the sphincter is structurally intact but functionally weak (e.g., pudendal neuropathy). Biofeedback from a specialist pelvic floor physiotherapist is the first-line intervention recommended by NICE CG49 for FI not responding to basic conservative measures — delivered by specialists, with benefit depending on selection and training. The biofeedback session uses anorectal manometry probes that display real-time sphincter squeeze pressure on a screen, allowing the patient to learn to coordinate and strengthen their sphincter contraction. Typical course: 6–8 sessions over 3 months. The lack of access to NHS specialist pelvic floor physiotherapy in many areas is a significant barrier — Refer early to the continence service when symptoms are severe or quality of life is badly affected, and when initial management fails.

Treat

Treatment Ladder

Step 1
Stool consistencyAim for a stool consistency that is predictable and manageable for the individual (Bristol 3–4 is often useful but not mandatory). If loose (Types 5–7): loperamide 0.5–16 mg/day (CG49), started low and titrated to stool consistency and lifestyle. Do not use in hard or infrequent stools, suspected faecal impaction or bowel obstruction, acute undiagnosed diarrhoea, bloody diarrhoea or suspected invasive infection, severe or acute ulcerative colitis, suspected toxic megacolon, or significant abdominal distension or ileus. Prescribe and monitor according to the current product information and local formulary — higher or prolonged doses require particular caution because of cardiac toxicity associated with loperamide misuse or excessive dosing. If hard (Types 1–2) or overflow: a suitable laxative (e.g. macrogol) for constipation; if impacted, disimpact first (exclude obstruction; phosphate enema only if no renal impairment, heart failure, frailty, electrolyte disturbance or rectal mucosal disease). Do NOT give loperamide for overflow FI.
Step 2
Pelvic floor exercisesPelvic-floor and anal-sphincter exercises — encourage regular exercises if appropriate (e.g. squeeze as if stopping wind, hold, then relax — an example, not a universal prescription), but refer for a patient-specific programme based on digital assessment, with progress monitored by an appropriately trained professional, where symptoms persist, sphincter weakness is suspected, or the patient cannot identify the correct muscles (NICE CG49). Biofeedback is a specialist continence intervention. Exercises may not help in neurological bowel dysfunction, severe cognitive impairment or major structural disruption — these groups may need specialist neurological bowel management or alternative strategies. Toilet positioning: a sitting or squatting position where possible (lean forward, footstool) may aid evacuation — not a proven treatment.
Step 3
Scheduled defecationBowel retraining — attempt defecation at the same time each day (30 min after breakfast — gastrocolic reflex is strongest). Use glycerol suppository if needed to trigger predictable defecation and reduce unexpected episodes. Transanal irrigation for selected neurogenic FI or incomplete evacuation — via the specialist continence service after assessment.
Step 4
SpecialistSpecialist decisions — not GP-initiated. Sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) — NICE HTG61 (migrated IPG99; recommendations unchanged), performed in specialist units with appropriate consent, audit and governance. Injectable bulking agents — specialist procedures with limited and uncertain evidence; NICE IPG210 recommends special arrangements for consent, audit or research, and they should only be performed in specialist units. Sphincteroplasty (surgical repair of an anterior sphincter defect — not automatically indicated; long-term benefit can be variable). Antegrade continence enema (ACE — caecostomy/appendicostomy for irrigation — for complex neurogenic FI). Stoma as last resort.
Loperamide's dual mechanism is important to understand — it is both an antidiarrhoeal (reduces gut motility via opioid receptor agonism in the gut wall) and an anal sphincter tonic agent (directly increases IAS resting tone via opioid receptors in the sphincter). This sphincter-tonic effect is independent of its antidiarrhoeal effect and is the reason loperamide is effective even in patients with formed stools who have IAS weakness — it supplements the resting tone of a weakened sphincter. Start low and titrate — the goal is a predictable, manageable stool (often Type 3–4), not constipation. A useful patient instruction is "take 2 mg half an hour before activities or situations where you most fear incontinence" (before going out, before a social event), which provides targeted symptom relief. Retrograde colonic irrigation (Peristeen, Qufora) is significantly underused in primary care — it helps selected neurogenic FI (spinal cord injury, MS, spina bifida) and some non-neurogenic incomplete evacuation, through specialist services. It works by flushing the left colon and rectum with warm water through a rectal catheter, producing a predictable complete evacuation and leaving the rectum empty for 24–48 hours. Training by a specialist nurse takes 3–4 sessions. NHS provision varies — many continence nurse specialists offer this service.

Treat

Overflow Incontinence & Specific Causes

Overflow (faecal impaction)
Rectal treatment first for acute severe faecal loading — glycerol or bisacodyl suppositories, or an appropriate enema, according to tolerance and local protocol (before a phosphate enema review hydration, renal function, heart failure, frailty and electrolyte risk). If rectal measures fail or are inappropriate, oral macrogol after bowel obstruction has been excluded, per the product information and local formulary, with adequate hydration. Once disimpacted: maintenance laxative per product information and local formulary to prevent recurrence + bowel habit diary. Do NOT give antidiarrhoeals — worsens impaction and bowel obstruction risk.
Drug-induced diarrhoea causing FI
Metformin — if contributing, consider modified-release (individualise). Excess laxatives — reduce or change. SSRI-associated diarrhoea — review. Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea — consider C. difficile; no routine probiotics. Suspected microscopic colitis — review culprit drugs and refer for colonoscopy with biopsies; budesonide only after diagnosis via gastroenterology.
IBD-related urgency FI
Active UC / Crohn's — treat underlying inflammation (5-ASA, steroids, biologics — gastroenterology). Loperamide may be considered cautiously for persistent loose stools in established IBD when severe active colitis, infection, obstruction and toxic megacolon have been excluded. Avoid in acute severe colitis or significant systemic illness and seek gastroenterology advice where appropriate. Biofeedback for FI when disease in remission.
Diabetic neuropathic FI
Optimise diabetes care with individualised targets (frailty, hypoglycaemia risk). Loperamide for loose stools once loading excluded. No routine amitriptyline (constipation/overflow, retention, falls, cognition) — any neuromodulator is specialist-led. Biofeedback for sphincter strengthening. SNS referral if refractory.
Post-radiotherapy FI
Assess first for radiation proctopathy, recurrent malignancy, strictures or other structural disease, and IBD or alternative causes. Sucralfate enemas, hyperbaric oxygen, argon plasma coagulation and SNS are specialist options, not GP-initiated.
If metformin is contributing to loose stools, a modified-release formulation may help. The mechanism is that metformin IR is absorbed rapidly in the proximal small bowel, leaving a high concentration of drug in the distal small bowel and colon where it causes osmotic diarrhoea; modified-release metformin is often better tolerated; individualise the decision. Microscopic colitis (lymphocytic and collagenous colitis) is an important and frequently missed cause of watery diarrhoea causing FI in middle-aged and older women — it is strongly associated with PPI use (especially lansoprazole), NSAIDs, SSRIs, and statins. Colonoscopy appears macroscopically normal — the diagnosis requires colonic biopsies. Diagnosis and budesonide treatment are via gastroenterology. GPs should suspect microscopic colitis in any middle-aged woman with watery diarrhoea, incontinence, and a recent medication change to a PPI or NSAID.

Lifestyle

Diet, Bowel Habits & Practical Strategies

Dietary fibre optimisation Do not advise increased fibre in every patient; aim for a predictable, manageable stool consistency (Bristol 3–4 often useful, not mandatory). If loose stools: soluble fibre (e.g. ispaghula) may bulk stool — one intervention at a time, improving stool consistency without causing constipation. Reduce insoluble fibre (bran, wholegrains) if contributing to urgency. If hard stools: increase soluble fibre and fruit/vegetable intake only with adequate fluid and after considering impaction or obstruction. Avoid artificial sweeteners (sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol) — osmotic diarrhoea.
Fluid intake At least 1.5 L/day for people with hard stools or clinical dehydration unless contraindicated (NICE CG49). Do not deliberately restrict fluids because of FI — this can worsen constipation and impaction. Caffeine (coffee, tea, cola) and alcohol can worsen loose stools. Caffeine is a bowel stimulant — reducing it may help. Carbonated drinks worsen flatulence and urgency.
Food trigger diary Identify and reduce personal dietary triggers. Common triggers: caffeine, alcohol, spicy food, onions, lactose (dairy — especially if lactose intolerant), gluten (coeliac), high-fat meals (bile acid release → diarrhoea in bile acid malabsorption). Keep a 7-day food and symptom diary and adjust one dietary factor at a time. Low-FODMAP diet is an optional specialist dietitian-led intervention for IBS-D, not a routine FI treatment.
Toilet posture A sitting or squatting position where possible (NICE CG49) — a footstool to raise the feet may help more complete emptying and reduce post-defecation soiling, but is not a proven treatment. Lean forward with elbows on knees. Avoid straining. Complete evacuation on the first visit reduces the residual stool that causes later passive leakage.
Toilet access and clothing Regular post-meal toileting; improve toilet access (mobility aids, commode, bathroom adaptations) and use easily removed clothing.
Anal plug / body worn device Anal inserts — via a specialist continence service after assessment of dexterity, anatomy and preference; useful for some during activity.
Skin protection Chronic perianal moisture and faecal contamination causes severe excoriation dermatitis. Clean gently after soiling using lukewarm water or a mild, fragrance-free cleanser; avoid vigorous rubbing, scented wipes and harsh soaps. Pat dry and apply a suitable barrier product such as zinc oxide or another locally approved barrier preparation (specific brands per the local continence formulary). Change clothing promptly. Moisture wicking underwear. Cotton underwear preferred. Avoid scented wipes — contact dermatitis worsens skin breakdown.
Psychological support FI causes profound psychological distress — depression, anxiety, social isolation, and avoidance behaviours (not leaving home, restricted diet, restricting fluids). CBT for FI-related anxiety (NHS Talking Therapies referral). Peer support (Bowel and Bladder UK helpline: 0161 214 4591). Validate the impact: "This is common — you are not alone, and there are effective treatments." Normalising the conversation is the first therapeutic step.
Exercise Moderate regular physical activity (30 minutes × 5 days/week) improves gut transit regularity, reduces constipation, and reduces overflow risk. High-impact exercise (running, aerobics) may worsen urgency FI — substitute with lower-impact options (swimming, cycling) if exercise triggers leakage. Consistent pelvic-floor exercises help many patients.
NICE CG49 supports a sitting or squatting position where possible; a footstool is a low-risk measure but not a proven treatment. The aim for FI is that more complete evacuation leaves minimal residual stool in the rectal ampulla, reducing post-defecation passive leakage (the "soiling after passing stool" that many patients with IAS weakness describe). Ispaghula is a useful option for FI with loose stools — as a soluble fibre it absorbs water in the colon, converting liquid stool to a formed consistency (Type 3–4). It works equally well whether the underlying problem is IBS-D, post-infective diarrhoea, or mild IAS weakness — it is inexpensive, safe, and available OTC. The key instruction is to take it with adequate fluid (at least 200 ml per sachet) to prevent it from causing a bolus of dry fibre — without adequate fluid, Fybogel can worsen constipation.

Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Initial review — 4–6 weeks
Bowel diary reviewed. Stool consistency predictable and manageable for the patient? Loperamide dose effective? Pelvic floor exercises commenced (referral to specialist physio made)? Any red flag symptoms emerging? If not improving → review, and refer to the continence service (earlier if severe).
Specialist referral — no fixed wait
Refer after an appropriate initial assessment and trial of initial measures if symptoms persist, are severe, significantly affect quality of life, or if there is a likely structural, obstetric, neurological, postoperative or inflammatory cause — do not wait a fixed 3 months if earlier specialist input is indicated (NICE CG49; QS54). Persistent symptoms after initial management → specialist continence service (integrated pathway: pelvic-floor training, bowel retraining, biofeedback, irrigation; anorectal physiology/endoanal USS are specialist tests). Refer earlier if severe or major impact.
Ongoing monitoring
St Mark's score if locally used to track response. Weight (unintentional weight loss = new pathology). Repeat FBC/CRP if IBD suspected. Diabetes review with individualised targets. Skin integrity review (perianal excoriation).
Care home / frail elderly
Proactive bowel management plan for all care home residents — regular toileting schedule, laxative prescription review (avoid chronic stimulant laxatives unless constipation confirmed), dietary fibre + fluid targets, prompt management of new FI episodes. Continence care as a dignity issue — FI in care homes is frequently inadequately managed.
999 / same-day
New bilateral leg weakness, saddle sensory disturbance or urinary retention with incontinence → suspected cauda equina → emergency assessment · Suspected bowel obstruction from faecal impaction (severe abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, absolute constipation) → emergency assessment · Shock or sepsis → 999 · Perianal Fournier's gangrene (crepitus + rapidly spreading perianal necrosis) → 999
Same-day GP
New rectal bleeding developing during conservative management · Rapid weight loss identified at review · Worsening neurological symptoms (new leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia) · Perianal abscess developing (fluctuant, tender — urgent surgical review for drainage, not antibiotics alone) · Persistent unexplained symptoms despite a FIT below threshold → refer
Persisting with conservative care alone rarely helps when it has plateaued — patients plateau with lifestyle measures and pharmacotherapy alone if they have structural sphincter defects, neuropathy, or rectal compliance issues that require physiological assessment. The role of the GP is to initiate conservative management, exclude red flags, and refer to the continence service when improvement is insufficient (earlier if severe). The single most important quality indicator for FI management in primary care is asking the question — NICE QS54 (quality standard: Faecal incontinence in adults, 2014) identifies the proactive inquiry about FI in high-risk populations (multiparous women, elderly, post-pelvic radiotherapy, IBD, diabetes, pelvic surgery) as a key indicator. FI is among the most undertreated conditions in primary care, and the treatment gap is predominantly due to non-disclosure (patients do not volunteer the symptom) and non-asking (GPs do not ask). Incorporating a brief screening question ("Do you ever have difficulty controlling your bowels or have accidental leakage?") into annual reviews for high-risk patients would identify the majority of undiagnosed FI and initiate appropriate management years earlier.
