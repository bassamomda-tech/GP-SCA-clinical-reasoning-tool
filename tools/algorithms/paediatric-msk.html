1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude septic arthritis, malignancy, NAI

Urgent exclusion of limb-threatening and life-threatening conditions in any child with MSK symptoms.
Acute limp + fever A child with fever who is acutely unwell, unable to weight-bear, has a hot swollen joint, limb swelling, non-use of an extremity, severe pain on passive movement or suspected sepsis → Same-day hospital assessment; 999 if physiological instability, altered consciousness, shock, severe respiratory compromise or rapid deterioration (NICE NG254). Consider septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, discitis, pyomyositis or other infection. Do not rely on a low-grade fever, normal inflammatory markers, a normal X-ray or the child being briefly able to walk.
Non-accidental injury features Bruising in a non-mobile child, multiple bruises in protected areas, inconsistent history, delayed presentation, patterned injuries or injuries incompatible with developmental stage (bruising in a mobile child may be accidental; absence of an injury history does not prove abuse; do not rely on a single feature) → Same-day follow local safeguarding procedures and contact the designated safeguarding professional or children's social care where indicated
Night pain / bone pain Persistent or unexplained night pain, especially if focal, progressive or with swelling, limp, weight loss, pallor, bruising or fatigue → Urgent full examination; very urgent FBC when leukaemia is possible; X-ray when bone pain or swelling is present (NG12: very urgent direct-access X-ray within 48 hours for unexplained bone swelling or pain); discuss urgently with paediatrics if concern persists. Normal FBC or inflammatory markers do not exclude malignancy (leukaemia, bone tumour — NICE NG12, updated April 2026)
Systemically unwell Unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → Immediate specialist assessment (leukaemia — NICE NG12, updated April 2026). Unexplained fever, pallor, persistent fatigue, recurrent/persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, persistent unexplained bone pain, bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC within 48 hours and urgent paediatrics (malignancy, systemic JIA)
Adolescent hip pain Any adolescent (often around 10–16 years, not absolute) with hip, groin, thigh or referred knee pain, limp or reduced hip internal rotation — consider SUFE until excluded, including lean adolescents and those with endocrine or renal disease or previous radiotherapy → keep non-weight-bearing; Same-day orthopaedic assessment and imaging (SUFE — slipped upper femoral epiphysis). Do not encourage walking to imaging or repeat provocative tests.
Age <3 years with limp Low threshold — limp in a child under 3 requires careful Same-day assessment if unexplained, persistent, associated with fever, reduced movement, non-weight-bearing, abnormal examination, safeguarding concern or diagnostic uncertainty (DDH, septic arthritis, toddler fracture, malignancy). A well child with a clearly observed minor injury, normal examination and function, and reliable follow-up may be managed per local paediatric/urgent-care pathways. Do not diagnose growing pains in this age group.
Persistent limp Not improving as expected, worsening, or inability to weight-bear → Reassess — urgency of X-ray and paediatric review depends on examination, age, trauma, systemic symptoms and weight-bearing (fracture, Perthes, malignancy)
Red/hot/swollen single joint Monoarthritis with effusion → Same-day (septic arthritis, reactive arthritis, haemarthrosis)
Back pain in a young child Warrants assessment; imaging depends on persistent or severe symptoms, night pain, neurological signs, fever/systemic symptoms, trauma, spinal deformity, or known malignancy/infection risk → Paediatric review (discitis, malignancy, spondylolisthesis)
Bilateral hip pain Bilateral Perthes (usually unilateral but may be bilateral), systemic disease → Specialist imaging + paediatric/orthopaedic assessment; urgency depends on clinical status

Septic arthritis is a surgical emergency — delayed treatment (>24 hours) leads to irreversible joint destruction, growth plate damage, and chronic disability. Kocher/Caird-type criteria (fever, refusal to weight-bear, raised WCC, ESR, CRP) are supportive risk tools for septic arthritis of the hip, not diagnostic tests — they have limitations in different populations, early disease and infections such as Kingella kingae. A child with clinical concern requires urgent paediatric/orthopaedic assessment regardless of initial blood results.

Slipped upper femoral epiphysis (SUFE) requires urgent surgical pinning to prevent avascular necrosis of femoral head. Presents as hip, groin, thigh or knee pain; obesity is an important risk factor but lean adolescents can also develop SUFE. Keep non-weight-bearing while awaiting same-day orthopaedic assessment. Delay = slip progression = lifelong hip arthritis.

Childhood malignancy (leukaemia, neuroblastoma, osteosarcoma) can present with vague MSK symptoms. Night pain can occur with benign conditions, but persistent or unexplained night pain — especially when focal, progressive or with swelling, limp, weight loss, pallor, bruising or fatigue — should prompt urgent assessment. Use the full clinical picture rather than an automatic test rule. Missing malignancy has catastrophic consequences.

2
Diagnose

History — GALS approach and developmental context

Take detailed history focusing on onset, pattern, functional impact. Ask about play, sport, school attendance.
Pain characteristics
Onset: Acute (<24hrs) vs gradual. Pattern: Worse morning (inflammatory) vs evening (mechanical). Night pain: Prompts reassessment; red flag if persistent, focal, progressive or with systemic features. Severity: Impact on function (play, sport, stairs, dressing).
Limp pattern
Antalgic: Shortened stance phase (painful). Trendelenburg: Hip abductor weakness (DDH, Perthes). High-stepping: Foot drop (neurological). Circumduction: Hip/knee stiffness.
Systemic features
Fever, rash, weight loss, fatigue, poor appetite, night sweats, pallor. Presence = infection, malignancy, systemic JIA. Absence = mechanical or benign.
Trauma history
Witnessed fall, mechanism, immediate vs delayed pain. Exclude NAI if history inconsistent or multiple injuries.
Developmental stage
<3 years: Pre-verbal, examine carefully (septic arthritis, DDH, NAI common). About 3-10 years: Transient synovitis; Perthes (commonly 4–10, may occur outside this range). Adolescents (often 10-16, not absolute): SUFE, Osgood-Schlatter, anterior knee pain.
Family history
Juvenile arthritis, autoimmune disease (JIA risk ↑), hypermobility syndromes, sickle cell disease (avascular necrosis).
Activity level
Sports, dancing, gymnastics (overuse injuries). Sudden increase in activity (Sever's disease, stress fractures). Sedentary (obesity → SUFE, Blount's disease).

Age is the strongest diagnostic clue — different conditions cluster at different developmental stages. Toddler fractures (<3 years), Perthes (commonly 4-10 years), SUFE (early adolescence, often around 10-16 years) — age ranges are not absolute. Age-inappropriate diagnosis should trigger reassessment.

Functional impact distinguishes serious from benign pathology. Child still playing, running, climbing stairs = likely benign. Refusing to weight-bear, carried by parents, missing school = urgent assessment needed.

Night pain needs context in children. Growing pains are typically bilateral, in the legs (not the joints), occur in the late afternoon or evening and may wake the child, but resolve by morning with normal activity, a normal examination and no limp; pain that is unilateral, localised to a joint, present in the morning or during the day, or with systemic features needs assessment. They are uncommon below the age of 5, and should not be diagnosed in a child under 3 with a limp (low threshold for same-day assessment). Persistent or progressive night pain = consider malignancy, infection, inflammatory arthritis.

3
Diagnose

Classification — Age-based differential diagnosis

Use age to narrow differential diagnosis and guide investigation strategy.
Age <3 years
Common: Transient synovitis, toddler fracture (spiral tibia), post-viral arthralgia. Serious: Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, DDH, NAI, leukaemia. Rule: Low threshold for same-day assessment — cannot localise pain and transient synovitis is less common; same-day if unexplained, persistent, febrile, reduced movement, non-weight-bearing, abnormal examination, safeguarding concern or diagnostic uncertainty. Never growing pains.
Age 3-10 years
Common: Transient synovitis (irritable hip), growing pains, post-viral, minor trauma. Serious: Perthes disease (commonly 4-10 yrs; may occur outside this range; pain may be referred to thigh or knee), septic arthritis, malignancy, juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Imaging + paediatric/orthopaedic assessment if: persistent limp, restricted hip abduction/internal rotation, Trendelenburg gait, night pain, systemically unwell. Early radiographs may be normal — MRI or specialist follow-up may be needed.
Age 10-16 years
Common: Osgood-Schlatter, Sever's disease, anterior knee pain, growing pains, sports injuries. Serious: SUFE (consider particularly with obesity or endocrine/renal disease, but also in lean adolescents), bone tumours (osteosarcoma, Ewing's), JIA. Hip pathology can present as knee pain. Hip, groin, thigh or referred knee pain, limp or reduced internal rotation = SUFE until excluded — keep non-weight-bearing and arrange same-day orthopaedic assessment and imaging.
Growing pains
Criteria: Usually age 3-12 years, bilateral leg pain (thighs, calves, behind the knees), evening/night onset, normal by morning, normal examination, normal daytime activity and gait, no systemic features. May occasionally wake a child — not diagnostic of malignancy on its own but prompts reassessment. If atypical (unilateral, focal, daytime, limp, persisting/worsening, abnormal examination, systemic symptoms) → reassess or investigate. Clinical diagnosis
Hypermobility
Beighton score ≥6/9 in prepubertal children and adolescents (≥5/9 after puberty to age 50; ≥4/9 over 50 — 2017 international criteria): thumb to forearm, little finger >90°, elbow hyperextension >10°, knee hyperextension >10°, palms flat on floor. Symptoms: Joint pains after activity, easy bruising, poor stamina. Management: Physiotherapy, core strengthening.
Transient synovitis
Typical age: approximately 3-10 years. Features: Acute limp, hip pain, recent viral illness, systemically well, can usually weight-bear (features not absolute). Investigations: Do not diagnose solely because inflammatory markers are normal — normal CRP, ESR or WCC do not exclude early septic arthritis or osteomyelitis, and an ultrasound effusion does not distinguish septic arthritis from transient synovitis. Self-limiting 1-2 weeks. Diagnosis of exclusion

Growing pains are a clinical diagnosis made after considering alternative pathology. Typical growing pains can be managed without investigation when the history and examination are reassuring. Reassess or investigate if pain is unilateral, focal, occurs in the daytime, causes limp, persists or worsens, or is associated with abnormal examination or systemic symptoms.

Transient synovitis vs septic arthritis is the critical distinction. Kocher criteria are a supportive risk tool, not a diagnostic test. When in doubt = same-day hospital assessment; aspiration and microbiological assessment are determined by the hospital orthopaedic/paediatric team. Missing septic arthritis = permanent joint damage.

Age-specific pathology reflects skeletal maturity. Perthes (avascular necrosis femoral head) commonly occurs age 4-10 (may occur outside this range) when blood supply to epiphysis is vulnerable. SUFE occurs in adolescents when growth plate is weakest pre-fusion. Wrong age for diagnosis = reconsider.

4
Diagnose

Examination — GALS screen + focused joint examination

Start with GALS (Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine) screen, then focused joint examination.
Gait observation
Watch child walk, run, jump. Antalgic: Painful (shortened stance). Trendelenburg: Hip drops on opposite side (abductor weakness). Toe-walking: Achilles contracture, cerebral palsy. Refusal to weight-bear: Serious pathology until proven otherwise.
Arms (GALS)
"Put hands behind head" (shoulder abduction/external rotation), "Touch ceiling" (shoulder elevation), "Make fist" (finger flexion), "Turn hands over" (wrist/forearm supination/pronation). Abnormal = JIA, connective tissue disease.
Legs (GALS)
"Stand on tiptoes" (ankle plantarflexion, calf strength), "Squat down" (knee flexion, hip/knee pathology if unable). Check leg length (ASIS to medial malleolus). Palpate knees for effusion, warmth.
Spine (GALS)
"Touch toes" (lumbar flexion, hamstring tightness). "Bend sideways" (lateral flexion). Observe from behind for scoliosis (Adams forward bend test). Back pain in a young child warrants assessment; imaging depends on persistent/severe symptoms, night pain, neurological signs, fever/systemic symptoms, trauma, deformity or malignancy/infection risk.
Hip examination
Examine the hip in every child with knee or thigh pain (hip disease commonly refers pain to the knee). Look: Leg length, muscle wasting (gluteal, quadriceps). Feel: Groin tenderness. Move: Flexion, abduction, adduction, internal/external rotation. Limited internal rotation = Perthes, SUFE, slipped capital femoral epiphysis. Log roll test: Passive internal/external rotation — pain = hip pathology.
Knee examination
Effusion: Bulge test, ballotment. Tenderness: Tibial tuberosity (Osgood-Schlatter), patella (patellofemoral pain), joint line (meniscal tear). Stability: Collateral ligaments, cruciate ligaments (teens only).
Foot/ankle
Heel tenderness (Sever's disease age 8-14), flat feet (flexible normal), high arches (neuromuscular disease). Achilles tendon (tightness, swelling). Ankle range of motion (dorsiflexion, plantarflexion).
Systemically
Temperature, rash (JIA, HSP, Lyme), pallor (anaemia in leukaemia), bruising, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly (malignancy, JIA). Weight, height (growth plate damage in chronic disease).
Document & compare
Assess both legs and compare sides. Document weight-bearing, gait, range of movement and pain on passive movement. Assess pulses and neurological function after trauma. Examine the abdomen, spine and testicles where clinically indicated. Avoid repeatedly provoking severe pain.

GALS screen detects 96% of significant MSK abnormalities in 2 minutes. Quick screening tool before focused examination. Normal GALS screen makes serious pathology unlikely (high negative predictive value).

Hip pathology commonly presents as knee pain in children (referred pain via obturator nerve). Always examine hip in child with knee pain, especially if knee examination normal. Missing SUFE because you only examined the knee is a medicolegal disaster.

Refusal to weight-bear is never normal. Even with transient synovitis, children can weight-bear with pain. Complete refusal = septic arthritis, fracture, malignancy, or severe Perthes until proven otherwise.

5
Diagnose

Investigations — Selective based on clinical suspicion

Investigate if: unexplained limp under 3 years, systemically unwell, abnormal examination, persistent or worsening symptoms, or red flags. Investigations must not delay same-day specialist discussion.
Bloods — when to do
FBC, CRP, ESR if: fever, systemically unwell, suspected infection/inflammation. Interpretation: FBC, CRP and ESR may help stratify risk but cannot rule septic arthritis in or out — a child with clinical concern requires urgent paediatric/orthopaedic assessment regardless of initial blood results. Very urgent FBC when leukaemia is possible (NICE NG12). Normal inflammatory markers do NOT exclude serious pathology (leukaemia, bone tumour can have normal bloods initially).
X-ray — indications
Urgent same-day: Suspected fracture, severe pain, unable to weight-bear; suspected SUFE — imaging via same-day orthopaedic assessment (keep non-weight-bearing). Otherwise: Persistent limp, bone pain or swelling, abnormal examination — urgency by clinical findings. Views: for suspected Perthes or SUFE AP pelvis plus frog-leg lateral views of both hips; if an unstable SUFE is suspected (unable to weight-bear, severe pain), a cross-table (true) lateral replaces the frog-leg view — do not force positioning. Klein's line on AP hip X-ray (SUFE diagnosis). Normal radiographs do not exclude early Perthes.
Growing pains
Do NOT investigate if: bilateral, evening-only, normal examination, child well. Reassure parents. Reassess or investigate if: unilateral, focal, daytime, causes limp, persists or worsens, abnormal exam, systemic symptoms.
Hip ultrasound
Indications: Suspected effusion (transient synovitis vs septic arthritis), DDH screening in at-risk infants. Ultrasound detects an effusion but does not establish whether it is septic — significance depends on the clinical picture, age, laboratory results and specialist assessment.
MRI
Indications: Normal X-ray but high clinical suspicion; suspected osteomyelitis, early Perthes, stress fracture, malignancy or spinal disease; neurological signs; when the result will alter management. Arranged by: generally paediatrics, orthopaedics or radiology.
Do NOT investigate
Typical growing pains. Transient synovitis improving at 48 hours. Clear sports injury with normal examination. Overinvestigation causes anxiety, radiation exposure, false-positives.

X-rays in children = radiation — effective dose matters. Pelvis X-ray = 0.6 mSv (equivalent to 3 months background radiation). Only X-ray if result will change management. Normal X-ray does NOT exclude serious pathology (early Perthes, bone tumours, osteomyelitis may have normal X-rays initially).

Inflammatory markers help distinguish septic arthritis from transient synovitis but are not 100% sensitive/specific. Normal results do not exclude early septic arthritis, osteomyelitis or Kingella kingae infection. Clinical suspicion overrides bloods — if in doubt, refer for same-day specialist assessment.

MRI is gold standard for early Perthes, osteomyelitis, bone tumours, stress fractures, soft tissue masses. Avoid radiation. Arranged by specialists, not primary care. Do not delay referral waiting for MRI availability.

6
Refer

Referral Criteria — When to escalate urgently

Low threshold for urgent referral in children — consequences of missed serious pathology are catastrophic.
999/Same-day
Septic arthritis or osteomyelitis suspected: Fever + limb/joint swelling, non-use of an extremity, inability to weight-bear, hot joint (NICE NG143/NG254; 999 if physiologically unstable). SUFE suspected: Adolescent with hip/groin/thigh/knee pain, limp or restricted internal rotation — keep non-weight-bearing. Fracture: Significant trauma, deformity, unable to weight-bear. NAI concern: Inconsistent history, multiple injuries, safeguarding alert. Systemically unwell child. Acute neurological deficit.
Urgent paediatrics (NG12 48h tests)
Unexplained night pain. Persistent unexplained limp. Abnormal examination. Suspected malignancy. Persistent fever. Unexplained bruising or pallor. Hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy (unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly: immediate specialist assessment). Multiple joint swelling. Back pain with neurological or systemic features. Urgency depends on examination, age, trauma, systemic symptoms and weight-bearing rather than a fixed time threshold.
Urgent orthopaedics
Perthes disease on X-ray or suspected despite normal X-ray (commonly age 4-10, hip/thigh/knee pain, limp, restricted abduction/internal rotation, Trendelenburg gait). Persistent limp + abnormal X-ray (fracture, bone lesion). DDH suspected (clunk, asymmetric skin creases, leg length discrepancy). Severe Osgood-Schlatter unresponsive to rest.
Routine paediatrics
Suspected JIA: Persistent joint swelling, inflammatory morning stiffness or loss of function — do not wait 6 weeks; refer more urgently to paediatric rheumatology with multiple joints, systemic symptoms or eye symptoms. Hypermobility + significant functional impairment. Growing pains atypical or severe (for reassurance + physiotherapy).
Primary care
Typical growing pains (bilateral, evening, normal exam). Transient synovitis improving at 48 hrs. Minor sports injuries (Sever's, Osgood-Schlatter mild). Hypermobility mild. Manage with reassurance, analgesia, physiotherapy referral.

SUFE is a same-day surgical emergency — requires immediate surgical pinning to prevent further slip and avascular necrosis. Delay = slip progression = chronic hip arthritis requiring hip replacement in early adulthood. Any adolescent with hip, groin, thigh or referred knee pain, limp or reduced hip internal rotation should be considered to have SUFE until excluded — keep non-weight-bearing and arrange same-day orthopaedic assessment and imaging.

Perthes disease requires orthopaedic input to prevent femoral head collapse; normal radiographs do not exclude early disease and MRI or specialist follow-up may be required. It is usually unilateral but may be bilateral — bilateral disease needs specialist assessment, with emergency transfer depending on clinical status. Treatment (containment, bracing, or surgery) is time-sensitive — delayed referral worsens long-term outcome (hip arthritis, leg length discrepancy).

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis is a diagnosis of exclusion. JIA is classified as joint swelling (not just pain) persisting >6 weeks, but referral should not wait six weeks: persistent swelling, inflammatory morning stiffness or loss of function should prompt paediatric/rheumatology referral, especially with multiple joints, systemic or eye symptoms. Early rheumatology input prevents joint damage and disability.

7
Treat

Analgesia and Supportive Care — primary care management

Treat benign MSK conditions in primary care with analgesia, activity modification, physiotherapy.
Growing pains
Reassurance + simple analgesia First-line
Weight-based paracetamol PRN per BNFC/local formulary. Leg massage and heat packs may provide comfort. Benign, self-limiting; usually settles in later childhood.
Transient synovitis (improving)
Analgesia + relative activity restriction First-line
Use age/weight-appropriate analgesia (BNFC) and relative activity restriction — complete non-weight-bearing is not routinely required; the child should gradually resume weight-bearing as pain improves. Reassess within 24–48 hours if the diagnosis is uncertain or symptoms are not improving. Self-limiting 1-2 weeks.
Osgood-Schlatter / Sever's
Activity modification + physio First-line
Reduce painful high-impact activity; maintain general activity through pain-free alternatives. Ice/analgesia. Stretch and strengthen (quadriceps, hamstrings, calves). Physiotherapy if persistent or functionally limiting. Resolves with growth plate fusion.
Step 1Paracetamol — weight-based dose per BNFC/local formulary (no universal maximum for all children) PRN for mild pain (growing pains, minor trauma). Avoid regular dosing unless moderate-severe pain.
Step 2Ibuprofen — age/weight-appropriate dose per BNFC for inflammatory conditions (transient synovitis, Osgood-Schlatter, soft tissue injury). Give with food. Avoid in dehydration, renal impairment, NSAID-sensitive asthma, gastrointestinal bleeding and some infections. Reassess if regular dosing is needed beyond a short course.
Step 3Paracetamol + Ibuprofen may be used together or sequentially in selected circumstances for moderate-severe pain; routine alternating increases dosing errors — give a clear written schedule if both are advised. Review in 48 hours — if not improving, reassess.
Step 4Refer if: Analgesia ineffective, pain worsening, persistent >1 week, red flags emerge. Codeine or tramadol are not routine treatment for benign limb pain; any use requires age, weight, respiratory and contraindication checks.
Activity modification
Osgood-Schlatter / Sever's: Reduce running, jumping sports. Swimming, cycling OK. Stretching exercises (quadriceps, hamstrings, gastrocnemius). Ice after activity 15 minutes. Duration: 3-6 months until growth spurt complete.
Physiotherapy referral
Indications: Hypermobility with functional impairment, recurrent sports injuries, persistent pain >4 weeks, muscle weakness (post-Perthes, post-fracture). Focus: Core strengthening, proprioception, gait retraining.
Safety-netting
Return if: pain worsening, new systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss), unable to weight-bear, night pain develops. Growing pains: If persisting or worsening, becoming unilateral, focal, daytime or causing limp → reassess.

NSAIDs may help inflammatory MSK pain in children (transient synovitis, soft tissue injuries, overuse syndromes). Use an age/weight-appropriate dose and avoid in dehydration, renal impairment, NSAID-sensitive asthma, gastrointestinal bleeding and some infections.

Activity modification is treatment, not just advice. Osgood-Schlatter and Sever's disease are traction apophysitis — continuing high-impact sport perpetuates symptoms. Relative rest (reduce running/jumping, continue swimming/cycling) allows healing while maintaining fitness. Self-limiting once growth plate fuses.

Do NOT use codeine in children <12 years (MHRA warning — risk of respiratory depression in ultra-rapid metabolisers). Codeine or tramadol should not be routine treatment for benign limb pain; any use requires age, weight, respiratory and contraindication checks. If simple analgesia is ineffective, reassess the diagnosis.

8
Lifestyle

Parental Education and Injury Prevention

Educate parents on self-management, red flags, and injury prevention. Reduce anxiety about benign symptoms.
Growing pains reassurance Explain benign nature. Not harmful to bones/joints. Self-limiting, usually settling in later childhood. No long-term consequences. Massage and heat may provide comfort; evidence for pharmacological benefit is limited.
Red flag education Teach parents when to seek urgent care: fever + limp, refusing to weight-bear, night pain waking child, systemically unwell, persistent >1 week.
Activity balance Encourage diverse physical activity. Mix high-impact (running) with low-impact (swimming, cycling). Rest days essential — growth plates need recovery time.
Warm-up and stretching 5-10 minutes warm-up before sport. Stretching after activity (quadriceps, hamstrings, calves).
Appropriate footwear Supportive, well-fitting trainers for running sports; check fit as feet grow. Avoid worn-out shoes (increase injury risk).
Healthy weight Obesity is an important risk factor for SUFE (lean adolescents can also develop it), Blount's disease (tibia vara), anterior knee pain. Encourage active play, limit screen time <2 hours/day.
Graduated return to sport After injury: start low-impact, progress gradually. Pain during activity = stop. Progress gradually as pain allows.
Avoid over-training Young athletes: build training load gradually with rest days. Overtraining → overuse injuries (stress fractures, apophysitis).
Vitamin D supplementation Follow current national (UKHSA/Department of Health) and local public-health vitamin D advice, which differs for infants, children under 5, children with dark skin or limited sun exposure, and those at increased deficiency risk — account for fortified foods and existing multivitamins (NHS vitamin D guidance).
When to stop sport Temporary rest if: pain during activity, limp after sport, pain >24 hours post-activity, night pain. Resume when pain-free at rest and during activity.

Parental anxiety drives consultations — 80% of paediatric MSK consultations are benign (growing pains, transient synovitis, minor trauma). Reassurance is therapeutic. Clear explanation of red flags empowers parents to self-manage safely while seeking help when needed.

Varied activity may be a reasonable suggestion in selected sports settings but is not a universal clinical standard. Growth plates are vulnerable to repetitive stress. Diverse activity reduces single-muscle-group overload and prevents burnout.

Vitamin D deficiency is more likely with dark skin and limited outdoor play and increases rickets risk. Supplementation advice should follow current national vitamin D guidance for the child's age and risk group.

9
Safety

Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

Schedule reviews based on diagnosis. Clear safety-netting for red flags.
48 hours
Transient synovitis: Reassess within 24–48 hours if the diagnosis is uncertain or symptoms are not improving. If improving (reduced pain, walking better) → continue analgesia, review 1 week. If worsening or fever → same-day assessment (septic arthritis). Acute limp post-trauma: Review if not improving — consider X-ray.
1 week
Growing pains, minor injuries: Check symptom resolution. If persisting → examine, consider X-ray. Osgood-Schlatter / Sever's: Assess response to activity modification. Reinforce rest from high-impact sport.
4 weeks
Persistent symptoms: Re-examine, review X-rays if not done. Consider physiotherapy referral. If no improvement → paediatric/orthopaedic referral. Overuse injuries: Should be improving with activity modification — if not, refer physiotherapy.
3 months
Hypermobility: Review physiotherapy progress. Assess functional improvement (core strength, stamina, pain frequency). Discharge if improving. Re-refer if worsening or new symptoms.
Safety-net 999
Physiological instability, altered consciousness, shock, severe respiratory compromise or rapid deterioration. Sudden severe pain unable to weight-bear. Severe trauma (fall from height, RTA). Acute neurology (leg weakness, bladder/bowel symptoms).
Safety-net Same-day assessment
Fever + limp/joint pain. Systemically unwell (lethargic, not eating, vomiting). Refusal to weight-bear. Hot swollen joint. Persistent, focal or progressive night pain. Severe pain unrelieved by analgesia.
Re-refer if
Benign diagnosis develops red flags: Persistent or progressive night pain, systemically unwell, not improving as expected. Growing pains: Become unilateral, focal, daytime, cause limp, or persist/worsen. Action: Urgent paediatric re-referral (may be evolving pathology).

48-hour review for transient synovitis is mandatory — septic arthritis can evolve from initially mild presentation. If fever develops or pain worsens after initial improvement = urgent reassessment. Do not assume "it's just viral".

Persistent symptoms deserve reassessment even if the initial presentation was benign; urgency depends on examination, age, trauma, systemic symptoms and weight-bearing. Early Perthes, stress fractures, bone tumours can have delayed X-ray changes. Normal initial X-ray does not exclude serious pathology — clinical deterioration overrides imaging.

Night pain can occur with benign conditions but should never be dismissed — persistent, focal or progressive night pain needs urgent assessment. If benign diagnosis (growing pains) develops night pain = urgent re-referral for malignancy/infection exclusion. Changing pattern = changing diagnosis.

Educational use. Pathway based on NICE NG12 (Suspected cancer, updated April 2026 — children's leukaemia and bone sarcoma recommendations), NICE NG254 (Suspected sepsis in under 16s, 2025), NICE NG143 (Fever in under 5s), national vitamin D guidance, BNFC weight-based analgesic dosing, and the pGALS musculoskeletal screen. Always adapt to individual patient context and local safeguarding protocols.
