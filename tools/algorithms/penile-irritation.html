1
Safety

Red Flags — Penile Cancer, Fournier's Gangrene & Phimosis Emergency

Consider penile cancer, at any age, in any patient with a persistent penile mass, ulcerated lesion, indurated plaque, unexplained persistent glans/foreskin symptoms or a lesion that persists after appropriate STI treatment. Examine the glans and inner prepuce where possible.

Non-healing penile ulcer / indurated plaque Penile squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) → urology suspected cancer pathway referral. Most arise on the glans or inner prepuce — hidden under a non-retractile foreskin. Any persistent, ulcerated, indurated, changing or treatment-resistant lesion requires urgent urology/uro-oncology or dermatology assessment; biopsy is specialist-directed. Risk factors: phimosis, HPV, poor hygiene, lichen sclerosus, smoking.
Rapidly spreading penile / scrotal skin necrosis Fournier's gangrene — necrotising fasciitis of genitalia → 999. Extreme pain disproportionate to appearance + spreading erythema + crepitus + fever. Suspected Fournier's gangrene is a surgical emergency: arrange immediate hospital transfer, urgent surgical/urological assessment, resuscitation and broad-spectrum IV antibiotics according to the local necrotising soft-tissue-infection pathway. Diabetics especially at risk.
Paraphimosis Foreskin retracted and unable to return over glans → swelling of glans distal to trapped prepuce → vascular compromise. Penile gangrene risk within hours. Same-day urology / A&E emergency. Manual reduction is an emergency procedure — gentle compression and lubrication by a trained clinician; dorsal slit is a specialist procedure, not a GP one.
Penile irritation + urinary retention Phimosis causing urinary obstruction (stream reduced to a trickle, painful voiding, distended bladder) → same-day urology catheterisation. Chronic urinary retention from phimosis → CKD risk from backpressure. Elective circumcision once stable.
Penile lesion + inguinal lymphadenopathy + weight loss Advanced penile cancer with nodal metastases → urology suspected cancer pathway referral urgently.
Penile irritation + systemic features in diabetic / immunocompromised Invasive fungal infection, aggressive bacterial balanitis or necrotising infection → same-day hospital assessment. Same-day assessment is required if the patient is systemically unwell, has rapidly worsening pain/swelling, spreading erythema, necrosis, urinary obstruction, suspected deep infection, failure of appropriate treatment or inability to manage the condition safely.
Penile cancer is rare but is often diagnosed late, frequently because of attribution to benign conditions (balanitis, lichen sclerosus) without biopsy. Lichen sclerosus is an important risk factor — male genital LS is associated with penile intraepithelial neoplasia and penile cancer, although the reported risk varies considerably between studies. Any persistent erosion, ulcer, induration, lump or changing lesion in known lichen sclerosus requires urgent specialist assessment and possible biopsy. HPV infection (especially types 16 and 18) is causally implicated in a substantial proportion of penile SCCs — making HPV vaccination of adolescent males (now part of the NHS schedule) a penile cancer prevention strategy. Paraphimosis is a urological emergency that is frequently encountered in primary care — it can occur after a medical examination (failure to replace foreskin after catheterisation or examination), after sexual activity, or spontaneously in men with phimosis. The mechanism is that the tight retracted prepuce acts as a tourniquet, obstructing venous and lymphatic drainage → oedema → arterial obstruction → gangrene. The glans becomes progressively more engorged and cyanotic. Manual reduction (gentle compression and lubrication) is an emergency procedure for a trained clinician; escalate promptly to A&E/urology if it is not quickly successful. Dorsal slit is a specialist procedure.

Targeted History — Risk-Based STI Assessment

Take a confidential sexual history and offer STI testing where there is sexual exposure risk, urethral discharge, dysuria, genital ulceration, vesicles, a new sexual partner, partner symptoms, recurrent symptoms of uncertain cause or clinical suspicion of an STI. Strongly consider testing for urethral discharge or dysuria, penile ulceration or vesicles, a new or multiple sexual partners, condomless sex, MSM exposure, partner notification, recurrent or unexplained balanitis, and suspected syphilis or herpes. A man with clearly irritant dermatitis, no sexual exposure risk and no urethral, ulcerative or vesicular features does not require a "mandatory" STI screen.

Sexual history (confidential)
Last sexual contact and gender of partner(s) · New partner in last 3 months · Condom use · Oral / anal / vaginal intercourse · Partner symptoms (vaginal discharge, vulval itch — suggests candida) · Previous STI history · HIV status and last test · IVDU history
Symptom character
Itch (dermatological — candida, contact, lichen sclerosus, psoriasis) vs burning (UTI, urethritis) vs discharge (urethritis — chlamydia / gonorrhoea) vs sore / ulcer (herpes, syphilis, chancroid) vs rash / lesion (warts, syphilis secondary, fixed drug eruption, psoriasis, Zoon's balanitis)
Onset and triggers
After new sexual partner (STI) · After antibiotic course (candida) · After soap / washing product change (contact dermatitis) · Chronic recurring (lichen sclerosus, recurrent herpes, recurrent candida) · Worsening under foreskin (phimosis trapping moisture → candida / poor hygiene balanitis)
Circumcision status
Uncircumcised: higher risk of candidal and aerobic balanitis (moisture + smegma accumulation under prepuce), phimosis. Circumcised: candidal balanitis less common, contact dermatitis more visible. Both: equally at risk of STIs. Ask specifically about foreskin retractability.
Medical history
Diabetes (glycosuria → candida, impaired immunity → recurrent / resistant balanitis) · Immunosuppression (candida, viral infections, drug eruptions) · Skin disease history (psoriasis, eczema, lichen sclerosus) · Recent antibiotics (candida) · Current medications (fixed drug eruption — co-trimoxazole, NSAIDs, tetracyclines)
Partner notification context
If STI confirmed — recent partners need testing and treatment. Partner notification and look-back periods should follow the current BASHH infection-specific guideline and be coordinated by sexual-health clinicians.
The sexual history is the most important and most frequently incomplete part of the penile irritation consultation — many GPs find it uncomfortable to ask or assume the diagnosis is non-sexual based on age or relationship status. This is a well-documented cause of delayed STI diagnosis. BASHH guidelines and RCGP SCA competencies both emphasise that any genital complaint in a person of any age or relationship status warrants an STI risk assessment. The BASHH recommended approach is the "5 Ps": Partners (number, gender), Practices (oral, anal, vaginal), Protection (condom use), Past STI history, and Pregnancy plans. Asking this systematically rather than ad hoc is both more comfortable for the clinician and more acceptable to the patient. Diabetes-associated recurrent candidal balanitis is one of the most important presentations to recognise — recurrent, severe or resistant candidal balanitis in a man, especially if obese, middle-aged, or with other risk factors, should prompt an HbA1c. Undiagnosed type 2 diabetes frequently presents first as recurrent candidal balanitis — glycosuria provides a nutrient-rich environment for Candida albicans on the glans and prepuce.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Candidal balanitis
A common cause, but balanoposthitis is a heterogeneous group of infectious, inflammatory, irritant, premalignant and malignant conditions — candida may be secondary to another dermatosis, diabetes, antibiotics or a dysfunctional foreskin. Itch + erythema + white cottage-cheese discharge / satellite lesions under foreskin. Associated with: diabetes, antibiotics, partner with vaginal candidiasis, obesity. Clotrimazole 1% cream BD for up to 7–14 days (glans + foreskin); oral fluconazole only where clinically appropriate. Recurrent, severe or resistant → HbA1c, confirm the diagnosis and swab.
Aerobic balanitis (bacterial)
Mixed commensal overgrowth (Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, anaerobes) under non-retractile foreskin — poor hygiene / smegma accumulation. Erythema, malodorous discharge, no satellite lesions. Swab purulent or malodorous discharge for MC&S when bacterial infection is suspected. Treat according to clinical severity, culture and local antimicrobial guidance (BASHH balanoposthitis guideline 2026); avoid repeated topical antibiotics for non-specific erythema. If it recurs or does not respond, consider an STI, lichen sclerosus, irritant dermatitis or penile intraepithelial neoplasia. Hygiene education + retraction technique. Recurrent → consider circumcision.
Chlamydia urethritis
Often asymptomatic, or mild urethral discharge + dysuria + urethral itch. Chlamydia is one cause of non-gonococcal urethritis (NGU); NGU needs additional investigation (gonorrhoea, Mycoplasma genitalium) and follow-up. NAAT first-catch urine or urethral swab according to local sexual-health laboratory requirements. Uncomplicated chlamydia: doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days. Symptomatic urethritis → refer to or discuss with sexual health. Partner notification and treatment via sexual health. Test of cure not routinely required (re-test at around 3 months for reinfection).
Gonorrhoea
Purulent urethral discharge (yellow-green) + dysuria + urethral itch. NAAT + culture (essential for antimicrobial resistance testing). Ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose (BASHH 2025). Obtain culture wherever possible before treatment, particularly if discharge is present. Pharyngeal testing for all with urogenital gonorrhoea. Do not use ciprofloxacin empirically. Manage with sexual-health involvement for partner notification and public-health surveillance. Test of cure not routinely required for susceptible anogenital infection treated with ceftriaxone 1 g, but recommended for pharyngeal infection, persistent symptoms, unknown susceptibility, treatment other than ceftriaxone and pregnancy — NAAT at least 2 weeks after treatment (BASHH 2025).
Genital herpes (HSV-1 / HSV-2)
Clusters of vesicles → painful ulcers on glans / shaft / prepuce. Primary: systemic upset (fever, lymphadenopathy), very painful, dysuria. Recurrent: prodromal tingling → localised ulcers, milder, resolves in 5–7 days. Swab a fresh lesion for HSV PCR where possible. Refer or discuss with sexual health for a first episode, uncertain diagnosis, severe disease, urinary retention or immunocompromise. Antiviral treatment follows the current BASHH genital herpes guideline and local formulary (e.g. aciclovir 400 mg TDS × 5 days).
Syphilis (primary / secondary)
Primary: painless indurated ulcer (chancre) at site of infection — may be hidden under foreskin. Secondary: generalised rash (palmar / plantar rash), condylomata lata (warty perianal / penile lesions), mucous patches. Syphilis serology (TPHA + RPR/VDRL) — GUM referral for treatment and contact tracing (penicillin G IM).
Lichen sclerosus (BXO)
Balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO) = lichen sclerosus of penis. White atrophic plaques + fissuring of glans and prepuce → phimosis. Itch, white discolouration, tightening foreskin, dyspareunia. Can cause scarring, phimosis and meatal/urethral disease, and is associated with penile intraepithelial neoplasia and penile cancer — treat active disease and advise ongoing self-examination. Very potent (ultrapotent) topical corticosteroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05%) first-line, using a specialist-directed regimen. Any persistent erosion, ulcer, induration, lump or changing lesion → urgent specialist assessment and possible biopsy. Circumcision may be curative where disease is confined to the foreskin or pathological phimosis persists.
Contact / irritant dermatitis
Itch + erythema + oedema from contactant — soap, washing powder, condom latex, spermicide, topical creams, lubricants. Bilateral / symmetrical rash. Resolves on allergen removal. Mild topical steroid (hydrocortisone 1% BD × 1 week) + emollient. Patch testing (dermatology) if persistent. Switch to non-latex condoms if latex allergy suspected.
Psoriasis
Well-demarcated, glazed, erythematous plaque on glans (inverse psoriasis — no silvery scaling on moist surfaces). Check elbows, knees, scalp, nails for classic psoriasis. Mild topical steroid or calcitriol (Silkis). Avoid potent steroids long-term on genital skin. Dermatology referral if uncertain diagnosis or not responding.
Zoon's balanitis (plasma cell balanitis)
Middle-aged / elderly uncircumcised men. Shiny, moist, orange-red, well-demarcated plaque on glans — "cayenne pepper" spots on examination. Chronic. Persistent orange-red glazed plaques require exclusion of lichen sclerosus, penile intraepithelial neoplasia and SCC — Zoon's change may coexist with other dermatoses, premalignancy or malignancy. Diagnosis of exclusion; biopsy (plasma cell infiltrate) is specialist-directed. Circumcision may be effective in selected patients. Imiquimod is not a routine primary-care option — specialist-led only.
Syphilis is one of the most important diagnoses not to miss in penile ulceration — the primary chancre is classically painless and indurated, and is frequently located under the foreskin where it is invisible without retraction. Patients do not present with "a sore" because it does not hurt. They may present weeks later with secondary syphilis features (widespread rash, condylomata lata, constitutional symptoms) without ever having noticed the primary ulcer. Syphilis diagnoses in England are at their highest level since 1948 (UKHSA, 2024). Any genital ulcer, even if painless, must have syphilis serology alongside an STI screen. The painlessness of the chancre is the key teaching point — painful penile ulcers suggest herpes; painless indurated ulcers suggest syphilis; painful non-indurated ulcers in tropical settings suggest chancroid (Haemophilus ducreyi). Lichen sclerosus of the penis (BXO) deserves the same clinical attention as vulval lichen sclerosus — its association with penile intraepithelial neoplasia and penile cancer means ongoing self-examination and urgent specialist assessment, with possible biopsy, of any persistent erosion, ulcer, induration, lump or changing lesion. Many GPs are more familiar with vulval LS but the penile condition is equally important and equally undertreated. A very potent topical steroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05%) is first-line, using a specialist-directed regimen individualised to the patient — the vulval tapering schedule is largely extrapolated and is not a universal male protocol.

Diagnose

Examination & Investigations

Where safe and possible, gently retract the foreskin to inspect the glans, inner prepuce and meatus. Do not forcibly retract a scarred or painful foreskin. If the glans cannot be inspected because of phimosis and there are persistent symptoms or a suspicious lesion, arrange urgent urology assessment.

Penile examination
Retract the foreskin gently where safe — never forcibly if scarred or painful (note ease of retraction, phimosis grade; assess urinary flow). Inspect glans: colour, surface (smooth / ulcerated / plaques / vesicles / warts / white patches), discharge (colour, consistency, odour). Inspect inner prepuce (foreskin inner surface). Inspect shaft and base. Examine inguinal lymph nodes (tender = infection / reactive; hard, fixed = malignancy).
Phimosis grading
Grade 0: fully retractile. Grade 1: slight narrowing, full retraction possible. Grade 2: partial retraction, glans visible. Grade 3: partial retraction, meatus only visible. Grade 4: slight retraction only, meatus not visible. Grade 5: no retraction. Grades 3–5: functional phimosis — refer urology for consideration of topical steroid trial or circumcision.
Urethral inspection
Discharge: note colour (clear = NGU / reactive; yellow-green = gonorrhoea; white = chlamydia — though often absent). If no visible discharge: milk urethra from base to meatus. Dysuria + discharge = urethritis → NAAT (first-catch urine or urethral swab according to local sexual-health laboratory requirements) + MSU, and refer to or discuss with sexual health.
Swabs and investigations
NAAT urethral swab or first-catch urine (chlamydia + gonorrhoea — offer where there is STI risk or urethral symptoms; extragenital NAATs guided by the sexual history) · Gonorrhoea culture swab (if discharge present — for antimicrobial sensitivity testing before treatment) · HSV PCR swab (from vesicle / ulcer base — must swab early, virus clears quickly) · Syphilis serology (TPHA + RPR — any ulcer or STI risk) · HIV test (per sexual-health guidance and risk assessment — appropriate in confirmed STIs and many genital ulcer/urethritis presentations) · HbA1c (recurrent, severe or resistant candida/balanitis) · Skin scraping + KOH (candida confirmation) · MSU (UTI / urethritis)
Biopsy criteria
Any persistent, ulcerated, indurated, changing or treatment-resistant lesion → urgent urology/uro-oncology or dermatology assessment (penile cancer exclusion); biopsy is specialist-directed. White patches / plaques not clearly lichen sclerosus → biopsy. Recurrent lesion at same site → biopsy. Any indurated, ulcerated, or fixed lesion → urology suspected cancer pathway referral. Biopsy should be performed by a clinician experienced in penile dermatoses and cancer assessment.
Inspecting the glans and inner prepuce, where safe and possible, matters — an unexamined glans can delay penile cancer diagnosis — but a scarred or painful foreskin should never be forcibly retracted. The inner prepuce and glans are the two most common sites for penile SCC. Non-retractile foreskin (phimosis) is both a risk factor for penile cancer (HPV trapping, chronic inflammation) and a physical barrier to examination. When the foreskin cannot be retracted for examination and there are persistent symptoms or a suspicious lesion, urgent urology assessment is warranted — both to enable examination and to assess phimosis for treatment. Gonorrhoea culture (in addition to NAAT) is essential whenever gonorrhoea is suspected or when NAAT is positive — culture allows antimicrobial sensitivity testing, which is critical given the dramatic rise in antimicrobial resistance in Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Ceftriaxone-resistant gonorrhoea has been identified in England, several cases linked to travel. BASHH therefore advises obtaining culture wherever possible before treatment, particularly if discharge is present. A NAAT-positive result without culture means treatment is given empirically (ceftriaxone 1 g IM) but resistance data are not available for that specific isolate — culture is the safety net.

Refer

Referral Pathways

999 / A&E same-day
Paraphimosis (glans engorged, foreskin unable to return) · Fournier's gangrene (crepitus + spreading necrosis + extreme pain) · Urinary retention from phimosis (distended painful bladder)
Suspected cancer: urology
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): penile mass or ulcerated lesion where an STI has been excluded; persistent penile lesion after completed STI treatment; unexplained or persistent symptoms of the foreskin or glans · Any persistent, ulcerated, indurated, changing or treatment-resistant lesion (urology/uro-oncology; dermatology where appropriate) · Glans cannot be inspected because of phimosis with persistent symptoms · Hard inguinal lymph nodes + penile lesion · Known lichen sclerosus with new lump/ulcer · Persistent white penile plaques not responding to treatment
GUM / sexual health clinic
Suspected or confirmed STIs, symptomatic urethritis, genital ulcers and complex genital infection — gonorrhoea, syphilis, herpes and urethritis management is sexual-health-led: partner notification, HIV testing, test of cure for gonorrhoea where indicated. Recurrent or unexplained balanitis. PrEP for people at substantial HIV risk, PEP. Any STI in vulnerable patient. Recurrent herpes requiring suppressive therapy.
Urology
Symptomatic phimosis (urinary obstruction, recurrent balanitis, pain) — topical corticosteroid trial (preparation and regimen per the local urology pathway) or circumcision · Lichen sclerosus with significant phimosis, urinary symptoms, meatal stenosis or suspected neoplasia · Recurrent balanoposthitis not responding to medical management · Paraphimosis (reduction / surgical) · BXO (lichen sclerosus) with significant scarring
Dermatology
Penile psoriasis not responding to mild topical steroid · Lichen sclerosus — diagnostic uncertainty or difficult disease · Persistent dermatosis or recurrent balanitis of uncertain cause · Suspected contact dermatitis (patch testing) · Zoon's balanitis (confirmation by biopsy + management) · Penile fixed drug eruption (identification of causative drug)
A short course of a topical corticosteroid to the phimotic ring may be considered in selected adults with non-urgent pathological phimosis, using the exact preparation and regimen specified by the local urology pathway — it can avoid the need for circumcision in some patients. The mechanism is steroid-induced softening of the fibrotic phimotic ring through suppression of the local inflammatory response and promotion of collagen remodelling. This should be tried before circumcision is offered for phimosis without lichen sclerosus. If lichen sclerosus is the cause of phimosis (BXO), a very potent steroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05%) is used instead, under a specialist-directed plan. GUM clinic referral for all confirmed STIs is the standard of care — GUM clinics provide: partner notification services (index case is notified of all contacts being tested without naming the index patient), contact tracing infrastructure, free STI testing for contacts, PrEP (HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis for people at substantial HIV risk), PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis), psychosexual support, and surveillance data for public health. Gonorrhoea, syphilis, herpes and urethritis management should be sexual-health-led, and partner notification is a specialist sexual-health function.

Treat

GP-Initiated Treatment

Candidal balanitis
Clotrimazole 1% cream BD for up to 7–14 days
Apply to glans and inner prepuce. Wash with water or an emollient soap substitute and dry gently; avoid soap, antiseptics and irritants. Oral fluconazole 150 mg is not a routine alternative — consider only where clinically appropriate, taking account of liver disease, drug interactions, QT-prolonging medicines, renal function, local sexual-health guidance and diagnostic uncertainty. Do not routinely treat a female partner — assess and treat a partner only if symptomatic or with confirmed vulvovaginal candidiasis. HbA1c if recurrent, severe or resistant. Topical steroid-antifungal combination (Canesten HC) if significant inflammation.
Uncomplicated chlamydia
Doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days
First-line (BASHH 2018). Do not use a single 1 g dose of azithromycin routinely. Symptomatic urethritis → refer to or discuss with sexual health (gonorrhoea and Mycoplasma genitalium may need consideration; non-gonococcal urethritis needs additional investigation and follow-up). Partner notification and treatment are specialist sexual-health functions. No sex until treatment is completed and partners have been treated. Re-test at around 3 months for reinfection — not a routine test of cure.
Genital herpes (primary)
Aciclovir 400 mg TDS × 5 days
Swab a fresh lesion for HSV PCR where possible. Refer or discuss with sexual health for a first episode, uncertain diagnosis, severe disease, urinary retention or immunocompromise. Antiviral treatment follows the current BASHH genital herpes guideline and local formulary. Start within 5 days of onset (or while new lesions forming — any time). Valaciclovir 500 mg BD × 5 days is an alternative — choice depends on local formulary, renal function, adherence and specialist advice. Analgesia: paracetamol + topical lidocaine 5% gel for severe pain. Saline bathing for ulcer comfort. Suppressive therapy (e.g. aciclovir 400 mg BD) based on recurrence frequency, severity and patient preference rather than a rigid threshold.
Contact / irritant dermatitisIdentify and remove contactant — soap, condom (switch to non-latex / non-spermicidal), topical cream, washing powder. Soap substitute for washing (Dermol 500 lotion). Short-course topical hydrocortisone 1% cream BD × 1 week. Emollient (Diprobase) after washing. Patch testing via dermatology if contact allergen uncertain. Resolves fully on allergen removal.
Lichen sclerosus (BXO)Apply clobetasol propionate 0.05% according to the dermatology/urology treatment plan, typically as an initial daily course followed by a gradual reduction in frequency — duration and maintenance are individualised (the vulval schedule is extrapolated, not a universal male protocol). Emollient (50:50 white soft paraffin). Wash with water only. Review response, scarring, urinary flow, meatal involvement, phimosis, sexual dysfunction and any persistent suspicious lesion; advise ongoing self-examination. Refer urology for significant phimosis, urinary symptoms, meatal stenosis or suspected neoplasia; dermatology for diagnostic uncertainty or difficult disease. Circumcision may be curative where disease is confined to the foreskin or pathological phimosis persists.
GonorrhoeaSexual-health-led. Ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose (BASHH 2025). Obtain culture wherever possible before treatment, particularly if discharge is present; pharyngeal testing for all with urogenital gonorrhoea. Partner notification (look-back per current BASHH guidance) and public-health surveillance via sexual health. Abstain until 7 days after treatment and until partners are treated. Test of cure not routinely required for susceptible anogenital infection treated with ceftriaxone 1 g; recommended for pharyngeal infection, persistent symptoms, unknown susceptibility, treatment other than ceftriaxone and pregnancy — NAAT at least 2 weeks after treatment (BASHH 2025). Treat co-infection: doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days if chlamydia NAAT also positive (dual infection common; BASHH 2018). Do not use ciprofloxacin empirically — consider it only where susceptibility is known and current specialist guidance supports it.
The switch from azithromycin to doxycycline as first-line for chlamydia reflects important resistance data — the 2018 BASHH guideline change was driven by evidence that azithromycin 1 g single dose has higher treatment failure rates for rectal and pharyngeal chlamydia infection, and that single-dose treatment selects for macrolide resistance in Mycoplasma genitalium (a co-pathogen causing NGU that responds poorly to azithromycin after a single-dose exposure). Doxycycline 100 mg BD × 7 days is highly effective for urogenital, rectal and pharyngeal chlamydia. The GUM referral after chlamydia diagnosis is essential for partner notification — partner notification and look-back periods follow the current BASHH infection-specific guidance and are coordinated by sexual-health clinicians — the infrastructure of GUM clinics (trained health advisers, contact slips, anonymous notification) is more effective. Rising ceftriaxone resistance in gonorrhoea is an emerging global concern — BASHH increased the recommended ceftriaxone dose from 500 mg to 1 g in its 2019 guideline in response to rising resistance, and the 2025 guideline keeps 1 g as sole first-line therapy. Obtaining culture wherever possible before treatment ensures resistance data are captured for public health surveillance and individual treatment modification if culture-directed therapy is needed.

Treat

Phimosis, Recurrent Balanitis & Specialist Conditions

Phimosis — topical steroid trial
A short course of a topical corticosteroid to the phimotic ring may be considered in selected adults with non-urgent pathological phimosis, using the exact preparation and regimen specified by the local urology pathway (a specialist-directed very potent steroid if lichen sclerosus is suspected), with gentle stretching only as far as comfortable — never forced. Refer urology if no improvement. Urgent referral for paraphimosis, urinary retention, severe infection, suspected lichen sclerosus with significant scarring or progressive meatal stenosis.
Recurrent candidal balanitis
For recurrent or treatment-resistant balanitis, confirm the diagnosis, obtain appropriate swabs, assess diabetes (HbA1c) and phimosis, and refer to sexual health, dermatology or urology. Long-term oral antifungal prophylaxis should be specialist-directed. Treat a partner only if symptomatic or with confirmed candidiasis, not automatically. Circumcision may be curative in severe recurrent cases.
Recurrent herpes — suppressive therapy
Based on recurrence frequency, severity and patient preference rather than a rigid threshold, usually with sexual-health advice: aciclovir 400 mg BD or valaciclovir 500 mg OD continuously, per the current BASHH genital herpes guideline and local formulary. Reduces recurrences and asymptomatic shedding (reduces transmission risk). Review annually — can attempt cessation after 12 months to see if recurrence frequency has reduced naturally (recurrence frequency decreases over time in most patients).
Fixed drug eruption
Well-demarcated round/oval erythematous to violaceous plaque on glans — recurs at same site each time implicated drug taken. Common culprits: co-trimoxazole (most common), NSAIDs, tetracyclines, paracetamol. Resolves within weeks of stopping drug. Re-challenge confirms diagnosis but should not be done clinically — drug allergy testing via dermatology. Avoid implicated drug permanently.
Syphilis (primary / secondary)
Penicillin G benzathine 2.4 MU IM single dose (primary / secondary syphilis — hospital / GUM-administered). Early latent: same single dose (BASHH 2024 — primary, secondary and early latent are all early syphilis). Late latent: 2.4 MU weekly × 3 doses; cardiovascular / neurosyphilis: specialist management. Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction (fever + rigors 2–8 hrs post-penicillin) — warn patient, treat with paracetamol. Partner notification and look-back by sexual health per current BASHH guidance (depends on stage and timing).
The fixed drug eruption is clinically important because it is frequently misdiagnosed as herpes, balanitis, or contact dermatitis — particularly when it presents on the glans penis as a painful, erythematous, or ulcerated lesion. The distinguishing feature is recurrence at exactly the same site with each drug exposure — the lesion "heals" leaving a post-inflammatory hyperpigmented macule, then re-activates to a blistering erosive lesion the next time the drug is taken. Co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) is the most common cause on the glans. The reaction is T-cell mediated — there is residual memory T-cell infiltration at the site of the previous reaction, which is activated by systemic drug exposure. Definitive diagnosis requires identifying and documenting the implicated drug and its temporal relationship with the lesion. The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction in syphilis treatment is caused by massive cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8) from the rapid lysis of Treponema pallidum spirochaetes by penicillin. It is common in early syphilis, typically 2–8 hours after the first penicillin dose. It is self-limiting but can be alarming — patients must be pre-warned, given paracetamol at the time of injection, and advised to rest and remain well hydrated for 24 hours. It is not an allergic reaction to penicillin.

Lifestyle

Penile Hygiene, STI Prevention & Sexual Health

Daily penile hygiene Gently retract foreskin during washing (never forcibly) — clean the glans and inner prepuce with warm water or an emollient soap substitute only (no soap, shower gel, or antiseptics directly on glans mucosa — disrupts normal flora and causes irritant balanitis). Dry thoroughly after washing — moisture under foreskin promotes candida and bacterial overgrowth. Replace foreskin fully after washing and sexual activity.
Condom use — STI prevention Consistent condom use reduces STI transmission. For latex allergy: polyurethane or polyisoprene condoms (Durex Avanti, Skyn). Avoid spermicide-coated condoms (nonoxynol-9 disrupts mucosal integrity and increases STI transmission risk). Correct technique: leave reservoir tip, check expiry date, use water-based lubricant only (oil-based damages latex).
HPV vaccination Gardasil 9 (9-valent) prevents HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 — reduces HPV infection and HPV-related disease, including genital warts and HPV-associated penile, anal and oropharyngeal cancers, but does not remove the need to investigate penile lesions. Check eligibility (routine programme, catch-up and MSM access via sexual health) against the current NHS/UKHSA programme. Check vaccination status opportunistically in all young men presenting with genital complaints.
HIV PrEP and testing HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP — tenofovir/emtricitabine) should be offered through sexual-health services to people at substantial HIV risk; it is highly effective with adherence. Offer HIV testing according to sexual-health guidance and risk assessment — appropriate in confirmed STIs and many genital ulcer/urethritis presentations — HIV enhances STI transmission and acquisition (mucosal disruption + immune dysregulation). U=U: undetectable HIV viral load on treatment = untransmittable.
Partner notification and re-testing All new STI diagnoses: inform all recent partners. Partner notification and look-back periods should follow the current BASHH infection-specific guideline and be coordinated by sexual-health clinicians. GUM clinic health adviser provides anonymous patient-delivered partner notification. Re-test at 3 months post-treatment for re-exposure.
Diabetes management (balanitis) Recurrent candidal balanitis in diabetes may reflect suboptimal glycaemic control. Optimise HbA1c target (<48 mmol/mol type 2, <53 mmol/mol with comorbidities). SGLT2 inhibitors increase genital mycotic infections (glycosuria) . If recurrent genital candidiasis occurs on an SGLT2 inhibitor, assess glycaemic control, hygiene, phimosis and alternative causes. Discuss the balance of genital-infection burden against the cardiovascular/renal benefits of the SGLT2 inhibitor with the diabetes prescriber; do not switch automatically to metformin.
Lichen sclerosus — self-monitoring Teach ongoing penile self-examination — glans, shaft, inner prepuce. Report promptly: any persistent erosion, ulcer, induration, lump or changing lesion (urgent specialist assessment and possible biopsy). Follow-up according to the dermatology/urology plan. Written patient information (Lichen Sclerosus Support Network). Avoid trauma to affected area (increases fissuring).
Smoking cessation Smoking increases penile cancer risk (HPV persistence, impaired immune surveillance) and worsens lichen sclerosus. Stop Smoking Service + pharmacotherapy (varenicline most effective). Every penile irritation consultation is an opportunity for a brief smoking intervention in current smokers.
SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin) cause glycosuria as their mechanism of action — and glycosuria directly increases the risk of genital mycotic infections (candidal balanitis in men, vulvovaginal candidiasis in women) by providing a nutrient-rich, acidic environment on the genital skin. GPs should specifically counsel diabetic men starting SGLT2 inhibitors about this risk and advise vigilant penile hygiene. If recurrent genital candidiasis occurs on an SGLT2 inhibitor, assess glycaemic control, hygiene, phimosis and alternative causes, and discuss the balance of genital-infection burden against the cardiovascular/renal benefits with the diabetes prescriber — do not switch automatically to metformin. The U=U (Undetectable = Untransmittable) principle is now scientifically established by the PARTNER, PARTNER2, and Opposites Attract studies — these demonstrated no within-couple HIV transmissions during condomless sex when the HIV-positive partner had an undetectable viral load on effective ART. Communicating U=U to patients with HIV in primary care reduces stigma, improves treatment adherence, and reduces transmission anxiety.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

Candidal balanitis — 2–4 weeks
Resolved with treatment? HbA1c result? Recurrent, severe or resistant → HbA1c, confirm the diagnosis, swab, and refer (sexual health, dermatology or urology); long-term antifungal prophylaxis is specialist-directed. Symptomatic partner assessed? SGLT2 inhibitor discussed with the diabetes prescriber if applicable. Recurrent and HbA1c normal + phimosis → consider urology referral (circumcision often curative in recurrent candidal balanitis under non-retractile foreskin).
STI (chlamydia / gonorrhoea) follow-up
Chlamydia: partner(s) tested and treated? Re-test at 3 months (not test of cure — screening for re-exposure). Gonorrhoea: test of cure for pharyngeal infection, persistent symptoms, unknown susceptibility, treatment other than ceftriaxone or pregnancy — NAAT at least 2 weeks after treatment (BASHH 2025). Partner treated? Resistance data from culture reviewed? Re-test at 3 months. Document treatment and outcome in records.
Herpes — after primary episode
GUM clinic referral confirmed? Suppressive therapy needed (based on recurrence frequency, severity and patient preference)? Informed about asymptomatic shedding and transmission risk to partners? Psychosocial impact addressed — herpes diagnosis has significant emotional impact (shame, relationship fears). Written information (BASHH patient leaflets). Support: Herpes Viruses Association (HVA).
Lichen sclerosus — ongoing
Review according to the dermatology/urology plan: any persistent erosion, ulcer, induration, lump or changing lesion → urgent specialist assessment and possible biopsy. Steroid adherence? Urinary flow, meatal involvement, phimosis and sexual function? Circumcision discussed? Patient taught ongoing self-examination. Document findings — any change from baseline = action required.
Persistent / non-healing lesion
Any persistent, ulcerated, indurated, changing or treatment-resistant penile lesion, or a lesion persisting after appropriate STI treatment → urgent urology/uro-oncology (suspected cancer pathway) or dermatology assessment; biopsy is specialist-directed. Do NOT give repeated antibiotic / antifungal courses for non-healing penile lesions as a substitute for biopsy / urology referral. Document referral date.
Syphilis follow-up
RPR titres at 3, 6, 12, 24 months post-treatment (should fall 4-fold by 3 months). Persistent or rising titres = treatment failure or re-infection → GUM review. HIV co-infection (check in all syphilis) — GUM manages; neurological or ocular symptoms need specialist assessment including CSF examination.
999 / A&E safety-net
Foreskin retracted and cannot be replaced over glans (paraphimosis) → 999 immediately. Rapid penile / scrotal skin spreading with extreme pain (Fournier's). Inability to pass urine (urinary retention from phimosis).
Same-day GP
Penile lesion rapidly enlarging or changing appearance · Systemically unwell, rapidly worsening pain/swelling, spreading erythema, necrosis, urinary obstruction, suspected deep infection, failure of appropriate treatment or inability to manage the condition safely · Inguinal lymph nodes newly firm / enlarging in context of penile lesion · Any penile symptom associated with systemic fever in immunocompromised patient
Persistence despite appropriate treatment is the most important safety-netting trigger for this presentation — any persistent, changing or treatment-resistant lesion needs urgent specialist assessment, with biopsy by a clinician experienced in penile dermatoses and cancer, regardless of how benign it appears clinically. This is because penile SCC can mimic all of the benign conditions in this pathway: it can appear as a "chronic balanitis" (red granular plaque), a "chronic lichen sclerosus" lesion (white plaque), a "persistent wart" (papillomatous lesion), or an "ulcer from herpes" (shallow erosion). The biopsy is the only way to definitively exclude malignancy. For patients with lichen sclerosus, the threshold is even lower — any new area of induration, any ulcer, or any nodule in a patient with known LS should trigger urgent urology review within days, not weeks. The RPR (Rapid Plasma Reagin) titre monitoring after syphilis treatment is the serological test of cure — it is a quantitative test that measures the amount of non-treponemal antibody. A 4-fold fall (e.g., from 1:32 to 1:8) by 3 months indicates successful treatment response. Failure to achieve this fall raises concern for treatment failure or re-infection, requiring retreatment. GPs who have treated or are co-managing syphilis should ensure this follow-up serology is arranged and reviewed at each time point.
