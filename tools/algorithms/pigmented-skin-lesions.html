Red Flags — Melanoma, Rapid Change & Amelanotic Lesions

Pigmented lesion scoring ≥3 on the NICE weighted 7-point checklist Major features (2 points each): change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour. Minor features (1 point each): diameter ≥7 mm, inflammation, oozing, change in sensation. → suspected cancer pathway referral. Also refer if dermoscopy (by a trained clinician) suggests melanoma, or if clinical concern is high despite a lower score. If melanoma is suspected, avoid partial biopsy or non-specialist excision. Photography of a low-suspicion lesion supports short-term review only with a defined review date — it must not delay referral.
New flat pigmented lesion with rapid change over weeks in any adult, regardless of ABCDE criteria Rapidly changing pigmented lesion — nodular melanoma can grow quickly and lacks the classic ABCDE features (may appear as uniform dark or amelanotic nodule). → dermatology suspected cancer pathway referral. Nodular melanoma has higher mortality than superficial spreading type due to rapid vertical growth.
Flesh-coloured, pink, or red lesion that is growing, bleeding, or ulcerating — thought to be a benign lesion A new or changing pigmented or non-pigmented nodule, particularly one that is rapidly enlarging, bleeding, ulcerated or symptomatic, should prompt urgent specialist assessment. Consider nodular or amelanotic melanoma, SCC, BCC and pyogenic granuloma. → consider suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE: lesion suggesting nodular melanoma, pigmented or not).
Pigmented lesion in a child or adolescent + new change, irregular border, or bleeding Childhood melanoma is rare but does occur; large congenital naevi and suspected familial melanoma syndromes increase risk. → suspected cancer referral via the local paediatric dermatology pathway.
Nail pigmentation — longitudinal melanonychia with widening band, irregular edges, or Hutchinson's sign (pigment on nail fold) Subungual melanoma. → suspected cancer pathway referral (see nail disorders algorithm).
Melanoma survivor with a new, changing or clinically suspicious lesion Possible recurrence or new primary — previous melanoma lowers the threshold for referral. Discuss with or refer to the specialist melanoma/dermatology service according to the follow-up plan; a lesion meeting NICE suspected-cancer criteria → urgent referral.
Nodular melanoma is responsible for a disproportionate share of melanoma deaths — it grows rapidly, primarily in a vertical (downward) direction rather than radially across the surface, and therefore quickly reaches the dermis and deeper structures, producing early haematogenous metastasis. The critical clinical challenge: nodular melanoma often lacks the classic ABCDE features (asymmetry, border irregularity, colour variation, diameter >6 mm, evolution) because it is small, uniformly dark or amelanotic, and has relatively regular borders in its early stages. The EFG rule (Elevated, Firm, Growing) was developed specifically for nodular melanoma — any elevated, firm, growing nodule should be referred with the same urgency as a lesion scoring ≥3 on the weighted 7-point checklist. Amelanotic melanoma (lacking melanin pigment) may appear as a pink, red, or flesh-coloured lesion and can be misdiagnosed as a granuloma, pyogenic granuloma, dermatofibroma, or angioma. Any growing pink nodule on sun-exposed skin in an older patient needs urgent specialist assessment.

Classification of Pigmented Lesions

Melanocytic lesions
Common naevus (mole): uniform colour (brown or tan), regular borders, symmetrical, typically <6 mm, stable for years. Blue naevus: deep blue-grey colour, dome-shaped, benign. Atypical (dysplastic) naevus: larger (>5-10 mm), irregular borders, multiple shades of brown — atypical naevi are themselves benign but marker of elevated melanoma risk. Congenital naevus: present at birth; large or giant congenital naevi carry an increased melanoma risk — specialist assessment. Spitz naevus: dome-shaped pink or red, rapidly growing in a child — difficult to distinguish from melanoma histologically; refer.
Seborrhoeic keratosis (SK)
Most common benign pigmented lesion in UK — waxy "stuck-on" appearance, tan/brown/black, well-defined borders, rough cerebriform surface, follicular plugging (crypt and fissure pattern on dermoscopy). Reassure and discharge (unless sudden eruption of many SKs = Leser-Trélat sign — rare paraneoplastic feature of internal malignancy).
Vascular and other mimics
Dermatofibroma: firm dermal nodule, dimples with lateral compression (pathognomonic — Fitzpatrick sign), usually lower leg, brown-pink. Angioma: bright red, cherry angiomas (common, benign, on trunk of adults). Pyogenic granuloma: rapidly growing vascular nodule, bleeds easily — refer for excision (can mimic amelanotic melanoma). Lentigo (solar lentigo / age spot): flat brown macule on sun-exposed skin, uniform, stable — common, benign, no treatment needed unless symptomatic or concerning. Lentigo maligna (Hutchinson's freckle): flat, large, slow-growing brown patch with areas of darker and lighter pigment in elderly patients on sun-damaged skin — pre-melanoma; suspected cancer pathway referral.
Actinic keratosis and related
Actinic keratosis (AK): rough, scaly, pink-red lesion on sun-exposed skin — premalignant — a marker of cumulative UV damage that may coexist with or progress to SCC. Sebaceous nevus: yellow-orange hairless plaque on scalp in children — refer for excision in adolescence (risk of basal cell naevus + occasional rare neoplasm).
Lentigo maligna deserves specific attention as a pigmented skin lesion that bridges benign and malignant categories — it is a form of in situ melanoma (melanoma confined to the epidermis) that appears as a large, slowly growing, irregularly pigmented flat patch on sun-damaged skin, typically the face of elderly patients. It may have been present for years before a GP notices it or the patient presents with concern. The difficulty: lentigo maligna (LM) can be extremely difficult to distinguish clinically from a benign solar lentigo (age spot) — both are flat, brown, occur on sun-exposed facial skin. The distinguishing features: LM is larger (typically >10-15 mm), more irregular in shape, has areas of darker pigmentation within a lighter background (satellite spots), and may have lighter areas within it (regression). When LM progresses to invasive melanoma (lentigo maligna melanoma, LMM), there is typically a darker nodular area arising within the flat patch. Any flat irregular pigmented facial lesion in a patient over 60 that has been present for years but is gradually expanding deserves dermatology suspected cancer pathway referral.

ABCDE Criteria, Dermoscopy & Risk Assessment

NICE weighted 7-point checklist (primary-care referral standard)
Major features (2 points each): change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour. Minor features (1 point each): diameter ≥7 mm, inflammation, oozing, change in sensation. Score ≥3 → suspected cancer pathway referral. Also refer if dermoscopy suggests melanoma or a lesion suggests nodular melanoma; if clinical concern is high, refer even if the score is below 3 (NICE NG12).
ABCDE — supplementary prompt
A — Asymmetry: one half does not match the other half. B — Border: irregular, ragged, notched, or blurred edges. C — Colour: variation in colour within the lesion (shades of brown, black, red, white, or blue). D — Diameter: typically >6 mm (the NICE checklist uses ≥7 mm; melanomas can be smaller). E — Evolution: any change in size, shape, colour, or new symptom (bleeding, itching, crusting). ABCDE and the "ugly duckling" sign are supplementary prompts: a single feature may justify referral where concern is high, but not every lesion with isolated asymmetry, a diameter over 6 mm or colour variation needs an urgent cancer referral. "E" (evolution/change) is the most important single criterion — a stable lesion with irregular features may be benign; a changing lesion with regular features may be malignant.
Dermoscopy (polarised light microscopy)
Dermoscopy should be used by healthcare professionals with appropriate training and competence; completing a course alone does not establish competence for all lesion types. Key melanoma dermoscopic features: atypical pigmented network, irregular streaks/pseudopods, regression structures (grey-white areas), blue-white veil, atypical vascular patterns. Reassuring benign features (SK): cerebriform/gyri-sulci pattern, comedo-like openings, milia-like cysts. If the lesion is equivocal, refer rather than reassure.
Risk factors for melanoma
Personal melanoma history, multiple atypical naevi, a strong family history, large congenital naevi, immunosuppression and substantial UV exposure increase melanoma risk — these lower the threshold for specialist assessment but do not replace examination of the individual lesion. Suspected familial melanoma syndromes (e.g. FAMMM) → specialist dermatology/genetics assessment. Fitzpatrick skin type I-II (red/blonde hair, blue eyes, freckles, burns easily). Childhood and cumulative UV exposure. History of sunburn (especially blistering in childhood). Immunosuppression (transplant, HIV, long-term IS therapy). Xeroderma pigmentosum (rare).
Dermoscopy training for GPs represents one of the highest return-on-investment clinical skills in primary care — the RCGP Dermoscopy Certificate course (online + practical) trains GPs to use a hand-held dermoscope to distinguish benign from potentially malignant pigmented lesions, which can improve diagnostic accuracy when used with appropriate training and competence. The NHS benefit: a trained GP dermoscopist can confidently reassure and discharge patients with clearly benign lesions (seborrhoeic keratosis, dermatofibromas, angiomas, common naevi) that would otherwise be referred on the suspected cancer pathway — reducing unnecessary dermatology waiting times. Conversely, trained dermoscopy identifies subtle melanoma features (irregular pigmented network, atypical vascular patterns, regression structures) in lesions that appear benign clinically. The RCGP has an accredited dermoscopy learning pathway. For GPs without dermoscopy, local teledermatology services may allow dermoscopic images to be reviewed remotely by a specialist. If a lesion is equivocal, refer rather than reassure.

Actinic Keratosis & Non-Melanoma Skin Cancer

Actinic keratosis (AK) — management
AK is a premalignant keratinocyte intraepidermal neoplasm (not yet invasive SCC). Features: rough, scaly, pink-red lesion on sun-exposed skin (face, scalp, dorsum of hands, forearms). AK is a marker of cumulative UV damage and may coexist with or progress to SCC. A thick, tender, indurated, rapidly growing, ulcerated or bleeding lesion requires assessment for invasive SCC rather than field treatment alone. Single uncomplicated AK: cryotherapy in primary care if the clinician is competent and the lesion is not suspicious for SCC. Multiple or field change: refer dermatology or prescribe field treatment.
AK field treatment options
Fluorouracil 5% cream (Efudix) — field AK option; regimen depends on the preparation and product licence, anatomical site, extent of field change, immune status, clinician experience and local dermatology protocol (check the SPC). Explain the expected inflammatory reaction, and advise stopping and seeking review for severe pain, ulceration, infection or ocular exposure. Avoid eyelids, mucous membranes and broken skin; do not use where SCC is suspected. Imiquimod 5% cream (Aldara) — immune modulator; dosing is product- and indication-specific — follow the current product information/local formulary. Diclofenac 3% gel (Solaraze) — per SPC; generally better tolerated. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) — specialist, highly effective for extensive field AK.
Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) — GP recognition
Most common skin cancer. Features: pearly pink-white nodule with telangiectasia + raised rolled border + central erosion or ulceration (rodent ulcer), on sun-exposed skin (face, ear, scalp). Morphoeic BCC: ill-defined yellowish plaque, more aggressive. Superficial BCC: thin erythematous patch, scaly, on trunk. Management: suspected BCC usually needs a non-urgent referral through the local dermatology/skin-cancer pathway; consider suspected cancer pathway referral if on a high-risk site or where delay could significantly affect outcome (NICE NG12). Typical low-risk BCC may be managed by an appropriately trained GPwER or under local arrangements, depending on site, size, subtype and local services. Refer high-risk, recurrent, morphoeic/infiltrative, periorbital, perineural, ear, nose, lip or otherwise difficult lesions to specialist care. Suspected SCC is more urgent — consider suspected cancer pathway referral, particularly if indurated, painful, ulcerated, rapidly growing or in a high-risk (e.g. immunosuppressed) patient.
The fluorouracil 5% cream (Efudix) counselling is critical for patient adherence and avoiding premature treatment cessation — 5-FU works by inhibiting thymidylate synthetase, preventing DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing cells (AK keratinocytes). The treatment course causes a predictable inflammatory reaction: the treated skin becomes progressively more red, then blistered and crusted (from approximately day 10-14), reaching maximum inflammation by day 21-28. This reaction is EXPECTED and is a sign that the treatment is working — it represents the immune-mediated destruction of premalignant cells. Patients who are not warned about this reaction will stop treatment prematurely because they believe they are having an adverse reaction. GPs prescribing Efudix must: (1) provide written information about the expected inflammatory process; (2) explain when to stop and seek review — severe pain, ulceration, infection or ocular exposure — and that any interruption should follow product guidance and clinical review; (3) advise sunscreen on treated areas; (4) advise not applying to eyelids/periorbital, intertriginous, mucous membrane or broken skin, and not to a lesion that may be invasive SCC; and (5) book a review at 4 weeks to assess treatment response and consider repeat courses.

Dermatology (routine/urgent)
Suspected BCC · Multiple or refractory AK (field change) · Equivocal lesion not meeting urgent criteria · Large/atypical congenital naevus · Multiple atypical naevi requiring specialist surveillance · High-risk patient where diagnosis or surveillance is uncertain · Spitz naevus in child/adolescent (dermoscopy-equivocal)
GP management
Only when the diagnosis is clinically secure: seborrhoeic keratosis (typical "stuck-on" appearance): reassure and discharge. Dermatofibroma: reassure. Common naevus (stable, benign ABCDE): reassure, sun protection advice, return if change. Solar lentigo: reassure. Single uncomplicated AK (clinician competent, not suspicious for SCC): cryotherapy in primary care. Multiple AK (field): fluorouracil 5% (Efudix) with counselling, per SPC and local dermatology guidance. Low-risk BCC only under an appropriate GPwER/local protocol.
The GP cryotherapy of solitary actinic keratosis is a primary care procedure that reduces unnecessary dermatology referrals — GPs trained in cryotherapy can treat solitary or small numbers of clinically clear AKs in the practice using liquid nitrogen. The technique: single lesion treatment with liquid nitrogen spray or cotton swab, freeze-thaw cycle of approximately 5-15 seconds depending on lesion thickness, producing a halo of 1-2 mm around the lesion. Repeat at 4-6 weeks if residual lesion. The clinical governance requirements: GP confidence in the diagnosis (typical rough scaly lesion on appropriate location — if any doubt, refer rather than treat), documentation of the lesion (description, location, photograph), and follow-up at 4-6 weeks to confirm healing. Contraindications to primary care cryotherapy: uncertain diagnosis, periorbital location (risk to eye), lesion on dark skin (post-inflammatory hypopigmentation), very thick or hyperkeratotic lesions (require curettage or surgical excision rather than cryotherapy).

Melanoma Suspected — Primary Care Protocol

Immediate management of suspected melanoma
If melanoma is suspected, refer urgently to an appropriately experienced skin-cancer service and avoid partial biopsy or non-specialist excision — the specialist pathway determines whether excision biopsy, biopsy of a difficult-site lesion or another diagnostic procedure is required. Photograph the lesion with a scale where locally permitted (referral documentation; photography must not delay referral). Document: exact site, maximum diameter (mm), evolution and duration, symptoms, relevant personal/family history, dermoscopy findings if trained. Refer on the suspected cancer pathway (aims for diagnosis or exclusion within 28 days — do not promise an appointment within two weeks). While awaiting dermatology appointment: advise patient to avoid sun exposure to the area, avoid any further manipulation or treatment to the lesion.
Sun protection — primary prevention
Shade, protective clothing and a hat, avoiding sunburn and avoiding midday sun. Sunscreen of at least SPF 30 with good UVA protection (NICE NG34), applied generously and reapplied — SPF 50+ is reasonable for high-risk patients; sunscreen is an adjunct, not permission for prolonged exposure. Sunbeds: WHO Group 1 carcinogen — avoid.
Patient education on self-examination
Monthly total body skin self-examination (TBSE): examine all skin in a good light including scalp (part with comb), between toes, soles of feet, genitalia, buttocks. Use a full-length mirror + hand mirror for hard-to-see areas. Partner/family skin check for areas the patient cannot see. Seek GP review for: any new changing mole, any lesion that bleeds without trauma. UK melanoma patients: NICE NG14 (updated 2022) recommends specialist follow-up for 1 year after stage IA and for 5 years after stages IB–IV, with visit frequency set by stage.
Benign lesion reassurance protocol
Confirmed benign diagnosis (seborrhoeic keratosis, dermatofibroma, cherry angioma): patient education — explain what the lesion is, why it is benign, what features would require return (change, bleeding). Document diagnosis in clinical record. No routine follow-up needed. High melanoma risk (multiple atypical naevi, strong family history) lowers the threshold for specialist assessment — follow any specialist surveillance plan rather than a routine annual GP TBSE.
The melanoma staging system (Breslow thickness) explains why primary care excision is contraindicated — melanoma staging is determined by the Breslow thickness (the depth of invasion of the melanoma from the granular layer of the epidermis, measured in mm on histology), the mitotic rate, and the presence or absence of ulceration. These measurements require: (1) usually complete excision of the entire lesion rather than partial biopsy (the specialist decides); (2) the specimen must be oriented and processed with margins intact; (3) margins planned by the specialist team, with any wider excision determined by histology. Non-specialist excision of a suspected melanoma may be insufficient for staging and may compromise further management. The correct primary care action: suspected cancer pathway referral, avoiding partial biopsy or non-specialist excision.

Actinic Keratosis Field Treatment & BCC Management

AK field treatment — prescribing guide
Fluorouracil 5% cream (Efudix): regimen per product licence/SPC and local dermatology protocol, depending on site, extent of field change, immune status and clinician experience; applied to the affected field, not just individual lesions. Explain the expected inflammatory reaction, and advise stopping and seeking review for severe pain, ulceration, infection or ocular exposure. Avoid: eyelids/periorbital, mucous membranes, broken skin, intertriginous areas. Do not use where invasive SCC is suspected. Sunscreen essential on treated areas. 4-week review: confirm healing. Re-treatment if persistent lesions.
Imiquimod 5% cream (Aldara) for facial/scalp AK
Dosing is product- and indication-specific — follow the current product information and local formulary rather than a single universal regimen. Local reaction (erythema, flaking) is expected; any interruption or alteration should follow product guidance and clinical review. Use for superficial BCC is specialist/local-protocol dependent.
Cryotherapy (AK) — technique summary
Single lesion: cotton swab or spray (cryo spray gun). Freeze time: 5-10 seconds for thin AK; 10-15 seconds for thick. Allow complete thaw (approximately 1-2 minutes). Single freeze-thaw cycle sufficient for most AKs. Blister formation expected 24-48h post-treatment — reassure patient. Review at 4-6 weeks. Incomplete response: repeat cryotherapy or switch to field treatment.
BCC — management principles
Suspected BCC should be referred through the local dermatology/skin-cancer pathway. Low-risk lesions may be treated by an appropriately trained practitioner under local arrangements. High-risk, recurrent, morphoeic/infiltrative, periorbital, nasal, auricular, lip or perineural lesions require specialist management. Treatment — excision margins, Mohs micrographic surgery for selected high-risk tumours, topical therapy or PDT for superficial BCC — is risk-stratified and specialist/local-protocol dependent (BAD BCC guideline 2021).
Mohs micrographic surgery is a specialist margin-controlled procedure for selected high-risk BCCs, not a GP treatment option — it is particularly useful for BCCs at anatomically critical sites (periorbital, nasal tip, lip, ear) where tissue conservation is aesthetically and functionally essential. The technique: serial horizontal excision of thin layers of tissue, with immediate frozen section histological examination of the entire excision margin at each layer — the surgeon removes only tissue where tumour is identified. It achieves high cure rates while minimising normal tissue sacrifice. It is performed under local anaesthesia as a day case. In UK primary care, the GP's role is to recognise BCC (if melanoma is suspected — suspected cancer pathway referral) and refer to the appropriate service. High-risk facial BCCs are referred to specialist care, which decides whether Mohs surgery is indicated.

Lifestyle

Sun Protection, Surveillance & Patient Education

Sunscreen — an adjunct to shade and clothing NICE NG34 (Sunlight exposure, 2016) advises sunscreen of at least SPF 30 with good UVA protection (at least 4-star); SPF 50+ gives extra margin because most people under-apply. Apply generously and reapply regularly, including after swimming or sweating. Sunscreen is not permission for prolonged sun exposure — combine it with shade, protective clothing and a hat, and avoid sunburn and sunbeds.
Sunbed prohibition Sunbeds are a WHO Group 1 carcinogen — their use increases melanoma risk, particularly with use at a younger age. UK law prohibits sunbed use under age 18 (Sunbeds (Regulation) Act 2010). Any patient who asks about sunbeds should be clearly advised: no safe level of sunbed use exists. Self-tanning (DHA-based creams) and gradual tan (low SPF) products are safe alternatives for cosmetic tanning.
Skin checks for high-risk patients Surveillance for people at higher risk (previous melanoma, multiple atypical naevi, suspected familial melanoma, large congenital naevus, immunosuppression) should be personalised and specialist-led — follow the documented specialist plan rather than a universal annual GP total body skin examination (TBSE). TBSE technique: examine in good lighting in a systematic pattern — head to toe, including scalp (separate with comb), ears, genital area, between toes and soles. Dermoscopy of suspicious lesions.
Total body photography (TBP) for high-risk patients Total body photography (digital mapping of all naevi) allows year-on-year comparison of new or changing lesions — for selected patients with many naevi who cannot reliably track individual lesions. Its use is a specialist decision within personalised surveillance (NICE NG14), not a routine GP recommendation. FotoFinder + Molemax digital dermoscopy systems used in specialist centres.
Childhood sun protection and melanoma prevention School-age children should: wear a hat and UV-protective clothing at outdoor activities, use sunscreen (at least SPF 30 with good UVA protection) on exposed skin, seek shade and avoid midday sun, and avoid sunburn (blistering sunburn in childhood increases melanoma risk). Healthy Start: GP advice at baby/toddler checks — UV protective pram covers + avoiding direct sun for under-1s. Schools: encourage slip, slop, slap culture.
Self-examination education Teach the signs of change (ABCDE as a prompt) at every pigmented lesion consultation. Provide written information (British Skin Foundation leaflet — skinhealthinfo.org.uk). Monthly TBSE self-check: use timer, good lighting, systematic head-to-toe approach, photograph suspicious lesions for tracking. Key return criteria: any lesion that changes in any way, bleeds without cause, itches or crusts persistently, or any new rapidly growing lesion. Urgent return (same week): rapidly growing pigmented or bleeding lesion.
Vitamin D and sunscreen Concern that sunscreen prevents vitamin D synthesis: largely unfounded at population level — realworld SPF sunscreen use (as opposed to lab-perfect application) still allows sufficient incidental UV for vitamin D synthesis in most adults. UK advice: vitamin D 10 micrograms (400 IU) daily in autumn and winter, and year-round for higher-risk groups (e.g. housebound, covering clothing, darker skin). Test vitamin D only where clinically indicated — not routinely because someone uses sunscreen or avoids the sun.
Melanoma follow-up and surveillance Follow-up is stage-based and specialist-led (NICE NG14): stage IA — follow-up for 1 year (commonly two clinic appointments), with no routine imaging or blood tests; stages IB–IV — follow-up for 5 years per stage-specific schedules; personalised follow-up for people at increased risk of further primaries, children/young adults, pregnancy and advanced disease. The GP follows the documented specialist plan rather than creating an independent surveillance programme. BRAF testing and systemic treatment are specialist oncology decisions. GP awareness: side effects of immunotherapy (immune-related adverse events — colitis, thyroiditis, pneumonitis, hepatitis).
The immunotherapy-related adverse events (irAEs) from checkpoint inhibitors used in advanced melanoma treatment are an increasingly important area for GP awareness — as pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) and ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) become standard treatment for stage III-IV melanoma (and are used adjuvantly in resected stage III melanoma), GPs will encounter patients on these agents presenting with immune-related complications. The key irAEs by organ system: GI (colitis — diarrhoea, abdominal pain, bloody stool, fever → high-dose prednisolone 1-2 mg/kg/day + urgent oncology review); endocrine (thyroiditis — check TFTs in any patient on checkpoint inhibitor with fatigue or weight change; hypophysitis — headache + pituitary hormone deficiency; adrenalitis — Addison-like crisis); pulmonary (pneumonitis — new dyspnoea + bilateral infiltrates on CXR → urgent oncology); dermatological (severe rash, bullae, Stevens-Johnson syndrome → dermatology urgently). The key GP principle: any patient on immunotherapy presenting with a new organ-system symptom should have irAE considered and the oncology team contacted — early high-dose corticosteroid treatment prevents severe irAE.

Follow-Up, Surveillance & Safety-Netting

After suspected cancer pathway referral
Suspected-cancer pathways aim for diagnosis or exclusion within 28 days — do not promise an appointment within 2 weeks. Document the date referred and chase if not contacted as expected. If patient declines the referral: document informed refusal with safety-netting. Ensure patient understands: if the lesion changes rapidly, bleeds, ulcerates or becomes painful while awaiting review, contact the GP/dermatology service promptly so the referral can be updated. Attend ED only for acute uncontrolled bleeding, severe infection, airway compromise or another emergency.
Benign lesion surveillance in high-risk patients
Previous melanoma, suspected familial melanoma, immunosuppression or multiple atypical naevi: follow the personalised specialist surveillance plan, with a lower threshold for referral. Photograph a low-suspicion atypical lesion with a scale only with a defined review date. Refer on the suspected cancer pathway if NICE criteria are met.
After melanoma treatment
GP awareness of surveillance schedule (received from dermatology). Monitor for: recurrence (new or changing skin lesion), metastases (lymphadenopathy, systemic symptoms). Follow the specialist surveillance plan (no universal annual GP TBSE); a new, changing or suspicious lesion → discuss with or refer to the specialist service.
AK and BCC follow-up
AK after field treatment: review at 4-8 weeks (confirm healing); review sooner if lesions persist, recur or become thick, tender or ulcerated (assess for SCC). BCC after surgical excision: no routine GP follow-up needed unless recurrence symptoms. Educate patient: scar vs recurrence (BCC recurrence at scar margin — new pearly nodule at wound edge).
Suspected cancer — refer
Weighted 7-point checklist ≥3 · Dermoscopy suggests melanoma · Lesion suggesting nodular melanoma (new or changing nodule, rapidly enlarging, bleeding or ulcerated) · Strong clinical concern despite a lower score · Lentigo maligna (face) · Melanoma survivor with a lesion meeting these criteria
Same-week GP review
Patient declining suspected cancer pathway referral (document + safety-net) · Patient awaiting a suspected cancer appointment with a rapidly worsening lesion — contact GP/dermatology to update or chase the referral (ED only for acute emergencies) · New or changing lesion in a melanoma survivor — discuss with the specialist service per the follow-up plan
The 'EFG rule' for nodular melanoma identification is a clinical tool that complements ABCDE and should be in every GP's skin examination repertoire — Elevated, Firm, Growing. Any nodule on sun-exposed skin that is elevated (raised above the skin surface), firm to palpation (unlike the soft consistency of a seborrhoeic keratosis), and growing (patient or GP observes it is larger than a previous examination or photograph) should be referred on the suspected cancer pathway. The EFG rule has been specifically validated for identifying nodular melanoma, which represents the most dangerous subtype due to its rapid vertical growth phase. A GP who applies only ABCDE criteria and 'reassures' a patient with a firm growing skin-coloured or uniformly dark nodule on the nose or ear because it doesn't have irregular borders or multiple colours has missed nodular melanoma. The rule of thumb: any growing nodule on sun-exposed skin in a patient over 50 needs urgent specialist assessment — consider nodular or amelanotic melanoma, SCC, BCC and pyogenic granuloma.
