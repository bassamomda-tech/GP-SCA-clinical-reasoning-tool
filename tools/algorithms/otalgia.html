1
Safety

Red Flags — Malignancy, Mastoiditis & Dangerous Causes

Normal otoscopy should prompt assessment for referred causes — particularly dental disease, temporomandibular disorder, pharyngeal/laryngeal disease, cervical spine pathology and neuralgic pain. It does not itself prove that the pain is referred. Examine the oral cavity, tonsils, tongue, floor of mouth, neck, TMJ, dentition and cranial nerves.
Post-auricular swelling + otalgia + fever Mastoiditis (loss of post-auricular sulcus, protruding pinna) → same-day hospital/ENT assessment. Hospital management may include IV antibiotics, ear swab, blood tests and CT/MRI if complications, abscess or failure to improve are suspected.
Vesicles on pinna / EAC ± facial weakness Ramsay Hunt syndrome (VZV reactivation in geniculate ganglion) — facial nerve palsy is classically present, but the rash may be delayed or absent. Severe otalgia + vesicles without palsy = herpes zoster oticus, still needing prompt antiviral assessment → prompt antiviral (check renal function) + urgent ENT/hospital advice
Unilateral unexplained head-and-neck pain >4 weeks + otalgia + normal otoscopy NICE NG12 head-and-neck criterion (adults) → urgent suspected cancer pathway referral. Refer earlier where there is dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, persistent sore throat, neck mass, oral lesion, smoking/alcohol risk, progressive symptoms or significant clinical concern.
Facial nerve palsy + otalgia Ramsay Hunt, cholesteatoma, parotid malignancy → same-day ENT/medical assessment if acute onset. Incomplete eye closure → immediate eye-protection advice.
Necrotising (malignant) OE Diabetes / immunosuppression / age over 50 / previous malignancy + severe, persistent or disproportionate (often nocturnal) pain + granulation tissue or polyp in the inferior EAC ± cranial nerve signs or failure of topical treatment → urgent same-day hospital/ENT assessment. Do not manage persistent high-risk disease with repeated topical courses alone.
Sudden hearing loss ± vertigo Sudden SNHL over ≤3 days within the last 30 days → immediate ENT/ED assessment within 24 hours. Presenting later, or rapidly progressive over 4–90 days → urgent ENT/audiovestibular referral within 2 weeks. Severe vertigo with neurological signs → same-day assessment. Do not delay referral for audiometry.
Otalgia + neck mass Metastatic cervical lymph node from head and neck cancer → head and neck suspected cancer pathway referral. Examine oral cavity + throat carefully. A neck mass or persistent oral lesion may independently meet a suspected cancer criterion — do not wait for otalgia to develop.
Otalgia + dysphagia + weight loss Hypopharyngeal or tongue base cancer → head and neck suspected cancer pathway referral. Smoking and alcohol increase risk.
Meningism, severe headache or neurological deficit Suspected intracranial complication → immediate hospital assessment
Systemic illness, sepsis or airway compromise Including foreign body with TM injury → immediate hospital/ENT assessment
Referred otalgia with a normal ear is an important presentation in head and neck oncology — oropharyngeal cancers may present with referred otalgia due to shared sensory innervation (Arnold's nerve — CN X auricular branch, Jacobson's nerve — CN IX tympanic branch, auriculotemporal nerve — CN V3). A normal otoscopic examination in a patient with unilateral otalgia mandates thorough oral cavity and oropharyngeal examination. Necrotising otitis externa typically affects diabetic, older and immunocompromised patients (e.g. HIV, post-chemotherapy) with Pseudomonas aeruginosa osteitis of the skull base. Hospital teams arrange aural toilet, culture, inflammatory markers, imaging and systemic antipseudomonal treatment where indicated (guided by microbiology, imaging, severity and local protocols); not every patient needs admission or IV antibiotics, and biopsy may be needed because malignancy can mimic necrotising otitis externa (ENT UK).

Diagnose

Primary vs Referred Otalgia — Key Distinction

Is the ear normal on otoscopy? Normal ear → assess for referred causes (normal otoscopy does not itself prove referred pain). Abnormal ear → primary otalgia.
Primary otalgia (ear pathology)
Otoscopy shows abnormality. AOM (bulging, opaque, poorly mobile TM with effusion — redness alone is insufficient), OE (tender pinna, swollen EAC), OME (dull retracted TM), perforation, cholesteatoma (white debris, granulation tissue), foreign body
Referred otalgia (distant pathology)
Otoscopy normal. Sources: dental (lower molars — V3), TMJ (pre-auricular pain + clicking), tonsillitis/pharyngeal and laryngeal disease including cancer (several cranial nerve pathways), parotid and submandibular disease, cervical spine (C2/C3), neuralgia, temporal arteritis in older adults, external auditory canal malignancy. GORD/LPR is not a routine cause of isolated otalgia.
Onset and nature
Acute (hours) + fever → AOM, OE. Colicky (swimming) + itchy + discharge → OE. Dull aching + blocked sensation → OME. Sharp stabbing + jaw movement → TMJ / dental. Constant progressive → malignancy.
Associated symptoms
Discharge (otorrhoea — AOM perforation, OE, cholesteatoma), hearing loss (OME, AOM, cholesteatoma), tinnitus, vertigo (labyrinthitis), clicking jaw (TMJ), toothache (dental referred)
Risk factors
Swimming / water exposure → OE. Recent URTI / flying / diving → barotrauma, AOM. Diabetes / immunosuppression → necrotising OE. Smoking + alcohol → head and neck cancer. Age >40 + persistent otalgia. Previous ear surgery, grommets or TM perforation.
Focused history (document)
Onset, duration, progression; unilateral/bilateral; fever and systemic illness; discharge, hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo; recent URTI, swimming, instrumentation, flying or diving; dental pain, chewing pain, bruxism, jaw clicking; dysphagia, odynophagia, sore throat, voice change, weight loss, neck lump; smoking and alcohol; diabetes and immunosuppression; previous ear surgery, grommets or perforation; vaccination history where relevant.
The distinction between primary and referred otalgia starts with otoscopy — this makes proper otoscopic technique critical, but a normal ear is a prompt to look elsewhere, not proof of referred pain. The ear receives sensory supply from five different cranial and spinal nerves (V, VII, IX, X, C2/C3) — each nerve innervates a different anatomical region, making the ear a common site for referred pain from distant pathology. TMJ dysfunction is a common cause of referred otalgia — it is frequently missed because clinicians focus on the ear. The TMJ sits immediately anterior to the external auditory canal and tenderness is palpable pre-auricular on jaw opening.

Diagnose

Differential Diagnosis

Acute otitis media (AOM)
Age 6 months–6 yrs most common (but affects all ages). Acute symptoms (fever, otalgia) + otoscopic evidence of middle-ear inflammation and effusion — bulging, opaque or poorly mobile TM ± perforation with discharge. TM erythema alone is insufficient (crying, fever, instrumentation). AOM and OE may coexist. Post-URTI. Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Moraxella.
Otitis externa (OE)
Swimmer's ear. Tragus tenderness (strongly supports OE; not pathognomonic), swollen EAC, itching, discharge. Look for eczema, psoriasis, furuncle, cellulitis, foreign body and fungal debris. Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus. Diabetics: risk of necrotising OE.
Otitis media with effusion (OME)
Glue ear. Children: dull TM, fluid level, retraction. Usually painless (pressure discomfort). Conductive hearing loss. Associated with adenoid hypertrophy. Adults: persistent, unilateral or unexplained effusion needs ENT assessment (nasopharyngeal pathology).
Ramsay Hunt syndrome
VZV reactivation (geniculate ganglion). Severe otalgia + vesicles on pinna/EAC/palate + facial nerve palsy (classically present; rash may be delayed or absent) ± sensorineural hearing loss. Vesicles + severe otalgia without palsy = herpes zoster oticus. Start antivirals promptly, ideally within 72 hrs.
TMJ dysfunction
Pre-auricular pain worse with chewing/jaw opening, clicking/grinding, clenching/bruxism, morning jaw ache. Normal otoscopy. A common cause of referred otalgia.
Referred — dental
Lower molar pain (mandibular nerve — V3 auriculotemporal branch), dental abscess, impacted wisdom tooth, dry socket post-extraction → otalgia without ear pathology
Cholesteatoma
Chronic painless otorrhoea + conductive hearing loss ± otalgia. Attic perforation or retraction pocket on otoscopy. White keratin debris (pearly white mass). Always refer ENT.
Referred — pharynx
Tonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess, laryngeal, tongue base / hypopharyngeal cancer — referred otalgia may involve several cranial and cervical nerve pathways.
Referred — other
Parotid and submandibular disease, cervical spine pain, neuralgia, temporal arteritis in older adults, external auditory canal malignancy. Do not treat persistent otalgia with a normal ear with repeated ear drops without reassessment.
Fungal OE (otomycosis)
White, grey, green or black debris may suggest it, but appearance is not definitive. Repeated antibiotic/steroid drops can worsen fungal disease.
Cholesteatoma is a critical diagnosis to make in primary care — it is a locally destructive epidermal cyst that erodes ossicles, the mastoid, facial nerve canal, and tegmen (leading to intracranial complications). The classic description of painless, smelly, unilateral discharge should always prompt otoscopy looking for the attic perforation (superior to the pars tensa). Ramsay Hunt syndrome can cause lasting facial nerve palsy — start antiviral therapy promptly, ideally within 72 hours, after checking renal function and contraindications, and arrange urgent ENT or hospital advice. Evidence for corticosteroids in Ramsay Hunt is less robust than for Bell's palsy; consider them only after assessing contraindications and in line with the local pathway. AOM in adults is less common than in children and should prompt consideration of sinister causes if recurrent.

Diagnose

Targeted Examination

Otoscopy (essential)
Inspect pinna and canal before otoscopy for vesicles (Ramsay Hunt), eczema, psoriasis, furunculosis, cellulitis, foreign body, fungal debris, EAC swelling/erythema (OE), wax. Examine both ears. TM: is it visible and intact? Colour, landmarks, bulging/opacity (AOM), retraction (OME), perforation, retraction pocket, cholesteatoma debris (attic). Document whether the TM was visible and intact.
Tragal tenderness
Press tragus and move pinna before otoscopy — pain strongly supports OE (not pathognomonic; can occur with other external-ear conditions). Helps differentiate OE from AOM, but the two may coexist, particularly with discharge.
Post-auricular area
Tenderness over mastoid bone (not to be confused with Battle's sign — mastoid bruising after head trauma), swelling, loss of post-auricular sulcus, pinna pushed forward → mastoiditis → same-day hospital/ENT assessment
Facial nerve
Examine all facial nerve branches and other cranial nerves where indicated — any new weakness = urgent ENT/medical assessment (Ramsay Hunt, cholesteatoma, parotid pathology). Check eye closure; if incomplete, give immediate eye-protection advice.
Oral cavity and oropharynx
Mandatory in all adult referred otalgia (normal ear). Inspect dentition, tonsils, tonsillar pillars, soft palate, posterior pharyngeal wall, tongue base (depress tongue + torch), floor of mouth. Palpate tongue and floor of mouth.
Neck
Cervical lymphadenopathy — size, consistency, tenderness. Palpate parotid and submandibular glands. Jugulodigastric node (level 2) is the primary drainage node for oropharyngeal cancer.
TMJ
Palpate TMJ (just anterior to tragus) — tenderness, crepitus. Ask patient to open/close mouth — clicking, deviation, limited opening (<35 mm = restricted)
Hearing test
Whisper test (free-field hearing screen). Tuning fork: Rinne (air vs bone) + Weber (lateralises to affected ear = conductive, away = sensorineural). These are screening tests, not substitutes for formal audiometry.
Neurological examination
Where symptoms suggest a neurological or vestibular cause (severe vertigo, cranial neuropathy, headache, meningism).
The oral cavity and oropharyngeal examination is the most frequently omitted examination in otalgia — and the most consequential omission, as oropharyngeal cancers (HPV-associated tonsillar and tongue base cancers) are increasing in incidence and frequently present with otalgia alone. Weber test lateralisation is clinically important: sound lateralises to the affected side in conductive hearing loss (OE, AOM, OME) and away from the affected side in sensorineural hearing loss. Mastoiditis is a clinical diagnosis requiring immediate action — waiting for imaging before treatment is inappropriate when there is post-auricular tenderness, swelling, and displaced pinna.
5
Diagnose

Investigations

Ear swab
EAC swab (MC&S) — not required for every uncomplicated first episode of OE. Consider for treatment failure, recurrent or chronic disease, immunocompromise, suspected fungal infection, significant discharge or suspected necrotising OE (swab before systemic antibiotics where possible).
Audiometry
Pure tone audiogram — for hearing loss associated with otalgia, post-AOM resolution at 4–6 weeks (if persistent), suspected OME in adults, persistent or unexplained hearing loss. Sudden or rapidly progressive hearing loss → urgent ENT referral; do not delay referral for audiometry.
Tympanometry
Tympanogram — community audiology. Type B (flat) = OME/perforation. Type C (negative pressure) = Eustachian tube dysfunction. Useful where middle-ear effusion or Eustachian tube dysfunction is suspected — not for every episode of otalgia.
If referred otalgia suspected
Targeted by findings — no routine FBC, CRP, Monospot/EBV serology, dental OPG or imaging. Arrange dental assessment if examination suggests dental pathology. Do not use primary-care investigations or imaging instead of referring a patient who meets an urgent cancer criterion (NICE NG12). Nasendoscopy arranged by ENT if referred on the suspected cancer pathway
NOT routinely
FBC/CRP, EBV serology, OPG, CT/MRI, or tympanometry for every episode of otalgia. Imaging is generally arranged by ENT/hospital teams for cholesteatoma, skull-base disease, mastoid complications, malignancy or unexplained cranial neuropathy.
An ear swab is not needed for straightforward first-episode OE and should not delay treatment — reserve it for treatment failure, recurrence, immunocompromise, suspected fungal or necrotising disease. Persistent or unexplained adult middle-ear effusion warrants ENT assessment (NICE NG98); urgent referral is particularly important where unilateral, persistent after URTI, associated with hearing loss or present in a person of Chinese or South-East Asian family origin (nasopharyngeal pathology). AOM resolution should be confirmed with otoscopy at 4–6 weeks in adults — persistent OME after AOM warrants audiometry and ENT review.
6
Refer

Referral Pathways

Same-day hospital
Mastoiditis (post-auricular swelling + fever or protruding pinna), suspected intracranial complication, necrotising OE (diabetic/immunocompromised + granulation tissue), facial palsy with severe otalgia or vesicles (especially incomplete eye closure), sudden SNHL (≤3 days' onset within 30 days — within 24 hours), severe vertigo with neurological signs, foreign body with TM injury, systemic illness/sepsis/airway compromise
Suspected cancer: head & neck
NICE NG12 (updated April 2026): unilateral unexplained head-and-neck pain >4 weeks associated with otalgia and normal otoscopy; persistent unexplained sore or painful throat; neck mass; oral lesion or ulceration. Refer earlier (below 4 weeks) with dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss or progressive symptoms. Do not wait for otalgia if a neck mass or oral lesion is present.
Urgent ENT referral
Persistent or unexplained adult middle-ear effusion — particularly unilateral, persistent after URTI, with hearing loss, or in a person of Chinese or South-East Asian family origin (NICE NG98)
Urgent ENT (2 weeks)
Suspected cholesteatoma (attic perforation + white debris + offensive discharge), sudden hearing loss presenting after 30 days or rapidly progressive over 4–90 days (ENT/audiovestibular), herpes zoster oticus without facial palsy (prompt antiviral + ENT advice)
Routine ENT
Persistent hearing loss not explained by wax or acute infection, persistent otorrhoea, recurrent AOM (>4 episodes in 12 months) — grommets consideration, recurrent or chronic OE / OE not responding after 2 courses of treatment, chronic eustachian tube dysfunction, persistent TMJ-like symptoms where dental assessment is unrevealing, unresolved symptoms after appropriate treatment
Dental (emergency)
Arrange dental assessment where examination suggests dental pathology (impacted wisdom tooth, dental abscess, dry socket) — OPG not routinely required in primary care. Urgent dental/emergency referral for abscess, spreading facial swelling, trismus, systemic illness or airway risk. NHS 111 dental line for emergency access.
TMJ — self-management first
Most TMJ dysfunction managed conservatively. Dental review if persistent or significant (splints arranged by an appropriately trained dentist). Refer to maxillofacial surgery if: severe trismus, suspected internal derangement, no response to 6–8 weeks conservative management
Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is time-critical (NICE NG98): onset over 3 days or less within the previous 30 days → immediate ENT/ED assessment within 24 hours; later presentation or rapid progression over 4–90 days → urgent ENT/audiovestibular referral within 2 weeks. If SSNHL occurs with otalgia, ENT assess for Ramsay Hunt, labyrinthitis, acoustic neuroma, and perilymph fistula. Cholesteatoma diagnosis in primary care is highly impactful — identifying it early and referring to ENT for mastoidectomy prevents intracranial complications (meningitis, brain abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis) and facial nerve damage. Primary care doctors should view chronic unilateral offensive ear discharge as cholesteatoma until proven otherwise.

Treat

Treatment by Cause

Otitis externa
Analgesia + acetic acid 2% spray
1 spray TDS for 7 days (Greater Manchester antimicrobial guideline). Use a topical antibiotic/steroid preparation where clinically indicated (local formulary; prescribe generically). Consider an ear wick if canal oedema prevents drop penetration. If the TM is perforated, a grommet is present, or its integrity cannot be confirmed, avoid aminoglycoside (gentamicin, neomycin), polymyxin and acidic preparations; use a non-ototoxic quinolone preparation according to local formulary/ENT advice. Oral antibiotics only for spread beyond the canal, systemic illness or selected high-risk disease. Reassess if not clearly improving within 48–72 hours or not resolved after about 7 days.
AOM — adults
Paracetamol + ibuprofen
Adequate analgesia. No antibiotic or a delayed antibiotic strategy for most otherwise well adults. Consider an immediate antibiotic if systemically unwell, at high risk of complications, or symptoms have persisted for about 4 days without improvement. If an antibiotic is needed, follow the Greater Manchester antimicrobial guideline (amoxicillin 500 mg–1 g TDS × 5 days; local alternatives for penicillin allergy). Review if worsening or not improving.
AOM — children (NICE NG91)
Regular paracetamol or ibuprofen
No antibiotic or delayed antibiotic for most. Consider antibiotics more strongly for otorrhoea, or bilateral AOM in children under 2. Immediate antibiotics if systemically unwell, serious disease or high complication risk. Review if symptoms significantly worsen or do not start to improve within about 3 days.
Ramsay Hunt
Prompt antiviral, e.g. aciclovir 800 mg 5× daily × 7 days
Start promptly, ideally within 72 hrs, after checking renal function (dose adjustment in renal impairment), contraindications and drug interactions; or an appropriate alternative. Arrange urgent ENT or hospital advice. Consider corticosteroids only after assessing contraindications and in accordance with the local pathway (not an automatic Bell's palsy protocol). Protect the eye immediately if closure is incomplete (lubricants, tape closed at night). Warn about driving if facial palsy.
TMJSoft diet, jaw rest, avoid gum chewing and wide opening. Heat/ice packs. Simple analgesia or a short NSAID course if suitable. Address clenching, bruxism and stress. Dental review if persistent or significant — any splint is arranged by an appropriately trained dentist. Jaw physiotherapy exercises.
OME (children)Watchful waiting 3 months (most resolve spontaneously). Hearing support in classroom. Formal hearing assessment and tympanometry if persistent or functionally significant (NICE NG233). No routine antibiotics, oral/nasal steroids, antihistamines or decongestants. Grommet decisions are made by ENT according to hearing loss, duration and impact.
OME (adults)Persistent, unilateral or unexplained middle-ear effusion → ENT assessment; urgent if unilateral, persistent after URTI, with hearing loss, or in a person of Chinese or South-East Asian family origin (NICE NG98).
Fungal OEAural toilet and consider topical antifungal treatment or ENT advice. Avoid prolonged empiric cycling through multiple drops.
BarotraumaAdvise swallowing, yawning or gentle autoinflation. Avoid forceful Valsalva. A short course of topical nasal decongestant may be considered for selected adults, subject to contraindications and product limits, but it is not routinely required; no routine oral decongestants. Persistent hearing loss, vertigo, severe pain, otorrhoea or suspected perforation → assess.
Acetic acid 2% spray is an option for OE — an antiseptic with no antibiotic resistance risk. Topical aminoglycosides (gentamicin, neomycin) are potentially ototoxic — avoid them when the TM is perforated, a grommet is present, or TM integrity cannot be confirmed (e.g. swollen or debris-filled canal). Topical quinolones are non-ototoxic but should be reserved for appropriate indications in line with local antimicrobial guidance. NICE NG91 (children) separates paediatric AOM criteria from adult management; for adults use local antimicrobial guidance and clinical review rather than a rigid 72-hour rule.

Lifestyle

Self-Management & Prevention

OE prevention After swimming: tilt head to drain water; a cool or low-heat hairdryer held at a safe distance may be reasonable (no hot air; never insert it into the canal). Keep the ear dry during active OE. Avoid cotton buds (push wax deeper, traumatise skin). Custom earplugs for swimmers. Avoid hearing aids/earphones during OE episode.
Avoid cotton buds Avoid cotton buds and canal trauma. Ears are self-cleaning — earwax migrates naturally. Sodium bicarbonate ear drops (2 weeks) for symptomatic wax softening; refer for microsuction if impacted.
TMJ bruxism Stress reduction, mindfulness, CBT for teeth-clenching. If a splint is indicated, it should be arranged by an appropriately trained dentist. No routine magnesium supplementation.
Infant feeding Give routine infant-feeding advice. Breastfeeding has general health benefits and may reduce some childhood respiratory and ear infections.
Routine immunisations Ensure routine childhood immunisations are up to date. The pneumococcal programme is aimed primarily at preventing invasive pneumococcal disease and pneumococcal respiratory disease, not as an AOM treatment.
Smoking cessation Support smoking cessation and avoidance of tobacco smoke exposure in the household, including around children.
Flying with URTI Avoid flying when significantly congested — Eustachian tube dysfunction + pressure change = severe barotrauma risk. If unavoidable: swallowing, yawning or gentle autoinflation (avoid forceful Valsalva); a short course of topical nasal decongestant may be considered for selected adults, subject to contraindications — not routinely required.
VZV vaccination Shingles vaccine (Shingrix — 2 doses) protects against shingles. Check vaccination status. The NHS programme is being phased towards routine vaccination from age 60, with people remaining eligible until their 80th birthday, and Shingrix is recommended for severely immunosuppressed adults aged 18 and over — check the current cohort in the UKHSA Green Book chapter 28a at the time of use.
The herpes zoster vaccine (Shingrix) protects against shingles and postherpetic neuralgia. Opportunistically check shingles vaccination status in eligible cohorts (UKHSA Green Book chapter 28a). Cotton buds traumatise the canal and can cause foreign bodies and TM perforation — the message "never put anything smaller than your elbow in your ear" is worth repeating at every OE consultation.

Safety

Follow-Up & Safety-Netting

OE — 1 week
Reassess if not clearly improving within 48–72 hours or not resolved after about 7 days — reconsider the diagnosis. Swab if treatment failure, then topical antibiotic/steroid drops per local formulary, prescribed generically (Otomize has been discontinued); non-ototoxic drops if TM integrity cannot be confirmed. Canal occluded → wick. Suspected fungal (white, grey, green or black debris — not definitive) → aural toilet and consider topical antifungal or ENT advice; avoid cycling through multiple drops.
AOM — 72 hours
If antibiotic delayed prescription given: use it if not improving. If not improving: re-examine before changing antibiotics — confirm the diagnosis and assess for mastoiditis, perforation, otitis externa, dental/TMJ pain or another cause. If a second-line antibiotic is clinically indicated after an adequate first-line course, follow local antimicrobial guidance. Persistent perforation at 6 weeks → ENT.
Ramsay Hunt
Urgent ENT or hospital advice at diagnosis; review facial nerve recovery. Eye protection immediately if closure is incomplete (lubricants, nocturnal eye tape); ophthalmology if corneal exposure.
Referred otalgia
Adult with unilateral unexplained head-and-neck pain >4 weeks, otalgia and normal otoscopy = urgent suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12); refer earlier with dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, persistent sore throat, neck mass, oral lesion, smoking/alcohol risk or progressive symptoms. Do not repeat courses of ear treatment without reassessment.
Post-AOM hearing check
Audiometry at 4–6 weeks if hearing not fully recovered after AOM, or if >3 episodes in 12 months (recurrent AOM workup).
999 safety-net
Seek urgent help for: increasing post-auricular swelling; fever or systemic deterioration; severe or disproportionate pain; new discharge with hearing loss; sudden hearing loss; facial weakness; severe vertigo, imbalance or neurological symptoms; inability to open the mouth, swallow or manage secretions; new neck swelling, dysphagia, weight loss or persistent unilateral throat/ear pain
Same-day GP
OE in diabetic patient worsening despite treatment (necrotising risk — same-day hospital/ENT if suspected), new hearing loss in any otalgia patient (sudden SNHL → ENT within 24 hours), severe pain out of proportion to clinical findings
Document
Otoscopic findings, whether the TM was visible and intact, hearing findings, red flags, treatment, referral urgency and review plan.
NICE NG12 recommends urgent head-and-neck referral for unilateral unexplained head-and-neck pain lasting more than 4 weeks with otalgia and normal otoscopy — oropharyngeal cancers (including HPV-associated) may present with otalgia, and earlier referral is appropriate with additional risk factors or red flags. Apart from specified situations such as persistent hoarseness, primary-care investigations for suspected head-and-neck cancer can delay referral. Fungal OE (otomycosis) should be considered when OE fails to respond to topical antibiotic/acidic preparations — white, grey, green or black debris may suggest it, but appearance is not definitive. Arrange aural toilet and consider topical antifungal treatment (e.g. clotrimazole 1% solution) or ENT advice.
