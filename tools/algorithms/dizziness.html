Dizziness in adults — triage and diagnostic approach

AdultsGP + OOHDiagnosis firstv1.0 · Sep 2026
Core rule. "Dizzy" is four different complaints and the word is not a diagnosis. Ask first: does the room spin (vertigo), do you feel faint (presyncope), are you unsteady (disequilibrium), or is it vague wooziness? Then answer the two that kill: is this a posterior circulation stroke, and is this cardiac? Check the pulse in everyone; lying and standing BP for presyncope, falls, older adults or a suspected drug or autonomic cause; ECG for presyncope, syncope, palpitations, exertional symptoms, unexplained collapse, relevant cardiac history or abnormal cardiovascular examination — and a 12-lead ECG for every transient loss of consciousness (CG109). ECG and bloods are not needed routinely for classic BPPV. Timing and triggers separate the causes better than how the patient describes it.

Before any history: is this an emergency now? Stop the consultation and call 999 if any of these is present
New vertigo with any central sign — diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial or limb weakness, limb ataxia, new deafness, Horner's · unable to sit or walk unaided · direction-changing or vertical nystagmus · sudden severe headache or neck pain with the dizziness · GCS <15 or seizure · systolic BP <90, pulse >120 or <40, new irregular rhythm · syncope on exertion or lying down, or preceded by chest pain or palpitations · bleeding with postural collapse · glucose <4 mmol/L uncorrected. ABCDE, observations, ECG if it will not delay transfer, exact onset time, hand over.
1
Define which complaint this is, and its timing Timing, triggers and targeted examination — not the adjective the patient chooses

Which of the four?

Vertigo — illusion of movement. Presyncope — about to faint, greying vision, clammy. Disequilibrium — unsteady only upright or walking. Light-headed — vague, often continuous. Ask which came first.

Timing

Seconds on head movement (BPPV) · minutes (presyncope, arrhythmia, vestibular migraine — dizziness alone is rarely TIA; sudden dizziness with focal signs or new gait ataxia is a possible posterior circulation stroke) · 20 min to hours with hearing change (Ménière's) · hours to days, continuous (neuritis, stroke) · weeks and progressive (central lesion, drug, PPPD) · only on standing (orthostatic).

Triggers

Rolling over or looking up (BPPV) · standing (orthostatic) · exertion (aortic stenosis, arrhythmia) · head turning, cough, micturition (vagal) · visual motion or busy places (PPPD, vestibular migraine) · post-viral (neuritis) · after neck trauma or manipulation (dissection).

Context

Age, frailty, falls · vascular risk, AF, structural heart disease, family history of sudden death under 40 · migraine · new or changed drugs (antihypertensives, nitrates, alpha-blockers, anticholinergics, opioids, gentamicin) · alcohol · anaemia, bleeding, dehydration, diabetes.

Drug-induced orthostatic hypotension · vestibulotoxicity · toxic cause

Aminoglycoside vestibulotoxicity is permanent: stop and seek specialist advice the same day. Suspected CO: remove from the source, 999 if symptomatic.
Recurrent falls or dizziness in a frail older adult, with polypharmacy, cognitive impairment, fracture risk, or a fall with head injury on an anticoagulant. Multifactorial falls syndrome · occult fracture · subdural haematoma

3
Focused examination and investigation Remote assessment that cannot measure a postural BP or examine eyes and gait is an escalation criterion

Measure, targeted

Lying and standing BP (presyncope, falls, older adults, suspected drug or autonomic cause) — supine 5 min, then at 1 and 3 min standing; a fall of ≥20 systolic or ≥10 diastolic, or systolic below 90, is orthostatic hypotension. Pulse and rhythm, RR, SpO₂, temperature, glucose. 12-lead ECG after any loss of consciousness or cardiac marker (CG109), and for presyncope, palpitations or exertional symptoms; not routine in classic BPPV.

Eyes and ears

Nystagmus: direction, whether it changes with gaze, vertical or torsional. Pursuit and saccades. Cover–uncover for skew deviation. Otoscopy both ears. Hearing: whispered voice, Weber and Rinne — document the side of any loss.

Positional and vestibular tests

Dix–Hallpike for posterior-canal BPPV: a few seconds' latency, up-beating torsional nystagmus, fatigues on repetition; supine roll test for horizontal canal. Trained clinician only; avoid in unstable cervical spine disease. HINTS+ only in acute continuous vertigo with nystagmus, by a trained and experienced examiner — never for BPPV; if none is available, immediate stroke-pathway referral.

Neurology, gait and tests

Cranial nerves, power, coordination, finger–nose, heel–shin, Romberg, and walking unaided. Bloods only when targeted to a suspected cause (FBC, U&E, glucose, TFT, LFT, pregnancy test) — none routinely for typical BPPV. Romberg does not reliably separate vestibular from proprioceptive disease. Not in primary care: CT for acute vertigo, or a vestibular sedative in place of a diagnosis.
4
Classify: three exits, not two "Dizziness" and "labyrinthitis" are not diagnoses

Serious cause likely

Any red flag, central sign, cardiac marker, postural drop with volume loss, or progressive deficit. Output: name the concern, use the destination above, record BP, ECG, gait and onset.

Cause established, treatable in primary care

No red flags, normal neurology and gait, and timing and triggers fit one syndrome below with a positive confirming test. Output: syndrome, positive finding, treatment, driving advice, review date.

Unclassified / uncertain

Vague or mixed symptoms, normal examination with persisting disability, recurrent syncope with a normal ECG, or frailty with several contributors. Output: never "just her age" — state the uncertainty, the §6 route, and who reviews when.
5
Syndrome classifier: timing and trigger, then the confirming test Only once the red-flag screen is negative
BPPVSeconds to under a minute, triggered by rolling over, lying down or looking up; no hearing loss; well between attacks. Positive Dix–Hallpike is the diagnosis — do the test rather than treating by description.
Vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitisContinuous vertigo over hours to days with nausea, worse on head movement, settling over days to weeks; often post-viral. Abnormal head impulse, unidirectional nystagmus, able to walk. Hearing loss makes it labyrinthitis — and mandates excluding sudden SNHL and AICA stroke first.
Ménière's diseaseAttacks of 20 minutes to 12 hours with fluctuating unilateral hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness. Needs audiometry and ENT confirmation. Progressive unilateral loss without vertigo is vestibular schwannoma until imaged.
Vestibular migraineRecurrent episodes of 5 minutes to 72 hours with migraine features in someone with a migraine history; triggered by visual motion, sleep loss or hormones. The commonest cause of recurrent spontaneous vertigo, and routinely missed because the headache may be absent in the attack.
Orthostatic and vasovagal — the presyncope familyOrthostatic: dizziness within seconds to 3 minutes of standing, eased by sitting; confirmed by the postural BP above. Ask what is causing it — drugs, dehydration, bleeding, autonomic failure, adrenal insufficiency. Vasovagal: prodrome of warmth, nausea, sweating, tunnel vision; provoked by standing, pain, heat, emotion, cough or micturition; rapid recovery. A clear three-P history (posture, provoking factor, prodrome) with a normal ECG needs no cardiac referral (CG109).
PPPD, and what not to labelPersistent postural-perceptual dizziness: 3 months or more of non-spinning unsteadiness, worse standing, walking or in visually busy places, usually after an acute vestibular event, anxiety or panic; normal examination. A positive diagnosis with its own treatment, not a synonym for anxiety. Do not label by default: "labyrinthitis" for anything continuous (most are neuritis, some are stroke) · "cervical vertigo" without neck findings · "anxiety" while any measurement is abnormal · disequilibrium in frailty is real but is a list of fixable contributors — vision, neuropathy, drugs, strength.

Refer routinely from this consultation Not same-day, not 999 — the cases that leave by letter; what follows in §7 is what primary care treats today
Asymmetric or progressive sensorineural hearing loss with imbalance → urgent ENT/audiology for MRI of the internal auditory meati (vestibular schwannoma, NG98) · suspected Ménière's → ENT with audiometry before long-term treatment · BPPV failing two Epley manoeuvres, or horizontal-canal BPPV → ENT or vestibular physiotherapy · neuritis beyond 6 weeks or PPPD → vestibular rehabilitation (SSRI in parallel for PPPD) · vestibular migraine uncontrolled after prophylaxis → neurology · unexplained recurrent syncope, normal ECG, no red flag → cardiology for ambulatory monitoring or tilt testing (CG109) · recurrent falls, or a fall with injury in frailty → multifactorial falls assessment (NG249).
7
The only treatment decisions that belong in this consultation What primary care treats today
BPPV, positive Dix–HallpikeEpley manoeuvre at the same visit (trained clinician; avoid in unstable cervical spine disease) — usually effective and may give rapid relief; symptoms may recur and repeat treatment may be required. Vestibular sedatives do not treat BPPV and impair compensation: do not prescribe them for it. Teach Brandt–Daroff exercises and review at 4 weeks.
Acute vestibular neuritis, first daysProchlorperazine, cinnarizine or cyclizine for symptoms, short course only — stop within about 3 days, as longer use delays central compensation. Buccal prochlorperazine if vomiting. Avoid prolonged use, particularly in older adults (sedation, falls, anticholinergic and extrapyramidal effects); check doses against the SPC/local formulary. Mobilise early. Betahistine has no role. Persisting at 6 weeks → section 6.
Orthostatic hypotensionTreat the cause: reduce or stop the culprit drug (highest yield), correct volume, treat bleeding or infection. Correct dehydration and anaemia; rise slowly in stages, physical counterpressure, avoid large hot meals and alcohol, consider compression hosiery. Extra salt or fluid only after considering heart failure, CKD, hypertension, electrolytes and supine hypertension. Refer for specialist treatment if persistent or neurogenic.
Ménière's, confirmedRefer for audiological/ENT assessment; discuss lifestyle measures and local specialist treatment options. Evidence for betahistine and dietary restriction is limited; review treatment according to attack frequency, hearing status and patient preference. Prochlorperazine for an acute attack only. Do not start long-term betahistine on an unconfirmed label.
Stroke, arrhythmia, sudden hearing loss, dissection, GI bleed, sepsisTransfer or the same-day route is the treatment. Do not give a vestibular sedative to a patient with central signs — it masks the picture and delays the scan. Do not start or stop an anticoagulant unilaterally. Do not treat new unilateral deafness with vertigo as labyrinthitis.

Endpoint and documentation Finish with one of four conclusions

1 · Time-critical serious

999 route taken; suspected diagnosis, onset, central signs, whether the patient walks unaided, observations, ECG, glucose, drugs and times.

2 · Serious possible

Same-day acute, ENT or cardiology assessment arranged; the question being excluded, postural BP, ECG result, who was spoken to, deadline.

3 · Cause established

Syndrome with its positive test; treatment including any manoeuvre performed; drugs stopped; driving advice recorded; review date.

4 · Unclassified

Uncertainty stated; §6 referral or NG12 route sent, with date; interim safety advice; review owner and timeframe; escalation criteria.
Why a primary-care diagnosis is safe today, in one line: no row on the page-1 screen applies · pulse recorded; postural BP and glucose where indicated · ECG done where there was any loss of consciousness, presyncope, palpitations or exertional symptoms · neurology, gait and hearing normal and the patient walks unaided · timing and trigger fit one syndrome with a positive confirming test · drugs reconciled · written safety-net and a booked review. Driving — record it every time: do not drive if dizziness, vertigo, imbalance, sedating medication or an unpredictable episode could impair safe control of the vehicle; advise the patient to check current DVLA guidance — notification is required where episodes are sudden, disabling and unprovoked/unprecipitated, or another relevant diagnosis applies (Group 2 stricter); after a single unexplained syncope driving stops pending assessment. Safety-net wording: call 999 for weakness, numbness, slurred speech, double vision, severe headache or neck pain, inability to walk unaided, or fainting on exercise or lying down; same-day contact for new deafness or tinnitus in one ear, black stools, chest pain, palpitations, or dizziness worsening rather than settling.
OOH / remote limitation. Convert to face-to-face or emergency assessment if a lying and standing BP, pulse, glucose or ECG cannot be obtained; the eyes, gait and hearing cannot be examined; the vertigo is continuous and present during the call; there was a loss of consciousness; the patient cannot walk unaided or describes new deafness, headache or neck pain; or the patient is frail or anticoagulated.
Clinical decision support only; follow local emergency-transfer and referral policies and check doses against the current BNF. 1
Safety

Red Flags — Exclude Life-Threatening Causes First

Dizziness is a common presentation in general practice. Before characterising type, screen for catastrophic causes that must not be missed. Act on any single red flag immediately.

Sudden severe headache Worst headache of life, thunderclap onset → 999 Subarachnoid haemorrhage until proven otherwise
Focal neurological deficit Facial droop, limb weakness, dysarthria, diplopia, ataxia → 999 Stroke / posterior fossa lesion (FAST+)
Acute severe vertigo + headache Cerebellar signs: finger–nose ataxia, nystagmus (direction-changing) → 999 Cerebellar stroke — HINTS only if trained and experienced; if no competent examiner, refer immediately for stroke assessment
Syncope / presyncope + chest pain or palpitations Cardiac arrhythmia, aortic stenosis, HCM → 999 or same-day ECG + cardiological review
Postural hypotension causing falls / collapse SBP drop ≥20 mmHg standing, loss of consciousness → Same-day assessment; exclude Addisonian crisis, haemorrhage
Acute deafness + tinnitus + vertigo Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) → Immediate referral NICE NG98: sudden loss (over 3 days or less) within the past 30 days → seen within 24 hours (ENT, audiovestibular medicine or emergency care); sudden loss more than 30 days ago, or rapidly worsening over 4–90 days → urgent referral within 2 weeks
New nystagmus + vertical/direction-changing Direction-changing nystagmus or pure vertical nystagmus → 999 Central cause — brain stem / cerebellum; immediate stroke/neuroimaging assessment, imaging modality per the hyperacute stroke pathway (MRI often required)
Unintentional weight loss + constitutional symptoms Night sweats, lymphadenopathy, anorexia → Suspected cancer Exclude haematological or systemic malignancy
Disequilibrium in elderly + acute confusion Sepsis, intracranial bleed, hyponatraemia → Same-day urgent bloods + clinical review
Recent head trauma Post-concussive dizziness, subdural haematoma → assess using the current NICE head-injury pathway (NG232). Emergency imaging or assessment if head-injury CT criteria are met, especially with anticoagulation, age-related risk, worsening headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness or focal neurology. Not every post-traumatic dizziness needs same-day CT.
Neck pain + vertigo (esp. young / after neck trauma, manipulation) → 999 Vertebral artery dissection — a leading cause of posterior stroke in the under-50s; do not attribute to 'cervicogenic dizziness'
Posterior circulation stroke can mimic benign vertigo. In acute vestibular syndrome, the HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) helps distinguish cerebellar stroke from vestibular neuritis only when performed by a clinician trained and experienced in its use (NICE NG127) — an abnormal head impulse test (HIT+) favours a peripheral cause; a normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation should prompt immediate stroke/neuroimaging referral. Do not describe a "negative HINTS" as reassuring if the examination has not been performed correctly. SSNHL needs to be seen within 24 hours (NG98); NICE says to consider steroids for idiopathic SSNHL, but GPs should not routinely initiate systemic steroids outside the local ENT protocol and contraindication assessment (the Cochrane review, Wei et al., 2013, found the evidence of benefit uncertain). Missing a cardiac arrhythmia is dangerous, especially in older people on QT-prolonging drugs.
2
Diagnose

Characterise the Dizziness — Which Type Is This?

The single most important diagnostic step is asking the patient: "What do you mean by dizzy?" The character of dizziness determines the likely diagnosis and drives the rest of the pathway.

Key question
"Can you describe exactly what you feel?" — avoid leading with "spinning" or "lightheaded". Let them describe unprompted. Record verbatim.
Vertigo
Illusion of movement (world or self spinning/rotating). Implies vestibular cause. Ask: onset, duration per episode, triggers (position, movement), associated hearing loss / tinnitus / ear symptoms.
Presyncope / near-faint
Feeling about to black out, greying of vision, legs giving way. Implies haemodynamic / cardiac / vasovagal cause. Ask: postural component? Triggers (cough, micturition, exertion, prolonged standing)? If there was actual transient loss of consciousness, follow the TLoC pathway (CG109): 12-lead ECG and urgent cardiovascular referral for red flags (ECG abnormality, exertional TLoC, heart failure, murmur, family history of sudden cardiac death, unexplained breathlessness).
Disequilibrium
Unsteadiness / imbalance on standing or walking, not true spinning. Implies cerebellar, proprioceptive, musculoskeletal, or multifactorial (esp. elderly). No abnormal sensation when sitting/lying.
Non-specific / vague
Floating, fogginess, "spacey", rocking sensation. Often anxiety, hyperventilation, medication side effect, anaemia, chronic fatigue. Assess mental health, medication review essential.
Duration per episode
Seconds: commonly BPPV, but brief arrhythmias and other causes occur  |  Minutes: migraine or arrhythmia  |  Hours: Menière's disease or vestibular migraine (5 minutes to 72 hours, NG127)  |  Days: vestibular neuritis, labyrinthitis or stroke. Duration guides but does not decide. Dizziness alone is not usually a TIA — sudden dizziness with new gait ataxia, dysarthria, diplopia, unilateral weakness, sensory deficit or new hearing loss is a possible posterior circulation stroke.
Symptom diary
For recurrent episodes: request 2-week symptom diary logging episode date/time, duration, severity (0–10), triggers, associated symptoms, effect on function. Invaluable for Menière's/migraine assessment.

Pathway at a glance — keep dizziness, syncope, acute vestibular syndrome, hearing loss and falls separate

1 · Define the symptom
Vertigo · presyncope · disequilibrium · non-specific dizziness.
2 · Screen for emergencies
Sudden focal neurological signs · new inability to walk · vertical or direction-changing nystagmus · sudden hearing loss · thunderclap headache · exertional syncope/presyncope · abnormal ECG · haemodynamic instability · recent head injury meeting NICE CT criteria.
3 · Identify the time pattern
Brief, triggered: BPPV · continuous hours–days: acute vestibular syndrome · recurrent spontaneous minutes–hours: vestibular migraine/Menière's/arrhythmia · postural near-faint: orthostatic/reflex/cardiac · persistent imbalance: multifactorial, vestibular, neurological or medication-related.
4 · Examine selectively
Postural BP when presyncope/falls suspected · ECG where a cardiovascular cause is plausible · gait, cerebellar signs, cranial nerves and nystagmus · Dix–Hallpike for suspected BPPV · otoscopy, hearing assessment and tuning fork tests where auditory symptoms are present.
5 · Treat the cause
Epley for confirmed BPPV · short-term antiemetic/vestibular suppressant only for severe acute symptoms · vestibular rehabilitation after the acute phase · medication review and orthostatic measures · ENT/audiological assessment for hearing loss or suspected Menière's · migraine-directed management for likely vestibular migraine · falls assessment and progressive strength/balance exercise for older adults.
Dizziness is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Failure to characterise type is the most common GP error in managing dizziness. Timing and triggers narrow a long differential to a short list. The TITRATE framework (Timing, Intensity, Triggers, Associated symptoms) gives structured enquiry, and is more useful than a "let's do some tests" approach. A symptom diary helps characterise recurrent unexplained dizziness.

Diagnose

Classify the Cause — Apply Diagnostic Framework

Using dizziness type from Step 2, apply the following differential framework. Most primary care dizziness is peripheral vestibular, vasovagal, or medication-related — but confirm systematically.

BPPV
Most common
Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo. Episodes <1 min triggered by head position change (rolling over in bed, looking up). No hearing loss. Age 40+. Positive Dix-Hallpike confirms. A common cause of vertigo in primary care. Do not use HINTS for BPPV.
Vestibular neuritis
Post-viral. Acute severe continuous vertigo lasting days–weeks. Recent URTI often precedes. No hearing loss (cf. labyrinthitis which has hearing loss too). Horizontal nystagmus fast phase away from affected ear. HINTS (trained and experienced examiner only): ABNORMAL head impulse test (corrective saccade) is consistent with a peripheral cause — reassuring only as part of a correctly performed full HINTS; a NORMAL head impulse in constant vertigo is the central alarm.
Menière's disease
Triad: episodic vertigo (20 min–12 h), fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, roaring tinnitus ± aural fullness. Diagnosis requires ≥2 episodes + audiological confirmation. Refer ENT/audiology.
Vestibular migraine
Episodic vertigo (5 minutes to 72 hours, NG127) + headache OR photophobia/phonophobia. Often misdiagnosed as Menière's. History of migraine. No consistent audiological change. Most underdiagnosed vestibular cause in primary care.
Vasovagal / orthostatic
Presyncope. Prodrome: nausea, pallor, sweating. Positional (lying→standing). Triggers: pain, emotion, dehydration, prolonged standing. HR/BP response on standing. Tilt-table testing if recurrent.
Medication-related
Review ALL medications. Offenders: antihypertensives (postural hypotension), antiepileptics, antihistamines, aminoglycosides (ototoxic), loop diuretics, SSRIs (especially on initiation), benzodiazepines.
Cardiovascular
Arrhythmia (AF, SVT, complete heart block), AS, HCM. Dizziness on exertion = cardiac until proven otherwise. Palpitations, syncope, family history of sudden death. ECG required when a cardiovascular cause is plausible.
Anxiety / hyperventilation
Persistent, non-episodic, worse in crowds/enclosed spaces, associated with breathlessness, tingling extremities, PHQ-9/GAD-7 elevated. Diagnose only after organic causes and abnormal measurements are addressed; consider PPPD.
Multifactorial (elderly)
Comprehensive falls assessment (NG249) — based on age, falls history, frailty, injury and gait/balance impairment; do not use falls-risk prediction tools. Often combination of: presbyvestibulopathy, cataracts, peripheral neuropathy, polypharmacy, musculoskeletal weakness. No single diagnosis — requires holistic assessment + MDT.
Correct classification determines treatment entirely. BPPV is treated with the Epley manoeuvre, which is usually effective — no drugs needed. Vestibular neuritis may need a short course of vestibular suppressant for severe symptoms, then rehabilitation. Menière's needs audiological/ENT assessment; evidence for betahistine and dietary restriction is limited. Vestibular migraine follows migraine principles and is often specialist-led. Medication-related dizziness often improves with deprescribing. Getting this step wrong means patients receive the wrong treatment for months. Classification also determines referral pathway and urgency.
4
Diagnose

Targeted Examination — Directed by Suspected Cause

Examination should be targeted based on the history. Do not attempt all components in one consultation — prioritise based on suspected type. Document findings clearly for medico-legal purposes.

Postural BP + HR
Highly appropriate in presyncope, falls, older adults and suspected autonomic/medication-related dizziness (not needed in every patient). Lying → standing, after 1 min and 3 min. Orthostatic hypotension: SBP drop ≥20 mmHg or DBP drop ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing. A heart-rate rise of ≥30 bpm alone does not diagnose POTS — POTS requires sustained orthostatic tachycardia (usually ≥30 bpm within 10 minutes of standing) without orthostatic hypotension, chronic orthostatic symptoms and exclusion of other causes; diagnosis is specialist-led. Record both arms if aortic dissection suspected.
Cardiovascular
Rate, rhythm, murmurs (aortic stenosis — ejection systolic radiating to carotids), JVP. 12-lead ECG: PR interval, QRS width, QTc, delta waves (WPW), ST changes, axis. AF, complete heart block, long QT.
Nystagmus assessment
Direction: Horizontal peripheral nystagmus (fast phase away from affected ear) → vestibular neuritis. Vertical or direction-changing nystagmus → central warning sign → immediate stroke/neuroimaging assessment (not an automatic CT head). Observe with and without fixation (Frenzel lenses optimal).
Dix-Hallpike test
Perform if BPPV suspected. Positive: torsional, upbeat nystagmus within 5–20s, duration <1 min, fatigable on repeat. Confirms posterior canal BPPV. Negative Dix-Hallpike does not exclude horizontal canal BPPV — perform a supine roll test when the history strongly suggests positional vertigo. Trained clinicians only; avoid in unstable cervical spine disease, and use caution with severe neck disease, vertebrobasilar insufficiency or inability to tolerate neck extension. Do not use HINTS for BPPV.
HINTS exam
Warning: HINTS is unsafe to interpret without specific training. For acute vestibular syndrome (sudden persistent vertigo, nausea/vomiting, gait unsteadiness, usually spontaneous nystagmus, lasting hours to days), use HINTS only if trained and experienced. If no competent HINTS examiner is available, refer immediately for stroke assessment. HINTS is not appropriate for brief, triggered positional vertigo, presyncope, chronic imbalance or isolated dizziness. H=Head Impulse Test (abnormal=peripheral), I=nystagmus (unidirectional=peripheral), TS=Test of Skew (vertical skew=central). A normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation → immediate stroke/neuroimaging referral (999).
Cerebellar signs
Finger–nose test, heel–shin test, rapid alternating movements, gait assessment. Ataxia + nystagmus = cerebellar lesion until proven otherwise → immediate stroke/neuroimaging assessment per the hyperacute stroke pathway. Use Romberg as part of a broader neurological and gait assessment. It does not reliably distinguish vestibular from proprioceptive disease, and marked gait/truncal ataxia with eyes open is a central red flag.
Hearing assessment
Whisper test (stand behind, 60 cm, 2–3 syllable numbers). Rinne and Weber tuning fork tests (512 Hz). Rinne negative (BC>AC) = conductive loss. Weber lateralises to bad ear = conductive; good ear = sensorineural. Refer audiology if abnormal.
Otoscopy
Cholesteatoma (keratin debris, attic perforation), otitis media with effusion, wax impaction. Cholesteatoma causes chronic vertigo + foul discharge — ENT urgent referral.
Gait and balance
TUG test (Timed Up and Go) can support gait/balance assessment, but no single TUG time is a universal threshold for significant fall risk. Observe: antalgic, broad-based (cerebellar), steppage (peripheral neuropathy), slow shuffling (Parkinsonism). In older adults, offer comprehensive falls assessment based on age, falls history, frailty, injury and gait/balance impairment (NG249).
Cranial nerves
If central cause suspected: CN III (ptosis, diplopia), CN VI (lateral gaze palsy), CN VII (facial weakness). Lateral medullary syndrome (PICA stroke): ipsilateral facial + contralateral limb sensory loss, Horner's, dysphagia, ataxia.
A positive Dix-Hallpike test supports BPPV — it removes the need for imaging and justifies an Epley manoeuvre. The HINTS exam performs well for central cause only in trained and experienced hands (Kattah et al., NEJM 2009; NICE NG127) — it is unsafe to interpret without specific training. Postural BP is often the treatable cause in older patients with presyncope or falls. Omitting neurological and gait examination in new dizziness risks missing posterior circulation stroke.
5
Diagnose

Investigations — Selective, Not Reflexive

Most dizziness diagnoses are clinical. Over-investigation delays treatment and causes anxiety. Order investigations to confirm, risk-stratify, or exclude specific diagnoses — not as a default screen.

Classic BPPV
Dix–Hallpike; no routine bloods or imaging.
Presyncope/syncope
Lying/standing BP, ECG, cardiovascular assessment.
Acute vestibular syndrome
Full neurological/gait examination; trained HINTS or immediate stroke referral.
Hearing loss/tinnitus
Otoscopy, tuning fork/audiological assessment; urgent pathway if sudden.
Falls/disequilibrium in older adult
Falls assessment, medication review, gait/balance, vision, hearing and cardiovascular assessment.
Suspected systemic cause
Targeted FBC, U&E, glucose, TFTs, LFTs, etc.
ECG Targeted
Perform ECG for presyncope, syncope, palpitations, exertional symptoms, unexplained collapse, relevant cardiac history or abnormal cardiovascular examination. It is not required routinely for classic BPPV or uncomplicated peripheral vestibular disease. Check: QTc (prolonged >450 ms M / >470 ms F — drug review), AF (irregularly irregular), complete heart block (PR prolonged, dropped beats), Brugada, delta waves (WPW), LBBB (structural disease).
Bloods — targeted
Not required for all dizziness; a patient with typical BPPV does not need routine bloods. FBC: suspected anaemia or bleeding. U&E: suspected dehydration, renal disease, electrolyte disturbance or medication toxicity. Glucose: diabetes, compatible symptoms or acute illness. TFTs: symptoms/signs of thyroid disease or relevant medication. LFTs: suspected liver disease or alcohol-related illness. Pregnancy test where clinically relevant. Troponin/D-dimer only when clinically indicated.
Postural BP monitoring
Do not arrange routine 24-hour ABPM for every person with confirmed orthostatic hypotension. First address medication, volume depletion, anaemia, autonomic disease, cardiac causes, and postprandial and environmental triggers. ABPM may help in selected cases (e.g. Parkinson's-related autonomic failure).
Audiology referral
If: asymmetric hearing loss, Menière's suspected, tinnitus + vestibular symptoms. Pure tone audiogram (PTA) shows low-frequency sensorineural loss in Menière's. Routine audiology referral unless sudden or rapidly worsening hearing loss (NG98 urgent pathways).
MRI brain + IAMs
MRI of the internal auditory meati, directed by: asymmetric or unilateral sensorineural hearing loss, unilateral persistent tinnitus, neurological signs, atypical features, or concern about vestibular schwannoma. Suspected central cause → stroke/neuroimaging pathway. NOT: routine for typical Menière's disease solely because symptoms persist, or first-line for classic BPPV or clear vasovagal. CT head after trauma only if NICE head-injury CT criteria (NG232) are met.
Holter/24h ECG
Arrange if: palpitations + presyncope, structurally normal heart but episodic arrhythmia suspected, syncope of unknown cause. 7-day event recorder preferred for infrequent episodes. Loop recorder (ILR) for recurrent unexplained syncope via cardiologist.
Tilt-table test
Via cardiology — for recurrent unexplained syncope/presyncope where vasovagal suspected but diagnosis unclear. Not a GP-initiated test. Document clinical history clearly in referral letter.
Do NOT order
Avoid: Routine CT head for classic BPPV or vasovagal. MRI for first-episode positional vertigo with positive Dix-Hallpike. Carotid Dopplers for isolated dizziness (not validated). Vitamin panel without clinical indication. Vestibular function tests (caloric) — specialist-initiated only.
Investigations in dizziness should be hypothesis-driven. BPPV is a clinical diagnosis — the Dix-Hallpike has higher positive predictive value than CT/MRI in classic presentations. Unnecessary MRI requests for BPPV cost the NHS a substantial amount each year. ECG is essential when the history suggests a cardiovascular cause (presyncope, syncope, palpitations, exertional symptoms), and QTc prolongation from commonly prescribed drugs (macrolides, antidepressants, antihistamines) is an underrecognised cause. Bloods are targeted to the suspected cause rather than used as a screen. If audiology confirms low-frequency SNHL, Menière's diagnosis advances significantly and appropriate ENT management begins.
6
Refer

Referral Criteria — Who, Where, and How Urgently

Most peripheral vestibular and vasovagal dizziness is managed in primary care. Use the grid below to determine when and where to refer. Document reason and urgency clearly in all referral letters.

999 / A&E
Focal neurological signs, direction-changing nystagmus, cerebellar ataxia, sudden severe headache, acute SSNHL (if same-day ENT unavailable), haemodynamic collapse, new AF with rapid ventricular response, complete heart block.
Same-day
Acute SSNHL (sudden loss over 3 days or less, within the past 30 days) — seen within 24 hours by ENT, audiovestibular medicine or emergency care (NG98). Severe postural hypotension with falls and injury risk. Dizziness after head trauma meeting NICE head-injury CT criteria (NG232), including anticoagulated patients. Arrhythmia on ECG requiring cardiology input. Syncope with CG109 red flags (ECG abnormality, heart failure, exertional syncope, family history of sudden death under 40, new breathlessness or murmur) → specialist cardiovascular assessment within 24 hours. Severe Menière's attack with vomiting — may need IV prochlorperazine in acute trust setting.
2 weeks
Sudden hearing loss more than 30 days ago, or hearing loss worsening rapidly over 4–90 days → urgent referral within 2 weeks (NG98). Asymmetric SNHL + tinnitus ± dizziness (vestibular schwannoma) → urgent ENT/audiology for MRI of the internal auditory meati (NG98; not a suspected-cancer pathway). Atypical nystagmus not fitting peripheral pattern — urgent MRI referral via neurology.
Routine ENT
Suspected Menière's disease — audiological/ENT assessment; thiazides, intratympanic treatment and surgery are specialist-led decisions. Chronic otitis media with hearing loss. Cholesteatoma (urgent routine if suspected).
Routine Neurology
Vestibular migraine not responding to primary care management. Recurrent unexplained episodic vertigo where Menière's/migraine not confirmed. New-onset central nystagmus (after acute exclusion). Multiple sclerosis suspected.
Routine Cardiology
Palpitations + presyncope + structurally normal heart (for Holter/loop recorder). Unexplained recurrent syncope. Suspected POTS (chronic orthostatic symptoms with sustained orthostatic tachycardia, usually ≥30 bpm within 10 minutes of standing, without orthostatic hypotension — specialist-led diagnosis). Brugada or WPW on ECG.
Audiology
Asymmetric hearing loss, bilateral hearing loss affecting function, tinnitus + hearing impairment. Pure tone audiogram required for Menière's diagnosis — refer early in the pathway.
Falls service / Physio
Older patient with disequilibrium, gait/balance impairment, frailty, injury or previous falls — comprehensive falls assessment and progressive, individually tailored strength and balance exercise (NG249). Vestibular rehabilitation (VR) programme — evidence-based for chronic unilateral vestibular loss, post-vestibular neuritis recovery. Branded as "OTAGO" or "FallSafe" locally.
Primary care manage
Classic BPPV (perform Epley manoeuvre). Vasovagal/positional hypotension (lifestyle modification + medication review). Anxiety-related dizziness (CBT referral, NHS Talking Therapies). Medication side effect (deprescribe or substitute). Vestibular neuritis in recovery phase (VR exercises).
Much dizziness is safely managed in primary care when correctly assessed. However, vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma) presents with unilateral SNHL + tinnitus; early MRI detection (via urgent ENT/audiology referral) improves outcomes and preserves facial nerve function. Vestibular rehabilitation improves chronic unilateral vestibular dysfunction symptoms (McDonnell and Hillier, Cochrane 2015). Falls assessment and strength and balance programmes are recommended by NICE NG249. Timely Menière's ENT/audiology referral allows specialist treatment options to be considered.
7
Treat

Treatment — Cause-Specific Pharmacological Pathways

Treatment is entirely diagnosis-dependent. Do not prescribe vestibular sedatives as a default — they delay vestibular compensation and worsen outcomes in central or chronic causes. Match treatment to diagnosis.

BPPV — first line
Epley Canalith Repositioning Manoeuvre in clinic (trained clinician; avoid in unstable cervical spine disease). Epley is usually effective and may provide rapid relief; symptoms may recur and repeat treatment may be required. Can self-teach Brandt-Daroff exercises at home. No medication needed. Betahistine NOT evidence-based for BPPV. Review at 4 weeks — re-Epley if persists.

Acute Vestibular Syndrome (Vestibular Neuritis / Labyrinthitis)

If required for severe acute vertigo or vomiting, use a vestibular suppressant/antiemetic for the shortest possible duration, usually no more than 2–3 days. Avoid prolonged use, particularly in older adults because of sedation, falls, anticholinergic effects and extrapyramidal reactions. Check specific doses against the current SPC/local formulary.

Acute phase (<72h, severe)
Prochlorperazine Short course
Buccal (dose per current SPC/BNF/local formulary) for max 2–3 days; IM (dose per BNF) if vomiting prevents buccal use. DO NOT continue beyond 3 days — delays vestibular compensation. Caution in older people — extrapyramidal risk.
Alternative
Cinnarizine OTC available
Dose per current SPC/BNF/local formulary — shortest possible duration, usually no more than 2–3 days (like all vestibular sedatives it delays central compensation if continued). H1 antihistamine. Not safer than prochlorperazine in older people: can cause sedation and extrapyramidal/parkinsonian adverse effects — warn patient. Cyclizine (dose per BNF) is an alternative.
Post-acute (>72h)
Vestibular Rehabilitation Physio referral
Stop vestibular sedatives. Refer to physiotherapy for Cawthorne-Cooksey exercises. Active rehabilitation accelerates central compensation. Warn: symptoms worsen initially before improving.

Menière's Disease — Specialist-Led Management (no NICE treatment ladder)

Step 1Refer suspected Menière's disease for audiological/ENT assessment. Discuss lifestyle measures and local specialist treatment options. Evidence for betahistine and dietary restriction is limited; betahistine is used in practice but is not a proven disease-modifying therapy. Review treatment according to attack frequency, hearing status and patient preference. Avoid a fixed sodium target unless the local ENT service recommends one.
Step 2Thiazide diuretic — specialist-led decision only; electrolyte monitoring per the specialist plan.
Step 3Intratympanic therapy (specialist only): methylprednisolone (hearing preservation) or gentamicin (vestibulotoxic — for intractable vertigo, hearing already poor). Meniett devices are not a routine evidence-based treatment.
Step 4Surgical options (ENT only): vestibular neurectomy, endolymphatic sac decompression, labyrinthectomy (for non-hearing ear) — specialist decision.

Vestibular Migraine

Treatment follows migraine principles and is often specialist-led. Use local migraine guidance and document off-label use where applicable. There is no universally accepted primary-care vestibular migraine regimen. Choose drugs considering migraine phenotype, comorbidity, pregnancy potential, contraception, mood, blood pressure and cognitive adverse effects.

Acute attack
Triptans Migraine Rx
May be appropriate for migraine headache, but evidence for vertigo alone is limited. Doses per BNF/local migraine guidance. Aspirin 900 mg + antiemetic (metoclopramide 10 mg) as alternative first-line.
Prophylaxis (frequent or disabling attacks)
Migraine prophylaxis Off-label · specialist-guided
Propranolol, amitriptyline and topiramate are off-label for vestibular migraine — document off-label use; doses per BNF/local guidance. Topiramate is teratogenic: follow current MHRA pregnancy-prevention advice where relevant.
Non-pharmacological
Migraine triggers diary
Identify and avoid triggers: sleep disruption, dehydration, dietary triggers, stress, hormonal changes. Migraine Trust patient resources. Regular meals, sleep routine, hydration.

Orthostatic Hypotension / Vasovagal — staged approach

Step 1Medication review: deprescribe/reduce antihypertensives, alpha-blockers, diuretics, tricyclics. Correct dehydration and anaemia; address heat exposure.
Step 2Slow position changes and physical counterpressure manoeuvres: leg crossing, muscle tensing before standing. Consider compression garments. Raised head of bed. Any increase in salt or fluid only after considering heart failure, CKD, hypertension, electrolyte disturbance, urinary retention and supine hypertension.
Step 3Refer for specialist treatment if persistent or neurogenic. Drug treatment (e.g. fludrocortisone, midodrine) is a specialist decision, with monitoring for supine hypertension, electrolytes, oedema and heart failure.
Anxiety-related
dizziness
Do not prescribe vestibular sedatives — they reinforce somatic focus. NHS Talking Therapies referral for CBT (specifically vestibular-oriented CBT or dizziness-specific CBT). Breathing retraining for hyperventilation. Consider an SSRI (dose per BNF) if anxiety persists after psychological measures.
Medication-induced
Identify offending drug. Deprescribe or switch: antihypertensive class change, reduce/stop benzodiazepine (gradual taper — do not stop abruptly), switch aminoglycoside if alternative exists. Review at 2–4 weeks to confirm resolution.
The Epley manoeuvre is usually effective for BPPV yet is underused in primary care. Prolonged vestibular suppressant prescription is a common treatment error — it inhibits vestibular compensation and prolongs recovery from vestibular neuritis. Betahistine for Menière's: evidence of benefit is limited and it is not a proven disease-modifying therapy. Vestibular rehabilitation post-neuritis improves recovery (McDonnell and Hillier, Cochrane 2015). Drug treatment for orthostatic hypotension is specialist-led; most patients start with medication review and non-drug measures.
8
Lifestyle

Non-Pharmacological Interventions — Active Management, Not Passive Reassurance

Lifestyle modifications are first-line treatment for most dizziness causes and adjuncts in all. Frame these as active treatment with evidence-based efficacy, not vague reassurance or afterthoughts.

Vestibular rehabilitation exercises Cawthorne-Cooksey or Cawthorne Brandt-Daroff exercises daily. Gaze stabilisation, head movement exercises. Improves chronic vestibular symptoms after the acute phase. Prescribe as specifically as a drug — Physio referral or NHS resources (NHS website or local physiotherapy leaflets).
Hydration and sodium balance For Menière's: avoid a fixed sodium target unless the local ENT service recommends one — excessive salt restriction can be inappropriate. For orthostatic hypotension: correct dehydration; any extra fluid or salt only after considering heart failure, CKD, hypertension, electrolyte disturbance and supine hypertension.
Caffeine and alcohol reduction Both worsen Menière's (endolymph pressure) and vestibular migraine. Alcohol: national guidelines <14 units/week. Caffeine: reduce if it is a personal trigger. Track in symptom diary — often patient-confirmed trigger.
Sleep hygiene Sleep deprivation is a major migraine and vestibular migraine trigger. Target 7–9 hours, consistent sleep schedule. Avoid screens 1 hour before bed. Sleep disruption worsens vestibular compensation. CBT-I app (Sleepio — NHS-available) for insomnia.
Falls prevention (elderly) Home hazard assessment (GP referral form). Appropriate footwear — no loose slippers. Night lighting. Handrails in bathroom/stairs. Progressive, individually tailored strength and balance exercise, supervised where appropriate (e.g. OTAGO, prescribed by physiotherapist). Do not use falls-risk prediction tools to predict risk; offer comprehensive falls assessment based on age, falls history, frailty, injury and gait/balance impairment. Reduced falls by about 35% in the Otago programme trials (Robertson et al., meta-analysis 2002); NICE NG249 recommends strength and balance programmes for people at risk of falls.
Driving guidance Do not drive if dizziness, vertigo, imbalance, sedating medication or an unpredictable episode could impair safe control of the vehicle. Advise the patient to check the current DVLA guidance. Notification is required where episodes are sudden, disabling and unprovoked/unprecipitated, or where another relevant diagnosis applies (DVLA Assessing fitness to drive). Document advice given in notes. Refer to Gov.uk/DVLA.
Postural manoeuvres (orthostatic hypotension) Rise slowly from lying/sitting — 2-step stand (sit edge of bed 30s, then stand). Avoid prolonged standing. Avoid hot baths/showers. Eat smaller meals more frequently (post-prandial hypotension).
Stress management and psychological support Vestibular disorders commonly coexist with anxiety — often a vicious cycle. NHS Talking Therapies self-referral for CBT. Mindfulness-based approaches may help some people with chronic dizziness. Breathing control for hyperventilation-related dizziness.
Symptom diary use 2-week standardised diary: date, time, duration, severity (1–10), triggers, associated symptoms. Invaluable for diagnosis, specialist referrals (Menière's/migraine), and monitoring treatment response. Provide diary template (practice template or Vestibular Disorders Association).
Migraine lifestyle management Regular meals (no skipping), consistent sleep, hydration. Identify and avoid personal triggers (common: wine, cheese, stress, hormonal changes). Menstrual migraine: continuous combined hormonal contraception only if migraine without aura (check UKMEC; aura is UKMEC 4); GnRH analogues are specialist-only via gynaecology.
Vestibular rehabilitation is the most evidence-based intervention for chronic vestibular disorders — the Cochrane review (McDonnell and Hillier, 2015) found moderate-to-strong evidence for improvement in dizziness symptoms, balance, and quality of life. BPPV can recur after Epley, and repeat treatment may be needed. Low-sodium diet in Menière's: evidence is limited; follow local ENT advice rather than a fixed target. DVLA notification: GP has a professional and ethical duty to advise patients about driving restrictions — failure to do so is a GMC fitness-to-practise matter. Falls prevention: NICE NG249 recommends progressive, individually tailored strength and balance programmes for people at risk of falls. Document lifestyle advice given in every consultation to demonstrate holistic management at SCA exam.
9
Safety

Follow-Up, Monitoring & Safety-Netting

Safety-netting is a GMC and RCGP core competency. Document clearly: what symptoms should prompt re-consultation, when to call 999, and the follow-up plan. Verbal safety-netting alone is insufficient — give written information where possible.

48–72 hours
Acute vestibular neuritis: Phone review if vomiting preventing oral medications or symptoms not improving. SSNHL: confirm seen within 24 hours by ENT/audiovestibular service; steroids only per local ENT protocol. Contact details given for out-of-hours.
1–2 weeks
Vestibular neuritis: assess progress, check vestibular exercises commenced.
4 weeks
Post-Epley for BPPV: Review in person or by phone. Most improve; symptoms may recur. If persisting — re-Epley or refer to physiotherapy / ENT. All treatment reviews: Menière's treatment per ENT plan (attack frequency, hearing status, patient preference), postural measures for orthostatic hypotension (BP check), migraine prophylaxis initiation (side effects, headache diary). Anxiety-related: NHS Talking Therapies referral accepted? PHQ-9/GAD-7 recheck.
3 months
Full dizziness review. Reassess diagnosis — has it changed? Functional impact (work, driving, mood). Falls diary in elderly. ENT/neurology/cardiology referral results reviewed. Advance or step-down treatment. Hearing reassessment if asymmetric.
6–12 months
Chronic conditions (Menière's, vestibular migraine): Annual review — hearing, attack frequency, medication efficacy/side effects, quality of life. Escalate to specialist if deteriorating. Ask about falls annually in people aged 65 and over and offer comprehensive falls assessment where indicated (NG249).
Safety-net: 999
New or worsening headache with dizziness. Facial drooping, arm/leg weakness, speech difficulty (FAST). Sudden loss of vision. Severe chest pain or palpitations with collapse. Inability to walk or severe unsteadiness developing suddenly. Loss of consciousness.
Safety-net: Same-day
New hearing loss (especially unilateral, sudden — SSNHL). Vomiting preventing medication. Increasing falls with injury. Symptoms not improving as expected after 1 week. New medication side effects (extrapyramidal symptoms from prochlorperazine — stop drug immediately, same-day review). Fever + vertigo (labyrinthitis/meningitis).
Review triggers
Recurrence after successful Epley — consider further Epley, refer physio if >3rd recurrence. Menière's symptoms not controlled — confirm diagnosis and discuss specialist options with ENT. Worsening anxiety despite NHS Talking Therapies — consider CPN referral or low-dose SSRI.
Medication monitoring
Thiazide (if started by ENT for Menière's): U&E per specialist plan. Propranolol (off-label vestibular migraine prophylaxis): BP, pulse at 4 weeks. Fludrocortisone/midodrine (if started by a specialist): lying/standing BP including supine hypertension, U&E, oedema per specialist plan. Cinnarizine: not for long-term use — sedation and parkinsonism risk; stop if tremor/rigidity develops.
Documentation
Record in every note: dizziness type characterised, red flags excluded, safety-netting given, driving advice documented, follow-up plan confirmed. This protects you medico-legally and demonstrates RCGP curriculum competency in safety awareness.
Structured safety-netting reduces medicolegal risk and improves patient outcomes. A missed posterior fossa stroke presenting as "vertigo" is one of the most common serious diagnostic errors in UK general practice. Missed strokes may return with escalating symptoms — clear safety-netting instructions help patients return promptly. SSNHL: NG98 asks for sudden hearing loss to be seen within 24 hours, so the safety-net must say same day. The BPPV review catches those who need repeat Epley before they develop secondary anxiety and fall into "chronic dizziness" pathways. Cinnarizine-induced parkinsonism is a recognised complication — vestibular suppressants should not be continued long-term. Asking older people about falls and offering assessment to those who have fallen is recommended in NICE NG249 (2025) and directly prevents hip fractures — a major cause of morbidity and mortality.
