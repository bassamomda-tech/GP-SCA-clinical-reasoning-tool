01
Safety

Red Flags — Surgical and life-threatening causes MUST be excluded first

Abdominal pain is a diagnostic minefield. The following require immediate action before any further assessment. Muted-presentation trap: the elderly, diabetics, the immunosuppressed and anyone on steroids can have a surgical abdomen with minimal tenderness and no fever — lower your threshold, not your guard.
Peritonism Generalised peritonism, involuntary guarding, board-like rigidity, or suspected perforation, obstruction or ischaemia → urgent surgical assessment; 999 if unstable, severely unwell, altered consciousness or unsafe to transfer. Bowel sounds are poorly sensitive — never use them to exclude obstruction or peritonitis
Haemodynamic compromise Judge the whole picture — mental state, perfusion, capillary refill, pulse, BP, RR, urine output, bleeding and trajectory; no single HR/BP cut-off defines shock, and beta-blockers, frailty, pregnancy and sepsis mask the numbers → 999 (AAA rupture, GI haemorrhage, ectopic pregnancy, sepsis)
Positive pregnancy test with pain Ask about pregnancy possibility and test (urine or serum hCG) when clinically relevant in anyone with pregnancy potential — including those using contraception if pregnancy cannot confidently be excluded. Cervical motion tenderness is not specific (also PID) but warrants urgent assessment in pregnancy; no routine outpatient ultrasound first. Stable — positive test with abdominal, pelvic or cervical-motion tenderness → immediate early pregnancy assessment unit / gynaecology today (NICE NG126). 999 / A&E if shock, syncope, severe or worsening pain, significant bleeding, peritonism, or shoulder-tip pain with instability. An IUD, previous ectopic or assisted conception raises suspicion but is not a prerequisite
Aortic aneurysm rupture Age over 50, tearing abdominal/back pain, pulsatile mass, collapse → 999 (50% mortality even with surgery). In older patients — particularly with vascular disease, smoking history, known aneurysm, atypical pain, syncope, abdominal/back pain or haemodynamic features — actively consider AAA before attributing symptoms to renal colic. Do not palpate a suspected ruptured AAA repeatedly or deeply; arrange emergency transfer
Torsion — two different destinations Suspected testicular torsion (acute scrotal pain, often with abdominal pain, high-riding or transverse testis) → immediate emergency urological/surgical assessment; 4–6 h window. Suspected ovarian or adnexal torsion → same-day emergency gynaecological assessment, 999 if unstable or safe transport cannot be arranged — not routine imaging or an outpatient letter
Mesenteric ischaemia Severe pain out of proportion to examination findings, AF history, vascular disease → 999 (high mortality if missed)
Unintentional weight loss + abdominal pain Age ≥40, if unexplained → FIT-guided colorectal assessment (NG12) — keep pancreatic, upper GI, gynaecological and other causes in the differential; age ≥60 with weight loss plus abdominal or back pain, new-onset diabetes, nausea, vomiting, constipation or diarrhoea → urgent direct-access CT (ultrasound if CT unavailable) for pancreatic cancer. Jaundice at 40 or over → suspected cancer pathway referral
Jaundice + abdominal pain → same-day assessment (hepatobiliary emergency, cholangitis: Charcot's triad = fever + RUQ pain + jaundice)
Suspected sepsis Assess on observations, mental state, perfusion, urine output, comorbidity and trajectory — with lactate where available (NG253). Fever or hypothermia neither rules sepsis in nor out, and NEWS2 supports escalation rather than replacing assessment. Pregnancy, immunosuppression and frailty are higher-risk groups → 999 if high risk
Change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding or iron-deficiency anaemia with pain Use FIT to guide colorectal referral where the NG12 criteria apply (abdominal mass, change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, ≥40 with weight loss and abdominal pain, <50 with rectal bleeding plus pain or weight loss, ≥50 with rectal bleeding, pain or weight loss, ≥60 with anaemia): FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → suspected cancer pathway. A low or unreturned FIT does not override an abdominal mass or strong ongoing concern
Diabetic ketoacidosis Vomiting or abdominal pain in T1DM, or T2DM on an SGLT2 inhibitor — check capillary blood ketones, not glucose alone (euglycaemic DKA). Escalate per the local DKA pathway, not a ketone number alone — symptoms, pH/bicarbonate, hydration and diabetes type decide; withhold SGLT2 inhibitors during acute illness (sick-day rules). Suspected DKA, significant ketonaemia, or significant hyperglycaemia, acidosis, dehydration, Kussmaul breathing or altered consciousness → emergency assessment via the local DKA pathway
New palpable mass Any abdominal mass not previously documented → urgent imaging / suspected cancer referral — a mass is referred on its own merit, whatever the FIT result
The pain that is not in the abdomen Inferior MI · lower-lobe pneumonia · pulmonary embolism · DKA and adrenal crisis in steroid-dependent or Addison's patients · hypercalcaemia · sickle-cell crisis · acute intermittent porphyria · urinary retention · shingles before the rash · abdominal wall pain (Carnett's positive) · testicular pathology · pregnancy-related disease beyond ectopic · severe drug-induced constipation
Abdominal pain accounts for ~5% of all GP consultations; the challenge is that serious diagnoses are uncommon but catastrophic if missed. AAA rupture carries very high mortality without intervention. Ectopic pregnancy remains a leading cause of first-trimester maternal death in the UK — which is why anyone with pregnancy potential gets a pregnancy test when clinically relevant, including on contraception if pregnancy cannot confidently be excluded, and why NG126 separates the unstable patient (999) from the stable positive test with tenderness (EPU today). Mesenteric ischaemia is missed because the examination is normal: "pain out of proportion to signs" is the cue. Torsion is time-critical, but the destination differs — testicular torsion is an immediate surgical/urological emergency, ovarian torsion a same-day gynaecological one. DKA on an SGLT2 inhibitor can be euglycaemic, so the test is ketones, not glucose.
02
Diagnose

Characterise the pain — SOCRATES and temporal pattern to narrow differentials

Site
RUQ = hepatobiliary (biliary colic, cholecystitis, hepatitis) · Epigastric = peptic ulcer, GORD, pancreatitis, MI · LUQ = splenic, pancreatitis · Periumbilical → RIF = appendicitis · RIF = appendicitis, ovarian pathology, Meckel's · LIF = diverticulitis, ovarian, constipation · Loin = renal colic, pyelonephritis · Suprapubic = UTI, bladder, gynaecological · Diffuse = IBS, peritonitis, mesenteric ischaemia, IBD
Onset
Sudden (seconds) = perforation, rupture, volvulus, torsion · Rapid (minutes) = colic (biliary, renal, uterine) · Gradual (hours) = appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis · Chronic / recurrent = IBS, IBD, functional
Character
Colicky (comes and goes in waves) = bowel obstruction, biliary/renal colic · Constant = inflammation/infection · Burning = PUD, GORD · Cramping = IBS, gastroenteritis
Radiation
RUQ → right shoulder tip = biliary / diaphragm irritation · Epigastric → back = pancreatitis, AAA · Loin → groin → testis/labia = renal colic · Central → RIF = appendicitis
Alleviating / aggravating
Food-related (postprandial) = biliary / mesenteric angina / PUD · Defaecation-relieved = IBS, IBD · Movement-worsened = peritonitis (patients lie still) · Position-related = pancreatitis (sitting forward better)
Severity + chronology
Acute (<72h) vs subacute (days–weeks) vs chronic (>3 months, ≥3 episodes). Escalating pain = worsening pathology. Static chronic = functional more likely
Associated symptoms
Fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, urinary symptoms, menstrual history, sexual history, jaundice, rectal bleeding, weight loss — all critically narrow the differential
A systematic pain history using SOCRATES correctly identifies the diagnosis in 60–70% of abdominal pain presentations before examination. The migration of pain from periumbilical to RIF is present in 60% of confirmed appendicitis cases. Colicky pain with complete absence in between strongly suggests hollow viscus obstruction or colic rather than inflammation. Understanding onset speed (seconds vs hours) is the single most important discriminator between surgical emergency and subacute disease.
03
Diagnose

Classify by acuity and likely system — guides investigation and referral urgency

Acute surgical (hours)
Appendicitis · Acute cholecystitis · Perforated viscus · Bowel obstruction · Ischaemia · Ruptured ectopic · Torsion. → Same-day A&E referral
Acute medical (hours–days)
Pancreatitis · Pyelonephritis · Diabetic crisis · Hepatitis · Inferior MI · Renal colic (if obstructed/infected) → Same-day assessment / A&E
Urgent (days–weeks)
Peptic ulcer disease · Biliary colic (uncomplicated) · IBD flare · Diverticulitis (mild–moderate) · New onset >50. → Same-day or urgent GP / suspected cancer pathway referral
Chronic / recurrent
IBS · Functional abdominal pain · Chronic pancreatitis · Endometriosis · Adhesions · Chronic pelvic pain → Planned workup in primary care with referral if unresolved
Gynaecological screen (women)
LMP, cycle regularity, vaginal discharge, dyspareunia, intermenstrual/postcoital bleeding, pregnancy test when clinically relevant in anyone with pregnancy potential. Ovarian cysts, PID, endometriosis, ectopic — all present with abdominal pain
Functional
Diagnose IBS positively on the symptom pattern (Rome IV) with appropriate baseline testing when there are no red flags — it does not require exhaustive exclusion of every organic disease, and over-investigation is its own harm. Age, alarm features and local pathways decide which baseline tests are needed (FBC, inflammatory markers, coeliac serology, and others guided by symptoms — FIT where NG12 applies, faecal calprotectin only where chronic diarrhoea raises IBD concern). Atypical age or presentation needs targeted tests: IBS rarely starts at 50 or over, so new bowel-habit change or "IBS-type" symptoms at that age, or any alarm feature (weight loss, anaemia, bleeding, nocturnal symptoms, family history), need investigation first — colorectal and ovarian cancer both masquerade as late-onset IBS
Acuity classification drives the time-critical decision: Can this patient safely wait for primary care workup, or do they need hospital today? Pancreatitis severity (GLASGOW score in hospital) determines HDU admission need. Mild diverticulitis (no systemic upset, able to eat) can be managed in primary care, often without antibiotics (NICE NG147); complicated or severe disease requires admission. Anyone with pregnancy potential presenting with lower abdominal pain should have a pregnancy test when clinically relevant — a missed ectopic pregnancy can be fatal.
04
Diagnose

Targeted examination — systematic and focused to confirm or refute differentials

Vital signs first
BP, HR, temperature, RR, SpO₂, capillary refill. Haemodynamic compromise → 999 before completing examination. NEWS2 score if any abnormality
Inspection
Distension (obstruction, ascites) · Visible peristalsis (obstruction) · Scars (adhesions) · Jaundice, pallor, cachexia · Cullen's / Grey Turner's sign (periumbilical / flank bruising = haemorrhagic pancreatitis — rare but critical)
Palpation — light
Start AWAY from site of pain. Tenderness, guarding (voluntary = patient tensing; involuntary = peritonism). Document site, character, severity
Palpation — deep
Masses · Organomegaly (liver, spleen) · Renal angles · Aortic pulsation (midline pulsatile mass >3cm = AAA — do not deeply palpate if suspected)
Specific signs
Murphy's sign (RUQ + inspiratory arrest = cholecystitis) · Rovsing's, psoas and McBurney's point · Carnett's test (pain worsens with abdominal wall contraction = abdominal wall source). None of these is a diagnostic test: they shift probability only, and normal blood tests do not exclude appendicitis — imaging and serial clinical review may be needed
Bowel sounds
Absent = ileus / peritonitis / late obstruction · High-pitched tinkling = early obstruction · Normal does not exclude pathology
PR examination
Only when it will alter management — suspected melaena, rectal bleeding, a rectal mass or pelvic pathology. Obtain consent and offer a chaperone; document both. It is not a blanket requirement in abdominal pain
Pelvic / genitourinary
Guided by symptoms — pregnancy status, vaginal discharge or bleeding, pelvic pain, suspected PID or ectopic pregnancy; with consent and a chaperone, and never a blanket requirement. Cervical excitation (PID) · adnexal tenderness (cyst, ectopic) · testicular examination (torsion, epididymo-orchitis) · hernia orifices, inguinal and femoral
Murphy's sign has 97% specificity for acute cholecystitis when positive. Involuntary guarding (rigidity) is the single most important sign of peritonitis — it is not suppressible by the patient and indicates surgical emergency. Carnett's test differentiates visceral from abdominal wall pain with 78–88% accuracy — positive Carnett's (worsens on raising head) suggests abdominal wall haematoma, hernia, or nerve entrapment, avoiding unnecessary investigations. A missed irreducible femoral hernia (most common in elderly women) causing obstruction is a preventable surgical emergency.
05
Diagnose

Investigations — match to acuity and differential; avoid blanket ordering

Acute / same-day
Targeted, not a blanket panel: FBC bleeding, infection, anaemia · U&E/creatinine dehydration, renal colic, obstruction, sepsis · LFTs/bilirubin jaundice, RUQ pain, biliary disease · Lipase suspected pancreatitis · CRP selected suspected infection/inflammation · Lactate/VBG shock, sepsis or mesenteric ischaemia · Urine dip + MC&S urinary symptoms or pyelonephritis · Pregnancy test where relevant · Glucose + ketones diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitor, vomiting or systemic illness
Biliary / hepatic
LFTs GGT Bilirubin · USS abdomen (gallstones, biliary dilation, liver lesions) — arrange same-day or urgent
Upper GI
H. pylori test (breath test / stool antigen — stop PPI 2 weeks prior) · OGD if ulcer / cancer concern
Pancreatic
Lipase (preferred where available) or amylase. Acute pancreatitis needs two of three: characteristic pain, enzyme >3× the upper limit of normal, or characteristic imaging — a raised amylase alone is not diagnostic, and a normal one does not exclude it. USS abdomen. Suspected pancreatic cancer: age ≥60 with unexplained weight loss plus abdominal or back pain, diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, constipation or new-onset diabetes → urgent direct-access CT (USS if CT unavailable). CA 19-9 is not a diagnostic or screening test — raised in benign biliary disease, normal in some cancers
Gynaecological
Urine hCG · High vaginal + endocervical swabs (PID) · pelvic USS (transvaginal preferred). Ovarian cancer (NG12): for persistent or frequent bloating, early satiety, pelvic/abdominal pain or urinary symptoms at 40 or over, measure CA-125 and arrange urgent ultrasound according to the age-specific CA-125 threshold — do not use CA-125 in isolation or a single 35 IU/mL cut-off. At 39 or under with persistent suggestive symptoms, consider urgent ultrasound rather than relying on CA-125
Lower GI / chronic
FBC + CRP Calprotectin Coeliac screen (anti-tTG IgA) Thyroid function · Colonoscopy if red flags or calprotectin elevated
Renal
Urine dip (haematuria, leucocytes) MSU · low-dose non-contrast CT KUB within 24 hours for suspected renal colic in adults (NG118) — ultrasound instead in pregnancy. Urgent urological assessment for fever, sepsis, a solitary kidney, anuria or suspected infected obstruction
Do NOT routinely order
Amylase for chronic pain · CT without clinical indication · OGD for functional dyspepsia <55 years without alarm features · Barium studies as first-line (CT/USS preferred)
Lipase is preferred to amylase where available because it stays elevated longer, but neither makes the diagnosis alone: acute pancreatitis requires two of characteristic pain, an enzyme over three times the upper limit, or characteristic imaging. A pregnancy test should be done when clinically relevant in anyone with pregnancy potential — and a negative test does not exclude a very early ectopic (serum beta-hCG is more sensitive). Faecal calprotectin is recommended in NICE HTG320 (formerly DG11, 2013) for distinguishing IBD from IBS and is a powerful primary-care test before referral. FIT is now the gateway to most colorectal referrals, but it is a rule-in test with a floor, not a rule-out: a mass, or persistent concerning symptoms, is referred whatever the number. CA-125 is interpreted against age-specific thresholds alongside ultrasound, never in isolation, and CA 19-9 has no place in primary-care assessment of abdominal pain.
06
Refer

Referral criteria — most acute abdominal pain requires same-day senior review

999 Emergency
Peritonism with instability · shock · ruptured ectopic or any suspected ectopic with instability, syncope or significant bleeding · ruptured or symptomatic AAA · mesenteric ischaemia with instability · massive GI bleed · obstruction with instability · severe sepsis · severe DKA
Same-day hospital / surgical
Suspected appendicitis · obstruction · pancreatitis · cholecystitis or cholangitis · infected obstructed kidney, or renal colic with fever or a single kidney · stable suspected ectopic pregnancy · suspected torsion — testicular to emergency urology/surgery, ovarian to emergency gynaecology · complicated diverticulitis (uncontrolled pain, peritonism, abscess, obstruction, fistula, sepsis, significant bleeding, dehydration or unable to tolerate oral treatment) · acute surgical abdomen without shock
EPU today
Positive pregnancy test with pain or abdominal, pelvic or cervical-motion tenderness in a haemodynamically stable woman → immediate early pregnancy assessment service, within hours (NG126) — not a routine scan request, and not automatically 999
Same-day assess
Severe pain not responding to analgesia · Haematuria + loin pain (rule out obstructed ureter) · Elevated amylase · New jaundice · SBAR call to surgical registrar acceptable in stable patients
Suspected cancer pathway
Colorectal (NG12, FIT-guided): offer FIT where the criteria apply — abdominal mass, change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, ≥40 with weight loss and abdominal pain, <50 with rectal bleeding plus pain or weight loss, ≥50 with rectal bleeding, pain or weight loss, ≥60 with anaemia even without iron deficiency; FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → urgent referral, and refer anyway for a mass or strong ongoing concern. Upper GI (NICE NG12 (updated April 2026)): dysphagia at any age · ≥55 with weight loss and upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia → suspected cancer pathway referral for oesophageal or stomach cancer. Pancreatic: ≥60 with weight loss plus abdominal/back pain, bowel change or new-onset diabetes → urgent direct-access CT; jaundice at 40+ → pathway referral. Ovarian: ≥40 with persistent or frequent bloating, early satiety, pelvic/abdominal pain or urinary symptoms → CA-125 with urgent ultrasound per the age-specific threshold, never CA-125 alone
Urgent routine
Uncomplicated biliary colic (elective cholecystectomy referral) · IBD flare without systemic compromise · Chronic pelvic pain for gynaecology · Dyspepsia failing treatment with no alarm features (alarm features → suspected cancer pathway above) · H. pylori positive (treat; re-test only where indicated — CG184)
Primary care manages
Mild IBS · Uncomplicated GORD · UTI · Constipation · Musculoskeletal abdominal pain (Carnett's positive) · Functional abdominal pain (with support)
The most dangerous error in abdominal pain is delayed referral. The classic Alvarado score for appendicitis (score ≥7 → surgery) is not validated for primary care use — clinical gestalt and examination findings should drive the referral decision. Cholecystitis with fever indicates bacterial superinfection and requires IV antibiotics and early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (within a week of diagnosis — NICE CG188). NICE NG12's criteria are set at a 3% positive-predictive-value threshold from UK primary-care evidence — adherence reduces time-to-treatment and improves survival outcomes. A threshold of uncertainty = refer: primary care cannot rule out surgical emergencies.
07
Treat

Treatment — condition-specific; analgesia and cause-directed therapy

Pain management should not be withheld pending diagnosis — this is outdated advice. Treat the cause in parallel.
H. pylori-positive dyspepsia / peptic ulcer (not all GORD)
PPI + H. pylori eradication If H. pylori positive
Eradication regimen and duration per current local antimicrobial guidance, taking account of previous clarithromycin or metronidazole exposure and allergy (CG184). Testing requires a 2-week PPI washout and no antibiotics for 4 weeks; confirm eradication per the local protocol. PPI 4–8 weeks for a proven ulcer
Uncomplicated UTI
Nitrofurantoin 1st line
Non-pregnant woman: nitrofurantoin MR 100 mg BD for 3 days per local guidance (trimethoprim if contraindicated). Men and pregnant women: a 7-day course with a pre-treatment MSU; pregnancy needs a pregnancy-safe choice. Not every suprapubic symptom is a UTI — pelvic pain, vaginal discharge or dyspareunia should prompt consideration of PID/STI, and abdominal pain with urinary symptoms may be a stone, obstruction, pyelonephritis, ectopic pregnancy or other pelvic pathology, and a dipstick alone does not diagnose UTI in older adults or anyone with pelvic pain
IBS
Antispasmodic 1st line
Mebeverine 135mg TDS (before meals) or Hyoscine butylbromide 10mg TDS. Add laxative for IBS-C, loperamide for IBS-D
AnalgesiaGive analgesia while arranging assessment — it must not be withheld for fear of obscuring the diagnosis. Paracetamol first line (maximum 4 g in 24 hours; reduce in low body weight, liver disease or high alcohol intake). An NSAID only where appropriate — avoid in GI bleeding or active ulcer, renal impairment or dehydration, heart failure, anticoagulation or high bleeding risk, pregnancy, significant cardiovascular disease, frailty and older age, and with an ACE inhibitor + diuretic ("triple whammy" AKI); NICE NG147 (2019) advises avoiding NSAIDs and opioids in diverticular disease where possible because of perforation risk. Opioids short-term and indication-specific only — they may worsen constipation, ileus and sphincter of Oddi-related symptoms. Check doses against the current BNF and local formulary
GORDOnly without alarm features — dysphagia, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, weight loss, anaemia or an abdominal mass need investigation/referral, not empirical treatment alone; consider H. pylori test and treat. Lifestyle first (Step 8) + Omeprazole 20mg OD before food × 4 weeks. Step up to 40mg OD if inadequate. Add Gaviscon Advance 10ml QDS after meals and at bedtime for immediate symptom relief
DiverticulitisSystemically well, uncomplicated: consider no antibiotic — simple analgesia and review (NG147); if managed without antibiotics, advise re-presenting if pain, fever, vomiting, rectal bleeding or systemic symptoms develop or fail to improve. Avoid confidently labelling uncomplicated diverticulitis without considering appendicitis, obstruction, malignancy, IBD, gynaecological and urinary causes; suspected complicated disease → CRP, FBC, U&E and hospital imaging. Systemically unwell, immunosuppressed or significant comorbidity without complicated features: oral antibiotics — first choice co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg TDS for 5 days. Ciprofloxacin is not a routine alternative (restricted; only via the local or specialist pathway). Complicated disease or any of uncontrolled pain, peritonism, abscess, obstruction, fistula, sepsis, significant bleeding, dehydration or inability to tolerate oral treatment → same-day hospital assessment. Avoid NSAIDs and opioids where possible
Biliary colicAn NSAID where not contraindicated (see the analgesia rung) is effective for biliary colic; paracetamol otherwise. Refer for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Fever, jaundice or RUQ pain with systemic upset → the acute biliary pathway with LFTs, bilirubin and inflammatory markers — not oral antibiotics and review
Renal colicAn NSAID is first-line where not contraindicated (NG118); paracetamol if it is. Low-dose non-contrast CT KUB within 24 hours; ultrasound in pregnancy. An alpha-blocker only as an adjunct for a distal ureteric stone under 10 mm, per local urology protocol — not for all stones or all colic. Do not encourage forced oral fluids during acute obstruction or colic — hydration advice belongs to prevention after recovery. Fever, sepsis, a single kidney, anuria or suspected infected obstruction → urgent urology
PIDTreat empirically to the current BASHH PID guideline and local protocol — regimens and resistance patterns change, so check rather than working from memory. Pregnancy and severe PID have different regimens; severe disease, pregnancy or a possible surgical cause → same-day assessment. Include STI testing, partner notification and sexual-health referral, and advise abstinence until both partners are treated
Analgesia does not mask diagnosis — multiple RCTs show opioids and NSAIDs in the acute abdomen do not reduce diagnostic accuracy and improve the patient's experience and cooperation with examination. Withholding analgesia is harmful. The safety work is in the contraindications, not the withholding: NSAIDs cause bleeding in ulcer disease, AKI alongside an ACE inhibitor and diuretic, and carry perforation risk in diverticular disease, where NICE NG147 (2019) advises avoiding NSAIDs and opioids where possible. H. pylori eradication substantially reduces ulcer recurrence, but the regimen depends on previous macrolide and metronidazole exposure and on local resistance — hence the link to local guidance rather than a fixed triple therapy. Alpha-blockers have a modest adjunctive effect on passage of distal stones under 10 mm, which is why they are not offered for all renal colic.

Lifestyle

Non-pharmacological interventions — condition-specific, evidence-based

GORD / PUD Elevate bed head 15–20cm · Avoid meals within 3h of bed · Reduce alcohol, smoking, NSAIDs, caffeine · Weight loss if overweight · Small frequent meals
IBS Dietitian-led low-FODMAP trial if first-line dietary advice is insufficient · Soluble fibre (oats, linseeds) · Regular meal pattern · Avoid gas-producing foods · Peppermint oil capsules (evidence-based)
Biliary colic prevention Low-fat diet reduces cholecystokinin stimulation and frequency of attacks. Weight loss (gradual — rapid weight loss paradoxically increases stone risk). Maintain physical activity
Renal stone prevention — after recovery, not during colic Adequate fluid intake, usually 2.5–3 L/day for adults · reduce salt · consider citrate (lemon juice in water) · moderate red meat for oxalate stones. This is prevention advice: do not force fluids to "flush the stone" during acute obstruction or colic
Diverticular disease High-fibre diet (25–30g/day) reduces recurrence risk · Regular physical activity · Adequate hydration · Avoid constipation — treat proactively
Functional / chronic pain CBT or other psychological therapy for persistent IBS or chronic primary pain, especially with distress or functional impairment · Mindfulness-based stress reduction · Pain management programme if severe
All patients Smoking cessation (increases risk of PUD, Crohn's, pancreatic cancer, renal stones) · Alcohol reduction (pancreatitis, gastritis, liver disease) · BMI optimisation
Hydration Sensible fluid intake for GI conditions, individualised to heart failure, CKD and any fluid restriction; 2.5–3 L/day for stone prevention once recovered. Not during acute renal obstruction or colic
Low-FODMAP diet (Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides and Polyols) is a dietitian-led option when first-line dietary advice (regular meals, fibre adjustment, limiting caffeine, alcohol and fizzy drinks) is insufficient. CBT or other psychological therapy is worth considering for persistent IBS or chronic primary pain, particularly with distress or functional impairment. After a stone has passed, good hydration is the main preventive measure; smoking cessation supports ulcer healing and reduces recurrence.
09
Safety

Follow-up & monitoring — structured review with clear safety-netting

48–72h (acute)
Selected cases only (e.g. diverticulitis managed without antibiotics, PID, biliary symptoms) — set follow-up by diagnosis, severity and uncertainty. Deterioration, persistent severe pain, fever, vomiting, poor hydration, jaundice or new bleeding needs reassessment. Not improving, or any complicated feature → same-day hospital assessment, not another course
2 weeks
H. pylori eradication: check compliance and side effects. IBS new diagnosis: review response to antispasmodics and dietary changes. Blood results review
4–6 weeks
GORD: review on PPI — step-down if well-controlled. Renal colic: follow-up per the urology plan (stone size and site, passage, renal function, obstruction) — no universal size threshold in primary care. IBD: assess flare response
3 months
IBS / functional pain: full review. Chronic pain: reassess analgesic needs. Refer if no improvement in chronic unexplained pain despite trial of treatment
H. pylori post-treatment
Not routine after uncomplicated dyspepsia; confirm eradication for a proven peptic ulcer (especially bleeding) or persisting symptoms, per the local pathway (CG184) — use a carbon-13 urea breath test at least 4 weeks after completing eradication (stop PPI 2 weeks prior). If positive → second-line regimen per local guidance
999 Safety-net
Sudden severe ("worst ever") pain · Haemodynamic collapse · Haematemesis · PR haemorrhage with shock · Rigid abdomen · Loss of consciousness
Same-day Safety-net
Any new fever with abdominal pain · worsening despite 48h treatment · vomiting preventing oral medication or fluids · new rectal bleeding · jaundice · a new mass felt by the patient · a positive pregnancy test appearing at any point
After diverticulitis
Follow the local post-diverticulitis imaging/endoscopy policy. Arrange interval investigation where the CT findings, persistent symptoms, complicated disease or an incomplete colorectal assessment raise concern about malignancy — not as a blanket rule for everyone
Re-screen for red flags
At every follow-up for chronic abdominal pain — re-check weight, rectal bleeding, change in character of pain. Maintain vigilance for new pathology
Diverticulitis that is not improving by 72 hours needs reassessment in hospital rather than a further primary-care course — perforation, abscess and fistula are the reasons. H. pylori eradication should be confirmed per local protocol, because treatment failure is common and undetected failure means continuing ulceration; testing needs a 2-week PPI washout and no antibiotics for 4 weeks. Structured review improves symptom control and satisfaction in IBS compared with as-needed appointments. Safety-netting must be documented — an RCGP SCA competency and medicolegally critical. Interval investigation after diverticulitis follows local policy and the CT findings rather than a blanket malignancy figure.
Educational use only. Pathway based on: NICE NG12 (Suspected cancer recognition, updated April 2026) · NICE NG126 (Ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage) · NICE NG147 (Diverticular disease) · NICE NG118 (Renal and ureteric stones) · NICE NG253 (Sepsis) · NICE CG184 (Dyspepsia/GORD) · NICE NG109 (Lower UTI antimicrobial prescribing) · current BASHH PID guideline · BSG IBS guidelines (2021) · Rome IV criteria (international) · BNF. Antibiotic, PID and H. pylori regimens must follow current local formulary and antimicrobial guidance, and separate pathways apply to children and to pregnancy. Always adapt to individual patient context and local clinical guidelines.
