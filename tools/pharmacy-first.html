Pharmacy First — who can go, and when to keep them

What Pharmacy First is. An NHS England advanced community pharmacy service (launched 31 January 2024). It has three parts: seven clinical pathways where the pharmacist can supply prescription-only medicines under patient group directions (PGDs) or a protocol; minor illness consultations (needs a referral); and urgent repeat medicine supply (needs a referral from NHS 111 or urgent care). England only — Scotland (Pharmacy First Scotland), Wales (Common Ailments Service) and Northern Ireland run their own schemes with different rules.

The seven clinical pathways age and sex limits set by NHS England

Patients can walk in for these, or be referred by the practice, NHS 111 or urgent care. The pharmacist follows the national clinical pathway (gateway questions and red flags), so a patient can still be sent back to you. Pathways were revised on 1 October 2025: more emphasis on red flags, a 999/A&E escalation gateway in every pathway, and clearer definitions.

Minor illness and urgent medicine supply

Needs a referral

Minor illness consultation

For low-acuity conditions outside the seven pathways (for example coughs and colds, congestion, mild rashes, hay fever, constipation, minor aches). The pharmacist gives advice and may suggest an over-the-counter product, which the patient buys. Referral from the practice, NHS 111 or urgent care is needed — a walk-in is treated as ordinary advice, not this service.

NHS 111 / urgent care only

Urgent repeat medicine supply

An emergency supply of a repeat medicine when the patient cannot reach their practice (usually out of hours). Referrals come from NHS 111, integrated urgent care or urgent and emergency care — not from GP practices. In hours, the practice should issue the repeat or an acute prescription. Usual prescription charges apply.

Not Pharmacy First

Other pharmacy services

Hypertension case-finding, the Pharmacy Contraception Service (oral contraception initiation and continuation), New Medicine Service and flu, COVID-19 and RSV vaccination are separate services. See the immunisation schedule for who is eligible.

How GPs refer the GP referral pathway (formerly "GP CPCS")

  1. Triage first. Care navigators or clinicians use a local triage protocol to spot suitable patients. Red flags and exclusions mean the patient stays with the practice.
  2. Agree it with the patient and let them choose the pharmacy (check it offers the pathway — AOM is not available from distance-selling pharmacies).
  3. Send an electronic referral through an NHS-assured referral route (integrated in EMIS or SystmOne, or a local platform such as PharmRefer or Refer-to-Pharmacy). Do not just tell the patient to "go to the chemist" — an unreferred minor-illness visit is not part of the service.
  4. Tell the patient what happens next: the pharmacy contacts them or they attend; the pharmacist may supply a medicine, give self-care advice or send them back.
  5. The outcome comes back to the GP record (GP Connect: Update Record) — code it and act on any onward referral the pharmacist makes.
Patients can also self-refer for the seven clinical pathways, so the practice may receive a consultation summary for someone it never saw. Look out for repeated pharmacy supplies (for example several UTI or sore throat episodes) — this is a reason to review in general practice.

When not to re-route to the pharmacy

  • Red flags or a possibly serious illness — sepsis, airway compromise, suspected meningitis, mastoiditis, orbital cellulitis, pyelonephritis, eye involvement in shingles. These need same-day clinical assessment, not a pharmacy appointment.
  • Outside the age or sex limits — for example a man with urinary symptoms, a woman of 65 or over with a UTI, a child under 5 with a sore throat, an adult with ear pain (until the new prescriber-only pathway is available locally), a child under 1.
  • Excluded groups — pregnancy (UTI and others), immunosuppression, catheter, recurrent episodes, treatment failure, recent antibiotics.
  • Possible cancer — persistent hoarseness, an ulcer or sore throat that will not settle, haematuria. Follow NICE NG12.
  • Safeguarding concerns, complex multimorbidity or interacting medicines, or a patient who cannot use the service (communication, mobility, capacity).
  • Patient choice. Pharmacy First is an option, not a barrier — do not refuse an appointment because a pathway exists.

Independent prescriber pathways announced September 2026

From autumn 2026, pharmacies employing an independent prescriber can work within the existing pathways and the contraception service. NHS England has named five prescriber-only Pharmacy First conditions: migraine, rhinitis, ear infections in adults, acne and scabies. Availability depends on local pharmacies having a prescriber and on the go-live date — check with your ICB or local pharmaceutical committee before referring for these.
