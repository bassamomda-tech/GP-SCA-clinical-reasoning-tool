Leucocytosis: the high white cell count CLINIC GUIDE

A raised total white cell count in adults 18+. Not for children, pregnancy (counts rise normally), or known leukaemia, lymphoma or MPN under haematology (follow the specialist plan).

Urgency comes from the differential, the film, the other counts, the trend and how the patient is — not the total WCC alone.

1Confirm it is realrepeat when the cause settles

  • Read the whole FBC: absolute differential, Hb, platelets, previous results and the laboratory comment or flags.
  • Transient rises: infection, exercise, acute stress, surgery or trauma, steroids, smoking. A single mild rise in a well patient with a clear cause is usually reactive.
  • Artefact: platelet clumps or nucleated red cells can inflate an automated count; a blood film settles it.
  • Repeat once the suspected reactive cause has resolved. Sooner, or discuss with haematology, if rising or with cytopenias, organomegaly, abnormal cells or systemic symptoms.

6Management by the commonest causesBSH 2017 · BSH CLL 2025 · Green Book

CauseFirst lineCheck and continue
InfectionTreat a clinically diagnosed infection per the current antimicrobial guideline and local formulary. A raised WCC alone is not a reason for antibioticsRepeat FBC after recovery if the count was unexplained or markedly abnormal
Steroids, other drugsReview the medicine. Do not taper steroids just to correct the WCC; never stop needed steroids abruptlyInterpret the count with symptoms, CRP and the differential
SmokingStop-smoking support with varenicline or NRT and the local service — for health, not a guaranteed WCC responseRepeat only if other features change
Eosinophilia (allergy, drugs, parasites)Stop a causative drug where possible. Antiparasitic treatment depends on the parasite, exposure and patient — follow specialist adviceBefore steroids with relevant travel: Strongyloides test or specialist advice (risk of hyperinfection)
MonocytosisLook for chronic infection (TB, endocarditis) and autoimmune disease; offer an HIV testHaematology if persistent, progressive or with cytopenias, spleen or dysplasia

7Living with CLL or an MPNUKHSA Green Book · NHS

InfectionsAnnual flu and pneumococcal vaccines (Green Book chapter 25). No live vaccines if immunosuppressed. Treat infections early.
Alcohol, smokingNo more than 14 units a week; none with liver involvement or on chemotherapy. Offer stop-smoking support.
SkinOn immunosuppression: SPF 30 or more with 4-star UVA protection, avoid midday sun, report new skin lesions.
ActivityAbout 150 minutes a week of moderate exercise helps fatigue. Avoid contact sports with low platelets or a big spleen.

8Follow-up and monitoringlocal laboratory pathway

  • Repeat FBC once the reactive cause has resolved: target back within the laboratory range
  • Normal on repeat → stop; no further routine testing
  • Persistent and unexplained → film and urgent haematology via the local pathway
  • Watch the trend: a steady rise matters even when each value looks modest
  • Ask each time about fevers, sweats, weight loss, nodes, abdominal swelling
  • Known CLL, CML or MPN: follow the specialist-written schedule
  • Rising count, new cytopenia or blasts → same-day haematology
  • Re-refer: new B symptoms or organomegaly, count rising despite treatment, new cytopenias
  • Diagnosis and plan in the problem list

9When and where to referNG12 · BSH · local laboratory pathway

EmergencySepsis or shock, suspected leucostasis, reduced consciousness, significant bleeding, priapism, unstable suspected acute leukaemia.
Same-day haematologyBlasts, WCC ≥100, symptomatic or fast-rising count, new anaemia or low platelets, marked spleen, leucoerythroblastic film, eosinophilia with organ damage.
Suspected cancer / urgentNG12 symptoms: very urgent FBC; nodes or spleen → lymphoma pathway. Persistent unexplained count of any lineage, or eosinophils ≥1.5 → urgent haematology.
Primary careMild rise, clear reactive cause, well patient, reassuring differential and film; settles once the cause is treated.

Other specialties: infectious diseases (persistent fever, travel, TB, HIV) · respiratory (eosinophilia with asthma) · rheumatology (suspected vasculitis, EGPA).

10Pitfalls and never-eventsBSH · NG12

  • Never judge urgency on the total WCC alone: blasts or cytopenias at a modest count matter more.
  • Never give antibiotics for a raised WCC without an infection.
  • Never start steroids for unexplained eosinophilia with relevant travel without thinking of Strongyloides.
  • Never call it CLL on the count alone: it needs clonal B cells confirmed by flow cytometry.
  • Never request BCR-ABL1 or JAK2 from primary care on the count alone — discuss with haematology.
  • Neutrophilia does not prove bacterial infection; a normal CRP does not exclude it.
  • Eosinophilia with asthma, sinus disease or neuropathy may be EGPA even if ANCA is negative.
  • No endocarditis prophylaxis just because of leucocytosis, CLL or a big spleen.

11Safety-nettell the patient

999 or emergency: sudden severe headache, confusion, vision loss, chest pain or breathlessness, a painful erection that will not go down, heavy bleeding, fever with rigors or feeling very unwell. Same day: new bruising or tiny red spots, new lumps in the neck, armpit or groin, a swollen tummy, drenching sweats or weight loss.

12Say it like thisplain English

"Your white cells, the cells that fight infection, are a little high. Most of the time that is the body responding to an infection, a medicine or smoking, and it settles. I'll repeat the test when you're better; if it stays high without a reason we'll ask a blood specialist."
