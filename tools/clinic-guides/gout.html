Gout in adults CLINIC GUIDE

For the flare, starting urate-lowering therapy (ULT) and the annual review. Adults. Not for suspected septic arthritis, calcium pyrophosphate disease (pseudogout) or children.

Page 1 · Diagnose and start hot joint → exclude sepsis → treat the flare → offer ULT

!Act today — the septic joint comes firstNG219 1.1.3 · refer immediately

  • Hot, swollen joint with suspected infection, fever or systemic upset, immunosuppression, diabetes, significant comorbidity, a prosthetic joint, or diagnostic doubt → refer immediately via the local pathway for urgent aspiration.
  • Gout and sepsis co-exist: a known gout patient can still have an infected joint. CRP and white count do not separate them; only the aspirate does.
  • Never inject steroid into a joint until infection has been excluded. Systemically unwell with a polyarticular flare → same-day assessment.

1RecogniseNG219 §1.1

  • Suspect: rapid-onset (often overnight) severe pain, redness and swelling of one or both 1st MTP joints, or tophi (ears, elbows, fingers).
  • Consider: the same picture in the midfoot, ankle, knee, wrist or fingers, or chronic inflammatory joint pain. Often mislabelled as cellulitis or OA.
  • Peaks within 24 h; settles over 1–2 weeks even untreated.
  • Mimics: CPPD (older, knee or wrist, chondrocalcinosis) · cellulitis (spreading erythema) · rheumatoid, psoriatic or reactive arthritis.
  • Examine: every joint, tophi, skin, temperature, BP.
  • Not gout: unexplained bone or soft-tissue lump, or bone pain with weight loss → the NG12 sarcoma or myeloma route.

3Work-up — every new diagnosisNG219 · baseline

  • U&E, eGFR (sets colchicine and allopurinol dosing)
  • Serum urate baseline
  • LFTs (before febuxostat)
  • HbA1c, lipids, BP, BMI
  • QRISK3: gout is a CV risk marker
  • Alcohol and sugary drinks
  • Drug list: diuretics, ciclosporin, azathioprine
  • Tophi, joint damage, urate stones

Young onset or unusual pattern → think renal disease or enzyme defects; specialist input.

First-line ULT: allopurinol or febuxostat by comorbidity and preference; allopurinol if major cardiovascular disease (previous MI, stroke, unstable angina). ULT is usually lifelong.

Explain at diagnosis: what gout is (urate crystals), that flares are treatable and preventable, that ULT is the treatment that stops them, and that it is usually for life. Give written information and the flare plan.

"There are two jobs: putting out today's fire, and draining the fuel that keeps lighting it. The daily tablet drains the fuel, but only if we push the dose until your blood level is under target. A few flares early on mean the crystals are dissolving, not that the tablet is failing."

Urate monthly while titrating; then consider annually once at target (NG219 1.5.15). Tophi take months to years to dissolve.

9Still flaring? Check firstNG219 §1.5

  • Adherence: taken daily, not "for attacks"? Check the repeat record
  • Dose: stuck at 100 or 300 mg with urate above target? Titrate on
  • Flare cover: was prophylaxis offered while titrating?
  • Drivers: alcohol, sugary drinks, the thiazide nobody swapped
  • Diagnosis: crystal-proven? CPPD, sepsis, other inflammatory arthritis?
  • At target, adherent, still flaring → consider <300, or refer

10The ULT ladder — start low, cover, titrate to targetNG219 §1.5 · SPCs

Step 1 · AllopurinolNICE: start low. SPC: 100 mg OD (lower in significant CKD or frailty). Raise every ~4 weeks on the monthly urate; smaller steps in CKD. Do not stop at 300 mg if above target.
Cover · prophylaxisIf the person chooses it, offer colchicine while the target is being reached (low dose, by renal function). Unsuitable → low-dose NSAID or low-dose steroid.
Or · Febuxostat80 mg OD → 120 mg if urate still >357 µmol/L after 2–4 weeks. Allopurinol, not febuxostat, first if major CV disease.
Step 4 · SpecialistTarget not reached on maximum tolerated dose, or both not tolerated → switch, then rheumatology (uricosurics, IL-1 blockers).

13Annual review checklistonce at target · NG219 1.5.15

  • Serum urate against the person's target
  • Flares since last review; tophi; joint damage
  • U&E/eGFR where relevant (CKD, diuretic, colchicine)
  • Adherence: daily, for life, through flares
  • Side effects: rash, GI upset, LFTs on febuxostat
  • New interacting drugs: azathioprine, clarithromycin, ciclosporin
  • BP, HbA1c, lipids, weight, QRISK3; alcohol
  • Written flare self-start plan in date

14Safety-net — say it and write itevery new prescription

  • Hot joint with fever or feeling unwell → same day (NHS 111 if closed): it could be infection.
  • Any rash on allopurinol, especially early → stop and contact us the same day.
  • Colchicine: diarrhoea, vomiting, muscle pain or weakness, tingling, bruising or sore throat → stop, same-day advice. Never stockpile.
  • The forever rule: the daily tablet continues through flares and good spells.

15Refer, special groups, never-eventsNG219 §1.6 · MHRA May 2023

  • Consider rheumatology: uncertain diagnosis · treatment contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective · CKD 3b–5 · organ transplant. Keep the flare plan and titration going while waiting.
  • CKD is not a bar to allopurinol: start lower, titrate more slowly, monitor renal function.
  • Major CV disease: allopurinol first. Febuxostat: MHRA advises caution, especially with a high crystal or tophus burden or when starting ULT.
  • Never: stop established ULT during a flare · inject a possibly septic joint · co-prescribe allopurinol with azathioprine routinely · settle for a dose instead of the target.
