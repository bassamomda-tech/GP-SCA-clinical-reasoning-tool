Hypernatraemia: the high sodium CLINIC GUIDE

Interpreting a serum sodium above 145 mmol/L in adults 18+. Not for infants and children (hypernatraemic dehydration → 999 or urgent paediatric admission), or hospital fluid and desmopressin treatment.

1Confirm it is realrepeat urgently, examine in person

  • Repeat U&E urgently if unexpected, with glucose and paired serum and urine osmolality. A spurious high sodium is uncommon: believe it if the patient is dry, drowsy or not drinking.
  • Sample error: drawn above a saline or sodium bicarbonate drip; evaporation from a delayed or uncapped sample. Resample from the other arm.
  • Sodium gain: saline, bicarbonate, hypertonic feeds, salt enemas, sodium-containing effervescent or soluble medicines.
  • Hyperglycaemia dilutes sodium: in HHS it may look normal, then rise as glucose falls. Check capillary glucose and ketones in every dehydrated patient.
  • Duration unknown? Treat it as chronic: most community cases have built up over days.
  • Not by phone: hydration, cognition and access to drinks cannot be judged remotely.

Unknown duration = chronic. In hospital, sodium should fall no faster than about 0.5 mmol/L per hour and 10–12 mmol/L in 24 h: too fast a fall causes cerebral oedema.

3Read the urine — the key discriminatorpaired with serum osmolality

Concentrated urineFor example >600 mOsm/kg: the kidney is saving water appropriately. Think intake, insensible or GI loss.
Inappropriately diluteDilute urine despite a high sodium: DI, central or nephrogenic. Same-day assessment; urgent endocrinology discussion.
Large volume, not diluteOsmotic diuresis: glucose (HHS) or urea. Check glucose and ketones first.
Interpret with careSuggestive, not diagnostic: allow for diuretics, recent intake, renal function and hyperglycaemia.

6Safe to manage at home only if — every box ticked and documentedCG174 · CQC reg 14

  • Na⁺ 146–149, not rising; no Act-today feature
  • Clinically well, cognitively intact, not polyuric
  • Can drink; a named person gives measured drinks
  • Glucose, calcium and U&E checked; drugs reviewed
  • Urgent repeat booked; safety-net given
  • Safeguarding considered for any dependent adult

8Follow-up and monitoring checklistCG185 · SfE · CG174

  • Urgent repeat U&E for mild cases at home; booked before the patient leaves
  • Target: sodium back in range, falling slowly, with the cause removed
  • Named person, measured drinks and fluid chart in place
  • Lithium: level every 3 months in year 1, then every 6 months (3-monthly if higher risk); U&E/eGFR, calcium and TFTs at least every 6 months (CG185)
  • Lithium with polyuria: urine volume and osmolality, then specialist advice
  • Desmopressin: sodium after every dose change or illness, per the endocrinology plan
  • Diuretics and laxatives reviewed against intake
  • Safeguarding question answered and recorded
  • Cause recorded in the problem list

10Special groupsCQC reg 14 · CG185 · SfE

  • Care home residents: a documented daily fluid target, monitored intake and thickened fluids where needed for dysphagia; hydration is a legal standard (CQC regulation 14).
  • Central DI: medical alert and written sick-day plan; unconscious patients given only isotonic fluid can become severely hypernatraemic.
  • After pituitary surgery: DI, then a low-sodium phase, then possibly permanent DI; sodium in either direction needs the specialist team.

11Pitfalls and never-eventsJBDS · SfE · CG174

  • "Not thirsty": thirst fails in older people and with reduced consciousness.
  • "Encourage fluids" when nobody can bring, hold or help with a drink.
  • "Poor intake" when the urine is dilute: think DI or osmotic diuresis.
  • A normal-looking sodium with high glucose: HHS.
  • Never fluid-restrict, never drop sodium fast, never arrange water-deprivation testing from primary care.
  • A sodium that settles in a dependent adult: the safeguarding question remains.
  • Never stop lithium abruptly, or start amiloride or a thiazide, without specialist advice.

12Safety-net and self-care — say it to the patient and carer, then document itCG174 · SfE

Call 999New confusion, drowsiness, a fit, collapse, or being unable to drink.
Practice or 111 todayVomiting, drinking less, passing much less urine, or large volumes of urine with thirst.
DrinksPlain water little and often, offered by a named person; keep a simple chart if a carer helps.
On desmopressinFollow the written plan; do not over-drink; vomiting or no water access → urgent help.

13Say it like thisplain English

"Your blood is too salty, which almost always means your body is short of water rather than that you've eaten too much salt. We'll put the water back slowly, because doing it too fast can be harmful, and find out why it happened. If you become confused, very sleepy or can't drink, call 999."
