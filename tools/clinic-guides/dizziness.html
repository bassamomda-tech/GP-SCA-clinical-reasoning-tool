HINTS is a specialist tool (NG127): use it only if you are trained and experienced. A normal head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation = stroke until proven otherwise. Untrained "negative HINTS" reassures no one.

1Focused history — what kind of dizzy?CKS vertigo

  • "Describe exactly what you feel": spinning (vertigo) · about to faint (presyncope) · off balance when walking (disequilibrium) · vague or light-headed.
  • Timing: seconds, triggered by rolling over or looking up → BPPV. Continuous for days → vestibular neuritis, labyrinthitis — or stroke. Recurrent attacks lasting hours with hearing change → Ménière's. Recurrent with migraine features → vestibular migraine.
  • Ears: hearing loss, tinnitus, fullness, discharge, ear pain or vesicles.
  • Neuro and vascular: diplopia, speech or swallowing change, weakness, headache or neck pain; AF, hypertension, diabetes, smoking.
  • Heart: dizziness on exertion, palpitations, chest pain, blackouts.
  • Medicines: antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers, sedatives, antiepileptics; ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides).
  • Impact: falls, work, and whether they drive.

4Investigations checklisttargeted, not a screen

  • Classic BPPV with positive Dix–Hallpike: no bloods, no imaging
  • Capillary glucose if diabetes and acute symptoms
  • 12-lead ECG for presyncope, palpitations, exertional symptoms or blackouts
  • FBC, U&E, glucose only if the story suggests anaemia, dehydration or metabolic cause
  • Audiology when hearing loss or tinnitus; MRI internal auditory meati via the specialist (NG98)
  • A normal CT head does not exclude posterior circulation stroke

5Dix–Hallpike, then EpleyCKS BPPV · trained clinicians only

BeforeAsk about neck problems: avoid in unstable cervical spine disease; take care with severe neck disease. Warn it may bring on the vertigo.
Dix–HallpikeSeated, head turned towards the tested side; lie back quickly with the neck slightly extended. Watch the eyes.
PositiveTorsional, upbeating nystagmus after a short delay, settling within a minute and fatiguing on repeat → posterior canal BPPV on that side.
ThenPositive → Epley on the same side. Negative but a strong positional story → think horizontal canal (supine roll test) or refer.

7Vestibular sedative limitsCKS · SPC

  • Prochlorperazine, cinnarizine and cyclizine are for acute severe symptoms only; continued use delays vestibular compensation.
  • Not for BPPV, chronic dizziness or "dizzy spells" in older people: drug-induced parkinsonism, postural hypotension, confusion and falls.
  • Prochlorperazine: avoid in Parkinson's disease; stop at once if dystonia or tremor.
  • Never issue on repeat. Record the stop date on the prescription.

9Driving — DVLAAssessing fitness to drive

  • Sudden, disabling dizziness without warning (for example Ménière's attacks): stop driving and tell the DVLA. The duty to notify is the driver's; record your advice.
  • Driving may restart once symptoms are controlled, subject to DVLA review. Bus and lorry (Group 2) rules are stricter.
  • Anyone: no driving while vertigo, imbalance or a sedating medicine could affect control.

10Safety-net and follow-up checklistrecord it in the notes

  • 999: new double vision, slurred speech, facial droop, weakness, cannot walk, sudden severe headache or neck pain
  • Same day: sudden hearing loss, ear vesicles or facial weakness, vomiting so they cannot keep fluids down
  • Sedative course length written down, with a stop date
  • BPPV: review if not settled after Epley; repeat or refer
  • Vestibular neuritis: review progress; exercises started
  • Driving advice given and documented
  • Older person: falls assessment offered

11Older people and fallsNICE NG249 (2025)

  • Aged 65 and over, or 50–64 with a condition that raises falls risk: ask about falls and offer a comprehensive falls assessment when indicated.
  • Check postural BP, medicines (sedatives, antihypertensives, anticholinergics), vision, hearing, feet and home hazards.
  • Refer for individually tailored strength and balance exercise.

12Vestibular rehabilitationCKS vestibular neuronitis

  • For persistent imbalance after vestibular neuritis or other one-sided vestibular loss, once the acute phase settles.
  • Home head and eye movement exercises, or physiotherapy referral. Warn that symptoms may worsen briefly as the exercises start.
  • Movement speeds recovery; bed rest and sedatives slow it.

13Never-eventsNG127 · CKS · DVLA

  • Call it "labyrinthitis" in acute continuous vertigo without examining gait, eyes and cranial nerves.
  • Rely on a HINTS examination done by someone not trained in it, or use a normal CT head to "exclude stroke" in vertigo.
  • Give prochlorperazine or betahistine for BPPV instead of an Epley manoeuvre.
  • Continue vestibular sedatives beyond the acute phase, put them on repeat, or give prochlorperazine in Parkinson's disease.
  • Miss sudden hearing loss: within 30 days of onset it needs to be seen within 24 hours.
  • Forget to ask about driving, or to document the advice.

"The tiny crystals in your balance organ have moved into the wrong place. This manoeuvre moves them back, and most people feel better quickly. Tablets would only slow your recovery — moving normally is part of the treatment."
