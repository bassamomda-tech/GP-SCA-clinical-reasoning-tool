Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in adults

For: adults with PTSD, acute stress disorder or post-trauma symptoms in primary care. Not for: children and young people under 18 (NG116 has separate recommendations; seek specialist CAMHS advice). Already on a benzodiazepine → see the benzodiazepines and z-drugs protocol.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG116NICE NG222MHRASPC (emc)
Core rule: assess risk first at every contact, then refer for individual trauma-focused therapy and confirm it was accessed — the referral matters more than the prescription, and benzodiazepines are not a treatment.

0Act now — assess risk first, at every contact

  • Imminent risk of suicide, serious self-harm or harm to others, psychosis, severe intoxication/withdrawal, or cannot maintain immediate safety → 999 or A&E.
  • High risk, not in immediate danger (mania, severe dissociation, serious safeguarding concern) → Same-day mental-health (crisis team) assessment; local urgent mental-health/crisis service or NHS 111 mental-health option.
  • Risk rising at any review, or agitation, low mood or self-harm thoughts after starting an antidepressant → Same-day review and escalation.
  • Domestic abuse, ongoing threat, children or dependants at risk → safeguarding at this consultation; ask about weapons where relevant.

1Confirm — timing and severity set the plan

Trauma history and date; re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal, negative mood and thinking, dissociation; functional impact; duration since the trauma; is the threat ongoing?

PresentationPlan
≤1 month, subthreshold symptomsConsider active monitoring; follow-up within 1 month
≤1 month, acute stress disorder or clinically important symptomsOffer individual trauma-focused CBT
>1 month, PTSD or clinically important symptomsOffer individual trauma-focused CBT; EMDR by timing (block 5)

Differentials: depression, bipolar/mania, psychosis, panic/GAD, OCD, traumatic brain injury, chronic pain, sleep disorder, substance or sedative dependence.

2Check — and document

☐ Suicide, self-harm, risk to others, protective factors  ☐ Safeguarding and dependants  ☐ Alcohol and drug use (self-medication)  ☐ Bipolar and psychosis screen  ☐ Physical injury and pain  ☐ Housing, finances, support  ☐ Preference, safety plan, crisis contact, review date

3Decide — therapy first

  • Active monitoring only for subthreshold symptoms ≤1 month; never alone if severe, impairing, risky or meeting PTSD/acute stress disorder criteria. Escalate to TF-CBT if persisting or worsening.
  • PTSD plus depression → usually treat PTSD first, unless depression is severe, risk is significant, or it prevents engagement.
  • Substance misuse → treat alongside; never exclude from PTSD treatment because of it.
  • Stabilise the context: housing, safety, finances; sleep support and grounding while awaiting therapy.

5Treat — therapy first, medication selectively

First lineIndividual trauma-focused CBT (CPT, cognitive therapy for PTSD, narrative exposure, prolonged exposure), usually 8–12 sessions
EMDRNon-combat trauma: consider at 1–3 months if preferred; offer >3 months. Combat-related: check local expertise
MedicationPrefers drug treatment → consider an SSRI or venlafaxine — not first-line in preference to therapy. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary
Not respondingCheck therapy accessed and completed, adequate trial, adherence; optimise or switch; reassess diagnosis
  • Drugs: sertraline and paroxetine licensed for PTSD; venlafaxine off-label. Screen for bipolar and psychosis first; counsel on delayed onset.
  • A good referral: validated, trauma-focused (not generic counselling), trained supervised practitioner; adapted for complex PTSD or dissociation.
  • Persistent severe nightmares despite treatment → specialist advice; do not start prazosin routinely.

6Monitor

  • Active monitoring: follow-up within 1 month.
  • Starting or changing an antidepressant: early risk-based review — within 1 week if aged 18–25 or at increased suicide risk. Watch for akathisia, agitation, suicidal ideation, mania.
  • Every few weeks: risk, adherence, alcohol/drugs, therapy attendance, symptoms and function.
  • Stopping: taper gradually; never stop abruptly.

7Refer — specialist (crisis in block 0)

  • Consider for complex PTSD, dissociation, repeated or prolonged trauma, severe comorbidity, diagnostic uncertainty, or no response to adequate trauma-focused therapy.
  • Use the local trauma pathway (e.g. Local Resilience Hub) and safeguarding contact.

8Never

  • Benzodiazepines (or any drug) to prevent PTSD, or benzodiazepines as routine treatment.
  • Stop an existing benzodiazepine or heavy alcohol use abruptly — assess withdrawal; agree a gradual, supported taper.
  • Psychologically focused debriefing to prevent or treat PTSD.
  • A drug first-line in preference to trauma-focused therapy, or re-escalating medication when therapy was never accessed.
  • Blame the patient for non-response — explore why they disengaged.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “If you feel you can't keep yourself or others safe — call 999 or go to A&E. If it's urgent but not immediate, call the crisis line or NHS 111 (mental-health option); Samaritans 116 123 for support.”   Same-day “If starting an antidepressant makes you feel more agitated or low in the first weeks, contact us the same day.”   Self-medication: “Using alcohol or drugs to cope tends to make PTSD worse — tell us and we'll help.”   Recovery: “PTSD is treatable — trauma-focused therapy helps most people; it's worth engaging with.”
