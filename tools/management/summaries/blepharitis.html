Blepharitis

For: adults with chronic anterior blepharitis, posterior blepharitis / meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) or ocular rosacea. Not for: a painful red eye, corneal, orbital or intraocular disease (block 0) or a suspicious lid lesion (block 7). Children, contact-lens wearers and suspected corneal disease → lower threshold for eye-care assessment. See also dry eye disease and chalazion.

Core rule: pain is not blepharitis — exclude a serious red eye first; daily lid hygiene is the treatment; antibiotics are not routine.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Moderate or severe pain, photophobia, new or persistent reduced vision, blur not clearing with blinking; corneal opacity, infiltrate, ulcer or staining, marked ciliary injection, hypopyon, suspected herpes simplex keratitis → Same-day eye assessment via the local urgent eye pathway (eye casualty or ED).
  • Painful red eye in a contact-lens wearer; proptosis, painful or restricted eye movements, new diplopia, peri-orbital swelling; recent eye surgery or immunosuppression with a red or painful eye → Same-day
  • Severe headache, nausea or vomiting and a fixed or irregular pupil (acute angle closure), trauma or corneal foreign body → Same-day, without delay. Chemical injury → irrigate immediately, then refer the same day.
  • While awaiting assessment: do not patch the eye; stop contact lenses; no topical ocular steroid or antibiotic–steroid combination.

1Confirm — is it blepharitis, and which type?

Usually bilateral gritty, itchy, crusty lid margins, worse on waking, relapsing. Moderate or severe pain suggests another diagnosis.

TypeSigns
AnteriorLash-base crusting, collarettes (staphylococcal or seborrhoeic)
Posterior (MGD)Thick lid margin, blocked or oily glands, foamy tear film; often rosacea
DemodexCollarettes or cylindrical dandruff around the lashes

Persistent unilateral → reconsider infection, foreign body, dermatitis, eyelid tumour, lacrimal disease.

2Check — examine and document

☐ Acuity each eye  ☐ Pupils, eye movements  ☐ Contact lenses  ☐ Cornea ± fluorescein  ☐ Lids, lashes, lesions  ☐ Dry eye, rosacea

Corneal involvement suspected or not excluded → Same-day eye-care assessment.

3Decide — what belongs in general practice

GP: triage, lid hygiene, dry eye, safety-net; aim is control, not cure. Antibiotics, doxycycline, ocular steroids → local eye-care pathway.

4Lid hygiene — first-line and long-term

StepHow
Warm compressClean, warm (not hot) compress or eyelid-warming device, 5–10 minutes
Massage, cleanMassage towards the lash line; clean lid margins with a lid wipe or cleanser
How oftenTwice daily in a flare, once daily as maintenance
EverydayNo eye rubbing, shared towels, eye make-up or contact lenses in a flare

5Treat — escalate only if persistent despite good lid care

Step 1Lid hygiene daily, long-term (block 4)
Step 2Dry eye: artificial tears; preservative-free if used frequently
Step 3 · anteriorEye-care advice first. A defined course of topical antibiotic (e.g. chloramphenicol 1% eye ointment) under the local pathway — off-label
Step 4 · MGD / rosaceaConsider referral to optometry or ophthalmology. Doxycycline 100 mg once daily, initially 6–12 weeks, per local or specialist regimen — off-label
  • Before every step: technique, adherence, drivers (rosacea, contact lenses, make-up), right diagnosis, focal lesion.
  • Doxycycline: avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding; ask about isotretinoin; photosensitivity; take upright with plenty of fluid; warfarin. Document indication, duration, review date. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Mild, improving: no routine review. Treated or persistent: review acuity if changed, pain, adherence, adverse effects, lid or lash change.
  • Chloramphenicol: no routine blood count for a short course; avoid prolonged or repeated courses (marrow toxicity).

7Refer

  • Refractory, diagnostic doubt, marginal keratitis, MGD needing systemic treatment, recurrent chalazion, unilateral disease → local pathway (community optometry or ophthalmology) by urgency.
  • Lid lesion: persistent, recurs at the same site, atypical, lash loss, ulceration, bleeding → oculoplastic or skin-cancer pathway, urgency by features.
  • NG12: melanoma → Suspected cancer pathway; SCC suspected → consider it; BCC usually routine, but the eyelid is a high-risk site.

8Never

  • Start topical ocular steroids or antibiotic–steroid drops in general practice (masked herpetic or fungal keratitis, glaucoma, cataract).
  • Patch the eye, or manage a painful red eye in a contact-lens wearer as routine blepharitis.
  • Repeat antibiotic courses without reassessing the diagnosis and lid-hygiene technique.
  • Doxycycline in pregnancy or breastfeeding, or continued indefinitely.
  • Hot compresses (burns), or tea-tree oil recommended directly in primary care.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “If your eye becomes painful, sensitive to light, or your vision blurs and does not clear with blinking — or if you wear contact lenses and your eye becomes painful, or you get swelling around the eye, pain on moving the eye or double vision — get checked the same day.”   Doxycycline: “Stop it and get medical help the same day for visual disturbance, severe headache with vomiting, or a severe rash, blistering or mouth sores.”
