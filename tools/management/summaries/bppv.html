Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

For: adults with brief, position-triggered vertigo. Not for: continuous vertigo or acute vestibular syndrome (use the vertigo pathway, vestibular neuronitis or Ménière's); red-flag presentations (block 0).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG127NICE NG249AAO-HNS 2017Bárány SocietyDVLA
Core rule: first decide brief positional episodes or continuous acute vestibular syndrome; treat BPPV with a manoeuvre, not a prescription, and never manoeuvre a red-flag patient.

0Act now — no positional testing or manoeuvre in any of these

  • Any focal neurological symptom or sign (dysarthria, diplopia, dysphagia, limb weakness or incoordination, facial numbness) → 999 emergency stroke pathway.
  • Central nystagmus (direction-changing gaze-evoked, vertical, persistent pure torsional), severe truncal ataxia or new severe headache or neck pain (dissection) → Emergency assessment
  • Acute vestibular syndrome (vertigo for hours to days, spontaneous nystagmus, vomiting, unsteady) → Stroke pathway; HINTS only if trained, otherwise refer immediately.
  • Sudden unilateral hearing loss with acute vertigo → Same-day; stroke pathway (not ENT alone) if continuous, unsteady, nystagmus, neuro signs or vascular risk.
  • Continuous vertigo not explained by BPPV → Same-day reassessment. Downbeat, no latency, non-fatiguing or other atypical positional nystagmus → Same-day neurological/stroke assessment; treat as central until excluded.

1Confirm — history, then Dix-Hallpike

History: rotational vertigo for seconds (usually <1 min) on rolling in bed, lying down, looking up or bending; normal between.

Negative test ≠ no BPPV: re-test another day; consider vestibular migraine, PPPD.

2Check — before the couch

☐ Cranial nerves, limbs, speech, swallow, diplopia  ☐ Gait, sitting balance, cerebellar signs  ☐ Hearing  ☐ New headache or neck pain  ☐ Neck safety: can lie flat and tolerate rotation and extension

Typical BPPV: no imaging, bloods or audiology. Document latency, duration, direction, fatigability.

3Decide

  • Confirmed BPPV → offer a repositioning manoeuvre if you are trained and there is no unstable cervical-spine disease.
  • Not trained, or unsafe neck → do not force; vestibular physiotherapy.
  • Declines, cannot tolerate, or mild and improving → observation with safety-netting; manoeuvre preferred if falls or occupational risk.

4Target — treatment goals

Settled after one or more manoeuvresResidual unsteadiness for several days is not failureReview within 1 month if persists, recurs often or uncertain

5Treat — Epley for left posterior canal; mirror for right

Steps 1–2Head 45° left; lie back, head 20–30° below horizontal. Hold ≥ about 30 s
Step 3Turn head 90° right, keeping it extended. Hold
Step 4Roll onto right side, head a further 90°, nose to floor. Hold
Step 5 · thenSit up slowly, chin down. Reassess; repeat only if tolerated
  • Before: warn vertigo and nausea may be intense but brief; vomit bowl in reach.
  • After: no routine restrictions; home exercises if suitable.
  • No routine vestibular suppressants; short antiemetic only if severe vomiting. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Still positive → reconsider canal, side, technique or diagnosis; do not just repeat Epley.
  • Recurrence is common → re-treat. Imbalance after success → consider PPPD, hypofunction, medication.
  • Suppressant on repeat → review and deprescribe (check why it was started).

7Refer — local pathway

  • Vestibular physio: not resolved at about 4 weeks; multi-canal, post-traumatic or apogeotropic.
  • ENT / audiovestibular: uncertainty, cochlear symptoms, Ménière's, refractory.
  • Aged 65+: fallen in the last year with frailty, injury needing treatment, loss of consciousness, unable to get up, or 2+ falls → comprehensive falls assessment (NG249).

8Never

  • HINTS for brief positional episodes, isolated dizziness or an asymptomatic patient.
  • A manoeuvre in a red-flag presentation, or forced neck movement.
  • A vestibular suppressant instead of a manoeuvre, or betahistine for typical BPPV.
  • Repeated Epley without documenting the nystagmus or reconsidering the diagnosis.
  • Exclude posterior-circulation stroke on a normal gait alone.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Sudden weakness, numbness, slurred speech, double vision, trouble swallowing, cannot walk or sit up unaided, or a sudden severe headache — call 999.”   Same day “If the dizziness becomes constant or you notice new hearing loss, contact us the same day.”   Meanwhile: “No driving, ladders, heights, machinery or swimming alone while dizzy or unsteady; get out of bed in stages.”   Driving: check DVLA guidance, especially Group 2 or sudden, unprovoked episodes; document the advice.
