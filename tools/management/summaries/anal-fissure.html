Anal fissure in adults

For: adults aged 18 and over with a typical anal fissure. Not for: children (constipation management first; persistent, recurrent, severe, atypical or bleeding fissures → paediatric assessment; see constipation in children), or atypical fissures (see Crohn's disease).

Core rule: soft, easily passed stools are the foundation every topical treatment depends on — and a lateral, multiple, painless, indurated, large or non-healing fissure is not a simple fissure: reassess and refer, don't repeat the ointment.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Perianal abscess or fistula, systemic illness or immunosuppression → Same-day assessment
  • Rectal or abdominal mass, unexplained anal mass or ulceration, persistent strong concern or progressive symptoms → consider Suspected cancer pathway — do not delay for a negative or unavailable FIT.
  • NG12 colorectal features → offer FIT: change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia; 40+ unexplained weight loss and abdominal pain; under 50 rectal bleeding plus unexplained abdominal pain or weight loss; 50+ unexplained rectal bleeding, abdominal pain or weight loss; 60+ anaemia. FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g → consider Suspected cancer pathway
  • Atypical fissure (lateral, multiple, painless, indurated, unusually large, non-healing or recurrent) → assess for Crohn's, HIV or immunosuppression, TB, syphilis, inflammatory or haematological disease, anal cancer, trauma; examine and refer.
  • Diarrhoea, abdominal pain or perianal Crohn's features → gastroenterology. A fissure does not exclude colorectal pathology.

1Confirm — typical history and site

Severe pain on or after defecation, bright-red blood on the paper, usually posterior midline, often after hard stool.

TypeDurationFeatures
Acute<6 weeksMost managed conservatively first
Chronic≥6 weeksSentinel pile, hypertrophied papilla, exposed sphincter fibres, indurated edges. Reaching 6 weeks alone does not mean GTN is needed

2Check — before treating

☐ Gentle inspection; abdomen where it alters management  ☐ Colorectal red flags  ☐ Bowel pattern: hard stool, loading, diarrhoea, opioids  ☐ GTN contraindications, PDE-5 inhibitors, pregnancy, breastfeeding

DRE often too painful at first — don't force it; incomplete examination → arrange review or specialist assessment.

3Decide — who gets a sphincter relaxant

  • Acute → stool softening and symptom relief; most improve.
  • Persistent despite conservative treatment, or chronic or recurrent → consider a topical sphincter relaxant and/or referral (earlier if pain persists despite initial care).
  • Everyone: individualised fluids (no fixed volume in heart failure, CKD, frailty), fibre gradually where suitable, no straining; warm baths for relief only.

4Target — treatment goals

GoalDetail
StoolsSoft, easily passed; continue after healing — recurrence is common
Topical coursesLidocaine ≤1–2 weeks; GTN up to 8 weeks; diltiazem about 6–8 weeks

5Treat — soften first, then relax the sphincter

SoftenOsmotic laxative (e.g. macrogol) if stools stay hard or fibre is poorly tolerated; titrate to avoid diarrhoea.
Pain reliefLidocaine before defecation for severe pain, ≤1–2 weeks; reassess if pain persists.
Relaxant 1stRectogesic (GTN 0.4%) by brand, intra-anally every 12 hours, up to 8 weeks. Licensed for pain, not proven healing.
Relaxant 2ndDiltiazem 2% twice daily, about 6–8 weeks — unlicensed, tell the patient. If GTN not tolerated or unsuitable, or breastfeeding.
  • Before GTN: no PDE-5 inhibitors or nitrates, hypotension, raised ICP, migraine, aortic or mitral stenosis, HOCM, marked anaemia, closed-angle glaucoma, pregnancy; breastfeeding only with specialist advice.
  • Headache very common (~57%) → stop; diltiazem or referral. Dizziness — care driving. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.

6Monitor — review at 6–8 weeks: healing, tolerability, stools, atypical features

  • Not healing? Stools genuinely soft? Course used as directed? GTN stopped for headache → diltiazem. Atypical → reconsider the cause, refer.

7Refer — colorectal surgery

  • Pain persists after about 6–8 weeks of appropriate conservative and topical treatment; earlier if severe, recurrent or atypical.
  • Refer earlier: recurrent, suspected Crohn's, immunosuppression, atypical site or appearance, significant continence risk, older adults with an unusual or non-healing lesion, diagnostic uncertainty.
  • Specialist chooses the procedure; sphincterotomy carries an incontinence risk — a shared decision.

8Never

  • GTN with a PDE-5 inhibitor (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) or other nitrates.
  • GTN in pregnancy, or Rectogesic routinely under 18.
  • Repeat the ointment for an atypical or non-healing fissure without reassessment.
  • Force a painful DRE, or assume an incompletely examined fissure is uncomplicated.
  • Let a negative or unavailable FIT delay referral for a mass, anal ulceration or strong concern.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “A painful swelling, fever or discharge near the back passage needs to be seen the same day — contact us or NHS 111.”   Within a week “Blood mixed in with your stool, a change in bowel habit, weight loss or tiredness — contact us to be seen within a week.”   GTN: “It often causes headaches — if it does, tell us and we'll switch you.”   Stools: “Keeping stools soft is the key — keep it up even once it heals.”
