Tennis elbow (lateral elbow tendinopathy) in adults

For: adults with gradual, use-provoked lateral elbow pain in primary care. Not for: children and adolescents (rethink the diagnosis; NG12 bone criteria), hot swollen joint, acute trauma, neck or nerve causes — use the routes in block 0.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
BESS pathway 2023NICE HTG201 · HTG299NG12 (Apr 2026)NG65MHRA NSAIDs 2013/2015
Core rule: confirm it is tendon pain, not the neck, a nerve or the joint; then load the forearm extensors and modify the task — and do not offer steroid injection routinely.

0Act now — exclude first, and again at any review that is off course

  • Hot, swollen elbow, fever or sudden loss of movement (septic or crystal arthritis) → Same-day assessment; septic or unstable → don't delay antibiotics. Trauma with deformity, neurovascular compromise or suspected fracture → Same-day emergency
  • Progressive motor weakness (finger or thumb drop, posterior interosseous nerve palsy), significant radicular deficit, myelopathy signs → Urgent spinal or upper-limb service. Mild stable paraesthesia → prompt outpatient review.
  • Adult, unexplained lump increasing in size → consider Urgent direct access ultrasound ≤2 weeks; X-ray suggests bone sarcoma → consider suspected cancer pathway referral.
  • Child or young person, unexplained bone swelling or pain → consider Very urgent X-ray ≤48 hours; enlarging lump → consider very urgent ultrasound ≤48 hours.

1Confirm — a clinical diagnosis; no single test proves it

Tender just distal and anterior to the lateral epicondyle; pain on gripping, resisted wrist and middle-finger extension; full elbow range. No imaging; X-ray only if diagnosis uncertain.

2Check — history and baseline

☐ Trigger task, job, load change  ☐ Past injections  ☐ Neck, sensation; radial tunnel/PIN if distal pain or weakness  ☐ Pain-free grip baseline

Don't miss: enthesitis without mechanical cause — persistent, several sites, or with psoriasis, IBD, uveitis, inflammatory back pain, dactylitis, GI/GU infection or family history → rheumatology.

3Decide — counsel, then share the injection decision

  • Outlook: most improve substantially within 6–12 months; may fluctuate or recur. Don't dismiss persistent symptoms.
  • Steroid injection: not offered routinely. Still requested → document uncertainty, alternatives, recurrence risk; consider specialist delivery; discourage repeats.
  • Shockwave, blood or PRP injection: not routine; specialist-led under NICE special arrangements.

4Target — the trade-off to explain

Recovered at 1 yearSteroid 83% · placebo 96%
RecurrenceSteroid 54% · placebo 12%

Steroid gives the fastest relief at 6 weeks. Goal: function, not just pain scores.

5Treat — exercise-based care is first line

Step 1 · from day 1Change the trigger task, not all use: palm-up lifting, bigger grip, lighter tools, breaks, desk set-up
Step 2 · earlyTolerable isometric or isotonic wrist-extensor loading; gentle stretching; grip work
Step 3 · as toleratedProgressive eccentric or heavy slow resistance; rotation and grip strength
Step 4 · returnFull work or sport; check technique; maintenance strength
  • Mild discomfort fine; no sustained next-day flare. Work: task modification, not routine sick leave. Strap: trial, modest expectations.
  • Topical NSAID first for short-term pain; not with an oral NSAID without a clear reason. Paracetamol if suitable.
  • Oral NSAID if topical not enough: short course; check GI, renal, CV, anticoagulant, antiplatelet, steroid, SSRI; no diclofenac in IHD, PAD, cerebrovascular disease or heart failure; no casual high-dose ibuprofen (≥2,400 mg/day); PPI per risk. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor — by severity, work impact and response

  • Often 4–8 weeks: exercises done and progressed? Task modified? Reinforce the timescale.
  • About 3 months, no progress → re-examine (neck, nerve, joint); consider supervised physio.
  • Review sooner for red flags, neurological symptoms or diagnostic doubt.

7Refer — routine (urgent routes in block 0)

  • Physio: needs help with loading, progression or adherence; symptoms affecting work (± occupational health).
  • MSK or upper-limb service: disabling symptoms despite a sustained programme (clinical judgement on timing); doubt; mechanical symptoms or abnormal X-ray.
  • Rheumatology: enthesitis criteria in block 2. Surgery is rare and decided in secondary care.

8Never

  • Routine or repeated steroid injection in primary care.
  • Routine referral for shockwave, or autologous blood or PRP injection.
  • Treat a hot, swollen or stiff elbow, or neurological signs, as tendinopathy.
  • Manage a child's lateral elbow or bone pain as ordinary tennis elbow.
  • Prescribe complete rest or prolonged splinting.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “If the elbow becomes hot or swollen, you can’t straighten it, or you get numbness or weakness in the hand — contact us or NHS 111 the same day.”   ≤7 days “Pain at night that stops you sleeping, or a lump that keeps growing — book to see us within a week.”   Outlook: “Most people improve a lot within 6 to 12 months, though it can come and go.”   Injection: “A steroid injection may help within weeks, but more people still have pain, or it comes back, a year later.”
