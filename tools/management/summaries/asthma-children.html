Asthma in children

For: children in primary care — the acute attack by age (under 5, 5 and over), diagnosis, and the NG245 ladder for ages 5–11. Not for: ages 12 and over (use the adult asthma protocol), pre-school wheeze episodes (use pre-school wheeze), or hospital care of the attack.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG245 (2024, with BTS/SIGN)SIGN 158 (acute)NICE asthma QSMHRA montelukastGreen Book ch 19
Core rule: children die when severity is under-recognised — a silent chest means less air moving, not less asthma; and recheck technique, adherence, spacer and smoke exposure before every step-up.

0Act now — assess under-5s and 5 and over separately; SpO₂, RR, HR, talking or feeding, air entry

  • Life-threatening — any one: silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, exhaustion, altered consciousness, confusion or agitation, arrhythmia, hypotension; severe deterioration or no response → 999 stay with the child; oxygen, repeated nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium, systemic steroid while waiting.
  • Acute severe: too breathless to talk or feed, RR or HR above the local age thresholds, accessory muscles, or SpO₂ <92% on air → immediate treatment (O₂ if SpO₂ <92%, prednisolone if able to swallow, ipratropium if nebulising) and Urgent hospital transfer Nebulising must not delay transfer.
  • Incomplete response, relapse within a few hours or ongoing frequent bronchodilator need → Urgent hospital assessment
  • Under 2 with suspected acute asthma → Urgent paediatric assessment Under 5: clinical assessment, not PEF; think bronchiolitis, croup, foreign body, pneumonia.
  • Mild/moderate, SpO₂ ≥92%, stable → salbutamol 100 micrograms by pMDI + spacer, one puff at a time; reassess and repeat per response and the local acute pathway.

1Confirm — variable symptoms, triggers, atopy; then test (5–16)

TestSupports asthma if
1 · FeNO, if available≥35 ppb with a suggestive history
2 · Spirometry + reversibilityFEV₁ up ≥12% from baseline, or ≥10% of predicted
3 · PEF twice daily for 2 weeksVariability ≥20%
4 · Still uncertainSensitisation/IgE, eosinophils, or refer

Under 5: testing not reliable — record suspected asthma, treat on symptoms and response, review regularly. Recurrent wheeze alone ≠ confirmed asthma.

2Check — diagnosis and every review

☐ Day symptoms, night waking, reliever use, activity limits, school absence ☐ Attacks and steroid courses ☐ Technique, spacer, adherence ☐ Triggers: viral, allergens, exercise, cold air ☐ Smoking or vaping at home, car, visitors; damp and mould

3Decide — every child

  • Reliever for all: salbutamol 100 micrograms, 1–2 puffs as needed via spacer (mask if young). Frequent use outside the plan = poor control.
  • 5–11 at diagnosis → twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS + SABA as needed; not SABA alone.
  • Selected under-5s (interval symptoms or severe episodes) → consider an 8–12-week trial of twice-daily low-dose ICS + SABA; if resolved, consider stopping ICS and SABA; review after a further 3 months.
  • Everyone: written action plan (shared with school or nursery with consent); reliever and spacer at school; smoke-free home and car — offer parents cessation; check flu-vaccine eligibility.

5Treat — NG245 ladder, ages 5–11

Step 1Paediatric low-dose ICS twice daily + SABA as needed
Step 2 · uncontrolledConsider low-dose MART if the child can manage it; if not, LTRA trial or ICS/LABA twice daily
Step 3Moderate-dose MART or moderate-dose ICS/LABA
Step 4 · still uncontrolledRefer paediatric respiratory — before any high-dose therapy
  • Before every step: technique, adherence, spacer, smoke. Step down when control is sustained.
  • LTRA 8–12 weeks, stop if ineffective. Montelukast (MHRA): discuss sleep, mood and behaviour changes first.
  • Not every ICS/formoterol inhaler is licensed for paediatric MART (SPC). Prednisolone by age and weight, never adult doses. Doses per BNFc and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • After every attack or steroid course → ≤2 working days of treatment or discharge: SABA use, technique, triggers, steroids, plan, referral need.
  • Every visit: technique, adherence. At least annually: control → step up or down.
  • Growth annually on ICS. Flu: repeated steroids or past admission = risk group; no live nasal vaccine in recent wheeze.

7Refer — paediatric respiratory

Diagnostic doubt; poor control despite good technique and adherence; before high-dose therapy; recurrent steroid-treated attacks; under 5 with recurrent wheeze needing escalation.

8Never

  • Read a silent chest as improvement, or leave a child with life-threatening features.
  • Use repeated spacer doses as the only treatment for a hypoxic or exhausted child.
  • Step up before checking technique, adherence, spacer use and smoke exposure.
  • Leave a child aged 5–11 with diagnosed asthma on SABA alone.
  • Let parents start leftover prednisolone unless it is in an agreed specialist-led plan.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “If the reliever isn't working, they're struggling to breathe, talk or feed, their lips look blue or grey, or they're very drowsy, confused or exhausted — call 999.”   Same-day “Needing the blue inhaler more often, waking at night, or struggling at school — contact us the same day to review the preventer.”   Plan: “Follow the written plan — it tells you what to do as symptoms change.”   Spacer: “Always use the spacer, even in an attack.”

✓Safe consultation check — all five done, case safely managed

SpO₂, RR, HR, speech/feeding recorded; block 0 excluded Diagnosis objective (5–16) or coded suspected (under 5) Technique, spacer, adherence, smoke checked before any step-up Action plan shared with school; review booked Safety-net given and documented
