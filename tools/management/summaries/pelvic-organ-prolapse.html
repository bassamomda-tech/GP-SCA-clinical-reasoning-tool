Pelvic organ prolapse

For: women, trans men and non-binary people with female pelvic organs and a vaginal bulge or prolapse symptoms. Not for: managing coexisting urinary incontinence (use the female urinary incontinence protocol).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG123NG12 (updated April 2026)NG210HTG444MHRA mesh guidance
Core rule: examine every bulge — a suspicious mass or unexplained post-menopausal bleeding goes on the cancer pathway; then treat by symptoms, not stage, with conservative care before surgery.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Irregular, friable, fixed or persistently ulcerated lesion, or one you cannot distinguish from malignancy → Suspected cancer pathway. Unexplained palpable mass in or at the entrance to the vagina → consider one (NG12). Do not manually reduce a suspicious mass.
  • Unexplained post-menopausal bleeding not attributable to HRT: 55 or over → Suspected cancer pathway; under 55 → consider one. Never blame a decubitus ulcer.
  • Acute urinary retention → Same-day assessment, bladder decompression, urgent gynaecology or urology advice.
  • Procidentia with flank pain, renal impairment or suspected hydronephrosis (upper-tract obstruction) → Same-day specialist
  • Severe pain or suspected strangulated or irreducible prolapse, heavy bleeding with haemodynamic concern, infected or severely ulcerated procidentia, a pessary that cannot be removed → Same-day

1Confirm — examine, never assume

With consent: speculum and bimanual; ask her to cough or strain. Name the compartment: anterior (cystocele), posterior (rectocele, enterocele), apical (uterus or vault).

2Check — selectively

☐ Bladder, bowel (splinting → rectocele) and sexual symptoms — ask  ☐ ICIQ-VS or PFDI-20  ☐ Dipstick if urinary symptoms  ☐ PVR if voiding difficulty or recurrent UTI  ☐ Previous mesh  ☐ Cervical screening

Before surgical referral: consider a cough stress test with the prolapse reduced (masked stress incontinence). No routine imaging or urodynamics. Nulliparous, or under 40 with little obstetric history → think connective tissue disorder.

3Decide — by symptoms, not the stage number

  • Asymptomatic, any stage → may observe, with examination, safety-netting and review.
  • Symptomatic stage 1–2 → consider supervised PFMT for at least 16 weeks as a first option (often self-referral).
  • Symptomatic → consider a pessary, alone or with PFMT.
  • Genitourinary symptoms or signs of menopause → consider vaginal oestrogen; not routine for every woman.
  • Lifestyle: weight loss if BMI over 30, treat constipation and chronic cough, minimise lifting that aggravates, stop smoking.

4Target — symptoms and quality of life

Success = less bulge and better bladder, bowel and sexual function, not anatomy. Compare ICIQ-VS before and after treatment.

  • Before a pessary: agree a follow-up and removal plan; explain refits, intercourse, discharge, bleeding, expulsion; can she self-manage? Not available locally → urogynaecology.
  • Vaginal oestrogen: check contraindications; after breast cancer, non-hormonal first, with oncology input. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Pessary: removed at least every 6 months; inspect for erosion or ulceration. Atrophic → topical oestrogen. Discharge or odour → HVS, exclude BV, check the size.
  • PFMT at 16 weeks: minimal improvement → gynaecology for pessary or surgery discussion.
  • After surgery: recurrent bulge symptoms → re-examine and refer back.

7Refer

  • Gynaecology (routine): troublesome despite conservative care or wants surgery; stage 3–4 with significant symptoms; apical or vault; persistent voiding dysfunction, recurrent UTI or obstructed defaecation; recurrence; uncertain diagnosis.
  • Suspected mesh complication (pain, exposure, bleeding, recurrent UTI, dyspareunia, fistula — even years later) → regional mesh-complication pathway (confirm the Greater Manchester route); Yellow Card; prescription-exemption letter.

8Never

  • Attribute post-menopausal bleeding to the prolapse or a decubitus ulcer.
  • Manually reduce a suspicious mass, or attempt mesh removal in primary care.
  • Refer for repair without the masked stress incontinence check.
  • Remove an impacted or retained pessary without examination.
  • Recommend abstinence — raise sexual function yourself.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “If you can't pass urine, have severe pain, the bulge won't go back, or you bleed heavily — contact us or A&E the same day.”   Pessary: “If you can't remove it, or you get new bleeding or a smelly discharge — contact us or A&E the same day.”   Bleeding: “Any new bleeding — contact us the same day; after the menopause it always needs checking.”   Mesh: “Pain, bleeding, discharge or pain with sex after mesh surgery, even years later — book to see us.”
