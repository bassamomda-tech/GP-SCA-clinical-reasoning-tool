Dementia

For: people with suspected or diagnosed dementia, and their carers, in primary care. Not for: an acute change in confusion (assess for delirium, block 0), Parkinson's disease itself (Parkinson's disease protocol) or the last days of life (care of the dying).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG97NICE CG103NICE NG71MHRADVLA
Core rule: establish the subtype, because it decides which drugs are safe; treat any acute change as delirium until assessed; and search for the cause of distress before any antipsychotic.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Acute or fluctuating change in confusion, alertness or behaviour over hours to days → assess for delirium, including delirium on top of dementia → Same-day clinical assessment; systemically unwell or unsafe → Same-day hospital admission
  • Look for: infection (especially urine and chest), constipation, urinary retention, dehydration, pain, hypoxia and any new drug (especially anticholinergics). Use the 4AT as an aid, with collateral history and examination.
  • Rapid decline over weeks to months → Urgent neurology referral
  • Dementia with Lewy bodies → avoid antipsychotics, including haloperidol and other dopamine-blocking drugs: sensitivity reactions can be catastrophic and irreversible. New hallucinations still need assessing for delirium.

1Confirm — diagnosis and subtype

  • Collateral history, cognitive and functional assessment, examination; structural imaging unless well established and subtype clear.
  • Memory service once reversible causes are investigated and dementia is still suspected, or subtype uncertain.
  • Subtype: Alzheimer's, vascular, mixed, Lewy body, frontotemporal. Do not diagnose vascular dementia from the burden of vascular lesions on imaging alone.

2Check — reversible contributors

☐ Blood and urine tests for reversible causes  ☐ Medication review: anticholinergic burden (oxybutynin, tricyclics, sedating antihistamines) → reduce or replace where possible  ☐ Delirium, depression, pain, hearing and vision, sleep  ☐ Capacity for each decision  ☐ Risk and safeguarding

Don't miss: unrecognised pain is a leading cause of agitation.

3Decide — the non-drug foundation comes first

  • Group cognitive stimulation therapy (mild–moderate); named coordinator and written care plan; carer needs assessment and respite.
  • Plan early: LPA (made with capacity, registered before use), advance statement and decision, ReSPECT. Never assume a relative can decide — check for a registered health and welfare LPA.
  • DVLA: patient must tell the DVLA and insurer; document. Lacks capacity or refuses, with significant safety concern → follow DVLA/GMC guidance on clinician notification.

7Refer

  • Old-age psychiatry: severe BPSD, or distress or risk not settling with non-drug measures.
  • Neurology or specialist: young-onset dementia or atypical features (rapid decline: block 0).
  • Social care and safeguarding: carer breakdown, safeguarding concerns, place-of-care decisions.

  • Start under local memory-service/shared care; GP continues once plan and monitoring are set. Doses: NICE/SPC (GMMMG).
  • AChE inhibitor: check pulse, falls, interacting drugs; consider ECG if bradycardia, syncope or cardiac/conduction disease.
  • BPSD: structured search for physical, psychological, environmental and communication causes; non-drug measures first. Antipsychotic only for risk of harm, or severe distress from agitation, hallucinations or delusions, after non-drug measures fail; discuss stroke and death risk.
  • Mild–moderate depression or anxiety in mild–moderate dementia → consider psychological treatments; antidepressants not routinely.

6Monitor

  • Each review: function, falls, weight, pain, mood, sleep, behaviour (not just cognitive scores); carer strain, safeguarding, driving, finances, advance care planning. Any decline → exclude delirium, pain, anticholinergics, mood and sensory loss first.
  • AChE inhibitor at start and titration: bradycardia, syncope, falls, GI upset, weight loss, vivid dreams.
  • Antipsychotic: lowest dose, shortest time; reassess at least every 6 weeks; stop if no clear ongoing benefit.

8Never

  • An antipsychotic, haloperidol or another dopamine-blocking drug in dementia with Lewy bodies.
  • Assume an acute change is "the dementia getting worse".
  • An antipsychotic before searching for the cause, or left without review for more than 6 weeks.
  • Valproate for agitation or aggression, unless indicated for another condition.
  • Stop an AChE inhibitor solely because dementia has become severe.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “A sudden change in confusion, alertness or behaviour over hours to days — contact us the same day (NHS 111 if we are closed).” 999 “Very drowsy or hard to wake, new weakness or slurred speech, or seriously unwell — call 999.”   Antipsychotic: “It is short-term and reviewed — tell us about drowsiness, falls or stiffness.”   Safeguarding: “Concerns about safety, neglect, finances or driving — raise them with us.”   Carer: “If you are struggling, tell us early — support and respite are available.”
