Prostatitis

For: adult men (and people with male reproductive organs) with suspected acute bacterial prostatitis or chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome. Not for: simple lower UTI (UTI in men protocol), urethritis or STI (sexual health), catheter-associated UTI (NG113 pathway), children.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG110MHRA fluoroquinolones (Jan 2024)NG12 (April 2026)UKHSA PCRMPEAU 2026 (international)
Core rule: decide first — febrile infected prostate or chronic pelvic pain. Infection: screen for sepsis and retention, send an MSU before the first dose, 14 days then review. Chronic pain: no repeated antibiotics.

0Act now — screen every febrile man with urinary symptoms before prescribing

  • Sepsis (rigors, high or low temperature, tachycardia, hypotension, raised RR, new confusion, mottled skin) → 999 / ED now IV antibiotics, not an oral course from the surgery.
  • Acute urinary retention (painful, palpable bladder) → ED now for drainage; do not force a catheter through an inflamed prostate in general practice.
  • Suspected prostatic abscess (fluctuant or exquisitely tender gland, persistent fever, deterioration) → Same-day hospital/urology and imaging.
  • Not improving 48 hours after starting the antibiotic, or can't take or keep down oral antibiotics → Same-day hospital Worse at any time → reassess.
  • Significant immunosuppression, diabetes or recent biopsy/instrumentation with systemic symptoms → Same-day hospital After a biopsy → the urology team that did it.
  • Sudden severe unilateral testicular pain → 999 / ED now torsion until excluded; do not delay for an ultrasound.

1Confirm — the category decides whether antibiotics have any role

PatternCategory → action
Abrupt fever, rigors, perineal or low back pain, dysuria; very tender glandI · Acute bacterial → culture, 14 days then review
Recurrent UTIs, often the same organismII · Chronic bacterial → urology/microbiology advice
Pelvic, perineal, penile or ejaculatory pain ≥3 of the last 6 months, no proven infectionIII · Chronic pelvic pain syndrome → pain approach, not antibiotics
Incidental, no symptomsIV → no treatment in most

2Check — in the room

☐ Temp, pulse, BP, RR, mental state  ☐ Can he pass urine? Palpable bladder?  ☐ Abdomen, flanks, genitals, scrotum  ☐ Gentle rectal exam only if useful — no massage  ☐ MSU culture before the first dose (don't delay if seriously unwell)  ☐ Previous cultures, antibiotics in last 3 months  ☐ NAAT if sexual risk, discharge or epididymo-orchitis  ☐ FBC, CRP, renal function, glucose if unwell, higher risk or uncertain

Don't miss: a hard, irregular gland is a different question from a tender, boggy one — re-examine once settled.

3Decide

  • Acute, not septic, passing urine, can take tablets → oral antibiotic at home, 14 days then review.
  • Fluoroquinolone only when other commonly recommended antibiotics are inappropriate (MHRA 2024); document why — prostate penetration alone is not a reason.
  • Pain: paracetamol ± low-dose weak opioid, or ibuprofen if suitable.
  • Chronic pelvic pain: offer analgesia, pelvic-floor physiotherapy where appropriate and psychological support; consider an alpha-blocker for prominent voiding symptoms within the first year.

5Treat — acute bacterial, 14 days then review

Fluoroquinolone inappropriateTrimethoprim 200 mg BD; specialist advice, susceptibility guidance. K⁺, creatinine; not with methotrexate.
Others inappropriateCiprofloxacin 500 mg BD or ofloxacin 200 mg BD — document why; counsel harms.
Specialist advice onlyLevofloxacin 500 mg OD or co-trimoxazole 960 mg BD.
Red flagsHospital: IV antibiotics and urology.

Choose on culture, past results, recent antibiotics, local resistance data, renal function. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • 48 hours: should be settling; not better → Same-day hospital
  • Culture resistant and not improving → susceptible, narrower agent.
  • Day 14: symptoms, examination, urine, bloods → stop, or a further 14 days if symptoms persist.
  • ≥6 weeks after infection: LUTS, ED, haematuria or abnormal gland → consider PSA (and DRE); recheck urine for blood.

7Refer

  • Prostate feels malignant on DRE → Suspected cancer pathway even if PSA normal or not yet available. PSA above age threshold (>2.5 at 40–49, >3.5 at 50–59, >4.5 at 60–69, >6.5 at 70–79 µg/L) → consider suspected cancer referral, not a repeat.
  • ≥45, visible haematuria persisting or recurring after successful treatment → Suspected cancer pathway
  • Urology, soon: recurrent or same-organism infection, after instrumentation, persisting after a second course. Routine: refractory chronic pelvic pain.

8Never

  • Prostatic massage in acute infection.
  • Nitrofurantoin when prostatitis is suspected — it does not reach the prostate.
  • A fluoroquinolone undocumented, for convenience, or for non-bacterial chronic prostatitis.
  • PSA during infection or within 6 weeks; or a high PSA blamed on prostatitis.
  • Repeated or empirical antibiotics for chronic pelvic pain, or a second oral agent at home at 48 hours.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 / A&E “Shivers, feel very unwell, confused or drowsy, or you can't pass water — go to A&E straight away or call 999.”   Same-day “Not starting to feel better within two days, or worse at any time — contact us the same day (111 if we are closed).”   Course: “Two weeks, not the usual one — I may extend it. PSA once this has settled, at least 6 weeks.”   Fluoroquinolone: “Tendon pain or swelling, pins and needles, marked weakness or severe mood changes — stop it and seek urgent medical advice.”
