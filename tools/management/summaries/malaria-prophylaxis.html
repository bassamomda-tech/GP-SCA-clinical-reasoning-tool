Malaria prophylaxis

For: pre-travel advice for adults and children travelling from the UK to malaria-risk areas, and anyone ill after return. Not for: treating confirmed malaria (hospital); pregnancy, young children to high-risk areas, asplenia or immunosuppression, or standby treatment → specialist travel-health advice.

UKHSA UKMEAG 2026MHRA DSU mefloquine (2013)BNF / BNFCUK malaria treatment guidelines (2016)Notification Regs 2010
Core rule: fever during travel or in the year after return (or later) is malaria until proven otherwise — test the same day; tablets reduce the risk but never exclude it.

0Act now — ask about travel in every febrile patient

  • Fever or flu-like illness during travel or after return from a malaria area (especially ≤3 months; up to a year or later) → Same-day malaria test: RDT + thick and thin films (FBC, renal, liver, glucose) via lab or acute team. Do not wait for fever spikes.
  • Severe features: confusion or reduced consciousness, seizures, jaundice, breathlessness, acidotic breathing, hypoglycaemia, severe anaemia, bleeding, low urine output, shock, or cannot take oral treatment → Emergency admission — do not delay transfer for results. Children: drowsiness, poor feeding.
  • First test negative, still suspected → repeat per local lab protocol, commonly 24–48 h. One negative test does not exclude malaria.
  • Asplenia or significant immunosuppression with fever after travel → Same-day hospital assessment, not just community testing.
  • Suspected malaria → notify the proper officer via the local UKHSA Health Protection Team On suspicion — do not wait for lab confirmation.

1Assess risk — A: awareness

Check the current UKMEAG country recommendation (TravelHealthPro) every time — some areas need bite prevention only.

2Check — every traveller

☐ Age, weight  ☐ Renal, hepatic  ☐ Asplenia, immunosuppression  ☐ Warfarin, antiepileptics, amiodarone, QT drugs  ☐ Cardiac disease  ☐ Past antimalarial intolerance

Don't miss: breast milk does not protect the baby — assess the infant separately.

3Decide — B: bites, C: a drug they can take

  • Bite prevention for everyone: DEET up to 50% (pregnancy, breastfeeding, infants over 2 months; sunscreen first), treated net, cover up from dusk to dawn.
  • Psychiatric history or seizures → no mefloquine. Under 12 → no doxycycline. Severe renal impairment → avoid atovaquone-proguanil.
  • Last-minute trip → atovaquone-proguanil often easiest. Long stay → any of the three if suitable.
  • Pregnancy, unavoidable travel → specialist-led; accidental exposure is no reason to terminate.
  • No care within 24 h → standby treatment only after specialist travel-medicine assessment.

5Treat — adult regimens; children by weight (UKHSA table / BNFC)

Atovaquone-proguanil1 adult tablet (250/100 mg) daily with food or milky drink. 1–2 days before → 7 days after
Doxycycline100 mg daily. 1–2 days before → 4 weeks after. Full glass of water, upright; sun protection
Mefloquine250 mg weekly. 2–3 weeks before → 4 weeks after. Prescriber checklist + patient alert card
Every travellerStart and stop dates in writing; explain the post-travel tail — where most adherence fails
  • Warfarin: agree a monitoring plan with the anticoagulation service; INR stable before departure.
  • Mefloquine: contraindicated with amiodarone; check QT drugs and cardiac disease. Antiepileptics → check the BNF.
  • Vomit within 1 h of atovaquone-proguanil → repeat dose. Doses per BNF/BNFC and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Record itinerary, drug, start/stop dates and fever advice (UKMEAG template). Warfarin: INR plan during and after travel.
  • Long-term atovaquone-proguanil: review adherence, tolerability, renal function and supply.

7Refer — specialist travel-medicine advice (fever is in block 0)

  • Pregnancy with unavoidable high-risk travel; young children to high-risk areas; asplenia or immunosuppression.
  • Complex or prolonged itineraries; severe renal or hepatic impairment; cannot take any standard agent; standby treatment.
  • Straightforward trip, standard agent suitable → primary care or pharmacy.

8Never

  • Mefloquine with any current or past psychiatric disorder or seizures, or with amiodarone.
  • Exclude malaria because tablets were taken, or on one negative test.
  • Manage suspected severe malaria in primary care awaiting outpatient tests.
  • Let tablets stop on the flight home — 7 days or 4 weeks after leaving.
  • Doxycycline under 12, routine prescribing in pregnancy, or homeopathic “prophylaxis”.

9Safety-net — say it, write it down, document it

999 “If you become confused or very drowsy, have a fit, are very short of breath, turn yellow or bleed unusually — call 999 or go to A&E.”   Same-day “A temperature or flu-like illness while away or in the year after — or even later — get a malaria test that day and say where you’ve been. No tablet is 100%.”   Tablets: “Keep taking them after you get home until the stop date.”   Mefloquine: “Low mood, anxiety, strange thoughts, dizziness or balance problems — stop it and get urgent advice about another tablet.”
