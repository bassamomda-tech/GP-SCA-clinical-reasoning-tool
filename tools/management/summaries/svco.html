Superior vena cava obstruction (SVCO)

For: adults in primary care with suspected SVCO — known or undiagnosed thoracic cancer, or a central line, PICC or pacemaker. Not for: the last days of life when admission does not fit the patient’s goals (care of the dying, specialist palliative care) or breathlessness without SVCO (palliative breathlessness).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG122GMEC Palliative Care Guidelines (6th ed, 2025)Palliative Adult Network Guidelines (2016)BNF
Core rule: SVCO is a clinical diagnosis — facial or neck swelling with a fixed raised JVP → sit up and admit immediately; never a suspected cancer pathway referral or outpatient chest X-ray.

0Act now — grade the severity at the bedside

  • Stridor, severe respiratory distress, hypoxia or cyanosis, reduced consciousness, seizures, syncope, haemodynamic compromise or rapid deterioration → 999 blue-light transfer; sit up, oxygen, dexamethasone urgently per the local acute-oncology/emergency protocol — without delaying transfer.
  • Suspected SVCO, currently stable (no airway or cerebral compromise) → Same-day immediate hospital assessment and admission via the local emergency/acute oncology pathway, with a telephone handover. Do not manage in the community while awaiting investigations.
  • Last days of life and admission not consistent with their goals → Urgent specialist palliative care advice for symptom control, alongside positioning and oxygen.

1Confirm — the constellation, not a single sign

FeatureLook for
SwellingFace, neck and arms; often noticed first on waking
Fixed elevation of the JVPWith facial or neck swelling
VeinsDistended neck veins, dilated chest-wall collaterals (more chronic), facial plethora
RespiratoryDyspnoea, cough, orthopnoea; stridor, voice change, dysphagia = airway red flags
NeurologicalHeadache, dizziness, visual symptoms, confusion, seizures
PostureWorse on stooping or lying flat

Pemberton’s sign may support but is not needed — do not provoke it with airway symptoms. It may be the first presentation of thoracic cancer: no cancer history does not lower suspicion.

2Check — and put it in the handover

☐ ABCDE: pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, conscious level  ☐ Rate of onset and progression  ☐ Can they lie flat?  ☐ Known cancer, current treatment  ☐ Central line, PICC or pacemaker  ☐ Anticoagulants, bleeding risk  ☐ DNACPR/advance care plan

Don't miss: angioedema, nephrotic syndrome, heart failure and cellulitis swell the face — but none gives a fixed raised JVP with chest-wall collaterals.

3Decide — steroid by severity

  • Airway or cerebral compromise, severe respiratory distress or rapid deterioration → dexamethasone urgently per the local protocol; do not delay transfer.
  • Clinically stable → discuss steroid with the receiving acute oncology/medical team where possible: steroids may reduce diagnostic yield in lymphoma. Do not delay transfer while seeking advice.
  • Rapid onset is more dangerous (no collaterals); a gradual onset can usually have a tissue diagnosis first — a hospital decision.

5Treat — before the patient leaves

PositionSit up; avoid lying flat if it worsens symptoms
OxygenIf hypoxic
Dexamethasone (if indicated)GMEC: 16 mg orally or 13.2 mg SC stat, then 8 mg BD orally (6.6 mg SC BD), morning and lunchtime. Record dose and time
AdmitAmbulance/acute transfer; pre-alert by telephone
  • High-dose steroid → PPI cover and blood glucose (baseline first where you can). Doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • No routine furosemide in primary care; no benzodiazepines or palliative breathlessness medicines as routine for reversible SVCO.
  • In hospital (specialist-led): contrast CT; stent considered urgently but not automatic; chemotherapy (small-cell, lymphoma, germ-cell), radiotherapy, or anticoagulation ± line removal for catheter thrombus.

6Monitor — after discharge

  • Steroid: glucose (actively in diabetes), GI bleeding, agitation, infection; keep the PPI; wean once the obstruction is relieved.
  • Swelling, dyspnoea or collaterals return after stenting (occlusion or re-thrombosis) → Urgent re-imaging.
  • Advance care planning alongside treatment: ceilings of care, preferred place of care, psychological impact on patient and family.

7Refer — to whom

  • Known cancer → acute oncology service (local Greater Manchester route; The Christie pathway for its patients).
  • New presentation → on-call medical or respiratory team: admission lets biopsy and treatment run in parallel.
  • Specialist palliative care → symptom control alongside active treatment, or when admission is not appropriate.

8Never

  • Send a suspected cancer pathway referral or 2-week chest X-ray for overt SVCO.
  • Delay transfer for a chest X-ray, outpatient imaging or biopsy.
  • Rely on a single sign, or wait for a classic Pemberton’s sign.
  • Give steroid routinely to a stable patient without discussing it (lymphoma histology).
  • Give furosemide routinely, or evening steroid doses.

9Safety-net — after treatment or stenting; record it

999 “If the breathlessness gets worse, you develop noisy or difficult breathing, the facial swelling comes back, or you become confused or have a fit — call 999. A stent can block, and that brings the problem straight back.”   Steroid: “Take it in the morning and at lunchtime, never in the evening, with the stomach protector.”
