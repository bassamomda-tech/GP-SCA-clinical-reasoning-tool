Harmful drinking and alcohol dependence

For: adults with hazardous, harmful or dependent drinking in primary care. Not for: under-18s (CAMHS or young people’s services; withdrawal only in an age-appropriate specialist setting). Pregnancy: dependent drinkers need urgent joint obstetric and specialist alcohol-service care.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE CG115NICE CG100NICE PH24NICE NG50UK alcohol treatment guidelines (OHID)
Core rule: never tell a dependent drinker to “just stop” — plan assisted withdrawal through the specialist alcohol service with thiamine, and treat DTs, withdrawal seizures and Wernicke’s as emergencies.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Delirium tremens or withdrawal seizures (confusion, hallucinations, autonomic instability, fits) → 999 / admit — no outpatient management.
  • Acute withdrawal with, or at high risk of, seizures or DTs → offer admission Same-day; lower threshold if frail, cognitive impairment, comorbid or no support.
  • Possible Wernicke’s: any of confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, unexplained hypothermia/hypotension, reduced consciousness → Emergency hospital transfer; parenteral thiamine (e.g. Pabrinex) first if available, without delaying transfer.
  • Haematemesis or melaena → 999. Jaundice, ascites, encephalopathy or suspected acute alcohol-related hepatitis → Same-day hospital
  • Active suicidal intent or plan, psychosis → Same-day crisis / mental health team   Child or vulnerable adult at risk → Same-day safeguarding lead / children’s social care
  • Dependent and pregnant → Urgent specialist alcohol service + maternity — no unsupervised abrupt stop, but never just carry on drinking.

2Check

☐ Units, morning/relief drinking ☐ Past seizures, DTs, detoxes; epilepsy; benzodiazepines, opioids ☐ FBC, LFTs/GGT, U&E, glucose; INR if liver disease ☐ Mood, suicide risk ☐ Children, pregnancy, driving. Don’t miss: normal LFTs do not exclude cirrhosis.

3Decide — intervene at the right level

  • Hazardous → structured brief advice; then extended if no change.
  • Harmful or mild dependence → psychological therapy ± drugs.
  • Suspected dependence (>15 units/day, withdrawal symptoms, AUDIT/SADQ) → specialist alcohol service.
  • Goal: abstinence if moderate/severe or comorbid.

5Treat — with a psychosocial intervention, always

WithdrawalChlordiazepoxide or diazepam, fixed-dose, reducing over about a week; specialist-led; daily contact first 3 days
ThiamineOral: withdrawal, malnourished, decompensated liver. Parenteral (hospital): suspected Wernicke’s
Relapse preventionAfter withdrawal: acamprosate or oral naltrexone; up to 6 months, longer if helping
Specialist-led optionsDisulfiram — consider if those unsuitable or preferred; ≥24 h after last drink. Nalmefene — reduce drinking
  • Before any: U&E, LFTs/GGT. Naltrexone/nalmefene → no opioids (codeine, tramadol, methadone).
  • Drinking on chlordiazepoxide → stop it, reassess. Small supplies.
  • AA, SMART Recovery. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Brief advice: review in a few weeks; escalate if not changing.
  • Acamprosate/naltrexone: ≥monthly for 6 months.
  • Disulfiram: 2-weekly ×2 months, monthly ×4, then ≥6-monthly.
  • Mood every review. Relapse → re-engage, re-refer; don’t discharge.

7Refer — routine

  • Men >50, women >35 units/week for several months → offer transient elastography; cirrhosis → hepatology.
  • Alcohol service letter: units, AUDIT, SADQ, withdrawal history, opioids, bloods, safeguarding.
  • Persistent misuse or dependence → must not drive and must tell the DVLA; won’t → follow GMC guidance.

8Never

  • Tell a dependent drinker to stop today — risks seizures and DTs.
  • Start a community detox with previous seizures/DTs, epilepsy, benzodiazepine dependence, no one at home, or no daily contact in the first 3 days.
  • Naltrexone or nalmefene in someone taking opioids.
  • Antidepressants to treat alcohol misuse alone; benzodiazepines beyond withdrawal or open-ended.
  • Forget the children — always ask who else is at home.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Don’t stop suddenly on your own. If you stop, or during the detox, call 999 for a fit, confusion, seeing or hearing things, shaking and sweating you can’t stop, vomiting blood, black sticky stools, yellow skin or eyes, or thoughts of harming yourself.”   Same-day “Drink on the detox tablets — stop them and ring us or the alcohol team that day; also worse shakes, not eating, running out.”   Disulfiram: “Unwell, fever or yellow skin — stop it and get same-day help.”   Driving: “You must not drive and need to tell the DVLA.”   “A slip is common, not failure — come back.”
