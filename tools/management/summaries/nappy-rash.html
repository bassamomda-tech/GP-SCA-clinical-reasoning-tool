Nappy rash

For: babies and children in nappies with a rash in the nappy area. Not for: a febrile or unwell baby (do not manage through this pathway — fever in under 5s, NG143). See also eczema, CMPA, faltering growth.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
PCDS napkin dermatitisNICE NG153NICE NG143Local formulary
Core rule: look at the folds (spared → irritant; involved ± satellite lesions → likely candida — a clue, not a rule), start nappy and barrier care, never put a potent steroid under a nappy, and treat a non-improving rash as a diagnostic question, not a dosing one.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Infant under 3 months with a temperature ≥38°C, however mild the rash → Same-day paediatrics
  • Purpura or a non-blanching rash with fever or an unwell baby → 999 / immediate transfer (possible meningococcal disease); baby well → Same-day paediatrics
  • Fever with ill appearance, poor feeding, lethargy, reduced urine output, rapidly spreading erythema, blistering, skin pain out of proportion or mucosal involvement → Same-day assessment; consider serious bacterial infection.
  • Eczema herpeticum (painful clustered vesicles, punched-out erosions, fever or rapidly worsening rash in a child with eczema) → Same-day paediatrics / dermatology
  • Severe, recurrent, untreated or unusually distributed rash, or poor growth, developmental concerns or other signs of neglect or harm → assess safeguarding per local procedures; consider practical barriers and offer support before drawing conclusions.

1Confirm — the folds are a useful clue

TypeAppearance
Irritant (commonest)Erythema on convex skin in contact with the nappy (buttocks, genitals); folds relatively spared. Urine, faeces, friction, moisture.
CandidalBeefy-red, folds involved, may have satellite papules or pustules (not always obvious early). Suspect after antibiotics, or no response after 3 days.

Not an absolute rule: severe irritant dermatitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, psoriasis and secondary infection can involve the folds — use the overall pattern.

2Check — every baby

☐ Duration, severity, feeding, recent antibiotics  ☐ Temperature and systemic features  ☐ Mouth for oral thrush  ☐ Growth  ☐ Other skin sites

Mimics: seborrhoeic dermatitis (cradle cap), atopic eczema, psoriasis, allergic contact dermatitis, impetigo or bullous impetigo, perianal streptococcal dermatitis (sharp bright-red perianal rash, mistaken for candida), eczema herpeticum, scabies; rarely Langerhans cell histiocytosis, zinc deficiency, immunodeficiency.

3Decide — medication only where indicated

  • Everyone → nappy and barrier care (block 5, step 1).
  • Candida suspected (folds, satellite lesions, after antibiotics, or >3 days unresponsive) → topical imidazole.
  • Markedly inflamed → consider a short course of mild hydrocortisone.
  • Clinical signs of secondary bacterial infection only → consider antibiotics.

5Treat — step up only where indicated

Step 1 · allHand hygiene; frequent changes, high-absorbency nappies; water or fragrance-free wipes; nappy-free time; pat dry; thin barrier (zinc and castor oil, or soft white paraffin) every change
Step 2 · candidaClotrimazole 1% or miconazole 2% cream. Examine the mouth; treat oral thrush only if present (paediatric formulary)
Step 3 · inflamedHydrocortisone 1% thin layer once daily, up to 7 days, inflamed skin only. With an antifungal only if the diagnosis is clear — not routine
Step 4 · bacterial signsSpreading erythema, pustules, pus, honey crusts, rapid worsening, fever → localised: topical; extensive or systemic: oral (NG153, local guideline)
  • Avoid: routine soap, antiseptics, bubble bath, talc, fragranced products, vigorous wiping. Suspected contact allergy → don't change several products at once.
  • Barrier products are usually self-care/OTC — check your local formulary. Check doses in the SPC and your local formulary.

6Review — at 3–7 days

  • Typical irritant rash begins improving within several days; severe rash may take longer. Not improving, worse or recurring → check treatment is applied correctly; reassess for candida, bacterial infection or another diagnosis — not simply stronger treatment.

7Refer — dermatology, lower threshold

  • No improvement despite correct barrier care and treatment; recurrent rash needing repeated antifungal or steroid courses; widespread or severe dermatitis; diagnostic uncertainty.
  • Suspected psoriasis, zinc deficiency, immunodeficiency or serious dermatosis; recurrent bacterial infection; poor growth, chronic diarrhoea or systemic symptoms.

8Never

  • A potent steroid, or potent steroid–antifungal combination, under a nappy (an occlusive dressing) unless under specialist supervision.
  • Repeated unsupervised or prolonged steroid courses in the nappy area.
  • Antibiotics for uncomplicated irritant or candidal rash, or for colonisation alone.
  • A febrile infant managed solely through the nappy-rash pathway.
  • Assuming every candidal nappy rash needs oral treatment.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Spots or a rash that do not fade when you press a glass on them, and your baby has a fever or seems unwell — call 999.”   Same day “Under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or above, the rash blisters, weeps or spreads quickly, or your baby seems unwell, feverish or is feeding poorly — contact us the same day (NHS 111 if we are closed).”   Not settling: “No better after a few days of barrier cream and frequent changes, or getting worse — come back so we can check for thrush or infection.”
