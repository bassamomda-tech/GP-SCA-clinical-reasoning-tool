Malignant hypercalcaemia

For: adults with cancer and hypercalcaemia, in primary and palliative care. Not for: general hypercalcaemia or primary hyperparathyroidism (use the hypercalcaemia pathway), children or pregnancy.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
GMEC Palliative Care Guidelines (6th ed, 2025)BNF / SPCNICE NG12 (myeloma)
Core rule: check a corrected calcium in any cancer patient who is suddenly deteriorating — it mimics the dying phase and is reversible; escalate the same day, rehydrate first, then ONE bisphosphonate, never two.

0Act now — assess ABCDE, send urgent bloods, stop contributing medicines

  • Symptomatic, or corrected Ca ≥3.0 mmol/L → Same-day acute oncology, palliative care or hospital advice/admission.
  • Any level with reduced consciousness or delirium, vomiting or unable to keep fluids down, severe dehydration, AKI or oliguria, arrhythmia or ECG changes → Same-day emergency hospital assessment.
  • Suspected spinal cord compression or pathological fracture → Same-day emergency pathway; do not encourage mobilising.
  • Unknown cancer with significant hypercalcaemia → Same-day admission or oncology referral, so diagnosis and treatment run together.
  • Do not delay hospital transfer while arranging bisphosphonate treatment.

1Confirm — low threshold to check

Suspect it with new drowsiness or confusion, nausea, vomiting, constipation, thirst, polyuria, dehydration, weakness, acute renal impairment or unexplained rapid decline. Commonest: myeloma, breast, lung, renal, squamous tumours.

Use the albumin-adjusted calcium and local reference range. Judge urgency on symptoms and rate of rise, not the number alone.

Corrected CaFeaturesAction
<3.0 mmol/LAsymptomatic, stableGMEC pathway: correct dehydration, recheck Ca and renal function in 2–3 days
≥3.0 mmol/Lor any symptomsSame-day specialist advice/admission (block 0)

Corrected Ca is unreliable in severe hypoalbuminaemia, critical illness, renal failure, acid–base disturbance or paraproteinaemia → discuss ionised calcium.

2Check — bloods now

☐ Corrected Ca, albumin  ☐ U&E, creatinine, eGFR  ☐ Phosphate, magnesium, ALP  ☐ PTH (usually suppressed)  ☐ FBC, ESR or plasma viscosity  ☐ ECG if severe, symptomatic or cardiac symptoms

Don't miss: raised or normal PTH → co-existing hyperparathyroidism; drugs (thiazides, calcium/vitamin D, lithium); dehydration. Unexplained hypercalcaemia, anaemia, renal impairment or bone pain → consider myeloma.

3Decide — goals of care as much as biochemistry

  • Weigh: first episode or long interval? Good prior quality of life? Willing and able to have IV treatment and blood tests?
  • Treatment takes about a week to work → may be inappropriate if the prognosis is very short; seek specialist advice.
  • Usually a marker of poor prognosis → advance care planning alongside, not instead of, acute treatment.

5Treat — specialist-directed; not a GP-administered pathway

<3.0, asymptomaticReview contributors; oral fluids if safe; recheck in 2–3 days. Rising, persistent or symptomatic → specialist advice
Symptomatic or ≥3.0Supervised IV 0.9% sodium chloride first; rate and volume individualised; watch for fluid overload
Then ONE bisphosphonateZoledronic acid OR pamidronate — a single IV infusion, never both
eGFR <30, AKI or changingNo bisphosphonate in primary care → seek specialist advice
  • Stop or review: thiazides, calcium and vitamin D supplements, vitamin A/retinoids, lithium, calcium-containing antacids.
  • Bisphosphonates act too slowly to use alone in a crisis (maximum effect: zoledronic acid 4–7 days, pamidronate 5–7 days). Doses per BNF, SPC and local formulary.
  • Specialist only: denosumab, calcitonin, corticosteroids (lymphoma). No routine loop diuretics.

6Monitor

  • Around 4–7 days: calcium and renal function per specialist plan; sooner if worse or renal function unstable.
  • Watch for hypocalcaemia, low phosphate and low magnesium after antiresorptive treatment.
  • No second bisphosphonate because the first has not worked in 24–48 hours; repeat treatment only on specialist advice.
  • Dental review before and during repeated bisphosphonates (osteonecrosis of the jaw).

7Refer — advice and myeloma route

  • Specialist advice (acute oncology or palliative care): any treatment decision, refractory calcium, eGFR <30.
  • NG12, aged 60+ with persistent bone pain (especially back) or unexplained fracture → FBC, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains together; results suggest myeloma → suspected cancer pathway.

8Never

  • Assume a drowsy, confused cancer patient is dying without checking a corrected calcium.
  • Give two bisphosphonates, or a bisphosphonate before rehydration.
  • Give a bisphosphonate in primary care with eGFR <30, AKI or changing renal function.
  • Delay hospital transfer to arrange a bisphosphonate.
  • Encourage mobilising with suspected pathological fracture, spinal instability or cord compression.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “If the drowsiness, confusion, vomiting, bad thirst or passing lots of urine come back, or you pass much less urine — contact us, the acute oncology or palliative care team the same day for a check and blood test.”   Treatment: “The drip works over several days to a week. This does tend to come back, so let's talk about what matters to you.”
