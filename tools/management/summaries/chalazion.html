Chalazion (meibomian cyst)

For: adults and children with an eyelid lump in primary care. Not for: orbital or preseptal cellulitis (block 0; treat per the local antimicrobial pathway) or a recurrent or atypical lid lump (block 7). Children → lower threshold. See also blepharitis, rosacea, cellulitis. Local pathways supersede this summary.

Core rule: exclude orbital and preseptal cellulitis first; then warm compress and massage, no routine antibiotics — and a same-site recurrence is not "just a chalazion" until a specialist has seen it.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Orbital signs: painful or restricted eye movements, proptosis, diplopia, reduced vision or colour vision, RAPD, chemosis, severe retro-orbital pain, systemic toxicity, severe headache or vomiting, meningism or altered consciousness, rapidly progressive swelling, or eye cannot be examined → Emergency hospital with ophthalmology/paediatrics; A&E or ambulance (999) if systemically unwell or vision compromised.
  • Unsure whether preseptal or orbital → Same-day senior or ophthalmology advice; do not manage as simple preseptal cellulitis.
  • Preseptal cellulitis (diffuse warm, tender, spreading lid swelling; normal eye movements and vision; no proptosis) → Same-day assessment. Child, systemically unwell, marked swelling or eye not examinable → Same-day paediatric/ophthalmic
  • Acute visual change, corneal involvement or red-eye signs → Same-day local urgent eye pathway.

1Confirm — chalazion, stye or something else?

PictureFeatures
ChalazionFirm, slowly enlarging lump in the lid; non-infectious (blocked meibomian gland); may be tender at first
Hordeolum (stye)Acute, tender, at the lash line or within the lid; usually settles with compresses
Preseptal cellulitisDiffuse red, warm, tender lid swelling — not a localised stye or mildly inflamed chalazion
Atypical lumpLash loss, ulceration, bleeding, lid-margin distortion, same-site recurrence → block 7

2Check — examine and document

☐ Site, size, duration, lashes, lid margin  ☐ Acuity (visual behaviour in a child) if vision may be affected  ☐ Cornea, red eye, contact lenses  ☐ If cellulitis possible: eye movements, proptosis, pupils, colour vision, temperature  ☐ Blepharitis, MGD, rosacea

Don't miss: a persistent unilateral lesion relabelled as benign again and again.

3Decide — what belongs in general practice

Uncomplicated → conservative care; small, asymptomatic lesions can be observed. Large, symptomatic, vision-affecting, recurrent or atypical → refer (block 7). Procedures are specialist-only.

4Conservative care — the treatment

StepHow
Warm compressComfortably warm (not scalding), closed lid, 5–10 minutes; wash hands before and after
MassageGently towards the lid margin; 2–4 times a day, regularly for several weeks
Lid hygieneWhere there is blepharitis, MGD, ocular rosacea, or recurrent or multiple chalazia
AvoidSqueezing, puncturing or lancing; eye make-up and contact lenses while inflamed

5Treat — conservative first, no routine antibiotics

Step 1Warm compress and massage (block 4); expect weeks or months
Step 2Lid hygiene where there is underlying lid disease
Step 3Review at about 4–6 weeks if not clearly improving — reassess, not automatic surgery
Step 4 · recurrent with MGD / rosaceaTime-limited oral tetracycline may be considered, specialist-guided only; may be off-label
  • No antibiotics for an uncomplicated chalazion, even if large, red or inflamed.
  • Stye: topical antibiotic only for secondary infection, blepharoconjunctivitis or discharge (local pathway); spreading redness → block 0.
  • Tetracycline: not routinely under 12; not started in pregnancy or breastfeeding unless a specialist advises; photosensitivity; upright with water; avoid with isotretinoin. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.

6Monitor

  • At about 4–6 weeks: diagnosis, vision, need for referral. Asymptomatic and not affecting vision → may observe longer.
  • Tetracycline: severe headache, vomiting or visual disturbance (intracranial hypertension) → stop it; Same-day assessment.

7Refer

  • Affects vision or cornea, large or painful, uncertain diagnosis, recurrent preseptal cellulitis → Prompt eye-care assessment, not removal.
  • Children: large, prolonged, recurrent, vision-affecting or lid-distorting lesion, or parental concern → refer promptly (amblyopia).
  • Same-site recurrence or atypical lump → specialist assessment with consideration of biopsy. NG12: melanoma → suspected cancer pathway; SCC → consider it; BCC usually non-urgent unless site or size.
  • Elective removal only when local EUR criteria are met.

8Never

  • Antibiotics for an uncomplicated chalazion, or repeated courses for a recurrent "chalazion" without reassessing.
  • Repeated drainage or curettage of a same-site recurrence without specialist review and histology.
  • Manage possible orbital cellulitis as simple preseptal cellulitis.
  • Incision, curettage or steroid injection in routine GP practice without training, governance and a local pathway.
  • Adult doxycycline advice in children without specialist input.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

A&E / 999 “If it hurts to move your eye, your eye bulges, you see double, your vision is reduced or changes, or you have a severe headache, vomiting or feel very unwell — go to A&E or call 999.”   Same-day “If the eyelid becomes hot, red and swollen and it's spreading, or you feel unwell — get seen the same day.”
