Epilepsy and first suspected seizure

For: a first suspected seizure or known epilepsy. Not for: undifferentiated blackouts (fits and funny turns pathway) or childhood febrile seizures (local paediatric pathway).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG217 (Aug 2026)DVLAMHRACoSRHNG12
Core rule: refer every first suspected seizure to a first-seizure/epilepsy specialist within 2 weeks without waiting for tests, stop driving from the date of the seizure, and never stop an antiseizure medicine (AED) abruptly.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Convulsive seizure ≥5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery (status) → 999 + prescribed buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam per the individual plan; do not delay transfer or repeat doses unless the plan says so.
  • First seizure with fever, neck stiffness or photophobia, significant head injury, new focal deficit or persistent impaired consciousness, or on anticoagulation, known cancer or immunosuppression → 999
  • Any seizure in pregnancy or postpartum (presume eclampsia) · airway or breathing compromise, significant injury, severe hypoglycaemia or major metabolic disturbance → 999
  • Seizure cluster (as defined in the rescue plan, or a clear rise in usual frequency) → follow the plan, Same-day advice; 999 if prolonged, no recovery, injury or breathing problems.
  • New seizure in an older adult, or progressive focal deficit, personality change or progressive headache → Urgent imaging ≤2 weeks (local pathway); papilloedema → Same-day

1Confirm — the witness account is the diagnosis

Patient (prodrome, aura, recall) and witness (onset, semiology, duration, post-ictal state); ask about morning myoclonic jerks. Triggers: sleep loss, alcohol, missed meals or doses, illness, stimulants, tramadol, bupropion, quinolones, clozapine, withdrawal.

Mimics: syncope (brief jerks are common), arrhythmia (exertional, no prodrome), non-epileptic attacks, hypoglycaemia, TIA.

2Check — in the first assessment; never delay the referral

☐ 12-lead ECG, every case  ☐ Capillary glucose  ☐ FBC, U&E, Ca, Mg, LFTs if metabolic cause suspected  ☐ Pregnancy test if relevant  ☐ Contraception, pregnancy plans. EEG/MRI via the first-seizure service; a normal EEG or CT does not exclude epilepsy.

3Decide — who starts treatment

  • Diagnosis and AED initiation are specialist-led; GP never starts or stops an AED without specialist advice. Rescue medication only under an individual plan.
  • Safety: shower not bath, door unlocked, swim only with a lifeguard, no heights, back hob rings.

5Treat — specialist starts; GP shares care

  • First line (specialist): focal → lamotrigine or levetiracetam; tonic-clonic → lamotrigine, levetiracetam or valproate (MHRA rules); JME → no carbamazepine or phenytoin, lamotrigine may worsen myoclonus.
  • Lamotrigine: slow titration, rash → urgent assessment. Levetiracetam: warn of mood change, suicidal ideas.
  • Contraception: enzyme inducers (e.g. carbamazepine, phenytoin, higher-dose topiramate) weaken combined pill, implant, POP (DMPA, IUS, IUD generally unaffected). Combined hormonal contraception lowers lamotrigine → specialist advice.
  • Valproate: not started under 55 unless two specialists agree; Pregnancy Prevention Programme (also topiramate); counsel men. Check contraception and specialist paperwork each review — absent → contact the epilepsy team urgently.
  • Planning pregnancy: folic acid 5 mg pre-conception to 12 weeks; specialist review. Doses per BNF/local formulary.

6Monitor — annual review

  • Seizures, adherence, side effects, interactions, contraception, pregnancy plans, DVLA, mood (PHQ-9), bone (enzyme inducers).
  • SUDEP: discuss proportionately (nocturnal or uncontrolled tonic-clonic seizures, poor adherence); adherence reduces risk.

7Refer

  • Every first suspected seizure → first-seizure/epilepsy specialist Urgent ≤2 weeks; send witness account, contraception, ECG, DVLA advice.
  • Breakthrough seizures; valproate or topiramate and planning pregnancy (urgently if pregnant) → specialist. Pregnancy → joint obstetric–neurology care. Risky job → occupational health.

8Never

  • Stop an AED abruptly (rebound seizures, driving consequences).
  • Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine or phenytoin for absence or myoclonic seizures (incl. JME) unless a specialist directs.
  • GP-started or switched valproate or topiramate; valproate without paperwork.
  • Delay referral for tests, reassure on a normal EEG or CT, or give vague, undocumented DVLA advice.

9Safety-net — say it, give it in writing, document it

999 “Call 999 if a seizure lasts 5 minutes or more, another starts before recovery, breathing doesn't recover, they don't come round fully, they're badly hurt, it's a first seizure, or they're pregnant or just had a baby.”   First aid: “Time it, protect the head, nothing in the mouth, don't restrain, recovery position once jerking stops; rescue medicine as the plan says.”   Same-day “Ring us the same day for a cluster, a seizure after a good spell, a rash on lamotrigine, or low mood or suicidal thoughts on levetiracetam.”   Medicines and driving: “Never stop tablets suddenly. No driving from the seizure date; tell the DVLA. Planning a baby — see us first.”
