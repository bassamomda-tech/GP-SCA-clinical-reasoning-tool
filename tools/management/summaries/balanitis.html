Balanitis

For: people aged 16 and over with balanitis or balanoposthitis in primary care. Not for: under 16s (use a separate paediatric/adolescent pathway), or urethritis or discharge (sexual-health assessment).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
BASHH balanoposthitis 2025 (v5, Mar 2026)NICE NG12GM formulary & self-care policyLocal antimicrobial advice
Core rule: balanitis is a description, not a diagnosis — find the cause, treat that, and biopsy or refer what persists rather than re-treating with more cream.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Retracted foreskin that cannot be returned over the glans (paraphimosis) → Same-day emergency urology.
  • Suspected Fournier’s gangrene or necrotising infection → surgical emergency: Immediate admission and sepsis management. Urinary retention, rapidly progressive swelling, severe pain, discolouration, systemic illness or spreading cellulitis → Same-day.
  • Penile mass or ulcerated lesion with STI excluded, persisting after STI treatment, or unexplained/persistent glans or foreskin symptoms → consider a penile Suspected cancer pathway referral (NG12).
  • Lesion clinically suspicious for malignancy (velvety red or glazed, warty, scaly, indurated, bleeding or non-healing ulcer, unexplained inguinal nodes, hidden by phimosis) → STI assessment and Urgent urology referral in parallel — do not wait for results.

1Confirm — the appearance suggests the cause, it does not prove it

Also Candida, other dermatoses, PIN, cancer. Examine glans, foreskin, shaft, meatus, stream, inguinal nodes. Never force back a non-retractile foreskin; replace a retracted one before the patient leaves.

2Check

☐ Sexual history, STI risk  ☐ Diabetes, immunosuppression, antibiotics, products used  ☐ Warfarin, methotrexate  ☐ Sub-preputial swab if infection suspected, severe, persistent, discharge or treatment failure  ☐ Glucose/HbA1c if severe, persistent or recurrent

A Candida or bacterial growth does not prove the cause. HSV NAAT from a fresh lesion; ulcer → syphilis serology; consider HIV test if severe, persistent, atypical or recurrent.

3Decide — everyone, then by cause

  • Everyone: wash gently with water; emollient as soap substitute; stop soaps, gels and perfumed products; dry gently; loose cotton underwear. Creams can weaken latex condoms.
  • Suspected STI → sexual-health pathway with partner notification. Candidal balanitis is not usually an STI.

4Target — response at review

SituationAction
Clearly resolving irritant episodeNo routine review
Not clearly improving, infection treated or diagnosis uncertainReview within 7–14 days: reassess cause, swab, STI tests, consider referral
Persistent or atypical lesionBiopsy or referral, not more cream

5Treat — the most likely cause, then reassess

IrritantStop irritants, emollient soap substitute; consider hydrocortisone 1% briefly if appropriate
CandidalClotrimazole 1% or miconazole 2% cream BD for 7–14 days. + hydrocortisone 1% only for marked inflammation, short course. Severe → fluconazole 150 mg single oral dose (check interactions, liver)
Anaerobic / bacterialAnaerobic → metronidazole 400–500 mg BD for 7 days; alternatives co-amoxiclav or topical clindamycin per local advice. Otherwise by clinical syndrome — never treat a culture alone
DermatosisSuspected lichen sclerosus → refer; a clinician competent in genital dermatoses may start an ultra-potent topical steroid per local protocol
  • Miconazole (oral or topical) + warfarin → INR rise: avoid or seek anticoagulation advice. Trimethoprim/co-trimoxazole + methotrexate → avoid.
  • Prescribe or advise purchase per the GM formulary and self-care policy; doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor — not resolving or recurrent? re-check

  • Re-check: right cause (re-examine), irritants, glucose/HbA1c, immunosuppression, HIV where indicated, HSV/syphilis/STI screen.
  • Lichen sclerosus: long-term safety-netting for new induration, ulceration, lumps, bleeding, persistent erosions or texture change.

7Refer — routine, by criteria

  • Urology: pathological or progressive phimosis; meatal stenosis or weak stream; recurrent balanitis with phimosis, scarring, meatal narrowing or poor response; lichen sclerosus with structural change.
  • Dermatology: uncertainty needing biopsy; suspected lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, Zoon’s, psoriasis or fixed drug eruption.
  • GUM/sexual health: suspected STI, urethritis or discharge; recurrent candidal disease.

8Never

  • Forcibly retract a non-retractile foreskin, or let a retracted foreskin leave unreduced.
  • Assume every red, sore glans is Candida, or treat a swab result alone.
  • Keep re-treating empirically — repeated antifungal or steroid–antifungal courses — for a persistent or atypical lesion.
  • Treat suspected lichen sclerosus repeatedly as infection.
  • Delay urology referral of a suspicious lesion while awaiting STI results.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

A&E now / 999 “If the foreskin is pulled back and won’t go forward again, you can’t pass urine, or there is rapidly increasing swelling, severe pain, discolouration or you feel unwell — go to A&E straight away, or call 999 if you feel very unwell.”   ≤7 days “A sore that doesn’t heal, a lump, a velvety red or scaly patch, bleeding, or a change you can’t see under a tight foreskin — book an appointment within a week.”   Not improving: “If it isn’t clearly better within one to two weeks, or keeps coming back, book a review.”
