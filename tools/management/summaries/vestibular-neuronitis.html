Vestibular neuronitis and labyrinthitis

For: adults over 16 with acute vestibular syndrome (AVS), after assessment for central causes. Not for: brief positional episodes (BPPV), episodic spontaneous vertigo (Ménière's, vertigo pathway), suspected stroke (stroke and TIA; block 0).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG127NICE NG98GM formularyMHRA SPCs
Core rule: acute sustained vertigo with nausea and unsteadiness is AVS, not vestibular neuronitis, until posterior-circulation stroke is assessed; HINTS only if trained, otherwise refer immediately.

0Act now — even when a peripheral diagnosis seems plausible

  • New neurological deficit, vertical or direction-changing nystagmus, skew deviation, normal head impulse in continuous AVS, severe headache, neck pain or suspected dissection → Immediate stroke pathway (999 if unstable).
  • AVS and no clinician trained in HINTS → Immediate stroke-pathway referral. HINTS suggesting stroke → immediate neuroimaging.
  • Cannot stand or walk unaided, marked truncal ataxia → Immediate stroke assessment; not diagnostic alone, but keep the threshold low.
  • Sudden hearing loss (≤3 days onset, within past 30 days, no outer or middle-ear cause) → Seen within 24 h ENT or ED; 999 or stroke pathway if focal signs, severe unsteadiness or reduced consciousness.
  • Ramsay Hunt (vesicles, facial weakness), mastoiditis, purulent otorrhoea with systemic illness, facial palsy, perilymphatic fistula → Same-day ENT or neurology.
  • Cannot keep fluids down, dehydration, cannot mobilise or diagnostic uncertainty → Same-day hospital assessment.

1Confirm — classify the dizziness first

PatternThink
Continuous for hours to days, nausea, spontaneous nystagmus, unsteadyAVS
Brief episodes triggered by positionBPPV (Hallpike by trained clinician)
Episodic spontaneous vertigoVestibular migraine, Ménière's, arrhythmia
Light-headed or presyncopeCardiovascular or orthostatic

Vestibular neuronitis = AVS without hearing loss; labyrinthitis adds hearing loss and/or tinnitus. Both are diagnoses of exclusion, especially in the first 24–72 hours.

2Check

☐ Sit, stand and walk unaided  ☐ Eye movements, nystagmus  ☐ Cranial nerves, coordination, speech, power, sensation  ☐ Otoscopy (vesicles, mastoid)  ☐ Bedside hearing  ☐ Glucose where indicated  ☐ Postural BP if presyncopal

Don't miss: a normal limb examination or normal CT head does not exclude posterior-circulation stroke.

3Decide

  • HINTS only in continuous AVS with spontaneous nystagmus and gait unsteadiness, by a trained, experienced clinician; all three parts together.
  • Incomplete HINTS, atypical patient or concern remains → refer despite a reassuring result.
  • Peripheral cause judged safe → short-term relief only if severe, oral fluids, gradual supported mobilisation.

4Target — treatment goals

Suppressant stopped: often no more than 2–3 daysHydrating orallyMobilising, supervised at first if unsafe

5Treat — one agent, only if severe

Option AProchlorperazine 5 mg oral three times a day, or 3 mg buccal per product SPC
Option BCyclizine 50 mg oral up to three times a day
SupportiveOral fluids; rest in worst phase, then mobilise gradually with falls precautions
RehabilitationConsider once vomiting settles if imbalance, visual-motion sensitivity or limited function persist
  • Lowest dose, shortest course; not prochlorperazine and cyclizine together routinely. Doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • Cautions: Parkinson's, Lewy body dementia, previous dystonia, QT-prolonging drugs, glaucoma, urinary retention, older or frail; pregnancy: avoid starting unless clinically necessary.
  • No routine IV fluids in primary care; no steroids instead of ENT for sudden hearing loss.

6Monitor

  • Review by severity, ability to hydrate and home support; earlier if deteriorating.
  • Still on suppressants → stop; no repeat courses without reassessment.
  • Not recovering → recheck central signs, hearing and other diagnoses; persistent nystagmus alone can occur in recovery.

7Refer — local pathway

  • Sudden hearing loss >30 days ago, or worsening over 4–90 days → Seen within 2 weeks ENT.
  • Function still limited → vestibular rehabilitation: physiotherapy, audiovestibular or balance service.
  • Persistent, recurrent, atypical or unilateral auditory symptoms → ENT, audiovestibular medicine or balance service.

8Never

  • Call it vestibular neuronitis before central causes are assessed.
  • HINTS if untrained, or for episodic, positional or non-vestibular dizziness.
  • Reassure on a normal CT head or normal limb examination.
  • Send sudden hearing loss to routine audiology.
  • Prolonged or combined vestibular suppressants.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Sudden severe headache, double vision, slurred speech, face or limb weakness, or being unable to stand or walk — call 999.”   Same day “If you lose hearing suddenly, contact us the same day — it needs to be seen within 24 hours. Ear pain with blisters or face weakness also needs seeing the same day. Contact us the same day if you can't keep fluids down.”   Meanwhile: “The tablets are usually for no more than two or three days. Don't drive while dizzy or on the tablets; no ladders, heights or machinery.”
