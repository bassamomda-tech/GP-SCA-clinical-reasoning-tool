Nausea and vomiting in palliative care

For: adults with advanced illness at home or in a care home. Not for: chemotherapy or radiotherapy nausea (oncology regimen and helpline), or the last days of life (care of the dying).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
GMMMG palliative guidelines 2025NICE NG31MHRA DSU 2013 · 2014Scottish Palliative Care Guidelines 2025
Core rule: treat the reversible cause, choose one antiemetic by mechanism, give it regularly by a route that works (subcutaneous if vomiting) — and no metoclopramide if obstruction or colic is possible.

0Act now — these change the drug and the setting of care

  • Colic, distension, large-volume or faeculent vomiting, little or no flatus/stool → suspect malignant bowel obstruction: stop metoclopramide and stimulant/bulk-forming laxatives; SC medicines → Same-day specialist palliative care (surgeons if fit for and wanting intervention). Admission per goals of care.
  • Thirst, polyuria, confusion, drowsiness, constipation → corrected calcium; hypercalcaemia needing treatment → Same-day hospital or hospice if in keeping with goals; haloperidol meanwhile.
  • Morning headache, new neurological signs → raised intracranial pressure → Same-day discussion if investigation or steroids would change management.
  • Vomiting or can't swallow → switch to subcutaneous PRN now. Severe dehydration, active treatment wanted → Same-day

2Check — reversible causes, every time

☐ Constipation (PR if acceptable)  ☐ New or increased opioid; antibiotics, SSRIs, NSAIDs, iron, digoxin  ☐ Infection, oral candida  ☐ Gastritis  ☐ Bloods if they change management: U&E, calcium, LFTs, glucose, CRP

Don't miss hypercalcaemia (myeloma, breast, lung, renal, bone metastases).

3Decide — how to give it

  • Persistent nausea → one cause-matched drug regularly + PRN; PRN alone may suit intermittent nausea.
  • Vomiting → SC PRN; consider a syringe pump if >2–3 PRN doses in 24 h, persistent symptoms or no oral route; check compatibility.
  • Anticipatory SC antiemetic in the home. Non-drug: small frequent meals, no cooking smells, mouth care, explanation.

Not settling at 24–48 h: taken and absorbed (else SC)? → re-assess cause, switch class → partial: add a different mechanism (e.g. haloperidol + cyclizine) → levomepromazine, replacing other D2 drugs → specialist.

6Monitor

  • D2 antagonists: akathisia, stiffness, abnormal movements → stop or reduce, seek advice.
  • Metoclopramide beyond MHRA 5 days or 30 mg/day is off-label → document indication, dose, duration, review. Domperidone ≤30 mg/day, usually ≤7 days.
  • QT: cumulative risk (haloperidol, levomepromazine, ondansetron). Syringe pump daily. Bowels every review; laxative with opioids, ondansetron.

7Refer — specialist palliative care advice, early

  • Refractory despite cause-matched and broad-spectrum drugs; complex pump mixes; Parkinson's; myasthenia gravis; complex renal impairment; QT-risk combinations; obstruction.

8Never

  • Metoclopramide in suspected high-grade obstruction or colic.
  • Two D2 antagonists together — switch, don't stack; or routine metoclopramide + cyclizine for gastric stasis.
  • Central dopamine antagonists in Parkinson's without specialist advice; stopping Parkinson's medicines abruptly.
  • Domperidone with cardiac disease, severe liver impairment, QT-prolonging drugs or potent CYP3A4 inhibitors.
  • Oral antiemetics in a vomiting patient; ondansetron as a default (constipating).

9Safety-net — say it to the person and carers, then document it

Same-day “Tummy swelling, cramping pain, vomiting large amounts or dark, smelly vomit, or no wind or stool — call the same day.”   Same-day “New confusion, severe headache, drowsiness, or abnormal movements or stiffness — call the same day.”   Not settling: “Still being sick 24 hours after the new medicine, or can't keep tablets down — call the same day so the nurses can give it as an injection.”   Fluids: “Can't keep fluids down, very dry or passing little urine — call the same day.”
