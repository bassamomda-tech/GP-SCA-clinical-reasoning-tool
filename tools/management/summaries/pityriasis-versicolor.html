Pityriasis versicolor

For: adolescents and adults (and the occasional child) with scaly pale, pink or brown patches on the upper trunk, in primary care. Not for: pale patches with no scale (think vitiligo and other mimics — block 1), or a separate changing pigmented lesion (assess it on its own merits; no NG12 criterion applies to PV).

Core rule: no scale, no PV — clear the yeast with a 5-day ketoconazole wash, keep itraconazole for extensive or resistant disease after checking interactions, and say before you prescribe that the colour takes months to return.

0Act now — PV is never dangerous; the risks are a missed mimic and oral antifungal harm

  • On itraconazole: jaundice, dark urine, severe nausea, marked fatigue or pruritus → stop it immediately → Same-day assessment with LFTs
  • Persistent, progressive, atypical or treatment-resistant pale patches, especially little or no scale, infiltration, poikiloderma, symptoms or sun-protected sites → Dermatology advice (hypopigmented mycosis fungoides, sarcoidosis).
  • Pale patches with sensory loss, nerve enlargement, or relevant travel or residence → consider leprosy → Dermatology advice; no biopsy in primary care unless within competence and the local pathway.

1Confirm — clinical; scale, pigment loss and site decide

Oval macules on the upper trunk, neck, upper arms, paler or darker than surrounding skin, with fine scale on stretching or scraping. Little or no itch.

2Check — tests are for doubt, not the typical case

☐ Scale  ☐ Typical site  ☐ Microscopy of scrapings if atypical, no scale, failed course, before oral treatment, or repeated courses  ☐ Relapsing: sweating, occlusion, oily products, immunosuppression/steroids

Don't miss: a scale-free white patch points away from PV. Wood's light is supportive only.

3Decide — topical for almost everyone

  • Most patients → ketoconazole 2% shampoo as a body wash. Small areas → azole cream.
  • Extensive, or a correctly applied topical course failed → itraconazole, preferably after microscopy if any doubt.
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding → topical only (away from eyes, mucosa, breasts); no itraconazole in pregnancy.
  • Children → check the shampoo SPC age limit; azole cream may be easier.

4Target — judge success by scale, not colour

GoalExpect
Scale goneWithin a few weeks = treatment worked
Colour back to normalSeveral months; not failure; more noticeable after tanning

5Treat — doses per BNF and local formulary

1st · washKetoconazole 2% shampoo on wet skin, lathered over all affected skin beyond the edge, 3–5 min (SPC; PCDS ~10 min), rinse — daily for 5 days
Small areasClotrimazole, miconazole, econazole or ketoconazole cream to patches plus a margin (duration per BNF)
Oral · extensive or resistantItraconazole 200 mg once daily for 7 days, immediately after a meal; continue or combine the wash. Fluconazole only per local formulary/specialist advice
Prevent · frequent recurrenceConsider ketoconazole short-contact wash every 2–4 weeks for about 6 months; not after one episode, not indefinitely
  • Before itraconazole: heart failure, liver, renal, pregnancy (contraception until the period after), breastfeeding (don't start routinely), CYP3A4/QT interactions, immunosuppressants (ciclosporin, tacrolimus, sirolimus).
  • Selenium sulfide 2.5% (Selsun) was discontinued in the UK in 2022 — only if on the local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Review: no scale = cured, whatever the colour.
  • No response to a correct course → check application, reconsider diagnosis, microscopy before repeating or escalating.
  • Negative scraping (esp. after recent antifungals) → reconsider, not indefinite empiric treatment.

7Refer — routine dermatology; advice and guidance first

  • Diagnostic uncertainty; no response to a correctly applied topical course; negative microscopy with persistent atypical lesions.
  • Persistent lesions without scale, or possible vitiligo needing confirmation.
  • Immunosuppressed with extensive, atypical or treatment-resistant disease.
  • Frequent relapse despite maintenance, or repeated oral courses needed.

8Never

  • Oral terbinafine (ineffective in PV) or oral ketoconazole (MHRA 2013: liver risk; topical is fine).
  • Itraconazole with simvastatin, in heart failure, or in pregnancy for PV.
  • Re-treat pale, scale-free skin — pigment lag is not treatment failure.
  • Months of antifungal for scale-free white patches — reconsider the diagnosis.
  • Repeat or escalate after a failed course without checking application and getting microscopy.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day Tablets: “Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, severe sickness, marked tiredness or itching — stop them and contact us the same day.”   Colour: “The flaking goes within weeks; the colour takes several months — that doesn't mean it failed.”   Relapse: “It often comes back; repeat the 5-day wash when the flaking returns.”   Routine “Spreading despite treatment, losing all their colour, or going numb — book an appointment so we can look again.”
