Pityriasis rosea

For: children and adults with a suspected pityriasis rosea rash in primary care, including in pregnancy. Not for: confirmed secondary syphilis or HIV (→ sexual health; see HIV), or a clear alternative — see psoriasis, fungal skin infection or eczema.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
PCDS guidanceBAD leaflet (2023)BASHH syphilis 2024UKHSA rash in pregnancyCochrane 2019
Core rule: pityriasis rosea is harmless — the risk is missing secondary syphilis. Look at the palms, soles, mouth and genitals, ask about sex and pregnancy, and test whenever the picture is not textbook.

0Act now — when it may not be pityriasis rosea

  • Palms or soles, mucosal, genital or perianal lesions, generalised lymphadenopathy, patchy alopecia, no herald patch → syphilis serology, offer HIV test. Positive or high suspicion → Urgent sexual health
  • Pregnant (especially onset <15 weeks) → syphilis serology (negative booking screen does not exclude) and UKHSA rash-in-pregnancy tests: parvovirus B19, rubella. Consider midwife or obstetric team.
  • Target lesions with mucosal involvement (erythema multiforme) → Same-day assessment
  • Blistering or purpuric rash, or feels unwell → Same-day reassessment Fever, sore throat, nodes, recent risk → HIV test and syphilis serology.
  • No herald patch, confluent, very itchy, new medicine in preceding weeks → drug eruption: review and, where possible, stop the drug.

1Confirm — clinical diagnosis

Herald patch (2–5 cm, red, fine scale, sharp edge), then days later smaller oval patches with a collar of scale along skin lines of trunk and upper limbs (fir-tree). Face, palms, soles usually spared. Mainly children and young adults. Darker skin: grey, violet or brown, more papular.

MimicClue → action
Secondary syphilisNo herald patch, palms/soles, nodes, not itchy → serology, sexual health
Tinea corporisSingle patch, active annular edge, asymmetric → scrape for mycology
Guttate psoriasisRaindrop papules, thicker scale, after strep throat → psoriasis
Persists for months (adult)Pityriasis lichenoides, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma → dermatology

2Check — every case

☐ Palms, soles, mouth, genitals  ☐ Lymph nodes  ☐ New partner, genital sore in last few months  ☐ Pregnancy  ☐ New medicines

Typical, no red flags: no routine tests. Never HHV-6/7 tests. Don't miss: a solitary tinea-like patch — consider tinea before any steroid.

3Decide — manage in primary care

  • Typical, syphilis excluded where indicated → reassurance, itch relief, safety-net.
  • No symptoms → no treatment needed (BAD). No time off school or work.
  • Pregnant: share the uncertain evidence honestly; never quote study percentages as her risk. Extra surveillance is for maternity care to decide.

5Treat — itch only; nothing shortens the rash

1 · EveryoneExplain: self-limiting, heals without scarring; may look worse for 1–2 weeks with new crops.
2 · Dry or itchyEmollient as soap substitute; lukewarm showers; loose cotton clothing.
3 · Itchy patchesMild to moderately potent steroid (hydrocortisone 1%, clobetasone butyrate 0.05%), thinly, short course.
4 · Sleep disturbedSedating antihistamine at night, short course (BNF/BNFC); warn drowsiness, driving, alcohol.
  • Aciclovir, antibiotics, phototherapy: not routine in primary care; aciclovir only by dermatology for severe disease. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Review

  • Course: most clear within about 12 weeks; some last longer. Pigment marks fade over months.
  • Still present at about 12 weeks → re-examine, syphilis serology if not done, consider look-alikes. Persistence means reassess, not proof of another diagnosis.

7Refer — rarely (urgent routes in block 0)

  • Dermatology: diagnosis uncertain, beyond expected course, refractory itch, blistering, purpuric or extensive atypical disease, suspected lymphoma.
  • Maternity (consider): onset <15 weeks, atypical or extensive, suspected syphilis, maternal concern.
  • No NG12 criterion applies.

8Never

  • Reassure without looking at the palms, soles and mouth.
  • Assume a negative booking screen excludes syphilis in pregnancy.
  • Put a steroid on a suspected fungal patch before considering tinea.
  • Prescribe aciclovir or antibiotics routinely, or any antiviral in pregnancy from primary care.
  • Treat persistence as failure — reassess the diagnosis instead.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same day “Contact us the same day if it blisters, you get spots on your palms or soles or sores in your mouth, you feel unwell, or you are or might be pregnant.”   Course: “New spots can keep coming for a few weeks; it can look worse before it settles.”   3 months: “If it’s still there after about three months, or it becomes more itchy, book an appointment.”
