Sciatica (lumbar radiculopathy)

For: people aged over 16 with sciatica managed in primary care. Not for: working out the cause of back or leg pain (use the back pain pathway), suspected cauda equina syndrome (cauda equina protocol), known cancer with neurological signs (MSCC pathway), or neuropathic pain other than sciatica (neuropathic pain protocol).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG59 (updated July 2026)NG215NG193NG234NG12
Core rule: give the cauda equina safety-net at every consultation, spoken and in writing, and document it — and do not offer gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, benzodiazepines or oral steroids for sciatica, or opioids for chronic sciatica.

0Act now — screen at every contact; the deficit can be painless

  • Saddle or perineal numbness, new urinary retention or altered urinary sensation, faecal incontinence, bilateral severe radicular symptoms or progressive bilateral weakness → emergency referral via the local suspected cauda equina pathway (ED or spinal services) Immediately
  • Known cancer + neurological symptoms or signs → MSCC coordinator Immediately; MRI within 24 hours; dexamethasone 16 mg orally via the MSCC pathway.
  • Known cancer + spinal metastasis pain, no neurological signs → MSCC coordinator Within 24 hours; MRI generally within 1 week.
  • Fever, systemic upset, injecting drug use, immunosuppression or recent bacteraemia (abscess, discitis) → Same-day emergency hospital assessment
  • New, severe or progressive motor weakness (e.g. worsening foot drop) → Same-day emergency spinal assessment. Severe leg pain without deficit or other red flags follows the sciatica pathway.
  • Suspected fracture or malignancy → Urgent investigation/referral

1Confirm — radicular, and grade the deficit

Leg-dominant pain in a nerve-root distribution ± sensory, reflex or motor change (straight-leg raise supports, not essential). Record power, reflexes, sensation, heel/toe walking as baseline.

MimicClue → action
StenosisBilateral leg pain on walking, eased by sitting or flexing → decompression opinion if walking distance significantly limited
Vascular claudicationAbsent pulses, eased promptly by rest → ankle-brachial index

2Check — ask the specific questions

☐ Sensation when wiping, perineal and genital  ☐ Bladder filling, hesitancy, retention, bladder/bowel control, sexual sensation  ☐ Cancer, infection, fracture flags

Don't miss (NG12): 60+, persistent bone (especially back) pain or unexplained fracture → FBC, calcium, ESR/PV, protein electrophoresis + free light chains together; suggests myeloma → Suspected cancer pathway. 60+, weight loss + back pain → consider urgent direct-access CT. Adult X-ray suggests bone sarcoma → consider suspected cancer referral.

3Decide — most sciatica is managed in primary care

  • No routine imaging in primary care, and say what you are NOT prescribing — explain why, then give a plan.
  • Prognosis, early: most improve, often gradually; pain can stay severe for weeks; slow recovery does not mean surgery.
  • Stay active as pain allows, no bed rest; fit note for modified duties or graded return; no driving while pain, weakness or sedation impairs control.

5Treat — within the NG59 limits

First lineConsider an oral NSAID, lowest dose, shortest time, ± gastroprotection (limited evidence of benefit). Not paracetamol alone.
NSAID unsuitableSevere ACUTE pain → consider a weak opioid, smallest quantity, stop date written. Never for chronic.
ExerciseConsider a group exercise programme; manual therapy only alongside exercise.
Acute and severeUncontrolled → consider referral for epidural local anaesthetic + steroid.
  • Before an NSAID: renal, heart failure, BP, GI, liver, age, pregnancy, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, steroids; state maximum dose and course. Doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • Already on a gabapentinoid, benzodiazepine or opioid → agree a gradual taper (NG215); don't stop abruptly.

6Monitor

  • Book a specific review date: neurology vs baseline, drug benefit and harm; re-screen red flags, repeat the safety-net.
  • Not improving → reassess diagnosis and red flags.
  • Low mood → treat in its own right (NHS Talking Therapies). Chronic primary pain → NG193.

7Refer — non-urgent

  • Spinal surgical opinion: pain or function still substantially limited despite non-surgical care (no fixed week threshold).
  • Physiotherapy/MSK: state diagnosis, neurology and functional goal.

8Never

  • Gabapentin, pregabalin, other antiepileptics, benzodiazepines (including diazepam as a "muscle relaxant") or oral steroids for sciatica.
  • Opioids for chronic sciatica.
  • Bed rest.
  • Belts, corsets, orthotics, rocker soles, traction, ultrasound, interferential, PENS, TENS or acupuncture; spinal injections for low back pain.
  • A routine outpatient scan or a wait for review when any cauda equina feature is present.

9Safety-net — every consultation; spoken, in writing, documented

A&E / 999 “Numbness around your back passage or between your legs, or when you wipe, can't pass urine, losing control of your bladder or bowels, or both legs becoming weak or numb — go to A&E straight away (call 999 if you can't get there safely). Don't ring us first.”   Same-day “New or worsening weakness — a foot that drags or catches, difficulty on stairs — rapidly worsening pain, fever with back pain, new night pain or weight loss — contact us the same day.”   Tablets: “Anti-inflammatory with food, never more than the stated dose; codeine is a small supply until the stop date — it can constipate, and don't drive if drowsy.”   Recovery: “Usually gradual and uneven; leg pain usually settles before back pain; some numbness can linger.”

✓Safe consultation check — all five done, case safely managed

Cauda equina, cancer, infection and fracture flags asked specifically Radicular pain confirmed; neurological baseline recorded NSAID risk checked (renal, GI, CV, pregnancy, anticoagulants) Activity, exercise and work plan; review date booked Safety-net given verbally and in writing, documented
