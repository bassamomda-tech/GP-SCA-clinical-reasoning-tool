Pre-school wheeze (under 5)

For: children under 5 with wheeze, including bronchiolitis (usually under 2). Not for: suspected or confirmed asthma aged 5 and over (use asthma in children); pneumonia (use the chest infections protocol).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG9 (updated 2021)NICE NG245 (2024)BTS/SIGN 158 acuteMHRA montelukastBNFc
Core rule: classify first — bronchiolitis gets supportive care only (no bronchodilators or steroids); recurrent wheeze gets a working description (“episodic viral wheeze” or “suspected asthma”) and a time-limited trial with a stop date, not a label.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Bronchiolitis: apnoea, central cyanosis, grunting, marked recession, RR >70, severe exhaustion or looks seriously unwell → 999, whatever the SpO₂.
  • Wheezy child with silent chest, exhaustion, poor respiratory effort, altered consciousness, agitation or cyanosis → 999 — do not wait for an SpO₂ reading.
  • Severe acute wheeze (SpO₂ <92%, too breathless to talk or feed; aged 2–5 HR >140 or RR >40) → oxygen, salbutamol 10 puffs via spacer, prednisolone → Emergency transfer
  • Drooling, unable to swallow, muffled voice, tripod, toxic-looking (epiglottitis) → 999 — do NOT examine the throat.
  • Sudden choking, then persistent cough or unilateral wheeze → Same-day paediatrics, even if well now.
  • Bronchiolitis: RR >60, feeding 50–75% of usual, dehydration, SpO₂ persistently <92% in air → consider Urgent hospital assessment; lower threshold if under 3 months or high risk.

1Classify — is it wheeze, and which?

PatternRecognise it
BronchiolitisUsually under 2; coryza, then cough, tachypnoea, crackles and/or wheeze, poor feeding
Episodic viral wheezeWheeze only with colds; well between
Multiple-trigger / suspected asthmaAlso between colds (exercise, laughing, cold air, smoke); often atopy
Croup — not wheezeBarking cough, hoarse voice, inspiratory stridor

Ask the parent to imitate the sound. Under 5, asthma usually cannot be confirmed — record the working description with a review date.

2Check

☐ SpO₂, RR, HR, recession  ☐ Feeding, wet nappies  ☐ Spacer technique seen  ☐ Smoke exposure  ☐ Atopy

Don't miss: wheeze from the first weeks, failure to thrive, wet cough, clubbing, focal signs, recurrent pneumonia → paediatric referral, not more salbutamol.

3Decide

  • Bronchiolitis → supportive only: feeding, fluids, oxygen if needed (block 8).
  • Episodic viral wheeze → no routine preventer; salbutamol via spacer, written plan.
  • Suspected asthma needing maintenance treatment, or severe episodes (admission or ≥2 oral steroid courses) → consider an 8–12-week trial of twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS + SABA as needed.
  • Every contact: ask about smoke and vaping → offer cessation support.

5Treat — the acute episode

  • Mild–moderate: salbutamol via pMDI + spacer (± face mask), 2–10 puffs, one puff at a time with 5 breaths; reassess before discharge.
  • Severe: oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, salbutamol 10 puffs via spacer or nebulised, ipratropium if poor response, prednisolone, admission.
  • Prednisolone: consider only for a severe episode or per the child's plan, not mild viral wheeze. Aged 2–5: 20 mg daily, up to 3 days.
  • Croup: single dose of oral dexamethasone per local pathway. Stridor at rest, marked recession, hypoxia → refer/admit.
  • Montelukast: discuss MHRA neuropsychiatric effects; stop if new or worsening. Doses per BNFc and local formulary.

6Monitor — a trial has a stop date

  • After an acute episode: review within 2 working days if managed at home, and within 2 working days of discharge after any hospital attendance.
  • ICS trial at 8–12 weeks: resolved → consider stopping ICS and SABA, review after a further 3 months; recurs, or attack needing systemic steroids or hospital → restart low-dose ICS; no benefit → stop, check technique and adherence, revisit diagnosis.
  • On a preventer: at least annually. At age 5, attempt objective testing if symptoms persist.

7Refer — paediatrics

  • Chronic red flags, diagnostic uncertainty, or atypical or treatment-resistant wheeze.
  • Frequent severe episodes, repeated oral steroid courses, or uncontrolled on moderate-dose ICS after an LTRA trial.

8Never

  • Salbutamol, steroids, antibiotics, montelukast or nebulised adrenaline in bronchiolitis.
  • Use a lack of response to salbutamol to exclude asthma, or say “viral wheeze is not asthma” with severe episodes, interval symptoms or atopy.
  • Label a wheezy under-5 as asthmatic, or let an ICS trial become an indefinite prescription.
  • Another salbutamol trial for chronic red flags, or for unilateral wheeze after choking.
  • Escalate treatment past poor spacer technique.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 / A&E “Blue lips, pauses in breathing, sucking in under the ribs, grunting, drowsy or floppy, a very quiet chest while working hard, too breathless to feed or speak, choked on something, noisy breathing in at rest, or the inhaler not helping or not lasting 4 hours — call 999.”
Same-day “Breathing harder work, inhaler needed more often, feeding less than about half or fewer wet nappies, fever with fast breathing, or you are worried — see us the same day.”   Spacer: “One puff at a time, then five normal breaths — show me.”   Expect: “The cough can last up to 3 weeks; come back if he has symptoms between colds or worse episodes.”
