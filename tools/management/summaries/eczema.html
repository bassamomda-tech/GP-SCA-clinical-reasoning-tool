Atopic eczema

For: children and adults with atopic eczema. CG57 figures apply to under 12s; adolescents and adults by clinical judgement and local pathways. Not for: starting systemic, biologic or JAK treatment (specialist), or one-sided nipple changes (NG12 breast pathway).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE CG57NG190TA81 · TA82MHRA DSUNG12 (Apr 2026)
Core rule: emollients are the treatment — lots, every day, even when clear; match steroid potency to the site; and a sudden painful deterioration is eczema herpeticum until you have looked — start aciclovir today.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Eczema herpeticum (painful, fast-worsening, punched-out erosions or blisters, ± fever) → systemic aciclovir now, not after PCR, + Same-day dermatology advice (not obtainable → urgent hospital assessment). Around the eyes → also Same-day ophthalmology
  • Systemically unwell with spreading infection, suspected sepsis, necrotising fasciitis, severe cellulitis or disproportionate pain; or erythroderma with systemic features → Same-day acute assessment; consider admission.
  • Aged 50+, one nipple only: discharge, retraction or other change of concern → Suspected cancer pathway (NG12). Persistent one-sided nipple dermatitis → consider breast referral (Paget’s); don’t repeat creams.
  • Child under 12: severe eczema not responding to 1 week of optimal topical treatment, or infected eczema failing treatment → Urgent dermatology (within 2 weeks)

1Confirm — is it atopic eczema?

Under 12 (CG57): itchy skin plus ≥3 of: visible flexural dermatitis (cheeks/extensors if ≤18 months); past flexural dermatitis; dry skin in the last 12 months; asthma or hay fever (atopic first-degree relative if under 4); onset before 2 (not used under 4).

Adults: clinical, not CG57. Skin of colour: extensor, discoid or follicular; redness harder to see. Grade: mild = dry, infrequent itch · moderate = frequent itch, redness · severe = widespread, incessant itch, ± oozing. Grade impact too (sleep, school, mood).

2Check — every review, before stepping up

☐ Sites mapped  ☐ Quantities issued  ☐ Technique, steroid fears  ☐ Infection  ☐ Triggers  ☐ Infant not settling: cow’s milk, egg. Don’t miss: contact allergy, tinea, scabies, T-cell lymphoma.

3Decide — treatment follows the grade

  • Mild → mild steroid. Moderate → moderate steroid, TCI, bandages. Severe → potent steroid, TCI; phototherapy, systemic = specialist. Impact can move up a step.
  • Infected, not systemically unwell → no routine antibiotic. Localised, if needed → consider fusidic acid 2% TDS 5–7 days.
  • Widespread or systemically unwell → flucloxacillin 500 mg QDS 5–7 days (adults); penicillin allergy → clarithromycin 250 mg BD (500 mg if severe); pregnancy → erythromycin. Children per BNFC. Swab only if recurrent or not improving.

4Target — right potency, right site

Face, neckMild; moderate 3–5 days only in a severe flare
Axillae, groinModerate or potent, 7–14 days only
ChildrenPotent ≤14 days, specialist only under 12 months; very potent specialist only

5Treat — build the base, treat the flare, prevent the next

Step 1 · everyoneEmollient several times a day, as soap substitute, even when clear. Children: 250–500 g a week.
Step 2 · flareSteroid once or twice daily to active areas (incl. last 48 hours) until settled, then stop.
Step 3 · 2–3 flares a monthUnder 12: consider steroid on usual sites 2 consecutive days a week; review at 3–6 months.
Step 4 · second lineTopical calcineurin inhibitor (TCI, TA82), age 2+: steroid not controlling and serious atrophy risk. Never first line.
  • Antihistamines (under 12): not routine. Severe eczema, itch or urticaria → 1-month non-sedating trial; flare with sleep loss, 6 months+ → 7–14-day sedating.
  • Bottle-fed under 6 months, moderate–severe, uncontrolled → offer 6–8 weeks of extensively hydrolysed or amino acid formula. Doses per BNF/BNFC and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Repeats: emollient fits the area; steroid lowest potency, shortest time. Redness or burning worse than before after stopping → consider steroid withdrawal (MHRA).
  • Prolonged potent or occluded use, young child → review growth. Wet wraps: trained professional only, not on infected skin.

7Refer — routine (urgent ones are in block 0)

  • Under 12: diagnosis uncertain; uncontrolled by family’s assessment; facial eczema not responding; contact allergy; major sleep or school impact; severe recurrent infections.
  • Adults: severity, impact, diagnostic doubt, treatment failure, patch testing, need for systemic treatment; fixed atypical plaques that never clear.
  • Suspected food allergy, young child, uncontrolled → allergy service, not home exclusion.

8Never

  • Wait for an HSV swab or PCR before starting aciclovir.
  • Potent steroid under 12 months or very potent in a child without a specialist.
  • Routine or repeated antibiotics (incl. fusidic acid) when not systemically unwell.
  • Bath additives for children; aqueous cream as a leave-on emollient.
  • Escalate steroid for tinea, or repeat creams on a one-sided nipple rash.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same day “Sudden painful spreading sores or blisters, sores near the eye, fever, or very unwell with spreading redness — get seen today.”   Emollient fire risk: “Cream dried on clothes or bedding burns easily — no smoking or naked flames; wash them often.”   Steroid: “Once or twice a day on the flare and patches from the last 2 days, until settled, then stop — that is safe.”   TCI: “Burning or stinging at first is common; protect from the sun.”
