Gallstone disease and cholecystitis

For: adults with right-upper-quadrant or epigastric pain from suspected gallstones, and after cholecystectomy. Not for: jaundice without a biliary picture (use the jaundice pathway) or abnormal LFTs alone (use the abnormal LFTs pathway). Acute cholecystitis, cholangitis and pancreatitis are hospital-managed (block 0).

Core rule: only uncomplicated biliary colic stays in the community. Fever, jaundice, rigors, persistent severe pain or systemic illness means hospital assessment, not stronger painkillers.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Sepsis, hypotension, confusion, respiratory compromise or organ dysfunction, or sudden generalised pain or rigid abdomen (perforation) → 999 Older, diabetic or immunosuppressed may look septic with few local signs.
  • Fever or rigors with jaundice = suspected cholangitis, even without RUQ pain; do not wait for the full Charcot's triad → Emergency hospital assessment (999 if unstable).
  • Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, with vomiting, in someone with gallstones (gallstone pancreatitis) → Emergency admission
  • Persistent RUQ or epigastric pain, especially with fever, local tenderness, vomiting or raised inflammatory markers (acute cholecystitis) → Same-day surgical assessment
  • Jaundice, dark urine and pale stools without fever (bile duct stone) → Same-day hepatobiliary or surgical with LFTs, renal function, clotting, imaging; not necessarily by ambulance if stable.
  • Aged 40 and over with jaundice → also a Suspected cancer pathway referral for pancreatic cancer (NG12). Painful jaundice may still be malignant.

1Confirm — colic or cholecystitis?

Biliary colic → communityAcute cholecystitis → same-day
Episodic steady RUQ or epigastric pain, minutes to a few hours, often after a fatty meal; settles; no fever; Murphy's negativePersistent pain, fever, vomiting, Murphy's positive, raised CRP and white count

Duration alone does not diagnose cholecystitis; a negative Murphy's sign does not exclude it (older, obese, immunosuppressed). Incidental stones, normal gallbladder and tree = asymptomatic.

2Check — every suspected case

☐ Temperature, pulse, BP, jaundice  ☐ LFTs and abdominal ultrasound, promptly  ☐ FBC and CRP if inflammation suspected  ☐ Renal function before an NSAID  ☐ ECG if the pain could be cardiac

Don't miss: inferior MI or ACS (999), right basal pneumonia, peptic ulcer, renal colic, hepatitis. Red flag → don't wait for community imaging.

3Decide — who needs surgery

  • Symptomatic gallbladder stones → offer referral for laparoscopic cholecystectomy; bile duct stones cleared at or before it (surgery or ERCP).
  • Asymptomatic stones → reassure; no treatment unless symptoms develop. Atypical pain more likely dyspepsia, reflux or IBS → don't offer surgery just because ultrasound shows stones.
  • Lifestyle: avoid trigger foods until surgery; gradual, not rapid, weight loss.

4Target — timing of surgery

Biliary colicElective laparoscopic cholecystectomy
Acute cholecystitisWithin 1 week of diagnosis
Mild gallstone pancreatitisSame admission once recovered

5Treat — uncomplicated biliary colic

  • NSAID usually preferred if no contraindications; paracetamol as adjunct or alternative; opioids only for severe pain when others fail. Antiemetic if vomiting.
  • Before an NSAID: renal impairment, peptic ulcer or GI bleed, anticoagulant or antiplatelet, heart failure or CVD, uncontrolled BP, pregnancy, NSAID hypersensitivity or asthma, frailty. PPI if a longer course.
  • Pain not settling, vomiting stopping fluids or unable to take oral medicines → Same-day surgical assessment, not repeated injections and home.
  • Antispasmodic may be considered (less effective). Ursodeoxycholic acid is not a routine alternative. Review fibrates. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor — waiting and after surgery

  • Waiting: referral actioned, analgesia adequate. New jaundice → Same-day LFTs and imaging via the acute team.
  • Days after surgery: pain, fever, distension or jaundice (bile leak) → Same-day operating or HPB team. Later, with cholestatic LFTs (retained stone) → Urgent hepatobiliary.
  • Persistent watery diarrhoea → exclude other causes; bile acid diarrhoea per local pathway.

7Refer — non-emergency

  • Surgery, elective: symptomatic stones. Hepatobiliary, urgent: duct stones without sepsis, retained stone, uncertainty.
  • 60+ with weight loss and diarrhoea, back or abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation or new diabetes → consider urgent direct-access CT for pancreatic cancer. Mass like an enlarged gallbladder → consider urgent direct-access ultrasound (gallbladder cancer).

8Never

  • Manage acute cholecystitis, cholangitis or pancreatitis in the community, or with oral antibiotics and an outpatient scan.
  • Antibiotics for uncomplicated biliary colic.
  • Stronger painkillers for persistent pain instead of same-day assessment.
  • Attribute jaundice to gallstones without imaging — painful jaundice may still be malignant.
  • Cholecystectomy for incidental asymptomatic stones in a normal gallbladder and biliary tree.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “If you feel very unwell, confused, faint or breathless, call 999.”   A&E now “A fever, shivering attacks, yellow eyes or skin, or severe pain, including pain going through to your back with vomiting — go to A&E straight away; don't drive yourself.”   Same-day “Pain that doesn't settle within a few hours — contact us or NHS 111 the same day. Pale stools with dark urine — contact us the same day.”   While waiting: “Avoid the foods that set off your attacks.”
