Surgical problems in children

For: infants and children seen in primary care with vomiting, abdominal pain, a groin or scrotal swelling, rectal bleeding or a change in bowel habit. Not for: adult hernias (use the hernia protocol). Related pages: undescended testis protocol, vomiting in children, abdominal pain in children, scrotal pain, UTI in children, constipation (child). Does not replace the local paediatric emergency, surgical or ambulance pathway.

Core rule: green or yellow-green vomiting is intestinal obstruction (possible volvulus) until proven otherwise, always examine the testes in a boy with abdominal pain, and never delay transfer of an unwell child to get imaging.

0Act now — immediate hospital assessment via the local pathway; 999 if shocked, unstable, peritonism, severe breathing difficulty, uncontrolled pain or rapid deterioration

  • Green or yellow-green (bile-stained) vomiting, any age → Immediately possible volvulus; a normal X-ray does not exclude it.
  • Episodic severe pain or drawing up knees, pallor or lethargy, vomiting, or unexplained rectal bleeding → Immediately possible intussusception; triad uncommon, redcurrant-jelly stool late, may look well between episodes.
  • Sudden severe one-sided scrotal or lower abdominal pain → torsion: Immediately direct to a centre that can do scrotal exploration (time-critical).
  • Irreducible, painful, red or tense inguinal hernia → Immediately, no forceful reduction. Button battery, multiple magnets, or magnet plus metal → Immediately, even if well.
  • Newborn: bile-stained vomiting, no meconium by 48 h, distension, or anorectal malformation / abdominal wall defect → Immediately. No meconium by 48 h in a term baby alone → Same-day paediatrics
  • Palpable abdominal mass, unexplained enlarged abdominal organ or unexplained visible haematuria → consider very urgent referral ≤48 h (NICE NG12). With shock, severe pain, hypertension or deterioration → Immediately. Do not palpate repeatedly.

1Confirm — think by age and pattern

AgeThink of
Newborn to 2 monthsPyloric stenosis (2–8 weeks: forceful non-bilious vomiting, hungry after); Hirschsprung's (no meconium by 48 h); atresias
InfancyIntussusception; inguinal hernia (commonest; in girls may contain an ovary); undescended testis
ChildhoodAppendicitis (atypical and often perforated in under-5s); hernia; hydrocele; Meckel's (painless rectal bleeding); acute scrotum — exclude torsion first

Mimics: UTI (appendicitis) and constipation.

2Check — whole child; tests never delay transfer

☐ Heart rate trend with temperature, capillary refill (>2 s = concern), RR, BP, mental state, urine output  ☐ Abdomen, hernial orifices and testes  ☐ Urine dipstick + MSU  ☐ FBC/CRP only if no delay — normal results do not exclude appendicitis  ☐ Ask about ingestion

No routine rectal exam. Don't miss: inconsistent history or unexplained injury → safeguarding procedures alongside urgent care.

3Decide — Same-day paediatric assessment

  • Suspected appendicitis without peritonism (peritonism, shock or toxicity → block 0). Never manage conservatively in the community.
  • Progressively forceful vomiting, infant under 2 months, with hunger, dehydration or poor weight gain (pyloric stenosis).
  • Painful scrotal or testicular swelling where torsion cannot be confidently excluded.
  • Hirschsprung red flags: constipation since birth, no response to laxatives, distension, faltering growth.

5Treat — GP management of the watch-and-wait conditions

  • Inguinal hernia: refer every child to paediatric surgery (prompt in young infants); teach parents the signs of incarceration.
  • Umbilical hernia: observe; refer if painful, irreducible, skin compromise, enlarging or persistent (local policy). No belts or strapping.
  • Infant hydrocele: observe. Refer earlier if painful, enlarging, hernia suspected or testis cannot be assessed.
  • Constipation: diet, fluids, laxatives; not labelled functional until obstructive and congenital causes considered.
  • Blocked tear duct: massage, chloramphenicol if infected. Red, tender tear-sac swelling → Same-day ophthalmology

6Monitor — after surgery

  • Wound infection: examine and discuss with the surgical team; no automatic GP antibiotic.
  • Pain after appendicectomy: think stump appendicitis or adhesion obstruction (a lifelong risk after abdominal surgery).
  • Orchidopexy: code it; teach testicular awareness in adolescence. Terminal ileum resected → B12 monitoring.

7Refer — undescended testis and planned routes

  • Unilateral at 6–8 weeks → re-examine at 4–5 months (corrected); still undescended or impalpable → paediatric surgery/urology, seen by 6 months (surgery ideally by 12, latest 18 months). No routine ultrasound.
  • Bilateral at 6–8 weeks → urgent paediatrician, seen within 2 weeks. Newborn: impalpable testis or undescended testis with hypospadias → senior paediatric review within 24 hours.
  • Later bilateral non-palpable testes or genital ambiguity → urgent specialist/endocrine assessment.

8Never

  • Delay transfer of an unwell child to get an X-ray or ultrasound.
  • Send suspected torsion for community ultrasound, or use a TWIST score to downgrade it.
  • Attempt forceful manual reduction of an irreducible hernia.
  • Assess a child's abdominal pain without examining the testes and hernial orifices.

9Safety-net — say it, give it in writing, document it

Hospital now 999 if unstable or getting worse: “Green or yellow-green vomit; bouts of severe pain, pale or drowsy, vomiting or blood in the poo; sudden pain in the testicle or lower tummy; a painful groin lump that won't go back.”   Hernia or hydrocele: “If the lump becomes painful or won't go back, come to hospital straight away.”   After intussusception: “The same symptoms again — A&E immediately; it can come back.”
