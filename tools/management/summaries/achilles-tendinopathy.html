Achilles tendinopathy in adults

For: adults with gradual, load-related posterior heel or ankle pain, mainly midportion disease. Not for: children (NG12 bone criteria; local paediatric pathway), confirmed acute rupture (fracture clinic), infection, DVT, enthesitis, malignancy or complex partial tears — use the local pathway.

Core rule: do the calf-squeeze test on every new posterior heel pain, ask about fluoroquinolones, then load the tendon progressively — and never inject steroid into the Achilles.

0Act now — screen every new posterior heel or ankle pain first

  • Snap or “kick” at the heel, weak push-off, or no plantarflexion on calf squeeze — or convincing history, equivocal test → Same-day fracture clinic or ED — no routine ultrasound first.
  • Hot, red, swollen heel with fever or systemic upset (septic bursitis, osteomyelitis, gout); open injury; neurovascular compromise; rapidly progressive pain, swelling or loss of function → Same-day assessment
  • New unilateral calf swelling, tenderness, warmth or disproportionate pain, especially after rupture or immobilisation → Same-day local DVT pathway. Chest pain or breathlessness → 999
  • Adult, unexplained lump increasing in size → consider Urgent direct access ultrasound ≤2 weeks. Child or young person, unexplained bone swelling or pain → consider Very urgent X-ray ≤48 hours

1Confirm — a clinical diagnosis; place the pain

Calf-squeeze test prone, both legs; walking or some plantarflexion does not exclude rupture.

2Check — history and baseline

☐ Recent antibiotics (abroad, sexual health, urology)  ☐ Steroid  ☐ Training, shoes, weight, diabetes, lipids  ☐ Baseline heel raises

Don't miss: non-mechanical enthesitis — persistent, several sites, or with back pain, psoriasis, uveitis, IBD, GI/GU infection or family history → rheumatology (NG65); normal CRP/ESR does not exclude it.

3Decide — fluoroquinolone in the history?

  • Current → stop; prescriber to switch; avoid corticosteroids. Finished → don't re-expose without documented specialist indication.
  • Record a serious suspected adverse reaction (not “allergy”); consider Yellow Card.
  • Onset 48 hours to months later; higher risk over 60, renal impairment, transplant, steroid.

4Target — the pain-monitoring rule

AcceptableSome discomfort during loading — no universal 5/10 threshold
Reduce load ifPain clearly escalates, alters gait, big next-day flare, stiffness worse week on week, swelling or bruising

5Treat — progressive calf loading is first line

Step 1 · weeks 1–2Cut aggravating load, not all activity; isometric holds; heel lift
Step 2 · weeks 2–12Single-leg → loaded: eccentric (midportion) or heavy slow resistance
Step 3 · athletesHop, skip, run drills; volume before speed and hills
Step 4 · ~3 months, no betterCheck adherence; re-examine (partial tear?) → MSK
  • At least 2–3 times a week, as heavy as tolerated, with education; complete rest does not help.
  • Analgesia only to enable loading: paracetamol; topical NSAID first for localised pain.
  • Oral NSAID: short, lowest dose; check GI, renal, CV risk; no diclofenac in IHD, PAD, cerebrovascular disease or heart failure; avoid routinely in pregnancy. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor

  • ~6 weeks if function-limited or adherence uncertain: done and progressed? Flaring → drop back a step.
  • ~12 weeks: adherence, diagnosis, function, heel raises. Little change → refer.
  • Steroid injected elsewhere → warn of rupture; calf-squeeze at every review. After rupture: check VTE risk assessment was done.

7Refer — routine (urgent routes in block 0)

  • Physio at diagnosis: needs help with the programme, athletes returning to running, pain limiting work or life.
  • MSK/orthopaedics: little better at ~12 weeks, doubt, partial tear, deterioration, insertional bony prominence or bursitis. Shockwave only under HTG426 special arrangements; PRP not routine.
  • Adult sarcoma: ultrasound suggestive, or uncertain with persisting concern → consider suspected cancer pathway referral.

8Never

  • Steroid into the Achilles, or empirical injection around it in primary care.
  • A routine ultrasound before same-day assessment of suspected rupture.
  • Reassurance because the patient can walk or tiptoe.
  • A fluoroquinolone again without a specialist indication, or with a corticosteroid.
  • Complete rest as the treatment.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Chest pain or breathlessness — call 999.”   Same-day “A sudden snap or kick at the back of your heel, or you suddenly can’t push off — come straight back or go to A&E. Swelling or bruising rapidly increasing, a hot red heel with fever, a swollen or painful calf, a lump that keeps growing, or persistent pain at rest or at night — contact us or NHS 111 the same day.”   Exercises: “A little discomfort can be fine; ease back if it’s much worse the next morning.”   Boot: “Don’t drive if the boot, pain or weakness stops safe control; check with your insurer.”
