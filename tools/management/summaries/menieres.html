Ménière's disease

For: adults with recurrent spontaneous vertigo attacks and fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus or aural fullness. Not for: brief positional vertigo (BPPV), a single prolonged episode (vestibular neuronitis) or undifferentiated vertigo (vertigo pathway); red-flag presentations (block 0).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG98NICE NG127NICE NG155AAO-HNS 2020DVLA
Core rule: exclude sudden hearing loss and central causes before you label it Ménière's; suppressants for attacks only; sudden disabling attacks mean stop driving and notify the DVLA.

0Act now — do not call it a Ménière's attack until these are excluded

  • Sudden hearing loss over ≤3 days, within the past 30 days, not explained by external or middle-ear disease → Seen within 24 h ENT or ED; do not wait for audiometry.
  • Sudden loss >30 days ago, or rapidly worsening over 4–90 days → ≤2 weeks urgent referral. Central signs: vertical or direction-changing nystagmus, focal neurology, diplopia, severe ataxia, new unilateral hearing loss with vertigo, severe headache or neck pain → 999 stroke pathway. HINTS only for acute vestibular syndrome, if trained.
  • Ramsay Hunt (ear pain, vesicles ± facial palsy); bacterial labyrinthitis or cholesteatoma (otalgia, discharge, systemic upset, attic crust) → Same-day ENT. Severe vomiting, dehydration, cannot mobilise or retain oral treatment → Same-day acute medical assessment.
  • Sudden falls without loss of consciousness → Urgent specialist assessment; also consider syncope, arrhythmia, seizure. No driving, swimming alone or heights meanwhile.

1Confirm — Bárány criteria support specialist classification

Probable≥2 spontaneous episodes of 20 min to 24 h, fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus or aural pressure in the affected ear, no better diagnosis
Definite≥2 spontaneous episodes of 20 min to 12 h + audiometrically documented low- to mid-frequency SNHL in that ear

Not positional, seconds or days. Do not confirm from duration alone. Vestibular migraine: co-equal differential, may lack headache, may coexist.

2Check

☐ Otoscopy  ☐ Tuning forks if competent  ☐ Nystagmus, cranial nerves, gait, cerebellar  ☐ Lying and standing BP, pulse  ☐ Ototoxic drugs  ☐ Audiometry (may need serial tests)

Don't miss vestibular schwannoma: MRI internal auditory meati — offer for localising signs; consider for SNHL asymmetry ≥15 dB at 2 adjacent frequencies or unilateral non-pulsatile tinnitus. It does not exclude central causes.

3Decide

  • All suspected cases → ENT/audiovestibular: audiometry, imaging by criteria, diagnosis.
  • Severe attack → short course of one antiemetic or vestibular suppressant only.
  • Recurrent attacks, after specialist assessment → a time-limited betahistine trial may be considered; benefit uncertain.
  • Lifestyle (may help): regular meals, consistent (not necessarily low) salt; cut caffeine or alcohol if triggers.

4Target — treatment goals

Fewer, less severe attacks; no suppressant on repeat; hearing, tinnitus and mood supported — "burn-out" is not cure.

5Treat — acute relief, then maintenance

Acute · attack onlyOne of: prochlorperazine 5 mg PO TDS or buccal 3–6 mg BD; cyclizine 50 mg TDS; cinnarizine 30 mg TDS
Maintenance · evidence limitedSpecialist-advised betahistine trial (commonly 16 mg TDS); review at agreed point; stop if ineffective
SpecialistDiuretic in selected patients; check U&E, BP, gout
RefractoryENT/neuro-otology: intratympanic steroid or gentamicin, surgery; specialist-led, informed consent
  • Before every step: one agent, no stacking sedatives; cautions: older adults, Parkinson's, glaucoma, urinary retention, QT drugs.
  • Betahistine: not in phaeochromocytoma; caution asthma, peptic ulcer.
  • Between attacks: vestibular rehab, hearing support. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.

6Monitor

  • Each review: attacks (refer if disabling), mood, driving and work advice.
  • Maintenance at the agreed review point → continue only if helping, not on repeat by default.
  • Features change: new asymmetry → imaging per criteria; central signs → 999.
  • Not controlled? Recheck diagnosis, audiometry, an adequate reviewed trial, suppressants still running.

7Refer

  • ENT / audiovestibular: all suspected cases; refractory or drop attacks.
  • Neurology: suspected vestibular migraine or another central cause.
  • Vestibular physiotherapy: chronic imbalance between attacks. Audiology: hearing aids, tinnitus support.

8Never

  • Label it Ménière's before excluding sudden hearing loss and central causes.
  • Wait for formal audiometry before a sudden hearing loss referral.
  • Leave prochlorperazine or cinnarizine on repeat between attacks.
  • Use HINTS for episodic attacks or reassure from peripheral-looking nystagmus alone.
  • Continue betahistine by default without review, or offer it for tinnitus alone.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “Sudden severe headache, double vision, slurred speech, weakness, or unsteadiness that stays rather than coming in attacks — call 999.”   Same day “A sudden drop in hearing — contact us the same day. It needs to be seen within 24 hours.”   Driving: “If attacks can come on suddenly without warning, you must stop driving and tell the DVLA; stop safety-critical work until assessed.” Group 2 is stricter.   Medicines: “The anti-sickness tablets are for attacks only — taking them all the time keeps you unsteady.”

✓Safe consultation check — all five done, case safely managed
