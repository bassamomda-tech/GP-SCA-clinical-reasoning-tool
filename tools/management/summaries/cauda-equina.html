Cauda equina syndrome (CES)

For: adults with back pain or sciatica in primary care — recognising and escalating suspected CES. Not for: ordinary sciatica without red flags (use the sciatica protocol and back pain pathway) or confirmed cancer-related spinal compression (use the MSCC protocol).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG127GIRFT CES pathwayNICE NG234GM MSCC protocol
Core rule: refer at the first suspicion, not on a complete syndrome — an incomplete or evolving picture still qualifies, and no normal finding excludes CES.

0Act now — escalate through the local emergency CES pathway

  • Severe low-back/radicular pain + new bladder, bowel, sexual or perineal sensory symptoms (complete or evolving) → immediate referral and emergency MRI Immediately
  • Painless urinary retention with overflow (established CES) → still an emergency Immediately
  • Bilateral or progressive radicular symptoms, major motor deficit or rapidly evolving neurology, no sphincter symptoms yet → Same-day spinal-team discussion
  • Known or suspected cancer + any red flag → MSCC coordinator/oncology Immediately — known cancer does not prove malignant compression.
  • Fever, systemic upset, injecting drug use or immunosuppression (epidural abscess) or anticoagulated with sudden onset (epidural haematoma) → emergency hospital assessment, state the concern Immediately
  • Escalate by direct verbal contact with the receiving team plus written information — never a routine e-referral or letter alone. MRI is not a precondition for referral.

1Confirm — ask specifically; patients do not volunteer it

Red flagAsk

New or changed symptoms in known back pain = a fresh assessment.

2Check — focused neuro exam; supports but never gates escalation

☐ Perianal/saddle light touch and pinprick S2–S5, both sides  ☐ Lower-limb power, reflexes (ankle jerks), sensation, gait if safe  ☐ Sensory level, upper motor neurone (UMN) signs, fever  ☐ Palpate bladder; post-void residual only if immediately available (record value and time)  ☐ Anal tone only if clinically appropriate, with consent and training

Don't miss: normal power, reflexes, anal tone or a low residual does not exclude CES. Sensory level or UMN signs → cord or conus, not cauda equina.

3Decide — describe the picture; don't delay to stage it

  • Suspected/evolving: new sacral or urinary symptoms, voluntary control retained → immediate referral and emergency MRI.
  • Established: painless retention with overflow → generally poorer recovery; still an emergency.
  • Benign (disc) or malignant cause is decided on the MRI, not clinically.

5Treat — while arranging transfer; never delay it

  • Disc-related CES: no routine corticosteroid — no empirical dexamethasone unless the receiving team advises. Spinal surgical referral for emergency decompression.
  • Suspected malignant compression: dexamethasone per the current local MSCC protocol unless contraindicated, with gastroprotection where indicated; monitor glucose — doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • Analgesia that works. Catheter only for confirmed acute retention, per local protocol — record pre-catheter residual, time and volume drained.
  • Fasting per local transfer instructions. Bloods (FBC, CRP/ESR, U&E, calcium, clotting) only if targeted and never delaying; not routine in disc CES.

6Monitor — after decompression

  • Bladder and bowel: self-catheterisation, bowel regimen, continence nursing — recovery can take months and may be incomplete.
  • Review function, continence, mood and sexual function at intervals; don't discharge after surgical follow-up.

7Refer — the call, and rehabilitation

  • On the call: red flags, time of onset and progression, lower-limb neurology, perineal findings, bladder symptoms and residual; record who you spoke to, destination and transport.
  • MRI contraindicated → urgent discussion with the spinal team for alternative imaging; not a reason to downgrade.
  • Afterwards: physiotherapy, OT, neuropathic pain service, sexual-function support, psychological and work support.

8Never

  • Wait for the complete syndrome or painless retention before referring.
  • Reassure on normal power, reflexes, anal tone or a low post-void residual.
  • Refer by routine e-referral or letter alone, or wait for outpatient imaging or bloods.
  • Give dexamethasone empirically for likely disc-related CES.
  • Prescribe stronger analgesia as a substitute for referral.

9Safety-net — every patient with sciatica; say it, then document it

999 / A&E “Numbness between your legs or around the back passage, difficulty passing urine, loss of bladder or bowel control, new sexual problems, or weakness in both legs — go to A&E straight away, even tonight, or call 999 if you can't travel safely. Don't wait to ring us.”   Same-day “New or worsening leg weakness, a foot that drags, rapidly worsening pain in both legs, or fever with back pain — contact us the same day.”   No red flags: routine review, not A&E advice.
