Plantar heel pain (plantar fasciitis) in adults

For: adults with typical, non-traumatic plantar heel pain and no red flags. Not for: acute traumatic heel pain, diabetic foot disease (diabetic foot pathway, NG19), inflammatory arthritis, infection, suspected fracture or tumour. See also the foot pain pathway and Achilles tendinopathy.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
JOSPT/APTA heel pain CPG 2023NICE HTG200/IPG311NICE HTG298NICE NG19BNF
Core rule: diagnose clinically, reconsider anything atypical, neurological, traumatic or systemic, and treat with education, load modification, stretching and strengthening — review adherence and the diagnosis before any injection, imaging or referral.

0Act now — red flags reconsider the diagnosis, at any stage

  • Suddenly cold, pale or pulseless foot → 999
  • Inability to weight-bear after trauma, suspected fracture or acute tendon rupture (sudden onset, bruising, palpable defect), neurovascular compromise → Same-day assessment
  • Hot, swollen foot with systemic illness, severe infection, infection or ulceration in diabetes, rapidly progressive swelling or severe unexplained pain → Same-day assessment
  • Diabetes with ulcer, infection, neuropathy or ischaemia → diabetic foot pathway (NG19) and local diabetic foot service, not routine plantar fasciopathy.
  • Focal calcaneal squeeze tenderness, progressive or constant pain, persistent night/rest pain, known malignancy, unexplained weight loss or mass → Urgent assessment and imaging

1Confirm — a clinical diagnosis; imaging not routine

Plantar fasciopathyFat-pad syndrome
HistoryWorst on first steps after rest; eases, then worse with long standingWorse with prolonged standing or hard surfaces
FindingsMedial calcaneal tubercle tenderness; pain on passive toe dorsiflexion; normal neurovascular exam. No single finding diagnosticCentral heel-pad tenderness

Nerve causes (tarsal tunnel, S1 radiculopathy): burning, tingling or numbness → Tinel's, sensation, reflexes, power and the back.

Imaging only if atypical: trauma or bony tenderness → X-ray; stress fracture concern with a normal X-ray → MRI or specialist; ultrasound or MRI for diagnostic doubt.

2Check — examine properly

☐ Gait, weight-bearing, exact site  ☐ Calf/Achilles flexibility, ankle and subtalar range  ☐ Foot posture, footwear  ☐ Neurovascular; swelling, warmth, mass  ☐ Sudden onset in sport → calf squeeze, Achilles palpation, defect

Don't miss: enthesitis — inflammatory back pain, long morning stiffness, psoriasis, uveitis, IBD, family history (bilateral pain alone is not enough).

3Decide — first line for everyone

  • Progressive, tolerable plantar-fascia/calf stretching and calf and foot strengthening; progress by symptoms and goals, not stopped for any pain.
  • Load: tolerable activity, not complete rest; no sudden jumps in running or standing; alternate sitting and standing at work.
  • Footwear: supportive shoes; optional low-cost heel cup for comfort. Custom orthoses not routine at first.
  • Ice or rolling = symptom relief only. Weight discussed sensitively.

5Treat — if persistent, escalate in order

Step 1 · reviewReassess diagnosis, adherence, footwear, loading and exercise delivery
Step 2 · physio/podiatry/MSKStructured programme, gait, taping, orthoses
Step 3 · night splintPersistent first-step pain after an adequate trial; caution with neuropathy or skin risk
Step 4 · selectiveSteroid injection; ESWT only under HTG200 special arrangements
  • Analgesia adjunctive only: consider a short topical or oral NSAID if pain limits activity; caution in CKD, heart failure/CVD, GI bleed, anticoagulant, antiplatelet, steroid, NSAID-sensitive asthma, frailty. Doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • Steroid injection: short-term relief only; discuss infection, depigmentation, fat-pad atrophy, fascia rupture, hyperglycaemia in diabetes. Experienced clinician; not intrafascial; record dates.

6Monitor

  • Course is variable: often improves gradually over weeks to months; may be uneven or recur.
  • A time point alone never triggers injection or imaging. Persistent cases: record first-step pain (0–10), walking tolerance, work or sport limitation and one goal — not tenderness.

7Refer — routine (urgent routes in block 0)

  • Physio, podiatry or MSK: failure to progress despite a proper programme, major work or sport limitation, orthoses, splint or gait assessment, recurrence.
  • Rheumatology: convincing inflammatory features, via the local inflammatory arthritis pathway.

8Never

  • Routine imaging for typical heel pain with a normal examination.
  • Steroid injection as first line, repeated regularly, or through infected or ulcerated skin.
  • Diabetic foot ulcer, infection or ischaemia managed as plantar fasciopathy.
  • A normal early X-ray taken as excluding a stress fracture.
  • Complete rest, or injection in place of stretching, strengthening and load management.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “If your foot suddenly goes cold and pale, call 999.”   Same-day “If you can't put weight on it, or it becomes red, hot or swollen, especially if you feel unwell or feverish — contact us or NHS 111 the same day.”   Urgent “Numbness or tingling, or pain that keeps going at rest or at night — contact us within a few days.”   Routine “Not improving, getting worse or stopping you working — book a review.”
