Threadworm

For: children and adults with suspected threadworm, and the household members who share their living or sleeping accommodation. Not for: perianal itch that is persistent, atypical or never improved (use the anal itch pathway) or suspected scabies.

Core rule: threadworm is a clinical diagnosis — treat the affected person and every eligible household member on the same day, repeat once at 2 weeks, pair every dose with hygiene, and re-examine rather than re-supply when symptoms never improve.

0Act now — rare, but check every time

  • Secondary infection: spreading erythema, increasing pain, pus or crusting, fever, significant skin breakdown, urinary symptoms or marked vulval inflammation → Same-day review No empirical antibiotics without clinical evidence of bacterial infection.
  • Persistent, atypical, painful or vulval symptoms, or never improved → examine and reconsider the diagnosis (block 2) rather than supplying more mebendazole.
  • Safeguarding: threadworm does not indicate neglect → follow local safeguarding procedures only when other history or examination findings raise a specific concern.

1Confirm — a clinical diagnosis

TypicalPerianal itch worse at night (vulval itch in girls) ± small, white, thread-like worms around the anus, perineum or in stool
Only if uncertainEarly-morning adhesive-tape test before washing or opening the bowels; several mornings if negative and suspicion stays high
Not usefulStool microscopy for uncomplicated threadworm

2Check — for each person you treat

☐ Age (<6 months, 6 months–2 years)  ☐ Pregnancy, breastfeeding  ☐ Comorbidity, contraindications  ☐ Excoriation, dermatitis, secondary infection

Don’t miss (persistent symptoms): perianal streptococcal dermatitis in children (marked erythema, pain, fissuring, blood-streaked stool); in girls irritant dermatitis, eczema, candidiasis, lichen sclerosus, foreign body, UTI; IBD; scabies.

3Decide — who to treat

  • Affected person + eligible household members (sharing living or sleeping accommodation), with or without symptoms → treat at the same time.
  • Not school, nursery or workplace contacts, or visitors. Contacts unsuitable for treatment → hygiene.
  • Usually self-care / over the counter. In GM, NHS prescribing is generally not supported for uncomplicated threadworm — follow local policy, document any exception.

4Hygiene — the tablet does not reliably kill eggs

At least 2 weeks alongside mebendazole; 6 weeks where medication is not used. Hands and nails scrubbed after the toilet or nappies and before eating; nails short; wash around the anus each morning; no nail-biting or scratching. Wash nightwear, underwear, towels and bedding on day 1 and regularly — no need to hot-wash everything daily.

5Treat — mebendazole 100 mg, repeat once at 2 weeks

Aged 2 years +Mebendazole 100 mg single dose (chewable tablet or 5 mL suspension); repeat once after 2 weeks if still eligible.
6 months – 2 yearsSame dose, off-label: individual risk–benefit decision, not automatic; hygiene and treating contacts may suffice if mild.
Under 6 monthsAvoid mebendazole. Hygiene alone for 6 weeks, with frequent perianal washing or wet wiping by day. Significant, persistent or atypical → paediatric or medicines advice.
PregnancyHygiene first for 6 weeks (especially first 12 weeks). Severe, persistent or sleep-disturbing → individual advice (GP, pharmacist, midwife, medicines-in-pregnancy service).

Breastfeeding: mebendazole can generally be used when clinically appropriate; no need to interrupt breastfeeding. Doses per BNF/BNFC and local formulary.

6Recurring or not settling — re-check before re-treating

  • Check: taken correctly, second dose at 2 weeks? Whole eligible household treated together? Hygiene practical and followed?
  • Never improved → examine, reconsider the diagnosis. Reinfection likely, diagnosis secure → a further simultaneous household course may be considered per local advice, with reinforced hygiene.

7Seek advice or refer — rarely needed

  • Persistent despite correct household treatment and hygiene; uncertain diagnosis; recurrent significant vulvovaginitis; very young infant; pregnancy with severe symptoms.
  • Significant or persistent eosinophilia, abdominal symptoms, weight loss, pulmonary symptoms or relevant travel/migration → broader assessment, often infectious-disease or tropical-medicine advice.

8Never

  • Treat one person alone — treat eligible household members on the same day.
  • Give mebendazole under 6 months.
  • Keep re-supplying mebendazole when symptoms never improved or the diagnosis is uncertain.
  • Routinely exclude from school or nursery, or treat school or nursery contacts.
  • Suggest threadworm means neglect or poor hygiene.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same day “If the skin becomes red and spreading, very sore, weepy or crusted, or there's a fever, contact us the same day.”   Everyone: “Everyone in the house who can take the tablet takes it on the same day, with a second dose two weeks later.”   Hygiene: “Wash hands, scrub under short nails, wash around the bottom each morning; wash underwear and bedding on day one.”   School: “Your child can keep going to school. If the itching hasn't settled after the second dose, come back to see us.”
