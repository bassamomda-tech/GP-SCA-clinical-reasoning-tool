Osgood-Schlatter disease

For: active adolescents (commonly 10–15 years, around a growth spurt) with activity-related pain at the tibial tubercle. Not for: a hot or swollen joint, acute trauma, a limp with hip signs, or night or rest pain — these are not OSD; act on block 0. Bolton and Greater Manchester paediatric pathways take precedence.

Core rule: it is a clinical diagnosis only once the hip has been examined and the red flags are absent — night or rest pain, a limp or systemic features are not “atypical OSD”.

0Act now — these send the patient down a different pathway

  • Hot, swollen or acutely painful joint, especially with fever or systemic illness (septic arthritis, osteomyelitis) → Same-day paediatric/orthopaedic assessment; early X-ray may be normal.
  • Cannot weight-bear from severe pain, suspected tubercle avulsion, or loss of active knee extension → Same-day emergency assessment.
  • Limp, hip/groin or referred knee pain, painful or restricted hip, externally rotated limb, loss of internal rotation = suspected SUFE → Same-day.
  • Night or rest pain, progressive pain, persistent unexplained limp, enlarging mass, bony tenderness away from the tubercle, unexplained fever, weight loss, pallor, bruising, significant effusion, locking or instability → ≤48 h assessment and imaging or specialist discussion.
  • NICE NG12: unexplained bone pain or swelling → consider very urgent direct-access X-ray ≤48 h; persistent or unexplained bone pain, or pallor, unexplained bruising or fever, persistent infections → offer very urgent FBC ≤48 h.

1Confirm — clinically, when typical and the examination is reassuring

Typical: well adolescent doing running, jumping or kicking sport; gradual, activity-related pain eased by rest; focal tibial-tubercle tenderness; pain on resisted knee extension. One or both knees.

2Check — document every time

☐ Temperature, appearance  ☐ Gait, weight-bearing  ☐ Knee movement, effusion, warmth, straight-leg raise  ☐ Hip flexion, abduction, rotation vs other side

Imaging: none if typical. X-ray or advice if atypical, severe, traumatic, progressive, night/rest pain, limp, mass, persisting despite a trial, or after skeletal maturity. A normal X-ray does not override concerning features.

3Decide

  • Typical, normal hip, no red flags → clinical diagnosis; conservative care; no routine imaging.
  • Activity modification, not total rest; stopping all sport is not usually needed.
  • Physiotherapy if restricting school or sport, not progressing alone, strength or control deficits, recurrent pain, or a structured return to sport is needed.
  • Return to sport (pragmatic): no limp on walking or stairs, near-full knee movement, squat, jog and hop with mild symptoms, no worse next morning → build up gradually.

5Treat — load management first

Step 1 · modifyReduce jumping, sprinting, kicking, kneeling; keep lower-impact activity; ice after activity, kneeling protection
Step 2 · rehabQuadriceps and hamstring flexibility; quadriceps, hip, trunk strength; movement control. Written plan, home programme
Step 3 · physiotherapyNot progressing, restricted function or recurrent pain despite modification
Step 4 · specialistPersisting despite appropriate management (block 7)
  • Analgesia: paracetamol and/or ibuprofen, shortest effective duration, for comfort — not to enable painful sport. Doses per BNFc and local formulary.
  • Before ibuprofen: NSAID reaction or NSAID-sensitive asthma, dehydration or vomiting/diarrhoea, renal disease, ulcer or GI bleed, anticoagulants, another NSAID. Gastroprotection not routine.

6Monitor

  • Expected: fluctuates with activity, often for several months; most improve by skeletal maturity; the bump may remain.
  • Stop sport if pain causes limping, marked pain during activity, or worse symptoms that evening or next morning.
  • Not improving after a reasonable trial of load modification and rehab → review; earlier if worsening, function deteriorating or diagnosis uncertain.

7Refer — routine, local pathway

  • Paediatric MSK/orthopaedics: persisting despite appropriate management, especially beyond about 18 months (pragmatic; never wait with red flags); marked functional restriction; diagnostic uncertainty; symptomatic ossicle; symptoms after skeletal maturity.

8Never

  • Diagnose OSD in a knee without examining the hip.
  • Call night or rest pain, a limp or systemic features “atypical OSD”.
  • Be reassured by a normal X-ray when infection, tumour or stress injury remains possible.
  • Give a corticosteroid injection around the patellar tendon or tibial tubercle.
  • Routinely prescribe prolonged NSAIDs, or analgesia to keep playing through significant pain.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “Fever, a hot or very swollen knee, severe pain, not able to walk or put weight on the leg, a limp, or pain in the hip or groin — get medical help the same day.”   ≤48 h “Pain at night or at rest, a new lump or swelling, or worsening symptoms — book an appointment within 48 hours.”   Expected: “It usually settles as growth finishes, though the bump may stay. Come back if it isn’t settling.”
