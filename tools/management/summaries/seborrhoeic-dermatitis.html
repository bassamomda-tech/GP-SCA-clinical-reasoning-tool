Seborrhoeic dermatitis

For: adults and infants (cradle cap, napkin area) in primary care. Not for: erythroderma, an unwell baby, psoriasis, rosacea or tinea (use those pathways); systemic treatment is specialist-only.
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
PCDS seborrhoeic eczema (2024)BHIVA/BASHH/BIA HIV testing (2020)MHRA DSU Sep 2021GMMMG formularyBNF / BNFC
Core rule: ketoconazole is the backbone and maintenance is the treatment; steroids stay mild and short, never continuous on the face; offer an HIV test when disease is severe, extensive, atypical or refractory.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Erythroderma (red and scaling over most of the body) that is acute, progressive, or with fever, dehydration, hypothermia, tachycardia, haemodynamic instability or a suspected drug reaction → Same-day assessment. Stable → Discuss with dermatology same day
  • Baby with a scaly rash who is unwell or failing to thrive, a petechial rash, or suspected sepsis → Same-day paediatrics. Persistent rash with diarrhoea, recurrent infection or severe itch → Urgent paediatrics
  • Eye pain, photophobia, reduced vision or significant ocular inflammation → Same-day ophthalmology — lid hygiene alone is not enough.
  • Widespread weeping, crusting or punched-out erosions (eczema herpeticum-like) → Same day
  • Positive HIV test → Urgent specialist HIV service. Suspected lupus or connective tissue disease with a facial rash → Urgent referral

1Confirm — clinical; no test confirms it

Greasy yellow scale on a red base: scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, ears, beard, central chest, flexures.

2Check — every new presentation

☐ Psoriasis elsewhere  ☐ Severity, extent  ☐ HIV indicator features  ☐ Parkinson's (tremor, masked face)  ☐ Drugs, alcohol  ☐ Eyes  ☐ Steroid use

Don't miss: a fixed patch that does not respond — a lesion.

3Decide — who needs what

  • Severe, extensive, atypical or refractory adult disease, or other indicator features (unusual infections, oral candida, weight loss, nodes, shingles) → offer an HIV test, routinely, with consent and results follow-up. Not for new onset alone.
  • Mild dandruff, no inflammation → over-the-counter shampoo; prescribe ketoconazole if inflamed or self-care fails (GMMMG).
  • Everyone: control, not cure — say so at the start; emollient soap substitute.

4Target — review points

WhenGoal
4 weeksImproved; if not, adherence, rethink diagnosis
Once clearIntermittent antifungal; steroid for flares only

5Treat — by site; doses per BNF / BNFC and local formulary

ScalpKetoconazole 2% shampoo, leave on per product information, avoid eyes: twice weekly for 2–4 weeks. Thick crust → descaling product first
Face and bodyKetoconazole 2% cream once or twice daily, usually 2–4 weeks; shampoo can be used as a body wash
FlareHydrocortisone 1% (± imidazole) for a few days to a week or two; itchy scalp → short steroid scalp application
Frequent facial flaresConsider a topical calcineurin inhibitor (off-label), especially eyelids; or dermatology advice
  • Infants: emollient or mineral oil, brush gently, wash out. Napkin: barrier cream; persistent → imidazole (+ hydrocortisone 1% a few days if inflamed). Clears in a few months.
  • Salicylic acid: not on large areas, broken skin or young infants. Calcineurin inhibitor: intact skin, no active infection, sun protection.

6Monitor — it will come back

  • Maintenance, planned before the flare clears: shampoo once every 1–2 weeks; cream at the first redness; restart twice weekly on relapse.
  • Steroid withdrawal (rare; burning, redness beyond treated areas, worse after stopping) → dermatology advice, don't escalate.
  • Blepharitis: warm compresses and lid hygiene; recurrent corneal disease → ophthalmology referral.

7Refer — routine dermatology

  • Diagnostic doubt; persistent disease or frequent flares despite treatment and maintenance; recurrent eyelid disease; significant psychological impact.
  • Systemic treatment (itraconazole, oral tetracyclines) is specialist-only.

8Never

  • A continuous or potent steroid on the face, or a steroid as the maintenance plan.
  • A steroid (or steroid–antifungal combination) on possible tinea without rethinking the diagnosis — tinea incognito.
  • Topical steroids when rosacea is suspected.
  • Topical treatment alone for suspected tinea capitis.
  • Labelling an unwell or failing-to-thrive baby with a scaly rash as cradle cap.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same day “Contact us the same day if the redness spreads over most of your body or you feel unwell, if painful weeping or punched-out sores appear, or if your eyes become painful or sensitive to light or your vision changes.”   Treatment: “We can't cure it but can keep it quiet: shampoo twice a week to clear it, then every week or two. The steroid cream is only for a few days when it's angry.”   Babies: “Contact us the same day if your baby seems unwell, is unsettled or feeding poorly, or has spots in the rash that do not fade when pressed.”
