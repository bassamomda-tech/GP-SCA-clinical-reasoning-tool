Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC)

For: adults with known or suspected cancer and new spinal pain or neurological symptoms, in primary care. Not for: a neurological emergency without known cancer (think cauda equina syndrome, epidural abscess or haematoma, fracture) or back pain without cancer (sciatica).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG234NICE QS56GMEC Palliative Care Guidelines (6th ed, 2025)PCF8NICE NG12 (myeloma)
Core rule: cancer + new neurology = suspect, steroid, scan in the same consultation; a normal neurological examination does NOT exclude spinal metastases.

0Act now — oncological emergency; do not wait for the scan

  • Known or suspected cancer + gait disturbance, limb weakness, sensory loss, paraesthesia or a sensory level, radicular pain, or bladder/bowel dysfunction → dexamethasone 16 mg now + gastroprotection → Immediately MSCC coordinator (or oncology out-of-hours) → whole-spine MRI Within 24 hours.
  • Unstable, rapidly deteriorating or unsafe to transfer → 999 emergency ambulance/admission; immobilise during transfer if spinal instability suspected — tell the ambulance crew.
  • Cancer + spinal pain alone (severe, progressive or unremitting, night pain, band-like or radicular, worse on cough/strain, localised tenderness) → Within 24 hours MSCC-team advice → whole-spine MRI Within a week.
  • Neurological emergency without known cancer → Same-day emergency spinal assessment and imaging; suspected cauda equina → immediate emergency referral.

1Confirm — separate the two pathways

Suspected MSCCSuspected spinal metastases
FeaturesNeurological symptoms or signsConcerning spinal pain, no neurology
SteroidDexamethasone 16 mg on suspicionNot routine (unless severe pain or haematological malignancy)

Thoracic back pain in a patient with cancer = suspected spinal metastases until assessed. Commonest primaries: prostate, breast, lung, myeloma. MSCC can be the first presentation of cancer.

2Check — and write the baseline down

☐ Ask: gait, weakness, sensory change, band-like/radicular or night pain, bladder and bowel  ☐ Power, reflexes (absent OR brisk), plantars, clonus, sensory level  ☐ Palpate for retention  ☐ Spinal tenderness  ☐ Ambulant status

Don't miss: bladder and bowel symptoms are late — waiting for them is waiting too long.

3Decide

  • Neurology present → steroid on clinical suspicion, before MRI.
  • Suspected lymphoma, no neurology → discuss urgently with haematology/MSCC team before steroids, if this will not delay emergency care (biopsy yield). Neurological deterioration → give dexamethasone now.
  • Dying → admission and radiotherapy may be inappropriate; seek specialist palliative care advice, focus on symptom control.

5Treat — dexamethasone, the one treatment you start

Load, on suspicion16 mg orally as soon as possible (or equivalent parenterally; GMEC: 13.2 mg SC)
MaintainNICE: 16 mg daily while awaiting surgery or radiotherapy (GMEC/PCF8: 8 mg BD, morning and lunchtime)
MSCC excluded on MRIStop dexamethasone
After surgery / start of RTReduce gradually until stopped per the treating team’s plan
  • Gastroprotection unless contraindicated; no evening doses. Doses per BNF and local formulary.
  • VTE: individual risk assessment — do not anticoagulate automatically.
  • Definitive treatment is specialist-led: radiotherapy for many, surgery for selected patients.

6Monitor

  • Blood glucose — more often in diabetes; mood, sleep, agitation.
  • Neurology worse during dose reduction → return to the last effective dose (GMEC).
  • New or progressing neurology after treatment → Urgent re-imaging and oncology review.
  • Pressure care, continence, bowel regimen, physiotherapy/OT; prognosis and advance care planning.

7Refer — what the referral needs

  • Tell the coordinator: primary and stage, neurological baseline, onset and rate of progression, ambulant status, steroid dose and time, performance status.
  • Unknown primary, aged 60+ with persistent bone/back pain or unexplained fracture → FBC, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains — in parallel, never delaying the steroid.

8Never

  • Reassure with a normal neurological examination.
  • Wait for the scan before giving dexamethasone when there is neurology.
  • Request a targeted film or routine/outpatient MRI — whole spine, on the acute pathway.
  • Absorb new back pain into a cancer patient’s existing pain without asking the neuro and bladder questions.
  • Wait for bladder or bowel symptoms before acting.

9Safety-net — every at-risk patient (prostate, breast, lung, myeloma, known bone metastases); record it

Immediately “If you get new or worsening back pain, weakness or numbness in your legs, or any change in your bladder or bowels — contact us or the oncology team immediately, or go straight to A&E if you cannot reach anyone. Do not wait for an appointment.”   Steroid: “Take it in the morning and at lunchtime, never in the evening, with the stomach protector. Tell us if you feel agitated or your sugars run high.”

✓Safe consultation check — all five done, case safely managed
