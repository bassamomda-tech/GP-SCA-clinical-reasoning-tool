Care of the dying adult — last days of life

For: adults who may be in the last days of life at home or in a care home. Not for: children, or detailed symptom control (use the palliative pain, breathlessness, nausea and vomiting, delirium and agitation, and respiratory secretions protocols).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG31QS144NG142CG140One Chance to Get it Right
Core rule: dying is a working diagnosis — exclude reversible causes, say it plainly, get individualised anticipatory medicines into the home, and reassess at least every 24 hours.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Drowsiness, myoclonus, hallucinations or agitation on morphine, especially with renal impairment or AKI → opioid toxicity until proven otherwise; Same-day specialist palliative-care advice before increasing opioids or adding sedatives.
  • Restless or agitated → examine first: full bladder, faecal impaction, pain, opioid toxicity. Never sedate a problem you have not examined.
  • Pain, vomiting, breathlessness, restlessness or distressing secretions → PRN SC anticipatory medicine via District nurse / OOH now, day or night
  • Recurrent ICD shocks near death → 999 / device specialists and follow the local protocol; a magnet only by trained clinicians per local policy.
  • Death unexpected, traumatic, suspicious or unexplained → 999 or police. A clearly expected natural death with an agreed plan → district nurse, practice or OOH, not 999.

1Recognise — a pattern over time, never one sign

SignsAgitation, Cheyne–Stokes breathing, falling consciousness, mottled skin, noisy secretions, progressive weight loss
SymptomsIncreasing fatigue, loss of appetite
FunctionCommunication change, declining mobility, withdrawal

Improvement may mean recovery → reassess at least every 24 h. Uncertain → ask an experienced colleague (e.g. specialist palliative care). Document it with a review date.

2Check — reversible causes, only if consistent with goals

☐ Opioid toxicity ☐ Hypercalcaemia (if treatment would be accepted) ☐ Infection ☐ Glucose if on insulin or sulfonylurea ☐ Retention, constipation ☐ Dehydration / AKI

Tests only if they would change care.

3Communicate & plan

  • Tell the person (if they wish) and family that death is likely within days — and that this is uncertain.
  • Named lead clinician (usually the GP); care plan: place of death, faith, who to call.
  • Consider DNACPR and ReSPECT by circumstances and wishes — not mandatory; DNACPR covers CPR only.
  • Lacks capacity → best interests; family are consulted, not decision-makers, unless they hold a relevant LPA.
  • Tell OOH and district nurses the same day (e.g. EPaCCS).

4Hydration & comfort

  • Assess hydration daily; support drinking if wanted; frequent mouth and lip care.
  • Assisted hydration: discuss honestly — may help or harm; uncertain if it prolongs life. Trial → monitor at least every 12 h. Oxygen only for symptomatic hypoxaemia.

5Medicines — review each one, then prescribe ahead

  • Stop what no longer helps (statins, bisphosphonates, vitamins, after discussion). Do not stop automatically: anticoagulants, antihypertensives, insulin (type 1 / insulin-deficient), opioids, steroids, anticonvulsants, Parkinson's drugs — convert essential ones to SC. ICD: discuss shock deactivation early.

Prescribe ahead (SC PRN, e.g.): pain or breathlessness → opioid (morphine if opioid-naïve with adequate renal function; ± midazolam for breathlessness) · anxiety, agitation → midazolam · delirium → haloperidol or levomepromazine · nausea → antiemetic by cause · secretions → glycopyrronium or hyoscine butylbromide.

  • Individualise — no bundle. Indication, dose, route, maximum frequency; community chart authorised; dispensed and in the home; OOH told.
  • Already on opioids → PRN from the current 24-hour dose. Renal impairment or Parkinson's → seek specialist advice. Doses: NICE/SPC, BNF, local formulary.
  • Syringe pump: consider if >2–3 PRN doses of any one medicine in 24 h, after reviewing the cause. Check compatibility; keep a PRN dose.

6Monitor

  • Review at least daily with the district nurse; titrate by PRN use.
  • Antimuscarinic: check preferably every 4 h, at least every 12 h; still distressing after 12 h → change or stop.

7Refer & after death

  • Specialist palliative care early for refractory or escalating symptoms.
  • After death: verification by a doctor or competent registered nurse (≥5 min observation: no pulse, heart or breath sounds, fixed pupils); MCCD by a doctor who attended the person in their lifetime → medical examiner office next working day. Offer bereavement support.
  • Notify the coroner if possibly unnatural (fall, injury, neglect), cause unknown or no doctor can certify — "expected" does not exempt.

8Never

  • A generic anticipatory bundle, or medicines prescribed but not in the house before the weekend.
  • Sedation for agitation you have not examined.
  • An opioid-naïve PRN dose for someone on regular opioids, or more morphine in renal impairment without specialist advice.
  • Stopping anticoagulants, type 1 insulin, opioids, steroids, anticonvulsants or Parkinson's drugs automatically.
  • "Fluids are not given at the end of life" as a rule — assess daily and discuss.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

What to expect: “He'll sleep more; breathing may pause or rattle, and hands and feet may go cold and mottled — this is common.”   Now, day or night “Pain, sickness, breathlessness, restlessness, or he seems distressed — ring the district nurse or out-of-hours now; the medicines are in the house.”   Death: “When he dies you don't need 999 — ring the nurses or out-of-hours doctor.” 999 “If he dies unexpectedly or after a fall or injury, ring 999.”
