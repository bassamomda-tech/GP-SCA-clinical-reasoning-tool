Delirium and agitation in palliative care

For: adults with advanced illness at home or in a care home, from months before death to the last hours. Not for: the whole last-days plan (use care of the dying) or planned alcohol detoxification (use the alcohol protocol). CG103 is applied proportionately, as an adaptation for community care.

NICE CG103 (updated 2023)NG31NG97 · NG71CG100MHRA DSU Dec 2021
Core rule: look for the cause before you sedate — check bladder, bowel, pain and opioid toxicity first, give non-drug care to everyone, use an antipsychotic only for distress or danger, and never a benzodiazepine alone for ordinary delirium.

0Act now — hospital only where active treatment fits the person’s wishes

  • Suspected sepsis → acute medicine or SDEC → 999 / Same-day
  • Hypercalcaemia, other cause needing hospital, suspected cord compression, stroke, seizures → Same-day admission
  • Delirium tremens, withdrawal seizures or high-risk alcohol withdrawal → Same-day hospital assessment, not home detox.
  • Immediate risk to self or carer that cannot be settled → 999
  • Significant opioid toxicity, respiratory depression or uncontrolled pain → Same-day specialist advice
  • Parkinson’s or Lewy body dementia → avoid dopamine antagonists; check last dose → Same-day specialist advice

1Confirm — ask the carer “is this new?”

Acute or fluctuating change in attention, awareness, cognition or behaviour → 4AT (an aid, not a diagnosis).

4AT ≥4 → possible delirium; needs clinical assessment. 1–3 → possible cognitive impairment. Item 4 (acute change) needs the carer’s history.

Hypoactive (drowsy, withdrawn) is the one missed. Unsure if delirium or dementia → treat as delirium.

2Check — proportionate; what would change management

☐ Drug changes in 2 weeks ☐ Alcohol, smoking ☐ Pupils, myoclonus ☐ Obs, SpO₂ ☐ Bladder, abdomen ☐ Glucose ☐ Pain. Bloods (U&E, calcium, FBC, CRP) and PR only if they would change care.

3Decide — set the goals with the person and family

  • Weeks to months → treat causes that match their wishes.
  • Last days → comfort first; still exclude bladder, bowel, pain, opioids.
  • Capacity is decision-specific; delirium is not incapacity. Else best interests.
  • Non-drug care for everyone: familiar people, light by day, dim at night, glasses, hearing aids. De-escalate; avoid restraint.

4Target — the reversible causes

Opioid toxicity, falling eGFRReview with palliative team
HypercalcaemiaCalcium same day if treatment wanted
Retention, impactionBladder scan or catheter if proportionate
Anticholinergics, steroidsStop or switch; steroids in the morning
Withdrawal (nicotine, benzodiazepines, Parkinson’s drugs)Patch; another route or taper

Also infection, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, sodium, brain tumour, pain.

5Treat — choose by the problem; doses per BNF and local palliative formulary

Step 1 · alwaysTreat the cause; non-drug care. Many need no drug.
Step 2 · distress or dangerDe-escalation fails → consider haloperidol, usually ≤1 week, lowest dose; oral, or SC if not swallowing.
Step 3 · specialist-guidedHaloperidol unsuitable → levomepromazine per local guideline.
Step 4 · benzodiazepineOnly for withdrawal, seizures, severe anxiety or last-days comfort. Midazolam SC.
  • Before haloperidol: Parkinson’s, Lewy body, QT risk, frailty, renal or hepatic impairment; baseline ECG and electrolytes where feasible.
  • Last days: consider PRN benzodiazepine and/or antipsychotic, with indication, dose, route, maximum frequency.
  • >2–3 PRN doses in 24 h → review the cause, then consider a syringe pump.

6Monitor

  • At least every 24 h: distress, pain, bladder, bowels, sedation, breathing, side effects, PRN use, carers.
  • Antipsychotic: daily; stop when settled (usually within a week).
  • Dying is a working diagnosis — recheck causes if things change.
  • After recovery: explain; update anticipatory drugs and care plan.

7Refer

  • Uncertain diagnosis, no response, unwanted sedation, continuous sedation considered → Same-day palliative advice
  • Carers exhausted or unsafe → Same or next day hospice at home, respite.
  • Hospice bed: refractory or complex delirium; care not possible at home.
  • Recurrent delirium → palliative team; advance care planning.

8Never

  • Sedation by phone without checking retention, impaction, pain, opioid toxicity.
  • A benzodiazepine alone for ordinary delirium, or a fixed benzodiazepine–antipsychotic combination.
  • Haloperidol in Parkinson’s or Lewy body dementia, or stopping Parkinson’s drugs abruptly.
  • An antipsychotic just for confusion or hard-to-care-for behaviour, or left running.
  • Calling quiet delirium “dying” or depression without a cause hunt.

9Safety-net — say it to the family, then document it

999 “If anyone is in immediate danger and he cannot be settled, call 999.”   Explain: “This is delirium. It comes and goes, often worse at night, and is caused by the illness or medicines, not by anything you have done.”   What helps: “Stay nearby, speak calmly, keep the room quietly lit, and don't argue about things he sees.”   24/7: “If he becomes frightened, violent, or you cannot manage, ring this number at any time.” Give district nurse, out-of-hours, 111 and palliative advice numbers; plan on the shared record.
