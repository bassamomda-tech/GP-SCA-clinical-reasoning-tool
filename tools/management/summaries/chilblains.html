Chilblains (perniosis)

For: people with cold-triggered red-purple acral lesions in primary care. Not for: frostbite (emergency), Raynaud’s phenomenon (use the Raynaud’s protocol), established SLE (rheumatology), or a solitary suspicious lesion (NICE NG12 melanoma pathway).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
PCDS perniosis guidanceBSR SLE 2026NICE CG147NICE NG12Nifedipine SPC · BNF
Core rule: typical chilblains need warmth, not tests or drugs — but feel the pulses, and never file a solitary, persistent, changing or periungual lesion as “just chilblains”.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Acute limb ischaemia: sudden painful, cold, pale or pulseless digit or limb, numbness or weakness → 999 immediate emergency vascular assessment; do not wait for ABPI.
  • Suspected frostbite (hard, white, numb tissue after sub-zero exposure) → Emergency department — do not rub or apply direct heat; avoid refreezing.
  • Rapidly progressive necrosis → Immediate emergency vascular. Rest pain, non-healing ulcer, gangrene or tissue loss with reduced pulses (chronic limb-threatening ischaemia) → Urgent vascular assessment
  • Systemic illness with suspected vasculitis or infection, or rapidly spreading infection around an ulcerated lesion → Same-day assessment
  • Solitary acral lesion that is changing, bleeding, nodular or involves the nail unit → Suspected cancer pathway if NICE NG12 melanoma criteria are met; otherwise advice-and-guidance or urgent dermatology, with safety-netting.

1Confirm — a clinical diagnosis: season, site, timing

FeatureTypical chilblain
TriggerCold and damp, above freezing (UK autumn and winter)
TimingLesions appear hours after exposure; each crop fades over 1–3 weeks; clear in spring
LookItchy, then tender red-purple swellings, often symmetrical; toes, fingers, heels, ears, nose. Constant rest pain → think ischaemia

Not a chilblain: colour change during cold, gone within minutes to hours (Raynaud’s); tissue that froze (frostbite); red, hot feet worse with warmth (erythromelalgia — check FBC).

2Check — every patient

☐ Femoral, popliteal and foot pulses, skin temperature, capillary refill  ☐ Every digit, heels, ears, nail folds  ☐ Face, scalp, mouth and joints for lupus  ☐ Any solitary lesion (dermoscopy if trained)  ☐ Smoking, weight and eating, vasoconstricting medicines, heating at home

Typical seasonal lesions in a well person with normal pulses and no ulceration → no routine blood tests.

3Decide — test the atypical, guided by the picture

  • Persistent, ulcerating, out-of-season or systemic → look for a cause. Lupus (arthralgia, photosensitive rash, mouth ulcers) → FBC, renal function, urinalysis, ANA and ENA, anti-dsDNA where appropriate, C3/C4, ESR or CRP.
  • Vasculitis or cryoprotein disease (purpura, livedo, ulceration) → add film, rheumatoid factor, immunoglobulins and electrophoresis, hepatitis B/C and HIV where indicated; cryoglobulins and cold agglutinins with the lab (temperature-controlled sample).
  • Reduced pulses → manual Doppler ABPI; normal or raised ABPI does not exclude disease in diabetes.

5Treat — warmth is the main treatment

Step 1 · everyoneWhole-body warmth, dry socks, gloves, roomy shoes, warm home; no caffeine or decongestants; stop smoking
Step 2 · symptomsEmollient, analgesia; short low-potency topical steroid for itch on intact skin only
Step 3 · off-labelConsider nifedipine only if severe, recurrent or disabling despite warmth; trials conflict; specify formulation
Step 4 · specialistRefractory, doubtful or chilblain lupus; hydroxychloroquine specialist-led
  • Before nifedipine: BP (often low-normal); aortic stenosis, unstable angina, recent MI, liver disease; CYP3A4 drugs, grapefruit; pregnancy or breastfeeding → exact SPC and UKTIS/BNF.
  • Broken skin clean and covered; infection assessed separately. Ask about heating costs and eating. Doses: NICE/SPC.

6Monitor — plan for next winter too

  • 2–3 weeks: not settling, ulcerating or systemic → investigate; unchanged solitary lesion → re-examine (NG12).
  • Spring: still present → look for a secondary cause; consider dermatology.
  • Nifedipine: hypotension, flushing, headache, oedema; ineffective → rethink diagnosis. Autumn: warmth before the cold.

7Refer — local Greater Manchester pathways

  • Rheumatology: positive ANA/ENA with features, low complement, cytopenias, proteinuria, synovitis, Raynaud’s with digital ulcers.
  • Haematology: abnormal film, paraprotein, positive cryoglobulins or cold agglutinins.
  • Dermatology: diagnostic doubt or biopsy, lesions beyond winter, skin-only chilblain lupus, severe despite nifedipine.
  • Vascular (routine): reduced pulses or abnormal ABPI without critical ischaemia.

8Never

  • Label a solitary, changing, bleeding, nodular or periungual lesion a chilblain.
  • Delay emergency transfer for an ABPI in acute limb ischaemia.
  • Rewarm on radiators, fires or hot-water bottles; rub or massage frostbite.
  • Bloods by reflex for typical chilblains — or none when lesions persist, ulcerate or come with lupus features.
  • Excise a suspected melanoma in primary care unless the local skin-cancer pathway supports it.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “If a toe or finger suddenly goes pale, cold, numb or very painful, call 999.”   Same-day “If a patch goes black, a sore breaks down, or there is spreading redness or a fever, contact us the same day.”   Routine “If a sore won’t heal, or they are still there when the weather warms up, come back.”   Nifedipine: “Dizziness, flushing, headache or swollen ankles — tell us.”
