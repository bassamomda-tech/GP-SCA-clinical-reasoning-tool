Coeliac disease

For: case-finding and testing at any age; diagnosis and long-term care of adults and young people aged 16 and over. Not for: confirming the diagnosis in children under 16 (refer positive serology to paediatric gastroenterology), or managing refractory coeliac disease (specialist centre).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG20UKHSA Green BookCoeliac UK
Core rule: test while the patient is eating gluten (in more than one meal a day for at least 6 weeks) with tTG-IgA plus total IgA, and never start a gluten-free diet before the diagnosis is confirmed.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Weight loss, abdominal mass, persistent unexplained abdominal pain, GI bleeding, anaemia, systemic or rapidly worsening symptoms (on or off the diet) → Urgent specialist reassessment under the relevant GI pathway (rare enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, small-bowel cancer).
  • Positive tTG → do not start a gluten-free diet; keep eating gluten until specialist confirmation.
  • Already gluten-free or reduced gluten → do not rely on a negative test; refer to gastroenterology for advice on a supervised gluten challenge.
  • Low total IgA → tTG-IgA is falsely negative; use the laboratory's IgG-based tests (IgG tTG, IgG DGP or IgG EMA).

1Confirm — who to test, and what the result means

Offer testing: persistent unexplained GI symptoms (incl. IBS in adults), prolonged fatigue, unexplained weight loss, severe or persistent mouth ulcers, unexplained iron, B12 or folate deficiency, faltering growth, type 1 diabetes or autoimmune thyroid disease at diagnosis, a first-degree relative. Unexplained iron deficiency and "IBS" most often hide it.

Consider testing: metabolic bone disorder or low bone density, unexplained neuropathy or ataxia, unexplained subfertility or recurrent miscarriage, persistently raised liver enzymes, dental enamel defects, Down or Turner syndrome.

HLA-DQ2/DQ8 is not an initial primary-care test. Suspected dermatitis herpetiformis → dermatology.

2Check — baseline bloods at diagnosis

☐ FBC  ☐ Ferritin/iron studies  ☐ B12 and folate  ☐ Calcium, albumin, ALP, vitamin D  ☐ Liver tests  ☐ TFTs where indicated

Replace deficiencies; individualise later tests.

3Decide — everyone with a confirmed diagnosis

  • Strict lifelong gluten-free diet: exclude wheat, barley and rye; oats only if tolerated and labelled gluten-free, on dietitian advice.
  • Refer every newly diagnosed patient to a dietitian: labels, cross-contamination, nutritional adequacy, adherence.
  • No drug substitute — adherence is the treatment. Signpost Coeliac UK; gluten-free prescribing varies locally.

5Treat — diet plus the easily missed extras

DietGluten-free for life with dietitian support; this resolves symptoms and reduces complications
DeficienciesIron, B12, folate, vitamin D and calcium as indicated — check and replace
BoneAdequate calcium and vitamin D; DEXA or active bone treatment by risk, not for everyone
SpleenAssess for functional hyposplenism; offer pneumococcal vaccine where indicated; consider flu, Hib, MenACWY per Green Book
  • Consider DEXA with prolonged untreated disease or severe malabsorption, fragility fracture, low BMI, delayed diagnosis, premature menopause or hypogonadism, older age, long-term steroids or other risk factors.
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis: gluten-free diet long term; dapsone is specialist-initiated and monitored only.

6Monitor — annual review

  • Annually: symptoms, weight, nutrition, adherence and accidental gluten; dietitian if symptoms or poor adherence; bone risk; vaccination status; growth in children (expect catch-up).
  • Bloods as clinically indicated — not a mandatory annual panel; check for associated autoimmune disease where indicated.
  • tTG-IgA is one part of follow-up: never judge adherence or healing on serology alone.
  • Persistent symptoms or high titres after 12 months on the diet → dietetic review, reassess the diagnosis, gastroenterology.

7Refer — routine

  • Persistent symptoms on the diet → structured reassessment (diagnosis, gluten exposure with a specialist dietitian, IBS, lactose intolerance, bacterial overgrowth, microscopic colitis, IBD). Suspected refractory disease → specialist centre.
  • Diagnostic uncertainty, especially IgA deficiency → gastroenterology.
  • First-degree relatives → offer testing; a negative test does not exclude future disease — retest if symptoms develop; no annual serology if asymptomatic.

8Never

  • Start a gluten-free diet "as a trial" before the diagnosis is confirmed.
  • Send tTG-IgA without a total IgA.
  • Accept a negative test taken while the patient was gluten-free.
  • Label persistent symptoms as refractory coeliac disease without structured reassessment.
  • Forget the hyposplenism vaccines, bone risk and relative testing because the patient feels well.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

Same-day “If you lose weight without trying, see blood in your stools or black stools, feel a lump in your tummy, or have tummy pain or symptoms that keep getting worse, contact us the same day — don't wait for your annual review.”   Before tests: “Keep eating gluten until the tests are done — a blood test and usually a camera-test biopsy — or we won't be able to confirm it.”   Once confirmed: “A strict gluten-free diet for life heals the gut. A dietitian will help, and we'll check your iron and bones and offer the vaccinations recommended because coeliac can affect how the spleen works.”
