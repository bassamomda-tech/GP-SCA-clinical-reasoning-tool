Bell's palsy in adults

For: adults aged 16 and over with acute, usually unilateral, lower motor neurone facial palsy. Not for: children and young people (use a separate paediatric pathway); central signs (use the stroke and TIA pathway); suspected Ramsay Hunt (see shingles and the local pathway).
Reviewed October 2026 · next review April 2027
NICE NG127Cochrane antivirals (2019)Greater Manchester formularyLocal Ramsay Hunt pathway
Core rule: examine fully — forehead sparing suggests a central lesion, but a weak forehead never rules out stroke; then start prednisolone within 72 hours and protect the eye.

0Act now — check first, every time

  • Acute forehead-sparing weakness, or any acute focal deficit beyond an isolated complete facial palsy (limb weakness, sensory loss, dysarthria or aphasia, visual loss, ataxia, other cranial nerves, altered consciousness, severe sudden headache), even if the forehead is weak → 999 emergency stroke pathway.
  • Eye pain, photophobia, foreign-body sensation, increasing redness, visual change, exposed cornea, reduced corneal sensation, poor Bell's phenomenon, or cannot reliably protect the eye → Same-day ophthalmology
  • Suspected Ramsay Hunt: severe or disproportionate ear pain, vesicles in or around the ear or mouth, hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo or other cranial-nerve findings (vesicles may be absent) → Same-day ENT, neurology or acute facial-palsy pathway; hospital assessment the same day if severe vertigo, hearing loss, multiple cranial nerves, disseminated zoster, immunosuppression or systemic illness.
  • Severe otitis, mastoid signs, systemic illness, rapidly progressive or bilateral palsy, significant trauma → Same-day specialist

1Confirm — a diagnosis of exclusion

Weak forehead, eye closure and lower face, no alternative cause; evolves over hours, may progress for up to 72 hours.

FindingMeaning

Forehead sparedSuggests central lesion (block 0)

2Check — examination first

☐ Exact onset  ☐ Forehead, eye closure, smile  ☐ Ear canal, drum, pinna, mastoid, mouth (vesicles)  ☐ Hearing, taste, vertigo  ☐ All cranial nerves, limbs, speech, coordination  ☐ Parotid, neck nodes

Typical case: no routine bloods or imaging. Targeted by history: Lyme serology, HbA1c, HIV test, MRI, ENT.

3Decide

  • Typical Bell's palsy → prednisolone as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours.
  • Diabetes, active infection, previous steroid-related psychiatric illness, severe hypertension, pregnancy, immunosuppression → individual risk–benefit decision, not exclusion.
  • Antiviral: not routine, never alone; discuss with ENT/neurology if severe paralysis, marked otalgia, vesicles, hearing loss, vertigo or immunosuppression.

4Target — treatment goals

Steroid started within 72 hoursCornea protected throughoutRecovery: weeks; maximum can take months

5Treat — start today

PrednisoloneGreater Manchester/local regimen, e.g. 60 mg once daily for 5 days, then reduce by 10 mg daily for 5 days; morning, with food
Eye by dayPreservative-free drops frequently (often at least every 2 hours if exposure is significant)
Eye at nightOintment plus gentle lid taping or a moisture chamber; nothing pressing on the globe
Ramsay HuntAntiviral plus corticosteroid, ideally within 72 hours, renal-dose adjusted, per local pathway
  • Counsel: insomnia, mood change, dyspepsia, hyperglycaemia; glucose monitoring in diabetes where appropriate.
  • Gastroprotection only by GI risk. Stop contact lenses; wraparound sunglasses. Doses per BNF and local formulary.

6Monitor — review points

  • About 3 weeks, no improvement → review or contact point (local safety-netting); reassess the diagnosis; consider ENT, neurology or facial-palsy service.
  • About 3 months, incomplete recovery → consider specialist facial-palsy referral.
  • From about 5 months: synkinesis, gustatory sweating or jaw-winking → consider neurological assessment.

7Refer — ENT, neurology or facial-palsy service

  • Gradual, recurrent or atypical palsy; persistent or severe facial pain; head and neck malignancy; persistent otorrhoea; immunosuppression.
  • Persistent weakness → specialist facial-palsy physiotherapy.
  • Typical uncomplicated Bell's → no routine specialist referral or imaging.

8Never

  • Rule out stroke because the forehead is involved.
  • Delay prednisolone for blood tests, imaging or specialist review.
  • An eye pad or dressing that presses on the globe.
  • A corticosteroid alone if herpes zoster oticus is suspected; an antiviral alone in Bell's.
  • Unsupervised forceful facial strengthening or electrical stimulation as routine acute treatment.

9Safety-net — say it, then document it

999 “New limb weakness, speech or language disturbance, visual loss, severe imbalance, new numbness, confusion or worsening symptoms — call 999 immediately.”   Eye: “Eye pain, light sensitivity, increasing redness or any change in vision — same-day eye assessment.”   Ear: “Severe ear pain, blisters in the ear or mouth, hearing loss, ringing or dizziness — contact us the same day.”   Recovery: “Most people recover well, though it can take months. Contact us if there is no improvement after about three weeks.”
