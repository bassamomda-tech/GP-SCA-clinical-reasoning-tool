📋 Clinical Stem — Diarrhoea
A 58-year-old man with 6 weeks of looser, more frequent stools, attributing it to a "bug", who on questioning has also lost weight and seen some blood
Trevor Hughes, 58, comes in because his bowels "still aren't right" after what he assumed was a stomach bug. For about 6 weeks he has been opening his bowels 3–4 times a day with looser stools, sometimes urgently. On gentle questioning he has lost about 5kg, noticed some blood mixed in once or twice, and feels more tired than usual. There is no foreign travel, no recent antibiotics, and no obvious unwell contacts. His father had bowel cancer in his 60s. He is otherwise well, eats a normal diet including gluten, and hoped for "something to settle it down".
This stem tests the ability to: separate acute from chronic diarrhoea and recognise that a persistent (≥4 week) change in bowel habit in a 58-year-old with weight loss, rectal bleeding, fatigue (?iron-deficiency anaemia) and a family history is a colorectal-cancer red-flag presentation requiring FIT and the NICE NG12 pathway — not a "bug"; to structure the chronic-diarrhoea work-up (bloods including FBC/ferritin/coeliac serology/CRP, FIT, faecal calprotectin where appropriate, stool tests if indicated); to consider IBD, coeliac, IBS and other causes; to safety-net and avoid false reassurance; and to handle the patient's wish for a quick fix while explaining why investigation is needed. The SCA challenge is converting a "settle my stomach" request into appropriate cancer-pathway investigation without causing undue alarm — and not anchoring on the patient's "bug" explanation.
Scenario A — Chronic change + red flags (this stem) ≥4 weeks looser stools, weight loss, rectal bleeding, fatigue, family history, age 58. FIT + bloods (FBC/ferritin/coeliac/CRP) + NG12 colorectal referral; examine incl. PR; safety-net.
Scenario B — Acute infective diarrhoea <2 weeks, self-limiting; assess dehydration, oral rehydration, hygiene/exclusion advice, no routine antibiotics/stool tests; stool test if bloody/systemic/recent travel/immunocompromised/public-health.
Scenario C — IBD Younger adult, chronic/bloody diarrhoea, nocturnal symptoms, weight loss, raised calprotectin/CRP. Refer gastroenterology; don't mislabel as IBS.
Scenario D — Coeliac / malabsorption Chronic diarrhoea, bloating, weight loss, anaemia; positive anti-tTG (on gluten). Confirm + gastroenterology; consider other malabsorption (pancreatic, bile-acid).
Scenario E — IBS / functional Younger, no alarm features, normal FIT/calprotectin/coeliac; positive symptom pattern. Positive diagnosis + management — a diagnosis of inclusion after excluding red flags, not pure exclusion.
Key variables to adapt for Acute vs chronic; dehydration/frailty; bloody diarrhoea; cancer red flags + FIT/NG12; IBD vs IBS (calprotectin); coeliac (on gluten); C. difficile (antibiotics/PPI); travel/immunocompromise; medication (laxatives, metformin, PPI, colchicine); overflow in constipation; public-health/exclusion.

1E — Psychosocial context
🫂 The "lingering bug", embarrassment, and the cancer that hides as diarrhoea

Bowel symptoms are embarrassing and easy to normalise — patients and clinicians both reach for "it's just a bug" or "a bit of IBS". That is exactly how colorectal cancer is missed. The consultation's job is to take a persistent change in bowel habit seriously, to ask the embarrassing questions (blood, weight, family history) without flinching, and to convert a request to "settle it down" into the right investigation — framed as being thorough, not as raising alarm — while keeping the patient engaged and not terrified.

🔎 Reframing the bug

Challenge the timeline gently but clearly.

"A bug rarely lasts six weeks, and the weight loss and blood aren't part of a simple bug — so rather than just settling it, I want to find out what's actually going on."
🧫 The embarrassing questions

Normalise asking about blood and bowels.

"I'm going to ask some questions people find awkward, and I'll need to examine your tummy and back passage — it's routine and important, and it's the quickest way to keep you safe."
⚖️ Thorough, not alarming

Pitch the cancer-pathway tests proportionately.

"Most of the time these tests are reassuring. I'd rather check properly now than miss something — that's just being thorough given your age and what you've told me."
👨‍👩‍👦 The family history

Acknowledge his father's cancer honestly.

"With your dad having had bowel cancer, I know this might be on your mind — that's another reason to do the right checks now and not just paper over it."

🚩 IBS is a positive diagnosis — and Trevor isn't IBS

The classic error is to reach for "IBS" or "a bug" in a patient with alarm features. IBS is diagnosed positively (typical symptoms, normal investigations, no red flags), usually in younger patients. For Trevor — 58, ≥4 weeks of looser stools, weight loss, rectal bleeding, fatigue and a family history — the formulation is a colorectal-cancer red-flag presentation requiring FIT and the NG12 pathway, with IBD and coeliac also in view. The discipline is to exclude the serious before comforting with the benign.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Trevor wants "something to bung it up" — acknowledge that, explain why tests come first, then agree what you can do today
1
Validate — name the expectation and the inconvenience

Six weeks of urgency and frequent loose stools is genuinely disruptive: planning journeys around toilets, broken sleep, worry at work. Trevor's request for a tablet is reasonable from where he sits. Naming it — and the impact behind it — before declining it stops him feeling dismissed and makes the explanation that follows land as care rather than refusal.

"I can hear this has really been getting in the way — rushing to the loo several times a day for six weeks is exhausting, and it makes complete sense that you'd want something to calm it down."
2
Explain — why a "plug" is the wrong first step

Loperamide would treat the symptom and could make Trevor feel better while hiding the very change that tells us something is wrong. With weight loss, blood, fatigue and a family history at 58, the priority is to find the cause. The explanation should be honest about why (bowel cancer is one of the things being checked for) but proportionate (most people tested this way do not have cancer).

"If I just gave you something to slow things down, you might feel better for a while — but it could hide what's causing this. With the weight loss and the blood, I want to find the reason first. Most of the time these checks come back reassuring, but bowel problems like this are exactly what the tests are designed to pick up early."
3
Negotiate — what he gets today

He should leave with something concrete: an examination done today, bloods and a FIT kit, an urgent referral explained, practical advice to manage urgency safely (fluids, knowing where toilets are, keeping a stool and food diary), clear safety-net symptoms, and a date to discuss results. Loperamide is best avoided while the cause is unknown — he has rectal bleeding and red flags — and is only revisited once the investigations have excluded colitis or cancer.

"Here's what I can do today: examine you, send some bloods and a simple stool test, and refer you to the bowel team quickly. Meanwhile, let's make the day-to-day easier — keeping your fluids up and noting down what's happening. And I'll ring you with the results so you're not left waiting and wondering."
Key principle: In diarrhoea, the expectation to "stop it" is met differently in each scenario. In acute infective diarrhoea the evidence supports fluids and time — antibiotics and antimotility drugs are usually unnecessary and sometimes harmful (E. coli O157, C. difficile). In chronic diarrhoea with red flags, symptomatic treatment must not delay diagnosis. Either way, validating the request and offering something practical keeps the patient on side.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Most diarrhoea is managed without drugs. Rehydration, diet, hygiene and exclusion, and temporary adjustment of risky medicines prevent most of the harm in acute diarrhoea. In chronic diarrhoea, a diary and targeted dietary change support diagnosis — but never replace investigation when red flags are present.
💧
Oral rehydration
Target: normal urine output; fluids little and often
Mechanism

Glucose-coupled sodium absorption in the small bowel stays intact in most infective diarrhoea, so a solution containing both sodium and glucose drives water absorption even while the gut is secreting. That is why oral rehydration salts (ORS) outperform plain water or sugary drinks in people at risk of dehydration.

Practical

Healthy adults: increase usual fluids; ORS for those at risk (older, frail, comorbid). Children under 5: continue breast or formula feeds; offer ORS as supplemental fluid if at risk of dehydration, and avoid fruit juices and carbonated drinks until diarrhoea has stopped (NICE CG84). Admit if unable to keep fluids down or clinically dehydrated and not improving.

NICE CG84: ORS is first-line rehydration in children; IV only if shock or ORS fails
🍽️
Keep eating — the right way
Target: normal diet as soon as tolerated
Mechanism

Early refeeding supports enterocyte recovery and prevents the nutritional deficit that prolongs illness. Starving the gut is outdated advice. In chronic diarrhoea, specific dietary triggers (lactose, sorbitol and other sweeteners, caffeine, alcohol, high-fat meals) can worsen symptoms and point to a cause.

Practical

Acute: small, frequent, plain meals once rehydrated. Chronic: a 2-week food and stool diary (Bristol stool form, timing, nocturnal symptoms, blood). Do not cut out gluten before coeliac serology (NICE NG20). Low-FODMAP diets belong to confirmed IBS and are best dietitian-led (NICE CG61).

Diary data often reveal sorbitol, alcohol or medication timing as the trigger
🧼
Hygiene, exclusion & public health
Target: 48 h symptom-free before return (longer for some)
Mechanism

Norovirus and many bacterial pathogens spread by the faecal–oral route and remain transmissible for a period after symptoms stop. Alcohol gel does not reliably kill norovirus or C. difficile spores — soap and water does the mechanical work.

Practical

Handwashing with soap and water after the toilet and before food; don't prepare food for others; don't share towels; clean toilet surfaces with bleach-based products. Stay off work or school until 48 hours after the last episode (UKHSA). Food handlers, health and care staff and young children are higher-risk groups — some organisms (e.g. STEC/E. coli O157, typhoid) need longer exclusion and clearance samples arranged by the local health protection team. Avoid swimming pools for 2 weeks after Cryptosporidium.

Registered medical practitioners must notify suspected food poisoning and infectious bloody diarrhoea (Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010)
💊
Sick-day medicine rules
Target: no AKI, no DKA, no lactic acidosis
Mechanism

Volume depletion reduces renal perfusion. ACE inhibitors/ARBs remove efferent arteriolar tone, NSAIDs remove afferent prostaglandin dilatation, diuretics deplete volume further, metformin accumulates as eGFR falls, and SGLT2 inhibitors can precipitate ketoacidosis with near-normal glucose when intake falls. Sulfonylureas cause hypoglycaemia if the person is not eating.

Practical

"SADMANS" (Sulfonylureas, ACE inhibitors, Diuretics, Metformin, ARBs, NSAIDs, SGLT2 inhibitors): advise temporarily withholding during significant diarrhoea/vomiting or inability to keep fluids down, and restarting once eating and drinking normally for 24–48 hours. Individualise — insulin must never simply be stopped, and heart-failure diuretics may need specialist advice. Check U&E if the patient is unwell or on several of these drugs.

Local NHS sick-day guidance varies — use your ICB's version and give it in writing
✈️
Travel advice & the returning traveller
Target: prevention before, safety-netting after
Mechanism

Travellers' diarrhoea is usually bacterial (e.g. enterotoxigenic E. coli, Campylobacter) and self-limiting, but parasites (Giardia, amoebiasis) cause persistent symptoms, and fever after travel to a malaria area is malaria until proven otherwise.

Practical

Pre-travel: food and water hygiene, ORS sachets in the travel kit, typhoid and hepatitis A vaccination where indicated (NaTHNaC country guidance). Post-travel: stool microscopy, culture and ova/cysts/parasites if persistent or bloody; same-day malaria testing if febrile after travel to an endemic area.

Persistent diarrhoea after travel → think Giardia; don't label as IBS without testing
🚻
Living with urgency while investigated
Target: maintain work and confidence safely
Mechanism

Urgency and fear of incontinence drive avoidance — not going out, skipping meals, reducing fluids — which worsens fatigue and dehydration. Practical coping lowers anxiety while the cause is found.

Practical

Plan routes with known toilets; a RADAR key or a "Just Can't Wait" toilet card (Bladder & Bowel UK) can help; carry spare underwear and wipes; keep fluids up rather than cutting them to reduce urgency. A fit note with adjusted duties is reasonable if symptoms affect work.

Reduces the pressure to "just plug it" before a diagnosis

Step 1 — Acute infective diarrhoea in adults (doses per BNF)

Fluids first; drugs only for selected adults

  • Oral fluids little and often; ORS for those at risk of dehydration (older, frail, comorbid)
  • Antiemetic for distressing vomiting in adults — dose per BNF
  • Loperamide for non-bloody diarrhoea in adults who need short-term relief: 4 mg initially then 2 mg after each loose stool, maximum 16 mg/day (BNF), for a few days only
  • Antibiotics not routinely: consider only if systemically unwell, immunocompromised, or a confirmed organism that needs treatment — guided by the stool result and microbiology advice
Most acute diarrhoea settles within a week without any prescription. Hydration assessment is the clinical skill; the prescription pad is mostly for sick-day advice.
Step 2 — C. difficile infection (NICE NG199)

Review the precipitants, assess severity, then treat

  • Review and, where possible, stop the antibiotic(s); review the need for PPIs and other medicines with GI activity (e.g. laxatives)
  • Do not offer antimotility drugs such as loperamide
  • First episode, any severity: oral vancomycin 125 mg four times a day for 10 days
  • Second-line (vancomycin ineffective): fidaxomicin 200 mg twice a day for 10 days
  • Relapse within 12 weeks of symptom resolution: fidaxomicin; recurrence after 12 weeks: vancomycin or fidaxomicin
  • Refer to hospital if symptoms or signs are severe or worsen rapidly; consider referral or specialist advice if at high risk of complications or recurrence (e.g. age, frailty, comorbidities). Life-threatening infection is managed in hospital with specialist advice
Metronidazole is no longer first-line for C. difficile under NG199. Send stool for C. difficile testing before treating where possible, and follow local infection-control advice.
Step 3 — Chronic diarrhoea: treat the confirmed cause

Diagnosis first, then cause-specific therapy

  • IBS-D (positive diagnosis, red flags absent, coeliac serology and calprotectin normal): loperamide first-choice antimotility; low-dose tricyclic second-line if laxatives, loperamide or antispasmodics have not helped (NICE CG61)
  • Coeliac disease: lifelong gluten-free diet with dietitian — no drug substitute (NICE NG20)
  • Bile acid diarrhoea: bile acid sequestrant (e.g. colestyramine) after SeHCAT or a specialist-guided trial
  • Microscopic colitis: stop likely culprit drugs; budesonide is the specialist treatment of choice
  • IBD: specialist-led induction and maintenance (see ulcerative colitis / Crohn's protocols)
  • Pancreatic insufficiency: pancreatic enzyme replacement with meals (dose per BNF / specialist)
For Trevor, no step on this ladder applies yet — the cause is unknown and red flags are present. The "prescription" is FIT, bloods and an urgent suspected cancer referral.
Loperamide — when NOT to use it (BNF · MHRA · NG199)
  • Bloody diarrhoea or high fever (dysentery) — risk of prolonging invasive infection; in E. coli O157 both antimotility drugs and antibiotics increase the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome
  • Suspected or confirmed C. difficile or antibiotic-associated colitis — NG199 says do not offer antimotility medicines
  • Acute ulcerative colitis / severe colitis — risk of toxic megacolon
  • Children — NICE CG84 advises against antidiarrhoeal medicines in under-5s; loperamide is not for acute diarrhoea in young children (BNF)
  • Undiagnosed chronic diarrhoea with red flags — masks the symptom that should drive investigation (Trevor)
  • High doses — MHRA Drug Safety Update (September 2017): serious cardiac adverse reactions (QT prolongation, torsades de pointes, cardiac arrest) reported with high doses, largely in misuse and abuse; naloxone is the antidote in overdose, with prolonged monitoring because loperamide outlasts it
Children under 5 — NICE CG84 in practice
  • Assess dehydration with the CG84 table: "red flag" signs include appearing unwell or deteriorating, altered responsiveness, sunken eyes, tachycardia, tachypnoea and reduced skin turgor; shock (pale/mottled skin, cold extremities, weak pulses, prolonged capillary refill, hypotension, decreased consciousness) needs emergency IV fluids
  • No clinical dehydration: continue feeds; encourage fluids; ORS supplementation if at increased risk
  • Clinical dehydration: ORS 50 ml/kg over 4 hours plus maintenance, often and in small amounts; IV fluids if shock or ORS not tolerated
  • Do not use antidiarrhoeal medicines; antibiotics are not given routinely — only in specific situations (suspected or confirmed septicaemia; extra-intestinal spread of bacterial infection; Salmonella gastroenteritis in a child under 6 months, malnourished or immunocompromised; C. difficile-associated pseudomembranous enterocolitis, giardiasis, dysenteric shigellosis, dysenteric amoebiasis or cholera). Seek specialist advice on antibiotics if recently abroad
  • Typical course: diarrhoea usually 5–7 days, stopping within 2 weeks; vomiting usually 1–2 days, stopping within 3 days — longer than this warrants review

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Diarrhoea — the toilet-mapped life, the fear nobody names, and the job that depends on being "clear"
Bowel symptoms shrink lives quietly. People plan journeys around toilets, stop eating before meetings, avoid social events and keep the blood to themselves. For Trevor the practical burden sits alongside a fear he has not voiced — his father's cancer. Addressing both is part of management, not an optional extra.
💼
Work, exclusion and money

For acute infective diarrhoea, occupation changes the advice: food handlers, health and care workers and those who care for young children or vulnerable adults must stay off until 48 hours after the last episode, and some organisms need longer exclusion with microbiological clearance (UKHSA). This can mean lost income for zero-hours or self-employed workers — acknowledge the pressure, but don't bend the rule. For chronic urgency, a fit note recommending adjusted duties or access to toilets can keep someone working.

"I know being off costs you. The 48-hour rule protects the people you serve and your colleagues — if it's a problem with your employer, I can put it in writing."
🚗
Driving and DVLA

Diarrhoea itself does not usually require DVLA notification. What matters is fitness at the time: dizziness from dehydration, fatigue from anaemia, or sedating medicines such as codeine. Urgency can make long journeys and professional driving hazardous. If Trevor later needs bowel surgery, he should not drive until he can safely perform an emergency stop — and a Group 2 (HGV/PCV) driver should check current DVLA guidance for any condition-specific rules.

"If you feel light-headed or wiped out, don't drive until that's settled. And if your job involves driving, let's think about how urgency affects that while we get to the bottom of it."
👨‍👩‍👦
Family and inherited risk

Trevor's father's bowel cancer shapes both his fear and his risk. If a cancer or a hereditary pattern is found, his children and siblings may need counselling about screening. Partners often carry the worry too — inviting them to results appointments can help. Families also matter for infection control: household hygiene limits spread of norovirus and C. difficile.

"Would it help to bring someone with you when we go through the results? And depending on what we find, there may be things worth your children knowing about too."
🙊
Embarrassment and masculinity

Men of Trevor's age often minimise bowel symptoms and delay presenting; rectal bleeding and the PR examination are particular barriers. Normalising the conversation, using plain words, offering a chaperone and explaining why the examination matters all increase uptake. Returning the FIT kit is another point where embarrassment causes delay — a clear explanation of how simple it is helps.

"Doctors talk about bowels all day — nothing you tell me will surprise me. The stool test is a tiny sample on a stick; it's much easier than people expect."
😰
Waiting for results

The interval between referral and diagnosis is often the most anxious part. Uncertainty feeds catastrophising, poor sleep and avoidance. Knowing exactly how and when results will be given, and who to call, reduces distress. The NHS Faster Diagnosis Standard aims for a diagnosis or all-clear within 28 days of an urgent referral.

"It's normal to worry while you wait. The aim is that within four weeks you'll know one way or the other. If the wait is getting to you, ring — you don't need a new symptom to call."
🍽️
Food, social life and identity

Chronic diarrhoea changes how people eat and socialise — skipping meals before going out, avoiding restaurants, drinking less fluid. A diagnosis such as coeliac disease or IBD brings lifelong change: gluten-free eating, flares, medication. Signpost support (Coeliac UK, Crohn's & Colitis UK, Bowel Cancer UK) and a dietitian when the cause is known.

"Once we know what this is, we'll make a plan that lets you get back to eating out and living normally — there's good support available for every one of the likely causes."

🚦 1 — Acute vs chronic
Acute (<2 wk)

Usually infective/self-limiting. Assess dehydration; oral rehydration; no routine antibiotics/stool tests; stool test if bloody/systemic/travel/immunocompromised/public-health. Anti-motility caution (not in bloody/O157/C. diff).

Chronic (>4 wk)

Work-up: FBC/ferritin, CRP, coeliac serology (on gluten), TFTs; FIT; faecal calprotectin (IBD vs IBS). Examine incl. PR.

🚩 2 — Red flags · refer

Persistent change in bowel habit, 50+, rectal bleeding, weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia, mass → FIT + NICE NG12 colorectal pathway. IBD (bloody/nocturnal, raised calprotectin/CRP) → gastroenterology. Coeliac → confirm + dietitian. IBS = positive diagnosis after excluding red flags. C. diff after antibiotics/PPI → stool + treat. Public-health notification/exclusion for relevant infections.
