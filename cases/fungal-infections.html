📋 Clinical Stem — Fungal Infections
A 56-year-old man with months of thickened, discoloured toenails and an itchy, scaly foot rash, who has tried steroid cream that made it worse, and asks for "the tablets to clear my nails"
Gary Pollard, 56, attends wanting "those tablets" to clear several thickened, yellow, crumbly toenails he's had for over a year, which embarrass him. He also has an itchy, scaly, peeling rash between his toes and on the sole, and a faint scaly ring on his groin. He used a steroid cream from a pharmacy on the groin patch, which initially soothed it but it then spread and became less obviously ring-shaped. He has put on weight, feels thirsty and tired lately, and his father had diabetes. He hopes to leave with a prescription for oral antifungal tablets today.
This stem tests the ability to: recognise the spectrum of superficial fungal infection (onychomycosis, tinea pedis, tinea cruris) and the classic clues (annular scaly edge, central clearing); to recognise STEROID-MODIFIED tinea (tinea incognito) from the history of a steroid cream making a "ring" worse and atypical; to insist on CONFIRMING nail/skin samples (microscopy & culture) before committing to oral antifungals (terbinafine) given their side effects/monitoring — rather than prescribing blindly on request; to search for the UNDERLYING CAUSE (this man has symptoms of undiagnosed diabetes — polyuria/thirst/weight change, fatigue, family history — check HbA1c), and to recognise athlete's foot as a portal for cellulitis; and to advise on hygiene/recurrence and topical-first treatment. The SCA challenge is to manage the request for oral tablets responsibly (confirm first), while diagnosing the bigger picture (likely diabetes) behind recurrent fungal disease.
Scenario A — Onychomycosis + tinea + underlying diabetes (this stem) Confirm nail samples before oral terbinafine; recognise steroid-modified tinea; check HbA1c (undiagnosed diabetes); treat tinea pedis/cruris topically; hygiene/recurrence advice.
Scenario B — Tinea corporis/cruris/pedis Annular scaly plaques; topical antifungal (terbinafine/imidazole) first-line; treat the feet (source); hygiene; avoid steroid monotherapy.
Scenario C — Tinea capitis / kerion (child) Scalp scaling/hair loss → samples + ORAL antifungal; treat contacts/fomites; kerion (boggy inflamed mass) → prompt treatment to avoid scarring.
Scenario D — Candida (intertrigo/oral/genital) Moist folds, oral thrush, vulvovaginal/balanitis; topical/oral azole; in recurrent/oral adult candida search for diabetes/immunosuppression (HIV).
Scenario E — The mimic / red flag Eczema/psoriasis, erythrasma (coral-red on Wood's light), seborrhoeic dermatitis, or a changing/ulcerating lesion that could be skin cancer — confirm; don't label "fungal" reflexively.

1E — Psychosocial context
🫂 The "simple" rash that's a window onto health

Fungal infections look trivial, and patients (and clinicians) can treat them as a quick prescription. But two things make them a richer consultation: the temptation to commit to months of oral therapy without confirming the diagnosis, and the fact that recurrent or florid fungal disease is frequently the visible sign of undiagnosed diabetes or immunosuppression. The thoughtful consultation confirms before treating with orals, treats the source and the predisposing factors, and reads the rash as a possible clue to systemic disease — turning a cosmetic complaint into a meaningful diagnosis.

🔬 Why confirm first

Explain the rationale for sampling before oral tablets.

"The nail tablets work, but they're a long course and can affect the liver, so it's worth a quick nail clipping to be sure it's a fungus first — about half of dodgy-looking nails turn out not to be."
🩸 The diabetes conversation

Connect recurrence to the bigger picture sensitively.

"These infections sometimes keep coming back when blood sugar's running high — and with the thirst and tiredness you've mentioned, I'd really like to check for diabetes. Sorting that out would help your skin too."
🦶 Treat the source

Explain athlete's foot feeding the rest.

"The athlete's foot is likely the source that's spread to your groin and nails — so treating your feet properly, keeping them dry, and not sharing towels stops it coming back."
🚫 Undoing the steroid

Explain why the steroid cream made it worse.

"That steroid cream calmed the itch but actually fed the fungus and spread it — so we'll stop it and use an antifungal instead."
🙈 Embarrassment and the groin

Patients often under-report groin symptoms, and nail disease makes people hide their feet — avoiding sandals, swimming, or intimacy. Normalising the problem helps them show you the whole picture, including the groin and a partner's symptoms.

"This is really common and nothing to be embarrassed about — it's the same fungus as athlete's foot. Is anyone at home, or your partner, getting anything similar? If so, it's worth treating everyone at once."
⏳ Realistic timescales

People expect nails to look normal when the tablets finish. Toenails grow slowly, so a cured nail can take a year or more to look clear. Setting this expectation early prevents "the tablets didn't work" and requests for repeat courses.

"If the tablets work, you'll see clear, healthy nail growing up from the base over the months — the old damaged part only goes when it's cut away, so for toenails it can be a year or more before they look normal."

Gary has done his reading, the nails embarrass him, and oral terbinafine is the treatment most likely to clear a confirmed dermatophyte toenail infection. His request is sensible. Say so first, before any "but" — otherwise the sample and blood tests sound like obstacles.

"You're right that tablets are the treatment most likely to clear toenails like yours — creams alone rarely get through a thick nail. I can see these really bother you, and I want to get you on the right treatment."
2
Explain — why confirmation and bloods come first

Several things make a thick, discoloured nail look fungal when it isn't: psoriasis, old injury, pressure from footwear, and occasionally a yeast or mould that terbinafine treats poorly. The BAD onychomycosis guideline (2014) advises confirming the diagnosis mycologically — clippings for microscopy and culture — before starting treatment. The course is months long and needs a baseline liver test. Gary's thirst, tiredness and weight change also need an HbA1c, and that result may change the rest of the plan.

"Not every nail that looks like this is actually a fungus, and the tablets are a three-month-plus course that can occasionally upset the liver. So I'd like a clipping to prove it's fungal, and a liver blood test before we start. While we're taking blood, I'd like to check your sugar level too, because of the thirst and tiredness."
3
Negotiate — something today, the tablets on a named date

Gary leaves with active treatment for the skin (antifungal cream for feet and groin, steroid stopped), practical foot care, the clipping taken, bloods booked, and a date to go through results. If microscopy is positive and LFTs are normal, terbinafine can start then — he does not need to wait weeks for the culture, as positive microscopy supports treatment.

"Today you'll start a cream for the feet and groin, and I'll take the nail clipping now. Once the clipping shows fungus and your liver test is fine — usually within a week or two — we'll start the tablets. Does that feel like a fair plan?"
Key principle: "Confirm, then treat" is not the same as "no". The candidate who says no to tablets loses the patient; the one who prescribes on request fails the Tasks domain. The pass is a clear, time-limited route: sample and bloods today, tablets when it is confirmed and safe, and the skin treated in the meantime. Also offer the option of no treatment — treatment is not essential for purely cosmetic, symptom-free nail disease — so the choice is his.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Dermatophytes live on shed keratin and thrive in warm, moist, occluded skin. Every non-drug measure below works by removing moisture, removing the reservoir (feet, footwear, towels, contacts, pets) or removing the host factor (steroid cream, high glucose). General hygiene and self-care measures are standard advice alongside antifungal treatment; the evidence for individual measures is mostly observational, but recurrence is common when the reservoir is ignored.
🦶
Dry the Toe Webs
Target: every wash, especially 4th web space
Mechanism

Macerated, moist web spaces let dermatophytes (and bacteria) multiply and create fissures. Drying removes the moisture the fungus needs and helps fissures heal, closing the portal for cellulitis.

Practical

Dry carefully between the toes after washing — a separate towel for the feet, or a hairdryer on a cool setting. Apply the antifungal cream to the web spaces and soles, not just the itchiest spot. Treat both feet even if one looks worse.

Treating tinea pedis removes a common portal of entry for leg cellulitis (NICE NG141 context)
🧦
Footwear & Socks
Target: daily clean cotton socks; rotate shoes
Mechanism

Shoes and socks hold infected skin scales and stay damp. Wearing the same pair every day re-seeds the feet after treatment — a common reason for "failed" treatment.

Practical

Cotton or moisture-wicking socks changed daily; alternate shoes so each pair dries for 24–48 hours; breathable footwear where work allows; antifungal powder in shoes; flip-flops in communal showers, gyms and pool changing rooms.

Cuts reinfection from footwear once the skin has cleared
🧺
Towels, Bedding & Spread
Target: own towel; dry the groin last
Mechanism

Tinea cruris is usually carried from the feet to the groin on fingers, towels and underwear. Drying the groin before the feet, and putting socks on before underwear, reduces transfer.

Practical

Don't share towels; wash towels, bedding and underwear regularly at a hot wash if the fabric allows; loose cotton underwear; avoid scratching and then touching other sites. Ask about a partner or household members with similar symptoms and treat at the same time.

Breaks the feet → groin → nails cycle within the household
🚫
Stop the Steroid Cream
Target: today; no steroid monotherapy
Mechanism

Topical steroids suppress the local immune response, so the fungus spreads while the redness fades — tinea incognito. Potent steroid–antifungal combination creams (common in products bought abroad or online) are also linked to the spread of resistant T. indotineae.

Practical

Bring all creams to the review. If the patch is very inflamed, only a hydrocortisone 1%–imidazole combination for up to 7 days, then antifungal alone (Canesten HC SmPC). Warn that itch may flare briefly after stopping the steroid.

Removes the main reason Gary's groin patch spread
🩸
Glucose Control & Weight
Target: individualised HbA1c (NICE NG28) if diabetic
Mechanism

Hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophil function and skin barrier repair, and diabetes is a recognised risk factor for tinea pedis, onychomycosis and candida. Weight and skin folds add moisture and friction in the groin.

Practical

If the HbA1c confirms diabetes: structured education, diet and activity, and medicines per NICE NG28, with individualised targets agreed. Weight loss reduces intertrigo. Check that the patient knows diabetes can cause recurrent skin, genital and foot infections.

Treats the cause of recurrence, not just this episode
🩺
Foot Checks & Podiatry
Target: annual foot risk assessment if diabetic (NICE NG19)
Mechanism

Fungal fissures, thick nails and reduced sensation combine to put the diabetic foot at risk of ulcers and infection. Thickened nails can also press on the nail bed and cause subungual ulceration.

Practical

If diabetes is confirmed, perform and record a foot risk assessment and give foot-care education. Nail thinning/debridement by podiatry helps symptoms and improves topical penetration, and is often the better option for frail, older people in whom oral courses carry more risk.

Earlier detection of a foot at risk
🐈
Contacts & Pets (Scalp Ringworm)
Target: examine household; ask about pets
Mechanism

In a child with tinea capitis, T. tonsurans spreads between people (siblings, school), and asymptomatic carriers can reinfect a treated child. Microsporum canis comes from cats and dogs.

Practical

Examine household contacts and consider sampling; antifungal shampoo (ketoconazole 2% or selenium sulfide) for the child and household to reduce spore shedding; don't share combs, brushes or hats; vet review for pets if Microsporum is grown (BAD tinea capitis 2014). School exclusion is not usually needed once treatment starts (UKHSA).

Prevents reinfection of a child after an oral course

Before the first oral tablet — the four checks
  • Diagnosis confirmed — positive microscopy or culture for nail disease; samples taken for scalp (treat without waiting in children)
  • Liver — ask about liver disease and alcohol; baseline LFTs before terbinafine (SmPC; BNF)
  • Heart — any heart failure or ventricular dysfunction rules out itraconazole for onychomycosis (SmPC; BNF)
  • Interactions and pregnancy — full drug list including OTC; itraconazole with simvastatin is contraindicated (MHRA DSU Aug 2012); pregnancy status and contraception, especially for griseofulvin
Combination steroid creams — the narrow window
  • Only hydrocortisone 1% with an imidazole (e.g. miconazole or clotrimazole), only for an intensely inflamed patch, only for up to 7 days, then antifungal alone (Canesten HC SmPC)
  • Never a steroid alone on a possible fungal rash
  • Avoid potent steroid combinations (clobetasone, betamethasone, clobetasol mixes) — they cause tinea incognito and are linked to resistant T. indotineae
  • If a patient has used one, record which product, for how long, and send scrapings if the rash is extensive

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Fungal infection — the embarrassing nail, the itchy groin, and the diagnosis behind them
💼
Work and Footwear

Safety boots, long shifts on foot, hot kitchens and manual jobs keep feet warm and wet. Advice that ignores the working day won't be followed. Tinea itself does not need time off work, and people with athlete's foot can keep working and exercising with simple precautions.

"What are your feet in all day? If it's boots, a second pair to swap each day and a change of socks at lunchtime will do more than you'd think."
🚗
Driving and DVLA — if diabetes is diagnosed

Fungal infection has no driving implications. A new diabetes diagnosis might: Group 1 drivers managed by diet or by medicines with a low hypoglycaemia risk (e.g. metformin) do not usually need to tell the DVLA. Insulin or sulfonylureas bring hypoglycaemia rules, and Group 2 (lorry/bus) licence holders have stricter notification rules — check current DVLA guidance.

"If the test shows diabetes, most people starting with diet or metformin don't need to tell the DVLA — but if you drive for work, or we ever need tablets that can drop your sugar, we'll go through the rules together."
❤️
Partner, Intimacy and Household

Groin tinea and genital candida cause embarrassment and can affect intimacy; a partner may have the same rash. Tinea spreads through close contact and shared towels. It is not a sexually transmitted infection, but framing it plainly avoids blame and gets everyone treated.

"This isn't something you've caught from being unclean and it isn't an STI — it's the athlete's foot fungus. If your partner has an itchy patch, it's worth them getting a cream too so you don't pass it back and forth."
🪞
Identity, Appearance and Avoidance

People with thick, discoloured nails often avoid sandals, swimming, the beach or bare feet in front of others. This is a real impact on quality of life. It justifies treatment when the diagnosis is confirmed and the patient chooses it, as long as the expected timescale is realistic.

"It sounds like the nails stop you doing things you'd enjoy. That's a good reason to treat them — as long as you know that the nail takes many months to grow out looking normal."
🎒
Children, School and Stigma (Scalp Ringworm)

Parents worry about hair loss, other parents' reactions and school. UKHSA guidance is that exclusion is not usually required for ringworm, but treatment is needed. Explain that it is common, treatable, and not a sign of poor hygiene.

"Scalp ringworm is common in children and isn't about cleanliness. Once the medicine has started, they can go to school as normal — we'll check brothers and sisters, and pets, too."
🩸
Taking in a Diabetes Diagnosis

Gary came in about his nails and may leave with a possible diabetes diagnosis — and his father had it. People often link diabetes with a parent's complications. Name that possibility, pace the information, and book a proper conversation when the result is back.

"I know you came about your nails, and I don't want to overload you. If the test shows diabetes, we'll sit down properly to talk it through — it's very different now from when your dad was diagnosed, and catching it early makes a real difference."

Serious terbinafine liver injury and severe skin reactions are rare but occur mostly in the first weeks; prompt stopping limits harm (SmPC; BNF). Itraconazole can cause or worsen heart failure. Document that this was given.
🟠 Not improving / changing lesion
"If the rash hasn't cleared by the end of the cream course, keeps spreading, or comes back quickly, come back — we'll take another sample rather than just trying something stronger. And if any mark changes shape or colour, bleeds, or you see a dark line appear in a nail, let us see it promptly."

🔍 1 — Diagnose

Tinea: annular, scaly advancing edge, central clearing — corporis/cruris/pedis/capitis/unguium. Candida: moist folds/mucosa, satellite pustules. Pityriasis versicolor: pigmented scaly macules. Steroid-modified tinea: atypical/spreading on steroid → stop steroid, scrape, treat as fungal. Confirm nails/scalp (microscopy & culture) before oral antifungals.

💊 2 — Treat & look deeper

Topical first for skin tinea/candida; oral (terbinafine/itraconazole, confirmed, LFTs/interactions) for nails; tinea capitis = oral + treat contacts (kerion promptly). Treat the source (feet) & hygiene; athlete's foot is a cellulitis portal. Find the cause: recurrent/widespread → HbA1c (diabetes), consider immunosuppression/HIV. Don't label a changing/ulcerating lesion "fungal" — consider skin cancer.
