Acting on suspicion is the duty

Children: types & red flagsAbuse types: physical, emotional, sexual, neglect, fabricated/induced illness. Red flags: injury inconsistent with the history/development, delayed presentation, a non-mobile child with a bruise/fracture, disclosure, neglect, FGM, child sexual exploitation/trafficking, parental risk factors. Adults at risk & the 6 principlesAn "adult at risk" has care/support needs and cannot protect themselves. Care Act categories: physical, psychological/emotional, sexual, financial, neglect/self-neglect, discriminatory, organisational, domestic, modern slavery. Principles: Empowerment, Prevention, Proportionality, Protection, Partnership, Accountability — making safeguarding personal
Capacity changes the routeFor adults, mental capacity (Mental Capacity Act) and the person's wishes matter — a capacitous adult can decline a referral for themselves, except where others (e.g. children) are at risk, where disclosure is needed to prevent or detect serious crime, or where the law requires it (e.g. information about terrorism, Terrorism Act 2000 s38B). The FGM mandatory reporting duty covers girls under 18 only, not adult women. Where capacity is lacking, act in best interests. Coercion/undue influence may impair capacity in domestic abuse
Domestic abuse — ask & actDomestic abuse (physical, sexual, psychological, financial, coercive control) is common and dangerous; enquire sensitively when indicated, assess risk (e.g. DASH), safety-plan, and refer (IDVA/MARAC for high risk). Children in the household are affected — consider them. Never raise it in front of the suspected perpetrator
Information sharing is justifiedConfidentiality is not absolute. Where there is a risk of serious harm, sharing relevant information without consent is justified and expected; for children, the child's welfare is paramount. Mandatory reporting applies to FGM in under-18s. Document the rationale for sharing or not sharing
📋 Clinical Stem — Safeguarding
A mother attends with her toddler for a minor rash — but you notice a finger-mark bruise on the child's arm, a delayed presentation, an inconsistent story, and the mother seems frightened of her partner
Chloe Davies, 24, brings her 14-month-old son for what she says is a rash. While examining him you notice a cluster of finger-tip bruises on his upper arm and an older bruise on his cheek. When you ask how they happened, Chloe gives a vague, changing account ("he's always bumping into things"), avoids eye contact, and flinches when her phone buzzes — it's her partner. She mentions, quietly, that things at home are "stressful" and that her partner "has a temper". She seems exhausted and low. There is a 6-year-old sibling at home. Chloe asks you not to "make a fuss" and says her partner will be angry if she's late.
This stem tests safeguarding reasoning across children and adults at once: recognising the red flags of possible non-accidental injury in a young child (bruising in a barely-mobile toddler, finger-mark pattern, an injury inconsistent and changing in the history, delayed presentation), AND the signs of possible domestic abuse of the mother (fear of the partner, coercive control, low mood) with a "Think Family" risk to both children; responding appropriately (listening, not promising confidentiality you can't keep, not raising domestic abuse if the perpetrator were present, ensuring immediate safety); referring correctly (children's social care for the child — the child's welfare is paramount and consent is not required where there is risk of significant harm; domestic-abuse services/MARAC and adult safeguarding as appropriate); recording factually in the child's and mother's own words; sharing information lawfully; and involving the practice safeguarding lead. The SCA challenge is to act decisively to protect the child and support the mother, balancing engagement with the duty to refer, without colluding with "don't make a fuss".
Scenario A — Child NAI + maternal domestic abuse (this stem) Recognise NAI red flags + domestic abuse; ensure safety; refer children's social care (child welfare paramount, consent not required where significant-harm risk); domestic-abuse/IDVA/MARAC; Think Family (sibling); record & share lawfully; safeguarding lead.
Scenario B — Child neglect / FII / CSE Neglect (failure to thrive, missed appointments, dirty/unsafe), fabricated/induced illness, child sexual exploitation/trafficking, FGM (mandatory reporting in under-18s). Refer per pathway.
Scenario C — Adult at risk (Care Act) Vulnerable adult with care/support needs unable to protect themselves — financial abuse, neglect, self-neglect. Adult safeguarding; capacity (MCA); making safeguarding personal.
Scenario D — Capacitous adult declining referral A capacitous adult can decline a safeguarding referral for themselves. The exceptions are where others (especially children) are at risk, where there is serious crime, or where a statutory duty applies (e.g. known FGM in a girl under 18; an adult woman with FGM is not a mandatory report, but any daughters need risk assessment). Support, safety-plan, keep the door open; document.
Scenario E — Immediate danger A child or adult in immediate danger → 999/emergency; don't delay for process.
Key variables to adapt for Child vs adult; abuse type; NAI red flags (non-mobile child, inconsistent history); domestic abuse & coercive control (DASH/MARAC); capacity & the person's wishes (MCA); Think Family/other dependents; consent & lawful information sharing; mandatory duties (FGM); immediate danger; recording; the safeguarding lead.

▲collapse
The first task is to recognise the concern and respond well: notice the red flags, listen without leading, respond with empathy but without promising a confidentiality you cannot keep, ensure immediate safety, and think about everyone in the household. Here there are two intertwined concerns — possible non-accidental injury to the toddler, and possible domestic abuse of the mother — with a sibling also at risk.
🎓 SCA framing — empathic, honest, child-focused
"Thank you for telling me things are stressful — that took courage, and I want to help. I need to be honest with you: my first job is to make sure your little boy and you are safe, and if I'm ever worried about that, I can't keep it just between us — but I'll always be open with you about what I'm doing and why."
Never promise to keep a safeguarding concern secret. Being honest about the limits of confidentiality, while remaining supportive and non-judgmental, is both ethical and a marked skill.

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you think has been happening — and what's worrying you about home?"
Surfacing the mother's understanding may bring a disclosure or reveal her fear and the coercive control. It also helps gauge insight and safety, and keeps her engaged in a process that must, regardless, protect the children.
😟 Concerns
"What are you most frightened of — for yourself and for the children?"
Her concern is likely her partner's reaction and losing her children. Naming and addressing these fears (you'll be honest, you'll support her, the aim is safety not punishment) is what allows her to engage rather than flee.
🎯 Expectations
"You've asked me not to make a fuss — let me explain what I have to do and why."
She wants you to do nothing. Naming this lets you explain, honestly and kindly, that you cannot ignore a concern about a child's safety — that you'll act transparently and supportively, but you must act.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The frightened parent, the silent child, and the duty that overrides discomfort

Safeguarding consultations are uncomfortable: they sit between compassion for a struggling, frightened adult and an absolute duty to protect a child who cannot protect themselves. The pull to collude — to accept the vague explanation, to "not make a fuss", to keep a parent on side — is exactly the pull that lets abuse continue. The skilled clinician holds empathy for the adult and decisive protection of the child together: listening without leading, being honest about confidentiality, ensuring immediate safety, and acting on reasonable concern by referring — because in safeguarding, doing nothing is itself a decision with consequences.

👶 The child comes first

The child's welfare is paramount and overrides the parent's wish for secrecy.

"I can hear you don't want a fuss, and I'll be as supportive as I can — but my first duty is your son's safety, and that's something I can't set aside."
🤝 Support, not punishment

Frame safeguarding as help for the family.

"This isn't about blame or taking your children — it's about getting your family the right support and keeping everyone safe. I'll be honest with you every step."
🔒 Honest about confidentiality

Never promise to keep safety concerns secret.

"I won't tell your partner anything, but I can't promise to keep worries about safety just between us — I'll always tell you what I'm doing and why."
🛟 Safety now

Address immediate safety for mother and children.

"Before anything else — are you and the children safe to go home today, or do we need to sort somewhere safe right now?"

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → agree what happens next
🤝
Chloe's expectation is that you will do nothing. You cannot meet it, but you can meet the fears underneath it: being punished, losing her children, and her partner finding out
1
Validate: name the expectation and the fear

Chloe wants "no fuss" and wants to leave quickly because her partner is waiting. Behind that are two fears: her partner's reaction, and the belief that social services will take her children. Say both out loud before you explain anything. A parent who feels judged will leave, and a parent who leaves before a plan is in place may take the child back into danger. Naming the fear is not collusion. It is what keeps her in the room long enough to make the child safe.

"I can see you're frightened, both about getting home late and about what might happen if I'm worried. Those are completely understandable fears, and I'd like to talk about them properly with you."
2
Explain: the duty, and what a referral actually means

Be plain that a bruising pattern like this in a young child, with an unclear explanation, is something every doctor has to act on. You do not need proof and you are not accusing her. Explain what a referral usually leads to. Children's social care decide within a working day what response is needed. Most referrals lead to assessment and support, not removal. Removing a child without the parents' agreement needs a court order, or police protection in an emergency, and that is reserved for serious risk. Being honest about the process takes away the imagined worst case that makes parents run.

"When a doctor sees bruises like these on a little one and we're not sure how they happened, we always ask children's services to help look into it. That isn't me deciding anything bad has happened. It's the system every doctor uses so that a child is never left at risk because someone wasn't sure. For most families it means support, not children being taken away."
3
Agree: offer control over what she can control

She cannot veto the child referral, but she can shape almost everything around it. That includes whether she stays while you make the call, how social care contact her safely (not via a phone her partner monitors), whether she agrees to a domestic-abuse referral for herself, who collects the 6-year-old, and whether she is safe to go home tonight. Offering real choices restores some of the control that coercive abuse takes away. If going home is unsafe, the plan changes to same-day protection: social care, and police if there is immediate danger.

"Here's what I'd like to do. I'll ring children's services now, while you're here, so nothing happens behind your back. You can sit in on the call. I'd also like to put you in touch with a domestic abuse worker, and that part is your choice. And we'll work out together the safest way for people to contact you."
Key principle — be open, unless openness increases the risk: in most cases you tell parents that you are referring and why. The DfE's Information sharing advice (2024) and GMC Protecting children and young people guidance both support this. The exception is when telling a parent could put the child or anyone else at greater risk of harm, or could compromise a police investigation. An example is where the suspected perpetrator might be told before the child is safe. If you are unsure, speak to children's social care before you tell the family and record that decision. In this stem, doing the referral call with Chloe present is usually the safest and most honest approach, because it keeps her engaged and the partner is not in the room. Do not let the child leave into possible immediate danger while you "think about it".

7C — Non-medication management: protective actions, with mechanism, evidence and target
In safeguarding, the "treatment" is a set of protective actions, not a prescription. Each card below explains why the action works, what evidence or guidance supports it, and the practical target for this consultation. NICE PH50 and quality standard QS116 on domestic violence and abuse set the expectation. Trained frontline staff should ask about domestic abuse in private when indicators are present, respond supportively, and refer to specialist services.
🛟
Immediate safety check & plan
Target: before she leaves the room
Mechanism

The most dangerous moment can be the one right after a disclosure or a professional's intervention, especially if the abuser finds out. A simple safety plan reduces that risk. It covers who is at home tonight, whether she can go somewhere else, an agreed safe way to contact her, a bag and documents, emergency numbers, and the Silent Solution (dial 999 from a mobile, then press 55 if unable to speak).

Practical

See her alone. Do not use her partner or family to interpret. Ask directly whether she and the children are safe to go home today. If not, contact social care (and police if in immediate danger) before she leaves. Give the National Domestic Abuse Helpline number (0808 2000 247, 24 hours), and only in a form that is safe to carry. Check whether her phone is monitored.

Safety today comes before any paperwork
🗣️
Ask safely & refer to specialist advocacy (IDVA)
Target: DASH offered; IDVA referral with consent; MARAC without consent only if high risk and justified in the public interest
Mechanism

Independent Domestic Violence Advisers (IDVAs) work with high-risk victims on safety planning, housing, legal protection (non-molestation orders, Domestic Violence Protection Notices and Orders) and the criminal justice process. Their job is to reduce risk. Asking in a private, supportive way makes disclosure more likely. Being dismissed or judged makes it less likely.

Evidence

In the IRIS cluster RCT (Feder et al., Lancet 2011), practices that had training plus a named advocate recorded 641 disclosures against 236, and 223 advocacy referrals against 12. NICE PH50 recommends that frontline health professionals are trained to ask about domestic abuse and to refer on. Use the SafeLives DASH checklist to structure the risk assessment. A score of 14 or more "yes" answers normally meets the MARAC threshold.

Training + a named advocate multiplies referrals (IRIS)

Family Help & universal services
Target: health visitor + school nurse linked in; Family Help as appropriate
Mechanism

Protection is not a single event. Families at risk need continuing, relationship-based support. Working Together 2026 brings targeted early help and section 17 "child in need" support together as one Family Help offer with a lead practitioner. The health visitor (universal service to age 5) and school nurse see the children regularly and can spot changes that a GP may not.

Practical

Suspected non-accidental injury (NAI) is not an early-help case. It needs a child protection referral first. Family Help is the right route for lower-level concerns, or alongside a protection plan. Tell the health visitor through the agreed safeguarding channel. Check whether the 6-year-old's school knows. Many police forces run Operation Encompass, which tells schools after a domestic abuse incident.

Many eyes over time pick up patterns that one appointment misses
🧠
Mother's mental health & substance use
Target: mood and risk assessed today; follow-up within 1–2 weeks
Mechanism

Chloe is exhausted and low. Depression, trauma and substance use, whether hers or her partner's, are common in households affected by domestic abuse. They reduce a parent's capacity to protect, and they are also consequences of the abuse. Treating her mood supports her own recovery and her ability to keep the children safe.

Practical

Ask about self-harm and suicidal thoughts. Her son is 14 months old, so specialist perinatal mental health services may still be relevant: in England these generally cover the first 24 months after birth (check local criteria). Offer NHS Talking Therapies. If you start medication, it is dosed per BNF like any other depression. Ask about alcohol and drugs in the household without judgement, because they change the level of risk.

Supporting the protective parent protects the child
📝
Documentation as an intervention
Target: same-day record in both records; body map; rationale
Mechanism

A clear record written at the time is what later conferences, courts and serious case reviews depend on. It also protects the next clinician, who can see the pattern. Record exact words in quotation marks, observations (site, size, colour, shape, with a body map), who was present, what you did and why, and what you decided not to do and why.

Practical

Use safeguarding codes and alerts according to practice policy. Record domestic abuse information in a way the partner cannot see, for example in online record access. Review third-party information and restrict online visibility where it could put someone at risk. The RCGP safeguarding toolkit has a whole section on documenting safeguarding concerns.

"If it wasn't written down, it didn't happen"
🧭
Safeguarding lead, supervision & practice systems
Target: lead informed today; case at next practice safeguarding meeting
Mechanism

Safeguarding decisions are better and safer when shared. The practice safeguarding lead, named GP for safeguarding and ICB designated professionals give advice, consistency and escalation routes. Regular practice meetings with the health visitor, where they exist, pick up children who have gone missing from services.

Practical

Talk to the lead the same day, but do not delay an urgent referral while waiting for them. The duty to refer is personal. The RCGP Safeguarding Standards (2024) set out training expectations for everyone in general practice. Reflect afterwards. These consultations are distressing, and supervision is part of doing this safely.

Shared decisions are safer decisions, but never a reason for delay

7G — Psychosocial impact: home, children, work and money, identity, and the clinician
🫂
Safeguarding: the frightened mother, the silent toddler, the watchful 6-year-old
A safeguarding referral reaches every part of a family's life: housing, money, work, school, identity and trust in professionals. Coercive control works by taking away independence, and a poorly handled intervention can repeat that. A well-handled one gives back choice wherever it can. Thinking about these effects is not a distraction from protection. It is how you keep the family engaged in a plan that protects the children.
🏠
Housing, refuge & leaving

Leaving an abusive partner can be the most dangerous time, and it may also mean losing a home, a school place and a support network. Many victims stay because of the practical barriers, not because of the relationship. An IDVA can help with emergency accommodation, refuge, local authority homelessness duties, and legal protection that can remove the abuser rather than the victim.

"You don't have to decide anything about leaving today. What matters today is that you and the children are safe tonight, and that you have someone to help you work out the options."
👧
The children

Under the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, children who live with domestic abuse are victims in their own right. The effects include anxiety, behavioural change, sleep problems, poor school attendance and developmental delay. The 6-year-old may have seen or heard things. Their school and school nurse are key sources of support and of information. A toddler cannot tell you anything, so what you see and write down speaks for them.

"Children often notice more than we think. His big sister's school can be a real source of support. Would it be all right for them to know she might need a bit of extra care right now?"
💷
Money, work & economic abuse

Economic abuse, such as controlling money, preventing work, or running up debt in the victim's name, is included in the statutory definition of domestic abuse. It keeps victims dependent. Chloe may have no money of her own, no bank card, or a job her partner controls. Benefits advice, and help with a fit note if her mental health affects work, are practical steps towards independence.

"Sometimes controlling money is part of what happens at home. Do you have access to your own money? There's help available with that too."
📱
Technology & safe contact

Her phone buzzing, and her flinching, suggest monitoring. Perpetrators often track location, read messages or control social media. Letters, texts, and even the practice app or online record access can give away a disclosure. Agree a safe contact method and record it prominently. Review what the partner could see online.

"Is it safe for us to text or call you on this phone? Is there a better way, or a time, when it's safe for us to reach you?"
🪞
Identity, shame & fear of judgement

Many parents in Chloe's position feel shame and self-blame, and fear being seen as a "bad mother". Coercive control wears down confidence and the ability to trust one's own judgement. Hearing a professional say plainly that the abuse is not her fault, while still acting to protect the children, can be a turning point. She may not disclose fully at first. Keeping the door open matters more than getting everything in one visit.

"Nothing you've told me makes you a bad mum. Asking for help when things are this hard is what good parents do."
🚗
Driving, practicalities & getting home

There is usually no DVLA question here. The practical question is how she gets home and whether it is safe. The partner is waiting outside, so running late matters. Think about how long the call will take, whether she needs a reason for the delay, and who will collect the 6-year-old from school. In immediate danger, the police can help her leave safely.

"You mentioned he'll be cross if you're late. Is it safe for you to leave with him today? If not, we can make other arrangements right now."
🩺
The clinician's own response

Safeguarding consultations bring out strong feelings: anger at a suspected abuser, sympathy that slides into collusion, fear of getting it wrong, and worry about the relationship with the family. These feelings are normal. They should be taken to supervision and to the safeguarding lead, and never allowed to drive the decision. Vicarious trauma is real, and debriefing is part of safe practice.

(To a colleague) "That was a hard consultation. Can I talk it through with you, both the decision and how it felt?"

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases, RAG marking and danger zones

⚠ Three scenario-specific safety-nets: use these word for word

🔴 Immediate danger
"If you or the children are ever in danger, call 999. If you can't speak, stay on the line and press 55 when asked, and they'll know you need help. You don't need to wait for anyone's permission."
The Silent Solution lets someone who cannot talk safely get a police response from a mobile. Giving this information says, without saying it, that you believe her.
🟠 New injury or disclosure
"If you notice any new marks or injuries on either child, or if anything happens at home that frightens you, please contact us or the social worker the same day. It's never making a fuss."
New information must reach the allocated social worker quickly. Reframing "fuss" as the right thing to do takes away the permission to stay silent that she asked you for.
🟢 Support 24 hours a day
"The National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free and open day and night on 0808 2000 247. Only take the number if it's safe for you to have it. We can also keep it on your record so you can ask us any time."
Written material can be found by the perpetrator. Offering the number, not insisting, and agreeing where to keep it, puts her safety first.

⚖️ 2 — Act & the law

Children: welfare paramount; consent NOT required where risk of significant harm; refer children's social care; FGM in under-18s = mandatory report. Adults: capacity (MCA) & wishes matter (making safeguarding personal) — a capacitous adult can decline for themselves unless others at risk / serious crime / statutory duty. Information sharing without consent is justified where risk of serious harm. Immediate danger → 999. Involve the safeguarding lead; act on reasonable concern (no proof needed).
