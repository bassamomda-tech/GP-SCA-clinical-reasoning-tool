📋 Clinical Stem — Amenorrhoea
A 26-year-old marathon runner whose periods stopped 8 months ago, training hard on a restricted diet, who is "not worried" but wants to know it's nothing serious
Sophie Carter, 26, hasn't had a period for 8 months. She is training intensively for a marathon, has cut her food intake to "stay lean", and has lost weight; her BMI is now 18. She is stressed at work. She is sexually active but uses condoms and is "sure" she isn't pregnant. She has no galactorrhoea, no excess hair or acne, no headaches or visual symptoms, and no hot flushes. She isn't trying to conceive and is mostly unbothered, but came because her mum nagged her and she wants reassurance. She has had a previous stress fracture in her foot.
This stem tests the ability to: always exclude pregnancy first; classify amenorrhoea (here secondary) and reason along the HPO axis; recognise functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea from low energy availability (intensive exercise + dietary restriction + low BMI + stress — the "female athlete triad" with the prior stress fracture signalling low bone density) while still excluding PCOS, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia and premature ovarian insufficiency with the core blood panel; appreciate the bone-health consequences of hypo-oestrogenism and that the treatment is restoring energy balance, not simply prescribing the pill; sensitively screen for disordered eating; and counsel on fertility and long-term health. The SCA challenge is engaging a patient who minimises the problem, and conveying that "no periods" is a meaningful health signal — especially for her bones — without lecturing.
Scenario A — Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea (this stem) Low energy availability (exercise/restriction/low BMI/stress); low/normal FSH-LH, low oestradiol. Restore energy balance/weight, screen eating disorder, protect bone (DXA, calcium/vit D), MDT; avoid masking with the pill alone.
Scenario B — PCOS Oligo/amenorrhoea + hyperandrogenism (hirsutism/acne) ± polycystic ovaries; raised free androgens, LH:FSH. Lifestyle, symptom control, endometrial protection, metabolic/CV risk, fertility pathway.
Scenario C — Premature ovarian insufficiency (<40) Amenorrhoea + raised FSH (×2) ± menopausal symptoms. HRT until ~51, fertility counselling, cause work-up (karyotype, autoimmune, fragile X), bone/CV protection.
Scenario D — Hyperprolactinaemia / pituitary Galactorrhoea, headache, visual field defect; raised prolactin. Exclude drugs/pregnancy/hypothyroidism; MRI pituitary; endocrinology (prolactinoma).
Scenario E — Primary amenorrhoea No periods by 15 (with) / 13 (without secondary sexual characteristics). Consider constitutional delay, Turner's, structural/outflow (imperforate hymen, Müllerian agenesis), androgen insensitivity; examine and image.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The minimising athlete, energy balance, and bones

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea in athletes is frequently normalised — by the patient and sometimes by sport culture — as a harmless sign of being "fit and lean". In reality it reflects insufficient energy availability and low oestrogen, with real consequences for bone density (the prior stress fracture is a warning) and future health. The consultation works by reframing absent periods as meaningful feedback from the body, by exploring eating and training without judgement, and by aligning the plan with what Sophie values — running well and staying injury-free — rather than simply telling her to do less.

🏃 Performance & bones

Frame energy balance around her goals — under-fuelling harms performance and bones.

"Training hard while under-fuelling actually works against you — it weakens bones (which is why you got that stress fracture) and limits performance. Getting your energy balance right will help your running, not hinder it."
🍽️ Eating, sensitively

Screen for disordered eating without accusation.

"Can I ask a bit about food — not to judge, but because how we fuel matters so much for this. How do you feel about eating and your body at the moment?"
💊 Not just the pill

Explain why the pill alone isn't the fix here.

"It's tempting to just start the pill to bring periods back, but that can mask what's going on and doesn't fully protect your bones. The real fix is restoring your body's energy balance."
👶 Fertility honestly

Reassure that this is usually reversible while taking concerns seriously.

"The good news is this type is usually reversible — when your body's better fuelled, periods and fertility typically return. If you ever have trouble down the line, there's plenty of help."

Menstrual timeline"How old were you when your periods started? What were they like before — how often, how long? When exactly was the last one?"Classifies the problem and dates the oestrogen deficit. Primary amenorrhoea is no periods by 15 with normal secondary sexual characteristics, or no breast development by 13 (ASRM committee opinion 2024; no UK national guideline sets these thresholds). Secondary amenorrhoea is usually defined as no periods for 3–6 months after previously regular cycles, or 6–12 months after previous oligomenorrhoea; ASRM advises evaluation after 3 months (regular cycles) or 6 months (irregular cycles). Medication history"Are you taking anything — prescribed, bought, or for sport? Any hormones, injections or a coil in the past year?"Drugs are the commonest pathological cause of a raised prolactin. 7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Sophie expects to hear "it's nothing" — or, if it is something, to be handed the pill. Neither is the safe plan, so name the expectation before changing it
1
Validate — name what she came for

She came because her mum insisted, feels well, and wants reassurance. She also half-expects to be told to stop running. Naming both expectations out loud stops her feeling ambushed, and shows you have heard that running is central to who she is. It is also honest to say that much of what she hopes is true: this is very likely a reversible, non-dangerous cause.

"You came in hoping I'd say this is nothing to worry about — and I suspect you're also bracing yourself for me to tell you to stop running. I'm not going to do that. But I don't want to just say 'it's fine' either, because your body is giving us a signal that's worth listening to."
2
Explain — share the reasoning in her terms

Link the three things she has not connected: the missing periods, the fuelling, and last year's stress fracture. Explain that the brain turns the ovaries down when energy intake does not cover training, so oestrogen falls; oestrogen is one of the main hormones protecting bone, which is why the fracture matters. Explain why the pill is not the fix: it produces a monthly bleed but does not correct the energy deficit, may not protect bone, and hides whether her own cycle has come back (Endocrine Society FHA guideline, 2017).

"When your body isn't getting enough fuel for the training you're doing, the brain switches off the hormones that drive periods to save energy. Low oestrogen also weakens bone — I think that's part of why you had that stress fracture. A pill would give you a bleed, but it wouldn't fix the fuel gap and it would hide whether your own cycle has come back."
3
Negotiate — something concrete today

She should leave with a plan that protects her running: a pregnancy test and blood panel today, a DXA scan, dietitian and sports-medicine referral, a sensitive eating screen, and a follow-up date. Negotiate the smallest effective change first — for example adding fuel around training and building in a rest day — rather than an ultimatum on mileage. Agree what would make you both change the plan, such as further weight loss or a new bone injury.

"Here's what I'd suggest: bloods and a pregnancy test today, a bone density scan, and I'd like you to see a sports dietitian who works with runners. The aim is to get you fuelled for the training you want to do. Let's review in about six weeks with the results — does that feel doable?"
Key principle: In functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea the clinician and the patient want the same thing — a strong, injury-free athlete. Framing the plan around performance and bone health turns "eat more, train less" (which she will resist) into "fuel for the training you want" (which she can own). If she presents instead wanting fertility, contraception, or treatment for hair growth, the same validate–explain–negotiate sequence applies to whichever cause the panel reveals.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
For functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea, non-drug treatment is the treatment. The Endocrine Society FHA guideline (2017) recommends correcting the energy imbalance — more intake, adjusted exercise, less stress — with a multidisciplinary team (medical, dietetic and mental health), and suggests cognitive behavioural therapy. The IOC 2023 REDs consensus sets the same priorities for athletes. For PCOS, lifestyle is first-line for everyone (International PCOS Guideline 2023), provided disordered eating has been screened for first.
🍽️
Restore Energy Availability
Target: intake matched to training; weight towards healthy, pre-restriction level
Mechanism

The hypothalamus reads low energy availability as a threat and slows GnRH pulses. LH and FSH fall, the ovaries stop maturing follicles, and oestradiol drops. Leptin, insulin and thyroid hormones fall and cortisol rises, all of which further suppress the axis and harm bone. Restoring available energy reverses this — often before much weight is regained.

Practical

Referral to a sports or specialist dietitian. Regular meals and snacks around training, especially carbohydrate before and after sessions; no training fasted. Avoid calorie counting in someone with restrictive traits. Recovery of menses commonly takes months after fuelling improves — say so at the start so she does not give up.

First-line treatment for FHA (Endocrine Society 2017; IOC REDs 2023)
🏃
Modify Training Load — Don't Ban It
Target: rest days, reduced volume/intensity while recovering
Mechanism

Energy availability is intake minus exercise expenditure. Cutting volume a little is often easier than eating much more, and reduces load on bone that is already under-mineralised. Bone stress injuries in amenorrhoeic runners tend to occur at trabecular-rich sites (pelvis, femoral neck, sacrum), which heal slowly and matter more.

Practical

Sports-medicine input using the IOC REDs CAT2 traffic-light system to guide participation. Agree a concrete plan (e.g. one extra rest day, a reduced long run) rather than "do less". Any new bone pain means stop and be assessed — do not run through it.

IOC REDs CAT2: severity-graded return-to-sport guidance
🦴
Bone Health Basics
Target: adequate dietary calcium; vitamin D sufficiency; no smoking
Mechanism

Oestrogen restrains bone resorption; undernutrition also reduces bone formation. Young women accrue most of their peak bone mass by their late twenties, so a deficit now may be permanent. Calcium and vitamin D are necessary but not sufficient — they do not replace oestrogen or energy.

Practical

Dietary calcium first (dairy or fortified alternatives); vitamin D supplement per UK public health advice, higher if deficient. Stop smoking; keep alcohol low. Bisphosphonates are not routine in young women and are specialist-only (they persist in bone and are a concern before pregnancy).

Reaching a healthy weight is central to treatment; bisphosphonates not advised in FHA (Endocrine Society 2017)
🧠
Psychological Support & Stress
Target: CBT-based support; eating-disorder service if criteria met
Mechanism

Stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis; cortisol and CRH suppress GnRH directly. Perfectionism and food rules keep the energy deficit going even when the athlete "knows" she should eat more. Changing the thinking changes the behaviour.

Practical

The Endocrine Society guideline suggests CBT for FHA. Screen with SCOFF; if an eating disorder is suspected, refer to the community eating disorders service straight away (NICE NG69) rather than waiting for dietetics to fail. Consider NHS Talking Therapies for stress and anxiety.

Small RCT evidence that CBT restores ovulation — basis of the Endocrine Society 2017 suggestion
📅
Cycle & Symptom Tracking
Target: record bleeding, training, injuries, weight trend
Mechanism

The return of a spontaneous period is the best biomarker of recovered energy availability — but only if she is not on a combined pill, which produces withdrawal bleeds regardless. Tracking also shows whether training blocks or stressful periods set her back.

Practical

Any app or diary: bleeding days, training load, rest days, injuries, mood. Bring it to reviews. Weigh in clinic (not at home) if weighing is triggering; blind weighing is acceptable where agreed.

Makes recovery visible — motivating and clinically useful
⚖️
If the Panel Shows PCOS Instead
Target: 5–10% weight loss if overweight; no restrictive diets
Mechanism

Insulin resistance drives ovarian androgen production and anovulation. Modest weight loss in those with excess weight improves cycles, ovulation, hirsutism and metabolic risk; physical activity improves insulin sensitivity even without weight change.

Practical

A healthy, sustainable eating pattern — no single "PCOS diet" is better. Screen mood and disordered eating before any weight conversation; weight stigma harms. Glycaemic screening at diagnosis (75 g OGTT preferred), lipids and BP, as in the site PCOS protocol.

Lifestyle is first-line for all with PCOS (International Guideline 2023)

PCOS / PMOS (Scenario B) — by goal

Lifestyle for all; then by what matters to her now (International PCOS Guideline 2023; site PCOS protocol)

  • Cycles and hirsutism: the COCP is first-line after checking UKMEC. Lower ethinylestradiol doses are preferred. Co-cyprindiol is second-line because of VTE risk.
  • Endometrial protection if COCP unsuitable: a cyclical progestogen to cause a withdrawal bleed at least every 3–4 months, or an LNG-IUS (RCOG GTG 33).
  • Amenorrhoeic for 3 months or more now: pregnancy test, progestogen withdrawal bleed, then transvaginal ultrasound of the endometrium. A thickened or irregular endometrium, or no withdrawal bleed, needs gynaecology assessment.
  • Metabolic: metformin alongside lifestyle, with the best evidence at BMI ≥25 (off-label).
  • Fertility: refer. Letrozole is first-line ovulation induction and is specialist-initiated.
Anti-androgens (e.g. spironolactone, off-label) only with reliable contraception, after ≥6 months of COCP plus cosmetic measures, and as locally agreed.
Premature ovarian insufficiency (Scenario C)

Sex-steroid replacement until at least the average age of natural menopause (NICE NG23; ESHRE POI 2024)

  • NICE NG23: offer HRT or a combined hormonal contraceptive unless contraindicated. It is given for bone, cardiovascular and symptom protection, not just hot flushes. Current evidence suggests its risks are not raised above those of women of the same age with normal ovarian function, because it replaces hormones they would otherwise have.
  • ESHRE 2024 prefers 17β-estradiol to ethinylestradiol for replacement. Younger women often need higher oestrogen doses than typical menopause starting doses. A woman with a uterus always needs a progestogen (or an LNG-IUS). Doses per BNF/specialist.
  • HRT is not contraception. A minority conceive spontaneously, so discuss contraception if pregnancy is not wanted. A COCP gives contraception and replacement together.
  • Refer to a specialist (menopause/reproductive endocrine) for confirmation of the cause, fertility counselling (donor-egg IVF is the most effective route to pregnancy) and a DXA plan.
Diagnosis: NICE NG23 = raised FSH on 2 samples 4–6 weeks apart. ESHRE 2024 = one FSH >25 IU/L plus ≥4 months of oligo/amenorrhoea, repeated if there is doubt. Follow your local pathway.
Hyperprolactinaemia (Scenario D) — site protocol sequence
  • Screen for mass effect first: visual fields, headache, cranial nerve palsy. A visual field defect with headache and a significantly raised prolactin needs urgent MRI pituitary and ophthalmology. A thunderclap headache in someone with a known adenoma is pituitary apoplexy: hydrocortisone 100 mg IM and 999.
  • Repeat properly: fasting, rested, morning sample, ideally by the cannula technique. Check TSH, hCG, U&E, and ask for PEG precipitation for macroprolactin in a mildly raised, asymptomatic result.
  • Drug-induced: discuss with the prescriber. Never stop or switch an antipsychotic without the psychiatric team. If prolactin is still raised 3 months after stopping the drug, arrange an MRI.
  • MRI pituitary if prolactin is >1000 mIU/L on two fasting samples with no cause, at any level with symptoms, or at >2000 mIU/L.
  • Prolactinoma: cabergoline is first-line and specialist-initiated.
What NOT to prescribe — the common errors
  • The COCP as the "treatment" for hypothalamic amenorrhoea. It produces withdrawal bleeds, hides recovery and does not fix the energy deficit (Endocrine Society 2017).
  • Any hormone before pregnancy is excluded, or before a primary amenorrhoea work-up. Starting a pill in a 16-year-old with no periods can delay the diagnosis of Turner syndrome or an outflow anomaly.
  • A COCP for hirsutism before a tumour is excluded. Rapid virilisation or testosterone >5 nmol/L needs endocrinology first (site PCOS protocol).
  • Unopposed oestrogen in a woman with a uterus. Always add a progestogen or LNG-IUS.
  • Levothyroxine for a low T3 in someone starving. Sick euthyroid is adaptive and resolves with refeeding (site eating-disorder protocol).
  • Bisphosphonates in a young woman without specialist advice. They persist in bone and raise concerns for future pregnancy.
  • Cabergoline started before macroprolactin, drugs and hypothyroidism are excluded, or for a stalk-compression mass.

7G — Psychosocial impact: identity, sport, fertility, body image, work and driving
🫂
Amenorrhoea — the athlete who feels fine, the young woman told her ovaries have stopped, the patient whose food rules run her life
Absent periods rarely hurt, so the patient often sees no problem. The real harms are hidden: bone loss she cannot feel, an eating disorder she may not name, fertility fears she will not raise unless asked, and, in POI, a sudden loss of expected futures. The GP who treats only the bleeding misses the person.
🏃
Sport & Athletic Identity

For Sophie, running is identity, stress relief and social life. Being told to "do less" feels like being told to be less. In sport culture, amenorrhoea is still sometimes seen as a sign of being fit and lean, and coaches or peers may reinforce under-fuelling.

Engaging a sports-medicine clinician or dietitian who works with athletes gives the advice credibility. The IOC REDs CAT2 frames modified participation as protective, not punitive.

"The aim isn't to take running away — it's to make sure you're strong enough to keep doing it for years. Lots of elite runners have been through exactly this."
🍽️
Food, Body Image & Control

Restriction can start as "clean eating for performance" and become rigid rules, anxiety around unplanned meals, and distress at rest days. Denial and minimisation are features of the illness, not dishonesty (site eating-disorder protocol).

If an eating disorder is suspected, refer promptly and hold the physical monitoring in primary care. A normal BMI does not exclude serious risk.

"Some people find food starts to feel like something they have to control. If that's ever true for you, it's not a failing — it's something we can get proper help with."
👶
Fertility — Spoken & Unspoken

Most patients will not raise fertility unless asked, yet it is often the hidden concern. In FHA and PCOS the outlook is usually good with treatment. In POI, the news that natural conception is unlikely is a bereavement and needs time, written information and specialist counselling, including the realistic role of donor eggs.

"Is having children something you think about for the future? I'd rather we talk about it now than leave you worrying."
💔
POI — Identity, Relationships & Menopause at 30

A menopause diagnosis in your twenties or thirties affects sexual function, relationships, self-image and mood. Low libido and vaginal dryness are rarely volunteered. Partners may need information too. Peer support from the Daisy Network, a UK POI charity, helps many women.

"This is a lot to take in, and it's not something most people your age have to deal with. How are you feeling about it — and is there someone at home who'd find it helpful to hear this too?"
💼
Work, Study & Stress

Work stress is a driver of FHA in its own right. Shift work, long hours and training fitted around a demanding job squeeze meals out. Clinic appointments for DXA, dietetics and review need to fit around work. A fit note is rarely needed, but advice on regular meal breaks can be.

"When does eating actually happen on a work day? Sometimes the fix is as practical as planning a snack before the evening run."
🚗
Driving — When the Pituitary Is the Cause

Most causes of amenorrhoea have no driving implications. A pituitary tumour does if it affects the visual fields. Under DVLA guidance, a pituitary tumour with a visual field defect must be reported to the DVLA, and the patient must meet the visual field standard to drive. Some pituitary surgery also carries its own rules. A drug-treated prolactinoma with normal fields usually needs no notification. Check the current DVLA Assessing fitness to drive guidance. Severe anorexia nervosa with syncope or cognitive impairment may also make driving unsafe.

"Because the scan showed the growth is pressing near the nerves to your eyes, I need to talk about driving. Until your visual fields are checked properly, please don't drive, and we'll look at whether you need to tell the DVLA."

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases + medico-legal rationale

⚠ Three scenario-specific safety-net phrases

🔴 Emergency — eating-disorder physical risk / pituitary
"If you faint, feel your heart racing or skipping, get chest pain, or find you can't get up from a chair or climb stairs without using your arms, that needs checking the same day. Call 111 or go to A&E, not wait for an appointment. And if your blood test ever shows a raised prolactin and you then get a sudden, severe headache or change in your vision, call 999."
MEED (RCPsych 2022) parameters (syncope, bradycardia, hypotension, proximal weakness) mark a medically unstable eating disorder. A thunderclap headache with visual change in a known pituitary adenoma is apoplexy until proven otherwise (site protocols). Both are rare in Sophie's scenario but carry the most harm.
🦴 Bone — new pain while training
"If you get new pain in your hip, groin, shin or foot that comes on with running and doesn't settle, stop running and see us within a few days. Don't train through it. With low hormone levels, a stress fracture can happen more easily and heals more slowly."
Bone stress injuries recur in amenorrhoeic athletes, and those at the femoral neck can displace. An early review allows imaging and offloading. A second injury also moves her up the REDs severity scale and strengthens the case for specialist and hormonal input.
🟠 Pregnancy & results
"Your periods can come back without warning, and you can get pregnant before the first one arrives. Keep using contraception, and do a test if you have any doubt. If you're pregnant and get one-sided tummy pain, shoulder-tip pain, or bleeding, get seen the same day. I'll contact you if any of today's results need action. If you haven't heard in two weeks, please ring."
Ovulation precedes the first period, so pregnancy, including ectopic pregnancy, can occur without a preceding bleed. Closing the results loop protects against a missed raised FSH or prolactin, which is a recognised source of delayed POI and prolactinoma diagnoses.

🚫 8 Danger zones
✗
No pregnancy test→ Always first, whatever the contraception history. It is the commonest cause of secondary amenorrhoea.
✗
COCP as the fix for FHA→ It masks recovery and does not restore energy or reliably protect bone (Endocrine Society 2017).
✗
Stress fracture not linked to low oestrogen→ Name the bone link and plan a DXA. Amenorrhoea and a bone stress injury are both REDs severity indicators in the IOC CAT2.
✗
Eating disorder not screened→ Use SCOFF sensitively as part of an assessment, not alone. If an eating disorder is suspected, refer immediately (NICE NG69) and check physical risk (MEED).
✗
Raised FSH filed as "normal for a runner"→ A raised FSH points to the ovary, not the hypothalamus. Repeat it and consider POI. HRT to ~51 if confirmed.
✗
Raised prolactin acted on without the sequence→ Visual fields, repeat sample, TSH, hCG, drug review, macroprolactin, then MRI by level (site protocol).
✗
PCOS endometrium forgotten→ Fewer than 4 bleeds a year and unprotected means a progestogen, COCP or LNG-IUS. Prolonged amenorrhoea means assess the endometrium.
✗
Contraception not discussed→ Ovulation returns before the first period. Avoid DMPA where bone density is already a concern.

🔍 1 — Work-up

Always pregnancy test first. Classify primary (no periods by 15/13) vs secondary (≥3–6 months). Core panel: FSH/LH, oestradiol, prolactin, TSH, testosterone (±FAI). Localise: high FSH → POI; low/normal → hypothalamic/pituitary; high prolactin → pituitary (MRI if symptoms); high androgens → PCOS. Pelvic USS/karyotype/DXA as directed.

💊 2 — Treat the cause

Hypothalamic: restore energy availability + dietetic/eating-disorder support + bone protection (not pill-only). PCOS: lifestyle, endometrial protection, metabolic/CV, fertility. POI: HRT to ~51 + fertility counselling + work-up. Hyperprolactinaemia: stop drug / dopamine agonist (specialist). Protect bone in all hypo-oestrogenic states; counsel fertility/contraception.
