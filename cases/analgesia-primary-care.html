📋 Clinical Stem — Analgesia in Primary Care
Neil Brandon, 49, has had chronic low back pain for 4 years with no red flags and normal imaging. He's been on increasing doses of opioids (now a high morphine-equivalent dose) plus a gabapentinoid, and attends asking for a stronger dose because the pain "still doesn't touch it". Despite the medication, his function is worse — he's stopped working and exercising, sleeps badly, and his mood is low. He's frustrated, feels no one takes his pain seriously, and is frightened that without more medication he won't cope. He drinks more than he used to. He's never been offered exercise therapy, psychological support or a clear explanation of his pain.
This stem tests rational analgesia and opioid stewardship: recognising chronic primary/non-cancer back pain where escalating opioids and gabapentinoids provide little benefit and cause harm (worsening function, mood, sleep, dependence, overdose risk — heightened by the alcohol and the opioid+gabapentinoid combination); naming the pain type and applying the right NICE guidance — NG193 for the assessment and for chronic primary pain (recommends against starting these drugs; exercise, psychological therapy and possibly an antidepressant), used alongside NG59 because this is low back pain (no opioids for chronic low back pain, no gabapentinoids, and no antidepressants for the back pain itself — though depression, if present, is treated in its own right); validating the patient's genuine pain and distress while honestly explaining the evidence; planning a collaborative, gradual deprescribing of opioids/gabapentinoid with support, functional goals, and treatment of the mood/sleep/alcohol drivers; and avoiding both uncritical dose escalation and abrupt, punitive stopping. The SCA challenge is the difficult conversation — declining to escalate, and proposing a different, evidence-based path, without the patient feeling dismissed or abandoned.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Real pain, the wrong tools, and the conversation everyone dreads

Chronic pain is real, disabling and isolating — and for years the reflexive response was escalating opioids, which we now know rarely help and frequently harm, leaving patients more disabled, dependent and at risk. The hardest, most valuable consultation in pain care is declining to escalate while keeping the person on side: validating their suffering, owning that the system (not the patient) reached for the wrong tools, explaining the evidence honestly, and offering a genuinely better path — exercise, psychological support, treating mood and sleep, and careful, supported deprescribing — without ever abandoning them.

✅ Validate first

The pain is real; the patient is not to blame.

"Your pain is real and I believe you — and none of this is your fault. The medication you were given seemed sensible at the time, but the evidence has moved on and it's not serving you well."
📉 Why not more opioids

Explain hyperalgesia/harm honestly.

"Strong painkillers actually do very little for long-term pain, and over time they can make the nervous system more sensitive — so more medication can mean more pain and less life, which is what's happened."
🏃 The better path

Offer credible, evidence-based alternatives.

"The things that genuinely help long-term pain are movement built up gradually, support to manage the impact, and treating your sleep and mood — and I can get you those, which you've never been offered."
🤝 No abandonment

Reassure you'll support the change.

"I'm not going to stop anything suddenly or leave you to struggle. We'll reduce slowly, together, while we build up the things that work — and I'll be here throughout."
1E — Psychosocial context: the person behind the prescription
🫂
Neil — four years of pain, a lost job, a shrinking life, and a prescription that became part of the problem
NICE NG193 asks for an assessment of all chronic pain that looks beyond the pain score: how the pain affects the person's life, work, relationships, sleep and mood, what they believe about it, and what they hope for. For Neil these questions are not optional extras — they are where the treatment targets actually sit. Each card below is something to ask about, why it matters, and a phrase to use.
💼
Work, money and identity

Neil has stopped working. Worklessness in chronic pain is both a consequence and a cause: loss of structure, income, purpose and social contact all amplify pain and low mood, and the longer someone is off, the harder the return. Benefits assessments and money worries add a constant background stress, and some patients fear that improving will threaten their income or be seen as proof they were "putting it on".

Ask what his job was, what he misses, and whether a phased or adjusted return is imaginable. NICE NG59 recommends promoting and facilitating return to work or normal activities for people with low back pain.

"What did you do for work, and what do you miss most about it? I'd like part of our plan to be about getting some of that back — not straight away, but step by step."
🏠
Family, relationships and carers

Partners and families often become carers, take over tasks and — out of kindness — encourage rest. Irritability from pain, withdrawal effects between doses, sedation and alcohol strain relationships. Family members are also the people who will find him if he overdoses, so they need to know about naloxone and the signs of too much medication.

Ask who is at home, how they are coping, and whether anyone else in the household takes medicines that could be shared or taken by mistake (including children and visitors).

"Who's at home with you, and how are they finding things? Would it help if someone came with you next time, so they hear the plan too — and know what to do if you ever became very drowsy?"
🍺
Alcohol, sleep and coping

Neil drinks more than he used to. Alcohol is often self-medication for pain, sleep and low mood, and with opioids and a gabapentinoid it multiplies the risk of respiratory depression (MHRA). It also worsens sleep architecture, mood and liver safety for paracetamol. Ask non-judgementally and quantify (AUDIT-C), and ask what else he uses to cope — including over-the-counter codeine, someone else's tablets, or cannabis.

"Lots of people in pain find a drink takes the edge off or helps them sleep — I'm not judging. Can you tell me roughly how much you're having in a typical week? It matters because of how it mixes with your tablets."
😔
Mood, hopelessness and safety

Low mood is common in chronic pain and runs both ways: pain worsens mood and low mood amplifies pain. Hopelessness, access to a large supply of opioids and gabapentinoids, and alcohol together make a direct question about self-harm and suicidal thoughts essential — asking does not increase risk. Screen formally (PHQ-9, GAD-7) and ask directly.

"When pain goes on this long, people sometimes feel it's never going to get better, or even that they'd rather not be here. Have you had thoughts like that? I ask everyone in your situation, because it matters for how we keep you safe."
🧠
Beliefs about pain and medication

Neil's model is "more pain means more damage, so I need stronger painkillers". Fear that movement causes harm (fear-avoidance) and catastrophising predict disability more strongly than scan findings. He may also fear that reducing medication will unleash unbearable pain, not knowing that withdrawal symptoms and opioid-induced hyperalgesia can make pain feel worse on high doses and that many people feel better as doses fall (site opioid protocol, Opioids Aware principles).

"What do you think is going on in your back? And what do you think would happen if we slowly lowered the tablets? I ask because what people believe about their pain makes a real difference to what helps."
⚖️
Past experiences with services

Neil feels no one takes his pain seriously. Previous consultations may have left him feeling dismissed, or accused of drug-seeking. Each dose increase was likely offered in good faith but without a plan to review. Acknowledging the system's part — rather than blaming him — is often what unlocks engagement. Check who prescribes (one prescriber, one pharmacy), and whether he has had pain clinic, physiotherapy or psychology input before.

"It sounds like you've had to fight to be believed, and that the answer each time was a stronger tablet. That's not your fault. I'd like to do things differently with you, and I'll be straight with you all the way through."

🚩 The wrong paradigm is the problem

Neil's formulation is chronic non-specific low back pain with the features of chronic primary pain, mismanaged with escalating opioids and a gabapentinoid that have worsened his function, mood and risk (compounded by alcohol). The plan: validate; decline escalation; apply NG193 assessment principles with NG59 for the back pain (exercise, with ACT/CBT for pain under NG193 for the chronic-primary-pain features; no opioids, gabapentinoids or antidepressants for the back pain itself); assess his low mood formally and, if he has depression, treat it in its own right — which may include an antidepressant such as duloxetine chosen with the pain in mind; collaboratively and gradually deprescribe the opioids and gabapentinoid with support; treat his mood, sleep and alcohol; set functional goals; provide naloxone and safety-net — never abruptly stopping or abandoning.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + target
For chronic low back pain NICE NG59 puts exercise and a return to normal activity at the centre; for chronic primary pain NG193 adds psychological therapy (exercise, ACT/CBT, and — for chronic primary pain but not low back pain — acupuncture). These are the treatments, not the consolation prize, and Neil has never been offered them.
🏃
Group exercise programme
Target: supervised programme, then 150 min/week of activity he chooses
Mechanism

Graded activity reconditions deconditioned muscles, reduces fear of movement by exposure, improves sleep and mood, and engages descending pain inhibition. "Hurt does not equal harm" becomes a lived experience rather than a slogan.

Practical

Refer to physiotherapy / an exercise-based back programme. Start below his current tolerance (pacing), increase by small fixed amounts rather than "until it hurts", and expect flare-ups — plan for them in advance. Choice of exercise matters less than doing it consistently.

NICE NG59: consider a group exercise programme for a specific episode or flare of low back pain; NG193 recommends supervised group exercise for chronic primary pain
🧠
Psychological therapy for pain (ACT / CBT)
Target: referral agreed at this visit
Mechanism

A CBT-informed approach addresses catastrophising, fear-avoidance, low mood and unhelpful beliefs about damage, while the physical component rebuilds capacity. ACT helps people pursue valued activities alongside pain rather than waiting for it to go.

Practical

Pain-management programme (usually group-based, via the local pain service or MSK service). 📚
Explanation & pain education
Target: Neil can explain "sensitised alarm" in his own words
Mechanism

Persistent pain reflects a sensitised nervous system more than ongoing tissue damage — the "over-sensitive alarm". Understanding this reduces threat, fear and the drive to seek stronger drugs, and makes exercise and tapering feel safe.

Practical

Use simple analogies (a car alarm that goes off when someone walks past), reassure that normal scans are genuinely good news, and give written or online resources. Revisit at each appointment — one explanation is rarely enough.

Mechanism

Poor sleep lowers pain thresholds and worsens mood; pain and alcohol fragment sleep further. Opioids themselves disturb sleep architecture and can worsen sleep-disordered breathing.

Practical

Behavioural approaches first (consistent wake time, limiting daytime naps, stimulus control; digital CBT for insomnia where commissioned). Avoid adding hypnotics — a benzodiazepine or Z-drug on top of opioids and a gabapentinoid is the combination MHRA warns can be fatal.

Target: ≤14 units/week spread over 3+ days; none with night-time doses
Mechanism

Alcohol adds to opioid and gabapentinoid respiratory depression, disrupts sleep, worsens mood, and increases paracetamol liver risk. Reducing it is one of the quickest ways to make Neil safer today.

Practical

Mechanism

Practical

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Changing the medication changes a life — plan for the knock-on effects
🚗
Driving and the law

Driving with certain specified drugs (including morphine) above set blood limits, or while impaired by any drug, is an offence; there is a medical defence if the medicine is taken as directed and driving is not impaired. Advise no driving when drowsy, on starting or changing a dose, during noticeable withdrawal, or after alcohol. Patients do not usually need to tell the DVLA simply because they take prescribed opioids, but must if a medical condition or the medication affects their ability to drive safely (check DVLA guidance).

"On the days after each dose change, or if you feel foggy or drowsy, please don't drive. If you ever feel the tablets affect your driving, you mustn't drive — and we should talk about it."
💼
Work and benefits

A taper and a rehabilitation programme can feel like a threat to a benefits claim or to "proof" of disability. Be explicit that the aim is his wellbeing, that fit notes and reports will reflect his condition honestly, and that improvement at his own pace is not a trap. Employment support services and occupational health can help with a phased return.

"Getting you more active isn't about pushing you back to work before you're ready. It's about you getting more of your life back — and when you feel ready, there's support for the next step."
👪
Family and carers

The family live with the effects of both the medication (sedation, irritability) and the taper (withdrawal, flare-ups). Involving a partner helps with pacing, storage, naloxone and noticing low mood. Securely store all opioids and gabapentinoids — one opioid tablet can be dangerous to a child.

"Would you like your partner to know the plan? It helps to have someone who understands that a rough few days after a reduction is withdrawal settling, not the pain getting worse."
🪞
Identity and stigma

Being on opioids can bring stigma — "addict", "drug-seeker" — which patients often feel keenly. Separate physical dependence (an expected effect of long-term use) from addiction, and use neutral language in the notes and at the pharmacy. Neil's identity as someone who worked and was active is a resource to draw on.

"Your body being used to these tablets is a normal effect of taking them for years — it doesn't make you an addict. We'll reduce them carefully because that's how your body stays comfortable."

🔍 1 — Name the pain type

Nociceptive (acute): WHO ladder, time-limited — paracetamol ± topical/oral NSAID (cautions), short opioid course with a stop date. Neuropathic: amitriptyline/duloxetine/gabapentinoid (not standard analgesics). Chronic primary pain (NG193): exercise, CBT/ACT, acupuncture, consider antidepressant; AVOID opioids/gabapentinoids/NSAIDs/paracetamol. Cancer: WHO ladder + palliative care.

⚖️ 2 — Stewardship & deprescribing

Opioids: little benefit, much harm in chronic non-cancer pain; don't escalate (esp. >90mg OME). Gabapentinoids = controlled drugs; never co-prescribe casually with opioids; opioid+gabapentinoid+alcohol = overdose risk → naloxone. Deprescribe gradually, collaboratively, with support; treat mood/sleep/alcohol; set functional goals; re-screen red flags; don't abandon.
