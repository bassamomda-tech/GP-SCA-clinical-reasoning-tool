📋 Clinical Stem — DVT
A 46-year-old woman with 3 days of a painful, swollen right calf two weeks after a long-haul flight, who is otherwise well but quietly worried about a clot
Grace Adeyemi, 46, presents with 3 days of an aching, swollen right calf that came on without injury. It is warm and tender, and the leg looks bigger than the left. She flew back from Lagos (an 8-hour flight) two weeks ago and has been less active since with a sprained ankle. She takes the combined oral contraceptive pill and is a non-smoker. She has no breathlessness, chest pain or haemoptysis. She is worried because a colleague "had a clot that went to the lungs." She has had some recent unintentional weight loss she attributes to a diet, and is otherwise well.
This stem tests the ability to: recognise the clinical picture of a possible DVT and apply the two-level Wells score to choose the correct pathway (USS within 4 hours if "likely"; D-dimer first if "unlikely", with interim anticoagulation if scanning is delayed); exclude pulmonary embolism (breathlessness, chest pain, haemoptysis, instability); identify provoking factors (flight, immobility, oestrogen-containing contraception) and address them (stop the COCP, switch to a non-oestrogen method); anticoagulate appropriately with a DOAC for confirmed DVT; and consider occult malignancy and thrombophilia where the event is unprovoked. The SCA challenge is communicating uncertainty and the testing pathway clearly while managing the patient's anxiety and the contraception implications.
Scenario A — Provoked DVT (this stem) Long-haul flight + immobility + COCP. Wells, USS pathway, DOAC ≥3 months, stop COCP and switch contraception; address modifiable factors; safety-net for PE.
Scenario B — Unprovoked DVT No identifiable provoking factor. Anticoagulate; consider extended anticoagulation; review for occult cancer (history, examination and baseline bloods — further tests only if symptoms or signs, NICE NG158) and consider thrombophilia/antiphospholipid testing only in the narrow groups NG158 describes.
Scenario C — Suspected PE DVT symptoms + pleuritic chest pain/breathlessness/haemoptysis/tachycardia/hypoxia. PE Wells + pathway; haemodynamic instability → emergency admission.
Scenario D — DVT in pregnancy Wells/D-dimer not validated; proceed to USS; treat with LMWH (not DOAC/warfarin); obstetric/haematology input.
Scenario E — DVT mimics Cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst, calf muscle tear/haematoma, chronic venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, superficial thrombophlebitis. Bilateral swelling → cardiac/renal/hepatic or IVC/pelvic obstruction.
Key variables to adapt for Wells category (likely vs unlikely → USS vs D-dimer); PE features; provoked vs unprovoked (duration, cancer/thrombophilia work-up); pregnancy (LMWH, no DOAC); oestrogen contraception/HRT; renal function and bleeding risk (DOAC choice); proximal vs distal; recurrence; massive/phlegmasia (limb-threatening); active cancer (LMWH/DOAC choice).

She may expect an immediate scan or an instant answer. Naming this lets you set realistic expectations about the timed pathway (Wells, possible D-dimer, ultrasound) and the possibility of interim anticoagulation if the scan is delayed.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Anxiety, the pill conversation, and the burden of anticoagulation

A suspected clot is frightening — patients have often heard of someone who died of a PE. Alongside the diagnostic pathway sit two human conversations: stopping the combined pill (and sorting alternative contraception, with its own implications) and, if DVT is confirmed, starting months of anticoagulation with its bleeding precautions and lifestyle adjustments. Handling the anxiety with clear, staged information, and treating the contraception and anticoagulation conversations as shared decisions, turns a scary episode into a managed one.

😟 Fear of PE

Acknowledge it and act on it visibly by screening the chest; explain that treatment markedly reduces embolism risk.

"I can hear the worry about a clot reaching the lungs — that's exactly why I've checked your breathing carefully, and why, if it is a clot, we start treatment quickly to stop that happening."
💊 The pill conversation

The COCP must stop; plan alternative contraception sensitively (progestogen-only/non-hormonal), as this affects her life.

"If this is a clot, the combined pill needs to stop because the oestrogen adds to the risk. Let's sort a different method that's safe — there are good options that don't carry the same risk."
🩸 Living with anticoagulation

Explain duration, bleeding precautions and what to watch for, framed as manageable.

"If confirmed, you'd take a blood-thinning tablet for at least three months. Most people get on with life normally — I'll go through the things to be careful about and the signs of bleeding to look out for."
🔎 The weight-loss thread

If the event is unprovoked or there are warning features, explain the cancer review without alarm.

"You mentioned losing some weight — because a clot can occasionally be the body's first warning sign, I'd like to do a few routine checks to be thorough. It's precautionary, not because I'm expecting to find anything."

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Grace wants an answer today and wants to know whether the clot will reach her lungs. Name both, then explain the plan.
1
Validate — name the expectation and the fear

Grace expects one of two things: a quick blood test that says yes or no, or reassurance that it is "just a strain". Behind both is her colleague's PE. Name the fear before you explain anything. A patient who is still frightened will not take in the information about the pathway.

"I can see you were hoping for a quick blood test to settle it today, and I can hear how worried you are after what happened to your colleague. Both make complete sense. Let me explain exactly how we'll get you an answer, and how we protect your lungs in the meantime."
2
Explain — share the reasoning

If examination confirms, for example, localised deep-vein tenderness, pitting oedema in the symptomatic leg and calf swelling >3 cm (1 point each), and no alternative diagnosis is at least as likely, her Wells score is 2 or more, so NICE NG158 says the next step is a proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours, not a D-dimer alone. A D-dimer can only rule a clot out when the score is low. If the scan can't be done within 4 hours, she gets a D-dimer and interim anticoagulation, and a scan within 24 hours.

"Your score puts you in the group where a scan is the right test, because the blood test can't rule a clot out on its own for you. 7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Anticoagulation treats the clot, but what the patient does alongside it reduces symptoms, removes the triggers and prevents the next clot. The protocol message is to mobilise, not rest. Stockings are for symptoms, not for routine prevention (NICE NG158). Remove the oestrogen, and plan ahead for future high-risk situations.
🚶
Early Mobilisation
Target: normal walking as pain allows from day one
Mechanism

The calf muscle pump drives venous return. Once therapeutic anticoagulation has started, walking does not "dislodge" the clot. Immobility slows flow and adds to the stasis that caused the clot.

Practical

Many patients assume they should rest, and some relatives insist on it. Say explicitly that bed rest is unnecessary. Elevate the leg when sitting. Short, frequent walks. Ankle-pump exercises while seated, which also helps the sprain rehab.

Early walking on anticoagulation is safe and eases pain and swelling faster than rest
💊
Stop the Oestrogen
Target: combined pill stopped today, safe method started today
Mechanism

Ethinylestradiol raises hepatic production of clotting factors and lowers natural anticoagulants. Carrying on with it keeps a provoking factor in place while you are trying to treat the clot.

Practical

Combined pill, patch and ring are all UKMEC 4 with current or past VTE (FSRH UKMEC 2025). Offer a progestogen-only pill, implant, LNG-IUS or copper IUD. Condoms until the new method is effective. The LNG-IUS also helps DOAC-related heavy periods.

Removes a modifiable cause; supports stopping at 3 months
🧦
Compression — Symptoms Only
Target: stockings only for persistent post-thrombotic symptoms
Mechanism

Graduated compression reduces venous hypertension and oedema. A large placebo-controlled trial (SOX) found it did not prevent post-thrombotic syndrome.

Practical

NICE NG158: do not offer elastic graduated compression stockings to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome or VTE recurrence after proximal DVT. They can be used for persistent leg symptoms such as aching or swelling. Check ABPI or pulses first if arterial disease is possible.

Stops a common outdated prescription; targets stockings at symptoms
✈️
Travel Advice
Target: a plan for every journey over about 4 hours
Mechanism

Prolonged seated immobility, cabin conditions and dehydration all add to venous stasis. After one VTE, the risk from future long journeys is higher than for the general population.

Practical

While anticoagulated she is already protected. Wait until she is stable on treatment and symptoms have settled before flying, and ask the anticoagulation service if travel is planned soon. After stopping, plan long-haul trips: aisle seat, walk regularly, calf exercises, hydrate, avoid alcohol and sedatives, and consider properly fitted flight stockings. Specialist advice on prophylaxis for very long or high-risk journeys. Declare the VTE for travel insurance.

7D — Prescribing guide: NICE NG158 anticoagulant pathway
NICE NG158 (2020, last updated August 2023) sets the anticoagulant choice for confirmed proximal DVT. Apixaban or rivaroxaban first-line. If neither is suitable: LMWH for at least 5 days followed by dabigatran or edoxaban, or LMWH with a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin). Choose by renal function, comorbidity, interactions, adherence and patient preference. There are four situations where the default changes: pregnancy, antiphospholipid syndrome, severe renal impairment and extremes of weight. Doses: always per current BNF/SPC. Both first-line DOACs start with a higher-dose loading phase that must be stepped down on time.
Step 1 — First-line: apixaban or rivaroxaban

Apixaban or rivaroxaban — dose per BNF (loading phase then maintenance)

  • No LMWH lead-in needed. Can be started as interim anticoagulation while waiting for the scan and continued if the scan confirms DVT (NICE NG158)
  • Apixaban loading phase lasts 7 days; rivaroxaban's lasts 21 days. Put the step-down date on the prescription and tell the patient
  • Rivaroxaban treatment doses must be taken with food; apixaban can be taken with or without food
  • Baseline FBC, U&E with creatinine clearance, LFTs, PT/APTT. Start without waiting for the results
Grace: apixaban (twice daily, no food requirement) or rivaroxaban (once daily after loading, with food). Choose by her preference and by heavy-period history.
Step 2 — DOAC first-line unsuitable

LMWH ≥5 days → dabigatran or edoxaban; or LMWH + warfarin

  • Dabigatran and edoxaban are licensed for VTE only after at least 5 days of parenteral anticoagulation (NICE NG158)
  • Warfarin: overlap with LMWH for at least 5 days and until the INR is in range (usual target 2.5, range 2.0–3.0) — run by the anticoagulation clinic
  • Warfarin is preferred in antiphospholipid syndrome, and is an option where DOAC interactions or renal function rule a DOAC out
  • LMWH dose is weight-based, per BNF and the local protocol
Dabigatran is the most renally cleared DOAC and is not licensed below CrCl 30 ml/min. Check the SPC before choosing it in CKD.
Step 3 — Duration decision at 3 months

Provoked → usually stop · Unprovoked → consider continuing

  • All confirmed proximal DVT: at least 3 months (NICE NG158)
  • Provoked, with the factor resolved: usually stop at 3 months. This fits Grace if the combined pill has been stopped and she is fully mobile
  • Unprovoked: consider continuing beyond 3 months (6 months if active cancer), balancing recurrence risk against bleeding risk, and discuss it with the patient. NG158 suggests considering the HAS-BLED score for bleeding risk, but not relying on risk tools alone (NICE NG158)
  • Some DOACs have a lower-dose regimen for extended prevention. Check the BNF/SPC and haematology advice
Don't let the 3-month review drift. Patients left on a DOAC "by default", with no decision recorded, are a common audit finding and a bleeding risk.
