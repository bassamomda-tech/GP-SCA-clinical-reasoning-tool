📋 Clinical Stem — Addison's Disease
A 38-year-old woman with months of "burnout" — exhaustion, weight loss, dizziness and salt craving — repeatedly attributed to stress, now looking tanned despite no sun
Hannah Whitfield, 38, attends for the third time in four months with overwhelming tiredness, low mood and "no appetite". She has lost about 6kg without trying, feels dizzy on standing, and has started craving salty food. Previous visits diagnosed "stress/burnout" and possible depression. She mentions, almost in passing, that friends keep saying how tanned she looks even though she hasn't been in the sun, and that her old appendix scar has gone dark. She has vitiligo and her mother has an underactive thyroid. Today she also feels nauseated and "wiped out", and her blood pressure is low with a marked postural drop.
This stem tests the ability to: recognise Addison's disease behind a "burnout/depression" presentation — the great mimic — by pulling together fatigue, unintentional weight loss, anorexia/nausea, postural dizziness, salt craving and (the specific clue) hyperpigmentation in a patient with personal/family autoimmune disease; check the right initial bloods (U&E for low Na/high K, glucose, 9am cortisol) and arrange/seek the short Synacthen test; recognise the features of impending adrenal crisis (postural hypotension, nausea, intercurrent stress) and know that crisis is treated immediately with parenteral hydrocortisone and fluids without waiting for results; explain lifelong steroid replacement, sick-day rules, the emergency injection kit and steroid alert card; and screen for associated autoimmune conditions. The SCA challenge is taking a repeatedly dismissed woman seriously, and conveying a serious lifelong diagnosis with appropriate safety-netting.
Scenario A — New diagnosis from chronic symptoms (this stem) The mimic picture + pigmentation + autoimmune background. Bloods (U&E, glucose, 9am cortisol, ACTH), Synacthen, urgent endocrine referral; assess for impending crisis; safety-net.
Scenario B — Adrenal crisis (EMERGENCY) Hypotension/shock, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, often precipitated by infection or missed doses. Immediate IV/IM hydrocortisone + fluids + glucose; 999/admit; don't wait for tests.
Scenario C — Known Addison's, intercurrent illness Established patient with infection/vomiting/surgery. Apply sick-day rules (double dose or parenteral); emergency injection if vomiting; low threshold for admission.
Scenario D — Secondary adrenal insufficiency Long-term/abruptly stopped exogenous steroids, or pituitary disease. No hyperpigmentation; ACTH low; aldosterone usually preserved. Steroid-withdrawal/pituitary management.
Scenario E — Autoimmune polyendocrine screen Coexisting thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anaemia, coeliac, vitiligo. Screen and monitor associated conditions.

📖 NICE NG243 — who to think about adrenal insufficiency in

NICE NG243 (2024) asks clinicians to think about adrenal insufficiency when a patient has symptoms such as fatigue, weight loss, postural dizziness, nausea or abdominal pain, salt craving or hyperpigmentation, or has unexplained hyponatraemia, recurrent hypoglycaemia, or a relevant history of glucocorticoid exposure. The trap Hannah has fallen into is that these symptoms are non-specific and easy to put down to psychological or functional causes. Every time a patient comes back with the same unexplained symptoms, it is a chance to reconsider the diagnosis. Three visits for fatigue plus weight loss is a pattern, not a coincidence.

Oral oestrogen raises cortisol-binding globulin, and recent exogenous glucocorticoids (including inhaled, topical and injected steroids) suppress the axis or cross-react with the assay (prednisolone does; dexamethasone does not). ⏱ Practical points for the GP
  • Timing matters. Cortisol peaks early in the morning, and a sample taken in the afternoon is hard to interpret. Book a slot between 8 and 9am. For a shift worker, discuss timing with the laboratory.
  • Pair it with ACTH if your laboratory accepts it (the sample usually has to reach the lab quickly, on ice or as an EDTA sample per local rules). A high ACTH with a low cortisol shows the problem is in the adrenal gland.
  • The short Synacthen test (tetracosactide, with cortisol measured at baseline and 30 minutes) is usually arranged by endocrinology or a hospital day unit, not in general practice. The pass threshold depends on the laboratory and assay.
  • Do not wait for results when the patient is unwell. If crisis is suspected, take a sample only if it causes no delay, then give hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV. Endocrinology can still confirm the diagnosis later, once the patient is stable.
  • Renin and aldosterone: a high renin with a low or inappropriately normal aldosterone confirms mineralocorticoid deficiency in primary disease. These are usually specialist-requested and guide the fludrocortisone dose.
  • A normal U&E does not exclude it. Hyperkalaemia is absent in a significant minority of cases, and secondary adrenal insufficiency causes hyponatraemia without hyperkalaemia.

"Here's what I'd like to do. I'll take some blood now and phone the hospital medical team so they're expecting you this afternoon. They can start treatment and do the confirmatory test. What would make it hard for you to go today? Let's sort that out together."
Key principle: In a patient on the edge of decompensation, the GP's job is not to make the diagnosis today but to prevent the crisis. NICE NG243 is explicit that steroid treatment for suspected adrenal crisis must never be delayed for tests. Being believed and being safe are the same message here: "I believe you, and because I believe you, I want you seen today."

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
In Addison's disease, the "non-drug" management is mostly education, equipment and systems, and it is what prevents deaths. Crises keep happening even in educated patients, and they are mostly triggered by gastrointestinal illness, infection, missed doses and procedures. Each card below targets one of those failure points.
📋
Sick-Day Rules Education
Target: patient and household can recite them
Mechanism

A healthy adrenal gland raises cortisol output several-fold during fever, injury or surgery. A patient on fixed replacement cannot do that, so the extra cortisol has to be taken as extra tablets or an injection. Without it, vascular tone, glucose and sodium balance fail and the patient goes into shock.

Practical

Rule 1: for fever or illness, increase the oral glucocorticoid. The site protocol says double; NICE NG243 gives a floor of at least 40 mg hydrocortisone a day in 2–4 divided doses (or at least 10 mg prednisolone) until recovered. Rule 2: vomiting, diarrhoea or unable to keep tablets down means IM hydrocortisone from the kit plus urgent help. The Society for Endocrinology leaflet (2025) adds that mild illness without fever needs no change, and that after a single vomit the patient should take an extra oral dose (hydrocortisone 20 mg or prednisolone 5 mg) and inject and call 999 if vomiting continues. Rehearse the rules at every contact and use teach-back.

Adrenal crisis is largely preventable through education (NICE NG243; site protocol)
💉
Emergency Injection Kit + Family Training
Target: in-date kit at home, work and when travelling
Mechanism

The patient who most needs the injection may be too confused or weak to give it. IM hydrocortisone works within minutes to hours and bridges the gap until paramedics arrive. There is no meaningful overdose risk from a single emergency dose, so doubt should never stop anyone giving it.

Practical

Prescribe hydrocortisone sodium succinate 100 mg vials with water for injection, needles and syringes (or a local equivalent kit). Train Hannah and a partner, friend or colleague, using the Addison's Disease Self-Help Group or Society for Endocrinology videos, the practice nurse, or the endocrine specialist nurse. Put expiry dates on recall. After injecting, always call 999.

Anyone, including the patient, a relative or a carer, can give the IM injection (site protocol, NG243)
🪪
Steroid Emergency Card, Alert ID and Flagged Record
Target: card carried 100% of the time; record coded
Mechanism

Adrenal crisis looks like sepsis, gastroenteritis or an acute abdomen. Without a prompt, a paramedic or emergency department team may give fluids and antibiotics but not hydrocortisone. The red NHS Steroid Emergency Card, introduced by National Patient Safety Alert NatPSA/2020/005/NHSPS in August 2020, tells any clinician what to give and when.

Practical

Issue the card, and suggest a photo of it on her phone plus a medical-alert bracelet or necklace. Code adrenal insufficiency and steroid dependence on the GP record, and consider an alert on the Summary Care Record or local shared record. Ask the ambulance service about registering her where a local scheme exists.

NatPSA 2020: all organisations that initiate or review steroids must issue the card to eligible patients
🧂
Salt, Fluids and Heat
Target: no salt restriction; extra fluids in heat and exercise
Mechanism

In primary disease, aldosterone deficiency causes renal sodium loss, volume depletion and postural hypotension. Salt craving is the body's attempt to compensate. Fludrocortisone replaces aldosterone, but heat, heavy sweating and endurance exercise increase losses further.

Practical

Do not advise the usual "low-salt diet" health message. Hannah can salt food to taste. In hot weather or on holiday in hot countries she should drink more and add salt. The specialist may adjust the fludrocortisone dose. Persistent salt craving or a postural drop on treatment suggests under-replacement, so check BP and U&E and discuss renin with endocrinology.

Salt craving on treatment is a signal of mineralocorticoid under-replacement
✈️
Travel Planning
Target: double supply + kit in hand luggage + doctor's letter
Mechanism

Travel concentrates the risk factors: gastroenteritis, heat, time-zone shifts that disrupt dose timing, lost luggage, and limited access to parenteral hydrocortisone abroad.

Practical

Carry at least double the supply of tablets, split between bags, with the injection kit and a translated or English doctor's letter in hand luggage. Take the card and alert ID. Get travel insurance that declares the condition. Take oral rehydration sachets. Keep dosing by waking time across time zones, and ask the specialist team about long-haul flights. Know how to reach emergency care at the destination.

Traveller's diarrhoea means sick-day rule 2: inject, then seek help
🛡️
Infection Prevention and Vaccination
Target: seasonal vaccines up to date; early action on infection
Mechanism

Infection, especially gastroenteritis and respiratory infection, is the commonest trigger for crisis. Replacement-dose hydrocortisone is not immunosuppressive, but people with adrenal insufficiency are more vulnerable to the physiological stress of infection.

Practical

Offer the seasonal vaccines she is eligible for under the current UKHSA Green Book and local call-recall; check eligibility each season. Infections should be assessed early rather than "sat out". Apply the sick-day rules at the first fever, not the third day. Live vaccines are not contraindicated by physiological replacement doses (UKHSA Green Book, chapter 6).

Infection is the commonest precipitant of adrenal crisis (site protocol)
🦷
Procedures, Surgery and Dental Work
Target: every procedure planned in advance
Mechanism

Anaesthesia, surgery and bowel preparation (fluid loss) are major physiological stressors. The patient cannot mount a stress response and may be nil by mouth.

Practical

The 2020 guideline from the Association of Anaesthetists, the Royal College of Physicians and the Society for Endocrinology (Woodcock et al., Anaesthesia) recommends hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction then 200 mg/24 h by infusion for major surgery, with procedure-specific cover for lesser procedures. The GP's role is to make sure every surgeon, anaesthetist, dentist and endoscopy unit knows in advance (the card, the referral letter), so cover is planned rather than improvised. Colonoscopy bowel preparation and significant dental work under local anaesthetic also need a plan agreed with the team or endocrinology.

Peri-procedure cover is a named duty in the site protocol and the NatPSA
🤝
Peer Support and Self-Management
Target: signposted at diagnosis
Mechanism

Quality of life is reduced in a proportion of treated patients, with residual fatigue, anxiety about crisis and "not feeling normal". Peer support normalises the experience and spreads practical know-how, such as how to explain the condition at work and how to travel.

Practical

Signpost the Addison's Disease Self-Help Group (ADSHG), and the Pituitary Foundation for secondary causes. Offer the Society for Endocrinology patient leaflets. Screen mood with PHQ-9 at review and offer NHS Talking Therapies if needed. Hannah's earlier low mood may lift with replacement, but check that it has.

Signposting the ADSHG is part of the site protocol's "Living with it" standard
7D — Prescribing guide: NICE NG243 crisis doses and lifelong replacement
NICE NG243 (2024) sets the UK framework. Treat crisis first with parenteral hydrocortisone. Lifelong replacement is started by the specialist and continued in primary care. In primary adrenal insufficiency, both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid are replaced. In secondary or tertiary insufficiency, only the glucocorticoid. Doses below come from NG243 where it states them; otherwise the dose is per BNF and specialist.
Step 0 — Adrenal crisis (any setting)

Hydrocortisone 100 mg IM or IV immediately (adults and children aged 6+)

  • Children: 25 mg under 1 year, 50 mg aged 1–5 years, 100 mg aged 6 and over (BSPED age-based doses adopted by NG243)
  • Call 999. In hospital: IV 0.9% saline (site protocol: 1 L over 30 minutes in adults), check glucose and treat hypoglycaemia, find and treat the trigger
  • Take a random cortisol ± ACTH first only if it does not delay treatment
  • No meaningful overdose risk: doubt is not a reason to withhold it
GP surgery: keep hydrocortisone sodium succinate 100 mg in the emergency drug bag, and check it with the rest of the emergency drugs.
Step 1 — Glucocorticoid replacement (all)

Hydrocortisone, usually 15–25 mg/day in adults, in 2–3 divided doses (NG243)

  • Largest dose on waking to mimic the circadian peak, e.g. 10 mg on waking, 5 mg at midday, 5 mg mid-afternoon (the site protocol's example)
  • Last dose not too late in the evening, to avoid insomnia
  • Titrate to symptoms, weight, BP and signs of over-replacement, never to a single random cortisol
  • Alternatives (specialist choice): prednisolone once daily; modified-release hydrocortisone (Plenadren, Efmody). If on modified-release, the patient also needs immediate-release hydrocortisone for sick days (NG243)
Supply extra tablets for sick-day dosing on the repeat template (NG243). Running out on a bank holiday is a crisis risk.
Step 2 — Mineralocorticoid (primary only)

Fludrocortisone once daily, dose per BNF and specialist

  • Needed in primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's). Not usually needed in secondary disease, because the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system still works
  • Titrated to postural BP, sodium and potassium, and plasma renin (specialist), plus symptoms such as salt craving
  • Too much causes oedema, hypertension and hypokalaemia. Too little causes postural dizziness, salt craving and a high renin
  • Do not double fludrocortisone on sick days. Only the glucocorticoid is increased
If a patient with Addison's develops hypertension, check for fludrocortisone over-replacement before adding an antihypertensive.

7F — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Addison's: from "just stress" to a lifelong, invisible, potentially fatal condition in one consultation
Hannah goes from being told she is stressed to being told she has a rare, lifelong hormone condition that could kill her if she gets gastroenteritis. The relief of being believed often comes with grief, anger at the delay, and a new fear of ordinary illness. The GP who addresses only the prescription misses the person who now has to rely on tablets, a red card and an injection kit, and has to teach her family to use them.
💼
Work

Months of untreated fatigue may already have hurt her performance or attendance. After starting replacement, recovery usually takes weeks, and some people have residual fatigue. Addison's is a long-term condition and is likely to count as a disability under the Equality Act 2010, which entitles her to reasonable adjustments: flexibility for dose timing, sick-day absence without penalty, a kit kept at work and a trained colleague.

Fit note for the recovery period if needed. Occupational health referral for shift work, heat or physically demanding roles.

"Would it help to talk through what you tell work? Many people keep a spare injection kit at work and show a colleague how to use it. Would anyone there be willing?"
🚗
Driving and DVLA

Addison's disease itself is not usually a condition that car or motorcycle (Group 1) licence holders must report to DVLA. Bus, coach and lorry (Group 2) licence holders must tell DVLA (GOV.UK / NHS inform guidance). The practical risks are driving while in impending crisis (dizziness, confusion) or hypoglycaemia. If the cause is secondary (a pituitary tumour), DVLA rules for the tumour and any visual field loss apply separately.

"You don't need to tell DVLA for an ordinary car licence. But don't drive if you feel faint, confused or are vomiting, and keep your card and kit in the car too."
👨‍👩‍👧
Family and Carers

Family members become part of the safety system. They need to recognise crisis and to give the injection. That can feel like a heavy responsibility, especially for a partner, older child or parent. Her mother's thyroid disease also raises the question of family members' own autoimmune risk.

"Could whoever you live with come to the nurse appointment? It's much less frightening once you've practised the injection together, and it means they know what to do."
🤰
Fertility, Pregnancy and Contraception

Women with Addison's can have healthy pregnancies with joint obstetric–endocrine care. Replacement may need adjusting in later pregnancy, labour needs stress-dose cover, and hyperemesis is a crisis risk (it is sick-day rule 2). Pre-conception advice matters. Oral oestrogen affects total cortisol measurements, which matters for interpretation rather than safety.

"If you're thinking about pregnancy at any point, tell us early. It's very possible, and the specialist team will plan the doses with you."
🧠
Mood, Anger and Trust

Anger at the diagnostic delay is common and legitimate. Some patients also have health anxiety about crisis and hyper-vigilance with every illness. Low mood may be partly due to the cortisol deficiency and improve with replacement, but it should be rechecked rather than assumed. An honest acknowledgement of the delay, rather than defensiveness, rebuilds trust. Significant events and learning should be handled through practice processes.

"I'm sorry it's taken this long. It's understandable if you feel angry. I'd like to make sure your care from here is joined up, and you can always come back to talk about how you're coping."
🪪
Identity and an Invisible Condition

Addison's is invisible: patients look well until they are very unwell. Carrying a red card, wearing an alert bracelet and explaining the condition to friends, schools, gyms or new clinicians can feel stigmatising at first. Peer support (ADSHG) helps people make the condition part of normal life rather than a threat hanging over it.

"Lots of people with Addison's run marathons, travel the world and have families. The card and the kit are what let you do all that safely."

7I — Safety-netting: exact phrases, RAG marking and danger zones

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — adrenal crisis
"If you start vomiting and can't keep anything down, or you feel faint, confused or much weaker, or you get severe tummy pain, that is an emergency. Ring 999 straight away, say 'I may have adrenal insufficiency', and show them this letter. Please don't wait to see if it passes."
Before diagnosis there is no kit or card, so the phrase itself and the letter or record flag have to do that job. Adrenal crisis can progress to shock within hours, and patients often delay because the early symptoms feel like gastroenteritis.
💊 After diagnosis — the two sick-day rules
"Rule one: if you have a fever or you're ill enough to need antibiotics, double your hydrocortisone until you're better. Rule two: if you're vomiting or have bad diarrhoea and can't keep tablets down, give the injection into your thigh and then ring 999. If in doubt, inject. It can't harm you."
Short, concrete, two-rule wording is remembered better than a list. "Double" matches the site protocol, and with Hannah's likely 20 mg/day it also meets the NICE NG243 floor of at least 40 mg/day. "If in doubt, inject" removes the most common delay.
🟠 Supply and procedures
"Never let your tablets run out. Order more when you open your last box. Before any operation, scan, bowel prep or big dental work, show them your red steroid card and ask about extra steroid cover. And if you're ever told to stop your steroid, check with us or your specialist first."
Missed or stopped doses and unplanned procedures are common, preventable triggers. The NatPSA (2020) exists because these hand-offs keep failing.
Why safety-netting matters beyond clinical care
Document the differential and the reason for same-day assessment: "suspected adrenal insufficiency; nausea and postural drop; same-day medical assessment arranged; handover call to medical registrar".
Document the crisis safety-net given, in words the patient understood, and that it was checked with teach-back.
Once diagnosed: code the condition, add a record alert, confirm the Steroid Emergency Card was issued (NatPSA 2020), and note that the IM kit was prescribed and training booked.
Repeated presentations with unexplained fatigue and weight loss: record the reconsideration of the diagnosis. Diagnostic delay in Addison's is a recognised theme in complaints and significant event reviews.

🚫 8 Danger Zones
✗
Re-labelling as stress or depression→ Third attendance, weight loss and pigmentation: reconsider the diagnosis. U&E + 8–9am cortisol (NG243).
✗
Routine outpatient test while decompensating→ Nausea plus postural drop: same-day assessment. The outpatient pathway is for stable patients only.
✗
Waiting for cortisol before treating crisis→ Hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV first. Sample only if it causes no delay (NG243).
✗
Starting levothyroxine first→ In combined deficiency, replace glucocorticoid before thyroxine, or thyroxine can precipitate crisis.
✗
No kit, card or trained second person→ Every patient: IM hydrocortisone kit, NHS Steroid Emergency Card (NatPSA 2020), alert ID, someone else trained.
✗
Doubling fludrocortisone on sick days→ Only the glucocorticoid is increased.
✗
Missing exogenous-steroid adrenal insufficiency→ Ask about inhaled, topical and injected steroids. Prednisolone ≥5 mg/day for ≥4 weeks, BDP >1000 µg/day or fluticasone >500 µg/day inhaled → card and sick-day advice.
✗
Low-salt advice or an antihypertensive without review→ In primary AI, salt is not restricted. New hypertension → check for fludrocortisone over-replacement first.
