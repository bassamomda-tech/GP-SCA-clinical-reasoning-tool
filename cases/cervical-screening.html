📋 Clinical Stem — Cervical Screening
A 34-year-old woman who has ignored three screening invitations out of fear and embarrassment, attending today about something else, who also mentions some bleeding after sex
Aisha Rahman, 34, books to discuss contraception, and her record shows she is overdue for cervical screening, having declined the last three invitations. When gently asked, she admits she finds the test embarrassing and is frightened it will hurt or "find something bad"; a previous smear years ago was uncomfortable. As the consultation develops, she also mentions she's had some bleeding after sex over the past couple of months, which she'd assumed was "normal". She has had a normal smear in the past and is otherwise well. She is unsure what HPV is and worries that a positive result means she's "done something wrong".
This stem tests two distinct things at once: (1) the SCREENING task — explaining HPV-primary cervical screening (what it tests, why intervals changed, what results mean), exploring and addressing the barriers to a fearful non-attender non-judgmentally, offering choice and support, and counselling about HPV without shame; and crucially (2) recognising that her postcoital bleeding is a SYMPTOM, not a screening matter — it requires clinical assessment with speculum examination of the cervix and, if the cervix looks abnormal, an urgent suspected-cancer (NICE NG12) referral, irrespective of any previous or current smear result. The SCA challenge is to support and encourage screening while not allowing a "normal smear" to falsely reassure away a red-flag symptom — separating screening from symptomatic assessment.
Scenario A — Fearful non-attender + a symptom (this stem) Explore barriers, explain HPV screening and results, de-shame HPV; but ALSO recognise postcoital bleeding as a symptom needing speculum examination ± NG12 referral — not a smear.
Scenario B — Explaining an HPV-positive result HPV+ with normal cytology → repeat 12 months; HPV+ with abnormal cytology → colposcopy; reassure re what HPV is, that it's common, and that positive ≠ cancer/blame.
Scenario C — Routine counselling/eligibility Ages/intervals, vaccination status, what the test involves; offering a female sample-taker, chaperone, longer appointment.
Scenario D — Special situations Pregnancy (defer routine), post-hysterectomy (usually ceases), immunosuppression/HIV (more frequent), previous CIN/test of cure, ceasing at 65.
Scenario E — Inclusion & access Trans/non-binary patients with a cervix, learning disability, previous sexual trauma, language/cultural barriers — tailored, trauma-informed, consent-led approaches; self-sampling where available.

This consultation runs on two tracks that must never be confused: the screening conversation (a fearful non-attender who needs barriers explored and HPV explained without shame) and the symptomatic alarm (postcoital bleeding, which is a clinical problem needing examination — not a smear). The single most important reasoning move is to hear the postcoital bleeding and pull it out of the "she just needs her smear" frame.
🎓 SCA framing — encourage screening, but don't let it mask a symptom
"I'd love to help you feel more comfortable about screening, and we'll come to that. But first — you mentioned some bleeding after sex. That's a symptom I always want to check properly, separately from screening, so let's make sure we look at that too."
The trap is to focus entirely on coaxing a reluctant patient into a smear and miss that her symptom needs a speculum examination and possibly an urgent referral. Screening is for the well; symptoms get assessed.

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you think the test is for, and what's worried you about it?"
Aisha fears pain and "finding something bad", and thinks a positive HPV result implies blame. Surfacing this lets you correct misconceptions (HPV is common, not shameful; the test is quick and you can stop at any point) and demystify the process — which is what actually changes attendance.
😟 Concerns
"Is it the discomfort, the embarrassment, or the fear of the result that worries you most?"
Naming the specific barrier lets you tailor support — a female sample-taker, a smaller speculum, a longer appointment, going at her pace, stopping if she wishes. It also opens the door to any past trauma that needs a trauma-informed approach.
🎯 Expectations
"What would help you feel able to do this — today or another time?"
She controls the decision. Naming her expectations lets you offer genuine choice (now, a booked longer appointment, with a chaperone), respect her autonomy, and — separately — explain why the bleeding needs examining regardless, so she leaves clear on both.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Fear, shame, autonomy — and the symptom hiding behind the screening conversation

Cervical screening sits at the intersection of fear, embarrassment, bodily autonomy and sometimes trauma — which is why invitations get declined and why a supportive, unhurried, consent-led, trauma-informed approach matters more than persuasion. But this case carries a second lesson: a reluctant-screener consultation can quietly contain a red-flag symptom. The skill is to do both well — to support and empower her around screening without any coercion, AND to lift her postcoital bleeding out of the screening frame and assess it properly, because a smear will never be the right answer to a symptom.

🙇 De-shaming HPV

Explain HPV is common and not about blame.

"HPV is incredibly common — most people who've ever been sexually active get it at some point, and it usually clears on its own. A positive result doesn't mean you've done anything wrong; it just tells us to keep a closer eye."
🤲 Choice & control

Offer options and emphasise she's in control.

"You're in charge here — we can use a smaller speculum, have a female nurse, take it slowly, and you can ask me to stop at any moment. We can also book a longer appointment if today doesn't feel right."
🧭 Separating symptom from screening

Be explicit that the bleeding is assessed regardless.

"Whatever you decide about screening, the bleeding after sex is something I'd want to look at properly — that's a check for a symptom, which is different from screening, and it's important we don't put it off."
🛡️ Trauma-informed care

Be alert to past trauma; never coerce.

"If there's ever been a difficult or painful experience that makes this hard, you don't have to tell me the details — but we can absolutely adapt how we do things so you feel safe and in control."
1E — Psychosocial context: who is under-screened, and why
🫂
Non-attendance is rarely indifference: it is usually fear, pain, shame, trauma, access or not having been told the right thing
Around a third of people eligible for cervical screening in England are not up to date (NHS England, August 2026). The people who have never been screened or are furthest overdue carry a disproportionate share of cervical cancer risk, so the fearful non-attender in front of you is exactly the person the programme most needs to reach. Ask about barriers in the history, not only in the plan: the barrier determines the support you offer, and the right support is what converts an invitation into an informed decision. Each card below is a common barrier with a question that opens it up.
😣
Fear of pain and a previous bad experience

A painful or rushed previous test is one of the commonest reasons people stop attending. Pain is often avoidable: a smaller speculum, more lubricant, a different position (left lateral, or hands under the buttocks to tilt the pelvis), inserting the speculum herself, and a sample-taker who explains and pauses on request. After the menopause, vaginal atrophy makes the test uncomfortable, and a short course of vaginal oestrogen beforehand can help (check local guidance on timing before sampling).

"You mentioned the last one was uncomfortable — can you tell me what happened? There's quite a lot we can change so it's not like that again."
🛡️
Past sexual trauma

A speculum examination can re-trigger memories of sexual violence, and people often do not disclose this. A trauma-informed approach does not need the details. It needs control handed back: agreeing a stop signal, letting her decide who is in the room, going at her pace, and offering a longer appointment or a pre-visit to meet the nurse first. Where trauma is disclosed, consider safeguarding, whether support services are wanted, and whether the abuse is ongoing.

"Some people find this test hard because of things that have happened in the past. You don't need to tell me anything, but if that's part of it, we can do things very differently so you're in charge."
🕌
Culture, faith, language and modesty

Modesty norms, family expectations, the assumption that screening is only for people with many partners, and stigma around anything sexual can all stop people coming forward. Offer a female sample-taker as standard, a professional interpreter (never a family member for an intimate consultation), and translated or easy-read resources. Frame screening as routine health care for everyone with a cervix, not a judgement on sexual behaviour.

"Everyone with a cervix is invited, whatever their relationship history — it's a routine check, like a blood pressure check, not a judgement about anyone's private life."
🏳️‍⚧️
Trans men and non-binary people with a cervix

People registered with the GP as male are not invited automatically, but anyone aged 25–64 with a cervix who has ever had sex can opt in to routine invitations through the practice (NHS Cervical Screening Programme opt-in guidance for transgender and non-binary people). Testosterone can cause vaginal atrophy, making the test more uncomfortable and cytology harder to read. Dysphoria is a real barrier. Use their chosen name and pronouns, offer the first or last appointment of the day, and make sure the lab request carries the right information. Trans women who have had vaginoplasty have no cervix and do not need screening.

"Because you're registered as male you won't get automatic letters, but you can still have screening. Would you like me to set up invitations for you, and talk about what would make the appointment easier?"
🧩
Learning disability, autism and physical disability

People with a learning disability are screened far less often than the general population, sometimes because others assume it isn't needed. The Equality Act 2010 requires reasonable adjustments: easy-read information, a pre-visit to see the room and the equipment, longer or first appointments, a familiar supporter present, and flexibility on position. Capacity to consent is decision-specific. Do not assume someone lacks it. If they do lack capacity, a best-interests decision under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 involves family, carers and the learning disability team, and taking into account whether the person has ever been sexually active. Wheelchair users need an accessible room and a height-adjustable couch, and may need a hoist.

"Would it help to come in first just to see the room and meet the nurse, without having the test that day?"
⏰
Time, work, childcare and access

Practical barriers are underestimated. Appointments that clash with shifts, no childcare, and not knowing that sexual health clinics can also take samples all add up. Offer early, late or weekend slots, and tell her about the HPV self-sampling kit. From August 2026 NHS England is rolling out, in phases, invitations to order a free home kit, starting with people aged 30–65 who are at least six months overdue. For someone who dreads a speculum, a self-sample may be the difference between no screening and some screening.

"If coming in for the test is the hard part, there's now a kit you can do at home — a simple swab you post back. It won't replace everything, but it's a very good first step."
💔
HPV, relationships and fear of blame

Aisha worries that a positive HPV result means she has "done something wrong". Many people fear it means their partner was unfaithful. HPV can lie dormant for years or decades. It cannot be dated to a particular partner, and most sexually active people have it at some point. Getting this message across before the test reduces the anxiety that stops people attending, and reduces the distress if the result is positive.

"HPV can stay quietly in the body for many years, so a positive result can't tell us when or from whom you got it. It isn't a sign that anyone has been unfaithful."
😰
Fear of cancer ("I'd rather not know")

Some people avoid screening because they fear a cancer diagnosis. Reframe it. Screening mostly finds changes before cancer develops, when treatment is simple and usually done in an outpatient clinic. For Aisha, this fear also explains why she half-ignored her bleeding. Name it gently, because it may make her more likely to put off being examined.

"Screening is really about finding changes long before they could ever become cancer. That's when they're easiest to treat — usually a quick clinic procedure."

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Aisha's expectation: "just do the smear today and that'll check the bleeding too". Acknowledge it, explain why the two are different, then agree a plan
1
Validate: name the expectation and the courage behind it

For someone who has put screening off three times, offering to "get it all done at once" is a big step, and it deserves to be recognised. Her request also makes sense: she thinks a smear is a general check of the cervix. If you say "no" without acknowledging this, you risk losing her to both the examination and the screening.

"I really appreciate you being willing to be examined today — I know it's the thing you've been dreading, and that's a big step. It makes complete sense to want to get everything done in one go."
2
Explain: a screening test cannot answer a symptom

Screening is designed for people without symptoms. It tests for high-risk HPV, not for cancer. A negative HPV result would not explain her bleeding, and it could reassure both of you when it shouldn't. With a symptom, the test is to look at the cervix directly, take swabs for infection, and refer if anything looks abnormal. If the cervix is healthy, the screening sample can be taken at the same visit once she is ready, but it is a separate decision with a separate purpose.

"The screening test looks for a virus, to find people who might develop changes in the future. It isn't designed to explain bleeding, and a normal result could give us false comfort. So for the bleeding, I want to look at your cervix myself and take some swabs — that's the right test."
3
Negotiate: offer both, at her pace, in her control

Agree what happens today: an examination with a chaperone and STI swabs, with her in control. Then give her a real choice about screening. If the cervix looks healthy, the sample can be taken at the same examination if she wants, and she is due, so she doesn't need another speculum. Or she can book a longer appointment with a female nurse. If she can't face a speculum for screening, the home self-sampling kit is an option. Whatever she chooses for screening, the bleeding is still examined, and a suspicious cervix is referred urgently.

"Here's what I'd suggest: I examine you today for the bleeding, with a chaperone, and you can stop me at any point. If your cervix looks healthy and you'd like to, we can take your screening sample at the same time so you don't need to come back — but that's entirely your choice."
Key principle: the NHS Cervical Screening Programme's guidance for sample takers is clear that a screening sample is not a diagnostic test for symptoms. A symptomatic person needs a clinical examination and referral based on the symptoms and what you see. It is reasonable to take a routine screening sample at the same visit when the person is due, the cervix looks normal and they consent, but you must still act on the symptom whatever the screening result is. Never let "the smear was fine" close a symptom pathway.

7C — Non-drug management: what actually gets people screened and protected
In this topic, "management" is mostly communication, access and prevention. The cards below describe the interventions that move a fearful non-attender to an informed decision, and the prevention messages that sit alongside screening. Each one gives the mechanism, how to do it in practice, and a target.
🗣️
Explain HPV without blame
Target: patient can say in her own words what the test looks for
Mechanism

Fear and shame about what a result "means" are major barriers. Explaining that high-risk HPV causes almost all cervical cancers, that most sexually active people get it, that it usually clears within a couple of years, and that it can lie dormant for years reduces stigma and anticipatory anxiety.

Practical

Use chunk-and-check. Explain the order: the sample is tested for HPV first, and cells are only looked at if HPV is found. Say plainly that a positive HPV result is not cancer, is not a sign of infidelity, and usually means "watch", not "treat". Offer the NHS leaflet in her language.

Informed choice is the programme standard, not uptake at any cost
🪶
Comfort adjustments
Target: a test she would be willing to repeat
Mechanism

A painful test causes future non-attendance. Much of the discomfort comes from speculum size, angle, a dry or atrophic vagina, and muscle guarding because of fear. Changing these, and giving back control, reduces pain.

Practical

Smaller or plastic speculum, water-based lubricant, alternative positions, self-insertion of the speculum, an agreed stop signal, a longer appointment and a female sample-taker. After the menopause or on testosterone, consider a short course of vaginal oestrogen first (follow local guidance on timing). Record what helped so next time is easier.

One good experience changes future attendance
📦
HPV self-sampling (home kit)
Target: offer to anyone eligible who won't have a speculum test
Mechanism

A vaginal swab taken by the person themselves detects high-risk HPV with accuracy comparable to a clinician-taken sample for HPV testing. It removes the speculum and the appointment. The UK National Screening Committee recommended self-sampling for under-screened people in 2025.

Practical

NHS England began a phased rollout in August 2026: people aged 30–65 who are at least six months overdue are invited (NHS App, text, email or letter) to order a free kit to their home. HPV-negative → routine recall. HPV-positive → invited for a clinician-taken sample so cytology can be done. It is a screening test only. It is never the answer for someone with symptoms, including Aisha's bleeding.

YouScreen: ~400,000 more people screened a year
💉
HPV vaccination
Target: every eligible person vaccinated before their 25th birthday
Mechanism

The vaccine prevents infection with the high-risk HPV types that cause most cervical cancers. Since 1 September 2023 the universal school programme (age 12–13, all genders) and the GBMSM programme for those under 25 use a single dose (GBMSM aged 25–45 still have 2 doses) (UKHSA, following JCVI advice). People who are immunosuppressed or living with HIV need more doses, as set out in the Green Book.

Practical

People who missed the school dose can catch up via their GP until their 25th birthday. Aged 34, Aisha is past this, so vaccination is not a routine NHS offer for her. Vaccinated people must still attend screening, because the vaccine does not cover every high-risk type and does not clear an existing infection.

Vaccination + screening = route to elimination
🚭
Smoking cessation
Target: offer a referral to stop-smoking support if she smokes
Mechanism

Smoking is an established co-factor for cervical cancer. It is associated with HPV persistence and progression to high-grade CIN, probably through reduced local immunity in the cervix. For someone who is HPV-positive, stopping smoking is one of the few things they can do themselves.

Practical

Ask very briefly and advise stopping. Refer to NHS stop-smoking services and offer pharmacotherapy or vapes as locally commissioned. Link it directly to HPV: "stopping smoking helps your body clear the virus".

Gives an HPV-positive patient something active to do
🧪
Sexual health for the symptom
Target: NAAT today; results and treatment within days
Mechanism

Chlamydia cervicitis is a common, treatable cause of postcoital bleeding, and the cervix may look inflamed and bleed on contact. Cervical ectropion (often on the combined pill) and polyps are other benign causes. None of them can be assumed without examining the cervix.

Practical

Take a vulvovaginal or endocervical NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea. Offer HIV and syphilis testing according to risk. Treat positives according to BASHH guidance (dose per BNF), and arrange partner notification and a test of cure where indicated. Advise condoms with a new partner. Condoms reduce, but do not eliminate, HPV transmission.

Treating an STI does not remove the need to refer a suspicious cervix
7D — Programme pathway guide: NHS Cervical Screening Programme (England), eligibility, intervals and special situations

Step 1 — HPV test on every sample
  • Clinician-taken liquid-based sample or home self-sample
  • HPV-negative → routine recall at the age-appropriate interval. No cytology is done
  • Most results are HPV-negative, which gives strong reassurance for years ahead
An HPV-negative result is not a "normal smear" to wave at a symptom. It reflects future risk, not current symptoms.
Step 2 — HPV+: reflex cytology
  • Cytology negative → repeat at 12 months
  • At 12 months, still HPV+ and cytology-negative → repeat at 24 months
  • HPV+ at 24 months (any cytology) → colposcopy
  • HPV-negative at either repeat → back to routine recall
  • Self-sample HPV+ → clinician-taken sample needed for cytology
Waiting 12 months gives the virus time to clear, which it does in most people. This is deliberate, not neglect.
Step 3 — HPV+ with abnormal cytology: colposcopy
  • Borderline or low-grade dyskaryosis → routine colposcopy referral
  • High-grade dyskaryosis, possible invasion or glandular neoplasia → urgent colposcopy (NHSCSP colposcopy standards set shorter waits for these)
  • Two consecutive inadequate samples → colposcopy
  • Referral is made by the programme or lab. The practice checks that the appointment happens
Symptoms bypass all of this. A cervix that looks suspicious → consider a suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12), whatever the screening history.

7G — Psychosocial impact of results and referral: relationships, work, fertility and identity
🫂
A letter, a referral or an examination can reach far beyond the cervix: into trust, sex, work and plans for a family
The wait between "HPV found" or "I'm referring you urgently" and the answer is when anxiety peaks. Aisha is in a new-ish relationship, came about contraception, and has avoided screening out of fear. Whatever the examination shows, how the news is framed will shape whether she keeps engaging, both with this pathway and with screening for the next 30 years.
💑
Relationships and disclosure

An HPV-positive result or an STI diagnosis can put strain on a relationship. HPV does not need partner notification and cannot be dated to a partner. Chlamydia does need partner notification, which sexual health services can do anonymously. Separate the two clearly so she doesn't mix up their implications.

"HPV isn't something partners need to be told about or tested for. If the swab shows chlamydia, that's different — both of you would need treatment, and the clinic can let partners know without using your name."
⏳
The wait after an urgent referral

"Suspected cancer referral" sounds terrifying. Explain that it is a fast-track route to a specialist, that most people referred don't have cancer, and exactly when she should expect contact. Check her contact details and make sure she knows who to call if she hears nothing.

"This referral means you'll be seen quickly by a specialist. Most people seen this way don't have cancer, but we don't take chances with symptoms. If you haven't heard within a week, call us and we'll chase it."
👶
Fertility and future pregnancy

People worry that colposcopy or treatment will affect fertility. LLETZ does not usually affect the ability to conceive, but it is associated with a small increase in the risk of preterm birth. That is why previous treatment is recorded at antenatal booking. Aisha is sorting contraception now. Choosing a method is not affected by screening, but she can raise this if she has plans for pregnancy.

"If you ever need treatment on the cervix, it doesn't usually stop you getting pregnant. We'd just let your midwife know, so they can keep a closer eye in a future pregnancy."
💼
Work and practical life

Colposcopy, repeat tests and a possible urgent referral mean time off work. Cancer-screening appointments are generally accommodated, but some people feel unable to ask. Offer appointment times that fit, and a fit note only if a procedure genuinely needs recovery time (most people return to normal activity quickly after LLETZ).

"Would it help if we booked this at a time that fits around your work? Most people go back to normal activities the same or the next day."
🪞
Body image, sex and identity

After bleeding with sex, and while waiting for results, many people avoid sex out of fear of causing harm or passing something on. Give specific advice: after LLETZ, avoid penetrative sex for the period the clinic advises. For trans and non-binary patients, a gynaecology pathway can deepen dysphoria. Correct names and pronouns on referrals, and forewarning the service, make a real difference.

"It's completely understandable if the bleeding has made you anxious about sex. Until we know what's causing it, using condoms is sensible, and we'll give you clear advice once we have the results."
🔁
Future screening engagement

How this episode is handled predicts whether she comes back in 3 or 5 years. Record what helped in her notes: female sample-taker, small speculum, extra time, trauma-informed approach. Then the next invitation starts from a better place, and the fearful non-attender becomes a regular attender.

"I'll make a note of what helped today — the smaller speculum, having extra time — so whoever sees you next time knows exactly how to make it easier."

🔀 1 — Screening vs symptoms
Screening (asymptomatic)

HPV-primary (HPV first, reflex cytology). 25–64; HPV− → routine recall; HPV+ normal cytology → repeat 12m (persistent → colposcopy); HPV+ abnormal cytology → colposcopy. Address barriers; offer choice; de-shame HPV.

Symptoms — NOT a smear

PCB/IMB/PMB/persistent discharge → speculum exam of the cervix + STI test; suspicious cervix → urgent NG12. A normal smear does NOT exclude cancer in a symptomatic woman.

📋 2 — Practicalities

Offer female sample-taker, smaller speculum, longer appointment, chaperone, self-sampling where available; trauma-informed, consent-led, never coercive. Special situations: pregnancy (defer routine), post-hysterectomy (usually ceases), immunosuppression/HIV (more frequent), prior CIN (test of cure), cease at 65 if adequately screened & negative. Vaccination + screening drive elimination.
