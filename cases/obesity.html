📋 Clinical Stem — Obesity
A 44-year-old man who has "tried everything", feels judged by previous appointments, and asks directly about "the weight-loss injections" he has seen online
Daniel Price, 44, a delivery driver, books in to ask about "those weight-loss jabs." His BMI is 38; his waist is 118cm. He has tried multiple diets over the years, losing and regaining weight each time, and says he feels like a failure and that doctors "just tell me to eat less and move more." He has borderline-high blood pressure, snores heavily and feels unrefreshed in the morning, and his father had type 2 diabetes and a heart attack at 55. He works long hours sitting in a van, eats on the go, and his mood is low. He is hoping a medication will finally work.
This stem tests the ability to: raise and discuss weight respectfully and without stigma (with permission), recognising obesity as a chronic disease rather than a willpower problem; assess holistically — BMI and waist (adjusted for ethnicity), drivers, previous attempts, mental health, and a comorbidity screen (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, NAFLD, OSA, cardiovascular risk); exclude secondary and drug causes where indicated; apply the tiered weight-management pathway and set realistic, agreed goals; explain the place of pharmacotherapy (orlistat, GLP-1 agonists) and bariatric surgery honestly, including eligibility and that they are adjuncts to lifestyle change; and screen for OSA and low mood. The SCA challenge is responding to the request for "the injections" with an honest, non-dismissive, shared plan, and repairing the sense of being judged.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Stigma, shame and the chronic-disease reframe

Weight is one of the most stigmatised health issues, and people living with obesity often carry years of blame — from society, from healthcare, and internalised as shame. That experience, not a lack of motivation, is frequently what stands between a patient and effective care. The single most powerful clinical move is to reframe obesity as a chronic, relapsing medical condition driven by biology and environment, to communicate with respect and permission, and to build a long-term partnership. Done well, it transforms a defended, disappointed patient into an engaged one.

🚫 Undoing "eat less, move more"

Acknowledge the simplistic advice he's had and explain the biology of weight regain — appetite hormones defend a higher set-point.

"You've been told to eat less and move more, and it hasn't held — that's not because you failed, it's because your body actively defends its weight. That's exactly why this is a medical condition and why we sometimes need medical tools."
💉 The injection conversation

Be honest: GLP-1-based drugs are effective but criteria-based (NICE TA875/TA1026 and the NHS phased rollout), adjuncts to lifestyle, with stopping rules and regain on stopping.

"The injections can genuinely help and I'm glad you asked. They work best alongside changes, the NHS offers them to people who meet certain criteria — some through specialist services and, increasingly, through GP practices — and the weight tends to come back if you stop, so we'd plan it properly, not as a quick fix."
🎯 Realistic goals

A 5–10% loss meaningfully improves health; reframe success away from an idealised number.

"We don't need a dramatic number. Losing even 5–10% would noticeably improve your blood pressure, your sleep and your energy — and that's a realistic, lasting target to aim for together."
🧠 Mood & the cycle

Low mood and weight feed each other; treating mood supports the weight work.

"You mentioned feeling low — that often goes hand in hand with this and can make changes harder. Let's look after your mood too, because it'll help everything else."

🚩 The formulation, not just the BMI

Obesity management fails when it is reduced to a single number and a single instruction. The useful "diagnosis" names the severity, the complications to treat, any reversible contributor, the drivers, and the patient's readiness — which together select the tier (lifestyle → community → specialist → surgery) and the modality. For Daniel: class 2 obesity with central adiposity, likely OSA, raised cardiovascular risk and low mood — a man who needs comorbidity treatment, a structured programme, a realistic goal, an honest conversation about GLP-1 eligibility, and his mood addressed.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Daniel came for "the jab" — honour the request as a legitimate option, be straight about the NHS route, and make sure he leaves with more than he came for
1
Validate — name the request

Daniel has done his research and is braced for a refusal and a lecture. Naming the request out loud, and saying it is a reasonable thing to ask about, removes the fight before it starts. GLP-1-based medicines are an effective, NICE-approved treatment for obesity, and treating his question as sensible is simply accurate.

"You've come in about the weight-loss injections, and I think that's a completely reasonable thing to ask about — they are a genuine medical treatment for a medical condition. Let's look at whether and how they could fit for you."
2
Explain — the real NHS route, honestly

There are two NHS routes. Semaglutide (NICE TA875) is available through specialist weight-management services for BMI ≥35 with at least one weight-related condition, for up to 2 years. Tirzepatide (NICE TA1026) is being rolled out in phases through primary care, starting with people who have the highest BMI and the most qualifying conditions (type 2 diabetes, treated hypertension, dyslipidaemia, confirmed OSA, established cardiovascular disease). Daniel does not yet have a confirmed diagnosis of any of them — which is exactly why his screen matters.

"At the moment the NHS gives these injections to people whose weight is already causing specific health problems — like high blood pressure, sleep apnoea or diabetes. You might have some of those that nobody has diagnosed yet. So the first step towards the injections is the same as the first step towards being healthier: let's find out."
3
Negotiate — something concrete today

He must leave with action, not a promise: bloods (HbA1c, lipids, LFTs, U&E), home or ambulatory BP to confirm or exclude hypertension, an Epworth score and sleep referral, a mood check, referral to a structured weight programme, and a plan to refer to the specialist service or the practice's tirzepatide pathway as soon as he meets criteria. And a clear, non-shaming conversation about why buying online is riskier than it looks.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Behavioural and lifestyle change is the foundation that every other treatment sits on — NICE TA875 and TA1026 recommend the drugs only alongside a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity. The skill is tailoring it to a man who works long hours in a van, eats on the go, sleeps badly and feels low.
🥗
A sustainable energy deficit
Target: around 600 kcal/day below needs
Mechanism

Weight loss follows a sustained energy deficit, but the body defends its weight — appetite hormones rise and energy expenditure falls as weight comes off. That is why modest, sustainable changes beat crash diets that trigger rebound hunger and the familiar cycle of loss and regain.

Practical for Daniel

Start with the biggest wins: swap energy drinks and sugary drinks for water or zero-sugar options (often several hundred kcal a day on their own); plan food for the van (a cool bag, pre-made lunches) rather than relying on garage food; regular meals rather than one large evening meal. No food is banned.

Around 0.5 kg a week is a realistic, sustainable pace
🧑‍🤝‍🧑
Structured weight programme (Tier 2)
Target: referral today; 12-week programme
Mechanism

Multicomponent programmes — diet, activity and behaviour change (self-monitoring, goal-setting, relapse planning) — deliver more loss than advice alone and give the regular contact that keeps people engaged.

Practical for Daniel

Local Tier 2 or commercial-partner programme. The NHS Digital Weight Management Programme (12 weeks, app-based — fits shift work) takes GP referrals for adults with BMI ≥30 (≥27.5 for people from Black, Asian and minority ethnic backgrounds) and diagnosed diabetes or hypertension — so confirming his BP opens this door too. HbA1c 42–47 → NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme.

Behavioural support is the evidence-based way lifestyle change is delivered
🚶
Physical activity — built into the job
Target: build towards 150 min/week moderate + strength twice a week
Mechanism

Activity improves insulin sensitivity, BP, mood and sleep independently of weight, and is one of the strongest predictors of keeping weight off. On its own it takes off less weight than people expect — say so honestly to prevent disillusionment.

Practical for Daniel

UK Chief Medical Officers' guidelines (2019): 150 minutes a week of moderate activity plus muscle-strengthening on 2 days. For a driver: a 10-minute brisk walk at each break, parking further from drops, stairs, and breaking up long sitting. Start from where he is — any increase counts.

Activity holds weight off more than it takes it off
😴
Sleep and OSA
Target: Epworth + STOP-Bang today; sleep referral
Mechanism

Short or fragmented sleep raises hunger hormones and cravings and saps energy for activity. OSA and obesity feed each other: in a cohort study, 10% weight gain predicted about a 32% rise in apnoea–hypopnoea index and 10% loss about a 26% fall (Peppard, JAMA 2000).

Practical for Daniel

Assess with the Epworth Sleepiness Scale and STOP-Bang, and refer to the sleep service if OSA is suspected (NICE NG202). If CPAP is needed, better sleep often makes every other change easier. Driving advice meanwhile — see 7G.

Treating OSA improves sleepiness, driving safety and quality of life
🧠
Mood and comfort eating
Target: PHQ-9 today; NHS Talking Therapies if indicated
Mechanism

Depression and obesity are bidirectional: low mood drives comfort eating and inactivity, and weight stigma worsens mood. Untreated depression is one of the commonest reasons weight plans fail.

Practical for Daniel

PHQ-9 and a risk question. Self-referral to NHS Talking Therapies. If binge-eating disorder emerges, NICE NG69 recommends a binge-eating-focused guided self-help programme first — and that treatment is not aimed at weight loss. If an antidepressant is needed, weigh the weight effects of mirtazapine and tricyclics.

Treating mood first often unlocks everything else
🍺
Alcohol
Target: within 14 units/week, spread over 3+ days
Mechanism

Alcohol adds energy with no fullness, disinhibits eating, disrupts sleep, raises BP and compounds fatty liver disease alongside obesity.

Practical for Daniel

AUDIT-C to quantify "moderate". UK Chief Medical Officers' low-risk guidance: no more than 14 units a week, spread over 3 or more days, with drink-free days. Brief intervention if above this. Cutting a few pints a week can be one of the easiest calorie wins.

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, stigma, family, money and identity
🫂
Obesity — the driver who is judged before he speaks, sleepy behind the wheel, and tired of being told it is his fault
Weight touches work, safety, relationships, money and how people see themselves. Weight stigma — in the street, in the workplace and in healthcare — is associated with avoiding appointments, comfort eating and low mood, and it is one of the main reasons people disengage. The GP who addresses the medicine but not the life around it will watch the plan fail.
🚐
Driving for a living — sleepiness and the DVLA

Daniel drives all day and reports afternoon sleepiness. DVLA guidance: anyone with excessive sleepiness that is, or is likely to be, affecting driving must not drive. With suspected OSA and excessive sleepiness he must not drive until the symptoms are satisfactorily controlled; the DVLA does not need to be told while OSA is only suspected, but must be notified if a diagnosis of moderate or severe OSA with excessive sleepiness is confirmed, or if mild OSA is confirmed and the sleepiness is not controlled within 3 months. If his vehicle needs a Group 2 (lorry or bus) licence, standards are stricter.

This is frightening for a man whose income depends on driving — handle it as a safety conversation, arrange the sleep referral promptly, and document the advice.

"Do you ever feel sleepy while you're driving, or have you had any near-misses? I have to ask because, if the sleepiness is affecting your driving, it isn't safe to drive until it's treated. The good news is that treatment usually works quickly, and I'll get the sleep referral moving today."

Daniel feels judged by previous appointments. Use person-first, neutral language — "weight", "BMI", "living with obesity" — not "obese", "fat" or "morbid". "I'm sorry you've felt judged before. That shouldn't happen. Weight is a medical condition with a lot of biology behind it, and I'd like us to treat it that way."

Long shifts, fixed delivery targets, few breaks and roadside food make "eat healthily and exercise" hard to follow. Household shopping, cooking and eating patterns shape everyone's weight. Involving a partner can help — but only with the patient's agreement, and never in a way that makes food a battleground. 🧠

✗

🔍 1 — Assess

Classify: BMI (overweight 25–29.9; obesity I 30–34.9, II 35–39.9, III ≥40) + waist-to-height ratio (<0.5 target), ethnicity-adjusted (overweight ≥23, obesity ≥27.5 for South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African and African-Caribbean backgrounds). Screen comorbidity: HbA1c, lipids, BP, LFTs (NAFLD), OSA (Epworth), QRISK, mood. Exclude secondary: hypothyroid, Cushing's, PCOS, weight-promoting drugs.

💊 2 — Tiered management

Tier 1–2: lifestyle/behavioural + community programmes; agree 5–10% goal. Tier 3: specialist MDT, pharmacotherapy (liraglutide/semaglutide ≤2 years; tirzepatide also via phased primary-care rollout; all with stopping rules, adjunct to lifestyle). Tier 4: bariatric surgery (BMI ≥40, or 35–39.9 with comorbidity, specialist assessment; expedited if T2DM diagnosed within 10 years; ethnicity-adjusted). Treat comorbidities now; non-stigmatising care; long-term follow-up.
