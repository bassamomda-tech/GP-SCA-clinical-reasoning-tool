No firm asthma label <5A definite diagnosis of asthma is hard to confirm under 5 (objective tests unreliable). Use a "suspected asthma" / watchful approach with a treatment trial and review, rather than committing a pre-schooler to a lifelong label prematurely
Spacer beats inhalerA pressurised metered-dose inhaler via a spacer (with a face mask in the young) is as effective as a nebuliser for most acute wheeze and is the mainstay of delivery. Check and teach technique every time — poor technique is the commonest cause of "treatment failure"

📋 Clinical Stem — Wheeze in Children
A 3-year-old brought by his anxious mother with a third bout of wheeze this winter — well between episodes — and a request for "an asthma inhaler and a proper diagnosis"
Theo Marsh, 3, is brought by his mother. He has had three episodes of wheeze and cough this winter, each starting with a runny nose and cough and settling over a few days; between episodes he is completely well, running around, eating and sleeping normally, with no night cough and no exercise symptoms. Today he is at the tail end of a cold — a little wheezy and coughing but feeding, talking in full sentences, playing, and not distressed. His mother is exhausted and worried; her sister's child "has bad asthma," and she wants Theo started on a regular preventer inhaler and given "a definite diagnosis." There is no personal history of eczema, no clear family history of asthma in Theo's parents, and he was born at term and growing well.
This stem tests the ability to: assess the acute episode for severity and decide it is safe for community management; distinguish episodic viral-induced wheeze (well between colds, no interval symptoms) from asthma, and explain why a firm asthma diagnosis and a regular preventer are not appropriate for this 3-year-old yet; teach spacer/inhaler technique; safety-net clearly for deterioration; and manage the parent's understandable wish for certainty and a preventer with empathy and shared decision-making rather than simply acquiescing or dismissing. The red-flag discipline is to keep foreign body, bronchiolitis (in younger infants) and the atypical/sinister causes in mind.
Scenario A — Episodic viral-induced wheeze (this stem) Well between colds, no interval symptoms, toddler. Manage the acute episode (salbutamol via spacer PRN), parent education and a clear self-management/safety-net plan; avoid premature asthma labelling and routine preventers; review.
Scenario B — Suspected asthma Interval symptoms (night cough, exercise wheeze), atopy (eczema, allergic rhinitis), family history, response to bronchodilator. Trial of treatment with structured review; in school-age children use objective tests to support the diagnosis.
Scenario C — Acute severe / life-threatening Marked work of breathing, can't complete sentences/feed, SpO2 low; or silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion, poor effort. → Oxygen, salbutamol (burst/back-to-back), steroids, 999 for life-threatening features; admit.
Scenario D — Inhaled foreign body Sudden wheeze/cough in a previously well child after eating nuts or playing with small parts; unilateral/monophonic wheeze or focal signs. → Urgent assessment ± rigid bronchoscopy; do not anchor on "viral wheeze."
Scenario E — Bronchiolitis (infant) First wheezy episode <1 year with coryza, fine crackles, poor feeding. → Supportive care; 999 for apnoea, severe respiratory distress (grunting, marked recession, RR >70), central cyanosis or a seriously unwell appearance; consider referral for RR >60, intake 50–75% of usual, clinical dehydration or persistent SpO2 <92% in air (NICE NG9). NG9 recommends against bronchodilators and steroids.
Key variables to adapt for Age (bronchiolitis in infants; firm asthma label avoided <5); pattern (episodic viral vs multi-trigger/interval symptoms); atopy & family history; acute severity (effort, SpO2, feeding, speech); onset (sudden + choking → foreign body); growth and atypical features (CF, bronchiectasis, cardiac, mediastinal mass); inhaler/spacer technique; smoke exposure; parental anxiety and expectations.

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you think is going on — and what's making you think it might be asthma?"
Theo's mother has an asthma model from her sister's child. Surfacing it lets you honour her observation (recurrent wheeze IS worth taking seriously) while explaining why the pattern matters — and why "asthma" is a label best earned over time, not stamped on at three.
😟 Concerns
"What's the thing that frightens you most when he's like this?"
Her concern is probably watching him struggle to breathe and feeling helpless — and exhaustion from broken nights. Addressing the fear directly (what severe looks like, when to act, what to do at home) gives her control and is more reassuring than a prescription alone.
🎯 Expectations
"You mentioned a preventer inhaler and a firm diagnosis — tell me what you were hoping those would do."
She equates a preventer and a diagnosis with being taken seriously and with stopping the episodes. Naming this lets you explain that a daily preventer is not indicated for episodic viral wheeze, offer what will help (a reliever via spacer, a written plan, review), and keep her on side.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The frightened, exhausted parent of a wheezy toddler

Watching a small child work to breathe is frightening, and repeated disturbed nights wear parents down. That fear and fatigue are the engine of the "give him a preventer and a diagnosis" request — it is a request for control and reassurance. A consultation that simply refuses the preventer, or stamps on an asthma label to satisfy the parent, both miss the mark. The work is to validate the worry, equip the parent with a clear plan and the confidence to recognise severity, and explain honestly why time and pattern — not a premature label — will give the real answer.

😴 Exhaustion & helplessness

Broken nights and the helplessness of watching a child struggle drive anxiety. Acknowledge it and give concrete control: what to do, what to watch, when to seek help.

"It's exhausting and frightening watching him like this — let's make sure you leave today knowing exactly what to do at home and exactly what would mean he needs to be seen urgently."
🏷️ The wish for a label

A diagnosis feels like certainty. Explain that under five, the honest answer is often "wait and watch the pattern," and that this is good medicine, not indecision.

"I know a clear name would feel reassuring. At three it's genuinely hard to be certain it's asthma — many children wheeze with colds and grow out of it. Watching how the pattern develops is the most honest way to get it right for him."
🚬 Smoke exposure

If anyone smokes at home, this is a major modifiable factor — raise it supportively, tied to the child's breathing, not as blame.

"One of the biggest things that helps a wheezy child's chest is keeping the home and car completely smoke-free — I can help anyone in the house who'd like support to cut down or stop."
👍 Empowerment through a plan

A written action plan and confident spacer technique turn a frightening situation into a managed one — often more reassuring than any prescription.

"I'll give you a simple written plan — how much reliever to give through the spacer, how often, and the signs that mean call 999 or come straight in. You'll know exactly what to do next time."

Step 4
Investigations — Mostly Clinical · The Treatment Trial · When to Test
▲collapse
Wheeze in young children is a clinical diagnosis — most need no tests, and the most useful "investigation" is often a structured trial of treatment with review. Objective tests (spirometry, FeNO, peak flow) become possible and useful in school-age children with suspected asthma; targeted tests are reserved for atypical or severe presentations.

🧪 The treatment trial

Where asthma is suspected (interval symptoms, atopy), a structured trial of inhaled treatment with a clear review date — assessing symptom response — is a legitimate and recommended way to build the diagnosis in young children.

Document the pattern at each contact. Avoid an open-ended preventer prescription with no review: that is how children end up on long-term treatment they may not need, or with under-treated asthma never properly assessed.

🚩 Don't anchor — the patterns that should stop you escalating inhalers

Two disciplines protect the wheezy child: don't over-diagnose asthma in a pre-schooler with simple viral wheeze (avoiding unnecessary long-term treatment and a sticky label), and don't under-investigate the child whose course is atypical — sudden onset (foreign body), faltering growth or wet cough (CF/bronchiectasis), or systemic features (mediastinal/haematological). For Theo, the pattern is reassuringly typical episodic viral wheeze; the formulation is "viral-induced wheeze, no features of asthma or sinister disease today, safe for community management with review."

7A — Address the parent's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Never dismiss the request for "a preventer and a diagnosis" — acknowledge it, share your reasoning, then agree a plan that gives the parent control
1
Validate — name the expectation and the fear behind it

Theo's mother wants two things: a regular preventer and a definite label of asthma. Behind both sits a fear shaped by her sister's child, who has been admitted with asthma, and the exhaustion of three wheezy winters. Naming the request out loud, and naming the fear, stops the conversation feeling like a refusal before it has begun. The preventer is a reasonable thing to ask about — it is the right treatment for some wheezy children, just not for Theo's pattern today.

"You came in hoping for a preventer inhaler and a clear answer about asthma — and after watching your nephew go through it, and three bouts this winter, I completely understand why. Let me explain what I think is going on, and then we'll decide together."
2
Explain — share the reasoning in plain language

Wheeze only with colds, and completely well in between, is the pattern of episodic viral wheeze — the commonest cause of wheeze at this age, and most children grow out of it by school age. Under 5, asthma cannot be confirmed with breathing tests (NICE/BTS/SIGN NG245), so any label now would be a guess that tends to stay in the record. A daily preventer treats the airway between colds, and Theo's airway is fine between colds. What changes the decision is clear: symptoms between colds (night cough, wheeze on running or laughing), frequent or severe attacks needing steroids or hospital, or signs of allergy.

"His airways are small, and when he catches a cold they narrow and whistle. Because he's completely fine in between, a daily preventer wouldn't have anything to work on most of the time. If he starts coughing at night, wheezing when he runs about, or needs steroid medicine or hospital for an attack, that changes things — and I'd then suggest a trial of a preventer with a set date to review it."
3
Negotiate — offer concrete control today

The mother must leave with more than a "no". A salbutamol inhaler with a spacer and mask, the technique taught and watched in the room, a written plan saying how many puffs and when to call 999, a smoke-free home conversation with an offer of help for Theo's father, and a booked review. Record the working description as "episodic viral wheeze" (or "pre-school wheeze") with a review date, not "asthma".

"Here's what I'll do today: a blue reliever and a spacer, and I'll show you how to use them now; a written plan with exactly what to do and when to call 999; help for his dad if he'd like to stop smoking; and a review so we can watch the pattern together. If things change, we change the plan."
Key principle: the parent's underlying need is control and safety, not a specific inhaler. A written plan, a confident technique and a clear threshold for escalation meet that need better than a preventer that is not indicated. Explaining what would change your mind converts "the GP said no" into "we have a plan and we know what to watch for".

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + target
In pre-school wheeze the non-drug work does as much as any prescription. Delivery technique decides whether the salbutamol reaches the lung; smoke exposure is the largest modifiable trigger; and a written plan is what the National Review of Asthma Deaths found missing in most fatal cases. These are the elements the NICE/BTS/SIGN NG245 and the practice protocols ask you to check before any step-up.
🫧
Spacer and mask technique
Target: technique watched and correct at every contact
Mechanism

A pMDI fired directly into a young child's mouth deposits most of the drug in the mouth and throat. A spacer holds the aerosol so it can be inhaled with normal breathing; a face mask gives a seal in children too young to close their lips around a mouthpiece. Delivered this way, salbutamol is as effective as a nebuliser in most acute attacks (BTS/SIGN 158), with less tachycardia and tremor.

Practical

Shake the inhaler, fit it to the spacer, mask sealed over nose and mouth, one puff at a time followed by about five normal breaths, then repeat. Never put several puffs into the spacer at once. Wash monthly in warm soapy water and let it drip-dry (rubbing causes static that holds the drug on the wall). Move to a mouthpiece once the child can reliably seal their lips around it. A calm child on a parent's lap breathes the drug in; a screaming child mostly does not.

The commonest reason an inhaler "doesn't work" is that the drug never reached the lung
🚭
Smoke-free home and car
Target: no smoking or vaping indoors or in the car; parental cessation
Mechanism

Tobacco smoke irritates and inflames the small airways, increases the frequency and severity of viral wheezy episodes and reduces the response to treatment. Smoking outside still leaves smoke residue on clothes, hair and skin that the child is exposed to when held. Smoking in a car carrying anyone under 18 has been illegal in England and Wales since 2015.

Practical

Ask every time, including about vaping and smoking "only outside". Offer Theo's father a referral to the local stop-smoking service and pharmacotherapy — very brief advice (ask, advise, act) is more effective than advice alone. Frame it around Theo's breathing, not blame. Record smoke exposure in the child's notes.

Named in both practice protocols as the single biggest modifiable factor in childhood wheeze
📄
Written action plan
Target: a plan at home, at nursery, and in the notes
Mechanism

A written plan converts the parent's fear into a sequence of actions: when to start the reliever, how many puffs, what "not working" looks like, and when to call 999. The National Review of Asthma Deaths (RCP, 2014) found that only 23% of those who died had a personal asthma action plan, and that 45% did not seek or receive medical help during the fatal attack.

Practical

Use a standard children's plan (for example the Asthma + Lung UK child action plan, adapted for a pre-schooler with viral wheeze). Fill it in with the parent in the room. Include the danger signs in plain words: too breathless to feed, talk or play; sucking in under the ribs; blue or grey lips; drowsy or floppy; reliever not lasting 4 hours. Give a copy for the nursery.

NRAD 2014: absence of an action plan was a recurring avoidable factor in asthma deaths
🏠
Damp, mould and triggers at home
Target: identify triggers; support housing repair where needed
Mechanism

Damp and mould release spores and irritants that worsen respiratory symptoms, and children with respiratory conditions are particularly vulnerable. The UK government's 2023 guidance for the housing sector on the health risks of damp and mould followed the death of two-year-old Awaab Ishak. In a child with multiple-trigger wheeze, house-dust mite, pets and cold air may also play a part.

Practical

Ask about damp, visible mould and condensation. Where present, a GP letter documenting the child's respiratory condition can support a repair request to a landlord. Avoid expensive allergen-avoidance products without evidence of sensitisation. Treat coexisting eczema and allergic rhinitis properly.

A housing letter is a practical, low-cost intervention that families rarely know to ask for
💉
Vaccination
Target: routine schedule complete; annual influenza vaccine where eligible
Mechanism

Viral infections are the trigger for almost every episode of pre-school wheeze. Influenza can cause severe attacks in children with asthma. Children from age 2 are offered the nasal influenza vaccine (LAIV) through the national programme — but not while acutely wheezy: a child with increased wheeze or extra bronchodilator use in the previous 72 hours should be offered an inactivated vaccine instead to avoid delay (Green Book, chapter 19) — and children with asthma needing continuous or repeated inhaled or oral steroids, or with a previous admission, are in a clinical risk group (UKHSA Green Book, chapter 19).

Practical

Check the immunisation record at the review. Offer influenza vaccine each autumn. The practice protocol for childhood asthma also asks that immunisations be kept up to date.

Prevents the infections that trigger the episodes
🏫
Nursery and school
Target: reliever + spacer + plan held by the setting
Mechanism

Episodes often start during the day at nursery. Staff who hold the child's own reliever and spacer, and know the danger signs, can start treatment early. Schools in England have been permitted since 2014 to keep an emergency salbutamol inhaler for children diagnosed with asthma and prescribed a reliever, with written parental consent (Department of Health guidance on emergency salbutamol inhalers in schools).

Practical

Give the parent a second inhaler and spacer for the setting if needed, and a copy of the plan. Correct the "the nursery says he's asthmatic" message gently: the plan says "wheeze with colds — give reliever via spacer as below". Revisit when the child starts school.

Treatment starts at the first sign, wherever the child is
7D — Prescribing guide: NICE/BTS/SIGN NG245 (chronic) and BTS/SIGN 158 (acute attack) by age
NG245 (2024, joint BTS/NICE/SIGN) covers diagnosis, monitoring and long-term management; it does not cover the acute attack, which remains under BTS/SIGN 158 (2019). Management is age-banded: under 5, 5 to 11, and 12 and over (who follow the adult pathway). Before every step up, check inhaler technique, adherence, spacer use, smoke and trigger exposure, and whether the diagnosis is right. Step down when control is sustained.
Under 5 — episodic viral wheeze or suspected asthma

Episodic viral wheeze (Theo): SABA via spacer and mask for episodes only

  • No routine preventer for infrequent, mild episodes with no symptoms between colds (practice protocol)
  • Suspected asthma with symptoms needing maintenance therapy, or severe acute episodes: consider an 8–12 week trial of twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS plus SABA as needed (NG245)
  • If symptoms resolve on the trial: stop ICS and review after a further 3 months. If symptoms recur by that review, or there is an attack needing systemic steroids or hospital, restart regular ICS at paediatric low dose (titrating to moderate dose if needed) with SABA (NG245). If symptoms did not resolve on the trial, reconsider the diagnosis
  • If uncontrolled on a paediatric moderate-dose ICS: consider an LTRA for an 8–12 week trial; if unsuccessful, stop it and refer to a specialist in asthma care (NG245)
The trial has a stop date. An ICS started as a "trial" and never reviewed becomes a long-term treatment and a label by default.
5 to 11 — confirmed asthma (NG245)

Twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS + SABA as needed

  • Diagnose first: FeNO ≥35 ppb; if not raised or unavailable, spirometry with BDR (FEV₁ ↑ ≥12%, or ≥10% of predicted); if unavailable or delayed, PEF variability ≥20% over 2 weeks; then HDM skin-prick or total IgE plus eosinophils; refer if doubt remains
  • Uncontrolled: paediatric low-dose MART if the child can manage the regimen; if not, consider adding an LTRA (8–12 week trial, stop if ineffective), then paediatric low-dose ICS/LABA + SABA
  • Still uncontrolled: paediatric moderate-dose MART (or moderate-dose ICS/LABA)
  • Refer to a specialist if uncontrolled on moderate-dose MART or moderate-dose ICS/LABA (± LTRA)
Licensing (SPC, BNFC): only one budesonide/formoterol 100/6 dry-powder inhaler is licensed for MART in ages 6–11; other ICS/formoterol MART use under 12 is off-label.
Acute attack in primary care (BTS/SIGN 158)

Grade severity first; SABA via spacer ± mask; steroid; reassess

  • Moderate (SpO₂ ≥92%, able to talk/feed): salbutamol 2–10 puffs via spacer, one puff at a time, titrated to response; consider oral prednisolone
  • Severe (SpO₂ <92%, too breathless to talk/feed, HR/RR over age thresholds): oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, 10 puffs via spacer or nebulised salbutamol, prednisolone; reassess; arrange admission if poor response
  • Life-threatening: 999, oxygen, nebulised salbutamol with ipratropium, prednisolone, repeat bronchodilator while awaiting transfer
  • Prednisolone: 20 mg age 2–5, 30–40 mg over 5, usually up to 3 days (BTS/SIGN 158); BNFC 1–2 mg/kg (max 40 mg) under 12
Lower the admission threshold if the attack is in the afternoon or evening, after a recent admission or previous severe attack, or if there is concern about the family's ability to cope at home.

  • First wheezy illness in an infant (most under 1; NG9 covers under 2s) with coryza, cough, tachypnoea, crackles and/or wheeze, poor feeding

What not to prescribe for Theo — and why
  • Regular ICS: not indicated for infrequent episodic viral wheeze with no interval symptoms; would expose him to a daily steroid with little to treat between colds
  • Routine oral prednisolone for mild viral wheeze in a well pre-school child: the evidence of benefit is weak; reserve for a severe episode or a significant attack in a child already on a preventer (practice protocol)
  • Antibiotics: not indicated for wheeze; only for genuine bacterial infection (focal signs, high fever, unwell child)
  • Montelukast as a default: evidence in episodic viral wheeze is inconsistent; if used, it should be a specialist decision or a time-limited trial, with MHRA neuropsychiatric counselling
  • Nebuliser at home: a spacer is as effective for most attacks and avoids delaying help by treating a severe attack at home

7G — Psychosocial impact: family, work, sleep, nursery and the label
🫂
Wheeze in a small child is a family illness — the parent carries the fear, the nights and the decisions
Theo is untroubled between colds; his mother is not. Each winter brings broken nights, time off work, nursery calls and the fear of a hospital admission like her nephew's. The consultation that treats only the chest leaves the family's burden untouched. The one that addresses the fear, the tiredness, the smoking and the nursery gives the parent a sense of control — which is what she came for.
😰
Parental fear and hypervigilance

Parents of wheezy children often lie awake listening to breathing, and a relative's severe asthma magnifies the fear. Fear without a plan leads either to repeated emergency attendances or, worse, to freezing when the child is truly unwell. Concrete danger signs and a practised response are the best treatment for parental anxiety.

"What would help you feel more confident at 2 o'clock in the morning? Let's go through exactly what you'd see and exactly what you'd do."
💼
Work and childcare

Each episode can mean days off work, nursery exclusion and financial strain, particularly for single parents or those in insecure work. Asking about it signals that you have seen the whole picture, and a note for the employer or nursery can help.

"How has work been with all these bouts? If a letter for your employer or the nursery would help, I'm happy to write one."
😴
Sleep and exhaustion

Night-time coughing and checking on the child disrupt the whole household. Exhaustion lowers the threshold for wanting "something stronger" and makes it harder to follow a plan. Acknowledge it; check the parent's own mood; involve the other parent in night-time care and in the plan.

"You sound exhausted — which is completely understandable. How are you doing yourself? Is there anyone who can share the nights when he's poorly?"
🚬
Guilt, blame and the smoking parent

A parent who smokes may feel accused, and the other parent may feel angry. Framing smoke exposure as a shared, fixable risk — with practical help — protects the relationship with the family and makes change more likely.

"Lots of parents smoke, and it's really hard to stop. The good news is that making the home and car smoke-free, and getting support to quit, makes a real difference to children's chests. Would Theo's dad be open to some help?"
🏷️
The label and identity

An "asthma" code in a pre-schooler's record tends to persist, shapes how nursery and school treat the child, and commits the family to long-term treatment decisions. A provisional description ("pre-school viral wheeze") with a review date protects the child while keeping the possibility of asthma open.

"I'll write it down as wheeze with colds for now. That doesn't mean we're ignoring it — it means we're keeping an open mind until the pattern is clear."
🚗
Driving and getting help

There is no DVLA issue for the child, but the practical question matters: families without a car, or a parent tempted to drive a struggling child to hospital, need clear advice. A child with severe or life-threatening features should travel by ambulance, not in the back of a car with one parent driving.

"If he's struggling to breathe, don't drive him yourself — call 999 so he can have oxygen and treatment on the way."

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases, RAG marking and danger zones

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — call 999
"Call 999 straight away if Theo is too breathless to talk, feed or play, if you can see him sucking in under his ribs or at his neck, if his lips look blue or grey, if he becomes drowsy, floppy or very agitated, or if the blue inhaler isn't helping. While you wait, keep giving the blue inhaler through the spacer — one puff at a time, up to ten puffs — and keep him sitting up. Don't drive him in yourself."
The National Review of Asthma Deaths found that 45% of people who died did not call for or receive medical help during the fatal attack. A quiet chest in a tiring child is deterioration, not improvement — parents need to know that a child who goes quiet and sleepy is more worrying, not less.
🟠 Same-day review
"Contact us the same day — or 111 out of hours — if he needs the blue inhaler more often than every four hours, if he's drinking much less than usual or has fewer wet nappies, if he has a high temperature with fast breathing, or if you're worried he's getting worse rather than better."
Reliever need more often than every 4 hours is the practical threshold in both practice protocols. Poor feeding and fever with fast breathing overlap with the NICE NG143 amber features (RR >40 over 12 months, SpO₂ ≤95%) and with pneumonia and bronchiolitis thresholds.
🟡 Routine — what should bring you back
"Book to see us if he starts coughing at night or wheezing when he runs, laughs or is well — without a cold — or if the episodes are getting more frequent. Also come back if he ever has a sudden choking episode followed by a cough that won't settle, or if the cough goes on for more than three to four weeks."
Interval symptoms change the working diagnosis towards multiple-trigger wheeze or asthma and justify an ICS trial. A choking episode followed by persistent cough is an inhaled foreign body until proven otherwise — the symptom-free interval after the event is what delays the diagnosis.

🚫 Danger zones — the errors that fail this case
✗
Reassured by a quiet chest in a tired child→ A silent chest means little air is moving. With SpO₂ <92%, poor effort or drowsiness it is life-threatening — 999 (BTS/SIGN 158).
✗
Labelling a 3-year-old with episodic viral wheeze as asthmatic and starting a regular ICS→ Under 5, asthma cannot be confirmed by objective tests (NG245). Record "episodic viral wheeze", treat episodes, and reserve an ICS for a time-limited trial.
✗
Prescribing salbutamol for first-episode bronchiolitis in an infant→ NICE NG9 recommends against bronchodilators and steroids; management is supportive, with 999 for apnoea, severe distress or cyanosis.
✗
Missing an inhaled foreign body→ Ask about sudden onset and choking; listen for unilateral or focal signs; same-day paediatric assessment even if the child now looks well.
✗
Stepping up treatment without checking technique, adherence and smoke exposure→ Watch the spacer technique and ask about smoking every time before changing medication (NG245; practice protocols).
✗
Giving an inhaler without a spacer, or without teaching it→ pMDI + spacer (+ mask) for all young children; demonstrate one puff at a time with tidal breathing.
✗
Vague safety-netting ("come back if he gets worse")→ Name the signs: too breathless to feed or talk, recession, blue lips, drowsiness, reliever not lasting 4 hours — and put them in writing.
✗
Routine oral steroids for every mild viral wheeze→ Reserve prednisolone for moderate to severe attacks or a significant attack in a child with asthma; use BTS/SIGN 158 or BNFC doses.
✗
Continuing to escalate inhalers in a child with faltering growth, wet cough or clubbing→ Stop and refer — CF, primary ciliary dyskinesia, structural or cardiac causes need investigation, not another inhaler.
