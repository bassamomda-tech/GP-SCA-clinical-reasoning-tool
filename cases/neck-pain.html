📋 Clinical Stem — Neck Pain
A 48-year-old office worker with 5 weeks of neck pain and recent tingling down one arm, anxious about "a trapped nerve" and pushing for an MRI scan
Hannah Webb, 48, presents with 5 weeks of aching neck and shoulder-girdle pain that came on gradually with no injury, worse at her desk and eased by movement and heat. For the past week she has noticed tingling and occasional pins-and-needles down the outside of her right forearm into the thumb, with the neck pain. She has no leg symptoms, no clumsiness using both hands, no balance or bladder problems, and is otherwise well with no weight loss or fever. She is worried it is "a slipped disc" or "a trapped nerve doing damage" and would like an MRI and "something strong" for the pain. She works long hours at a screen and is stressed.
This stem tests the ability to: take a structured neck-pain history that separates non-specific mechanical pain from cervical radiculopathy (dermatomal arm symptoms — Hannah) and from the red flag of cervical myelopathy (bilateral hand and gait involvement); screen for the sinister causes (trauma, infection, malignancy, vascular/dissection); examine the neck and perform an upper- and lower-limb neurological assessment; explain why imaging is not indicated for mechanical neck pain or early radiculopathy and manage the request for an MRI and strong analgesia with shared decision-making; and provide evidence-based self-management (keep active, posture/workstation, analgesia, no collar) with a clear safety-net for myelopathy and other red flags. The SCA challenge is reassuring and managing expectations without dismissing the genuine radicular symptoms.
Scenario A — Mechanical neck pain + early radiculopathy (this stem) Gradual mechanical pain with dermatomal arm symptoms, no myelopathy/red flags. Reassure, analgesia, keep active, posture/workstation; conservative course; image/refer only if not settling or progressing.
Scenario B — Cervical myelopathy (red flag) Bilateral hand clumsiness/numbness, gait imbalance, brisk reflexes, ± bladder change. Urgent MRI + neurosurgical referral; do not manage as mechanical.
Scenario C — Whiplash After a rear-end RTC. Apply Canadian C-spine rule to exclude fracture; then reassure, early mobilisation, analgesia; no collar; address anxiety and litigation context.
Scenario D — Sinister cause Fever/IVDU/immunosuppression (discitis), known cancer/weight loss/night pain (metastasis), or sudden severe pain + neuro/Horner's (arterial dissection / SAH). Urgent imaging/admission.
Scenario E — Severe / progressive radiculopathy Disabling arm pain, significant or progressive weakness, or failure to settle over 4–6 weeks → MRI and spinal/neurosurgical referral.

▲collapse
The history sorts neck pain into three buckets — non-specific mechanical, nerve-root (radiculopathy), and spinal-cord (myelopathy) — and screens for the sinister causes that masquerade as neck pain. The decisive thread is the pattern of any neurological symptoms: one-arm dermatomal symptoms suggest a root (Hannah), whereas both hands plus legs/gait suggest the cord — a red flag that changes everything.

Hannah wants an MRI and strong painkillers and fears nerve damage. The skill is to validate her symptoms, exclude the red flags, and explain why a scan isn't the right next step — without making her feel dismissed.

💭 Ideas
"What do you think is causing it — and what's worrying you most?"
Hannah believes it's a "slipped disc / trapped nerve doing damage." Surfacing this lets you correct the catastrophic belief (most radicular symptoms recover without damage or surgery) — fear-avoidance beliefs predict chronicity, so addressing them is therapeutic.
😟 Concerns
"Is the fear that the nerve is being permanently damaged?"
Naming her fear of permanent nerve damage lets you reassure specifically and explain the natural history, which both comforts her and supports the conservative plan and the decision not to scan.
🎯 Expectations
"You mentioned wanting a scan and something strong — can I explain when those help and when they don't?"
Naming the MRI and strong-analgesia request lets you have an honest, shared conversation: why imaging isn't indicated now (and can mislead), what analgesia actually helps, and what would change the plan — so she leaves with a rationale, not a refusal.
1E — Psychosocial context
🫂 Fear of "nerve damage", the MRI request, and the desk-bound life

Neck pain with arm tingling frightens people — the words "trapped nerve" conjure permanent damage, and the natural response is to want a scan and strong painkillers. Yet for mechanical pain and early radiculopathy, imaging rarely changes management, frequently shows age-related changes that alarm without helping, and can entrench illness behaviour. The consultation that works validates the genuine symptoms, excludes the red flags convincingly, explains the reassuring natural history, and turns the MRI request into a shared understanding — while tackling the real drivers (posture, screen time, stress) that will actually help.

🧠 Undoing "nerve damage"

Reframe the tingling as an irritated nerve that usually recovers, not a nerve being destroyed.

"The tingling means a nerve in your neck is irritated, not that it's being permanently damaged. In most people these symptoms settle over weeks to a few months as the irritation calms down — the nerve recovers."
🖥️ The MRI conversation

Explain why a scan isn't the right next step now, and what would change that.

"A scan sounds reassuring, but for this kind of pain it usually shows normal age-related wear that everyone your age has, and it wouldn't change the treatment. We'd scan if things didn't settle, the weakness got worse, or there were certain warning signs — which I've checked for and you don't have."
💊 Realistic analgesia

Be honest that "something strong" (opioids) isn't the answer; explain what helps.

"Strong opioids don't work well for this and bring real downsides. The most effective things are keeping it moving, regular simple painkillers or anti-inflammatories if suitable, heat, and the neck exercises — I'll show you."
🪑 The desk & stress

Address the modifiable drivers — workstation, breaks, stress — that genuinely change recovery.

"Long hours at a screen and stress both feed neck pain. Small changes — screen at eye level, regular movement breaks, and managing the pressure you're under — often make a real difference."

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Hannah wants an MRI and "something strong". Acknowledge both, give your reasons, then agree a plan she can see working
1
Validate: name the expectation and the fear under it

The scan request is really a request for certainty that the nerve is not being damaged. The request for "something strong" means the pain is disrupting her work and sleep, and whatever she has tried so far has not worked. If you name both before you respond, she is less likely to feel dismissed. That matters here, because feeling dismissed is what most often turns a reasonable "no scan today" into a complaint or a second appointment elsewhere.

"It sounds like two things would help most: knowing for sure the nerve isn't being damaged, and getting on top of the pain so you can work and sleep. Is that fair? Let me tell you what I've found, and then we can work out the best way to get you both."
2
Explain: share the reasoning, not just the decision

Imaging is not routinely indicated for non-specific neck pain or for early cervical radiculopathy without red flags or progressive neurology (McCartney et al., BJGP 2018: isolated radiculopathy is managed conservatively first, with MRI or referral if symptoms persist beyond 4–6 weeks or neurology is abnormal). Age-related disc and facet changes are almost universal by her age and correlate poorly with symptoms. A scan now would not change what she does, and it could create a new worry. Strong opioids have little evidence of benefit in this setting and real harms. The options that do help are regular, adequate simple analgesia, keeping the neck moving, exercise, workplace changes and time.

"If I scanned you today, it would almost certainly show wear-and-tear changes. Most people your age have them, even with no pain at all, so they wouldn't tell us why you hurt. The treatment would be the same either way. Where a scan does help is if the arm symptoms aren't settling after several weeks, if weakness develops or worsens, or if any warning signs appear. At that point it helps us plan an injection or an operation."
3
Negotiate: offer something concrete today

"No" on its own fails the Relating to Others domain. Offer a better analgesic plan (a regular NSAID if suitable, taken properly for a defined period), a physiotherapy or MSK self-referral, workstation changes, a written safety-net covering the myelopathy symptoms, and a clear point at which you will reconsider imaging. Once she knows the scan is deferred rather than refused, she no longer needs to fight for it.

"Here's what I suggest: a proper trial of an anti-inflammatory taken regularly, physio, and some changes to your desk set-up. I'll see you again in about four weeks. If your arm isn't clearly improving by then, or at any point you notice weakness or the warning signs I'll write down for you, we'll arrange the scan and a specialist opinion. Does that feel like a reasonable plan?"
Key principle: the evidence supports the GP's position here, so the task is communication rather than persuasion. Take the arm symptoms seriously, show that you have actively excluded the cord and the sinister causes, and give her a point at which imaging will happen. Most patients accept "not yet, and here is when" far more readily than "you don't need it".

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Active self-management is the core treatment for non-specific neck pain, early radiculopathy and whiplash (OPTIMa neck pain and whiplash guideline, Côté et al., Eur Spine J 2016; McCartney et al., BJGP 2018). Passive measures such as collars and rest delay recovery. Psychological support is for co-existing anxiety or depression (NHS Talking Therapies) or, if pain becomes chronic primary pain, CBT or ACT under NICE NG193. (NICE NG59, which covers low back pain rather than neck pain, withdrew its psychological and combined-programme recommendations in July 2026, so do not quote it as support.)
🔄
Keep Moving — No Collar, No Rest
Target: normal activities from day one, within comfort
Mechanism

Movement maintains blood flow to muscles and joints, limits guarding and spasm, and prevents the stiffness and deconditioning that come with immobilisation. Holding the neck still teaches the nervous system that moving is dangerous, which feeds fear-avoidance and chronicity.

Practical

Gentle range-of-movement exercises several times a day: rotation, side-bending, chin tucks and shoulder rolls, moving into mild discomfort but not sharp pain. Carry on with work and daily life, adapting rather than stopping. Do not wear a collar. After whiplash, early mobilisation gives better outcomes than immobilisation.

Collars and rest prolong symptoms. Active advice is the evidence-based default (OPTIMa 2016: do not offer a cervical collar)
💪
Exercise & Physiotherapy
Target: daily home programme; physio if not settling in 2–4 weeks
Mechanism

Deep neck flexor and scapular strengthening improves endurance of the postural muscles that tire with long periods at a desk. Graded loading reduces pain sensitivity. In radiculopathy, physiotherapists may add neural mobilisation and positions that ease root irritation.

Practical

Offer MSK or physiotherapy self-referral where available, or GP referral. The physiotherapist also screens for red flags, which gives a second safety check. Manual therapy may help in the short term but should be combined with exercise, not used alone. Cervical high-velocity manipulation carries a rare association with arterial dissection, so avoid it in anyone with dissection risk features.

Cochrane (Gross et al. 2015): moderate-quality evidence supports strengthening exercises for the neck, shoulder and shoulder-blade region in chronic neck pain and radiculopathy; stretching alone showed no clear benefit
🖥️
Workstation & Posture
Target: screen at eye level; a movement break every 30–40 min
Mechanism

Sustained neck flexion or a forward head posture loads the cervical extensors and facet joints continuously. Changing position often matters more than finding a "perfect" posture. Laptops used without a separate screen and keyboard are a common driver of neck pain.

Practical

Put the top of the screen at eye level, use a separate keyboard and mouse, sit with forearms supported, use a headset instead of cradling a phone, and set a break timer. Under the Health and Safety (Display Screen Equipment) Regulations 1992, employers must carry out workstation assessments, so Hannah can ask her employer for one. A fit note can recommend "workplace adaptations" or "amended duties".

Changes she can make, often with benefit within 1–2 weeks. Puts Hannah in control
🔥
Heat, Sleep Position & Pacing
Target: supportive pillow; heat before exercises
Mechanism

Local heat relaxes muscle spasm and makes exercise easier. Poor sleep increases pain sensitivity the next day, and pain disrupts sleep, so the cycle keeps itself going. Pacing (spreading activity rather than boom and bust) prevents flare-ups.

Practical

Use a warm wheat bag or shower before the exercises. One or two pillows that keep the neck level, not propped forward. Avoid sleeping face down. Keep the screen out of the bedroom and use a regular wind-down routine. Spread demanding tasks through the day.

Low cost, low risk, and gives the patient an active role
🧠
Explanation, Stress & Fear-Avoidance
Target: "hurt ≠ harm" understood; stress plan agreed
Mechanism

Catastrophic beliefs ("the nerve is being destroyed") and stress increase muscle tension and pain sensitivity. They also predict persistent symptoms and work loss. A clear explanation is itself a treatment. Hannah's fear is the most modifiable prognostic factor in the consultation.

Practical

Explain the root/cord distinction in plain words and the good natural history. Screen mood if indicated. Offer NHS Talking Therapies self-referral if anxiety or depression is present. Signpost to workplace support such as the employee assistance programme or occupational health. If pain persists beyond 3 months and meets the definition of chronic primary pain, NICE NG193 supports exercise programmes, CBT or ACT for pain, and acupuncture (within its criteria).

Addressing yellow flags early is the best protection against chronic pain
🚗
Whiplash Variant: Reassure & Mobilise
Target: fracture excluded → normal activity
Mechanism

Once the Canadian C-spine rule has safely excluded the need for imaging, or imaging has excluded fracture (NICE NG41), whiplash is a soft-tissue injury. Most people improve over weeks, although a substantial minority have symptoms at one year. High initial pain, distress and a catastrophic outlook predict a slower course.

Practical

Give the same advice: reassure, keep moving, use regular simple analgesia, avoid a collar, and add physiotherapy if not improving. Write objective notes, because they may be requested for a claim. Assess for post-traumatic stress symptoms after a frightening collision.

Early mobilisation gives better outcomes than immobilisation (whiplash trials)
7D — Prescribing guide: analgesia for neck pain and cervical radiculopathy (McCartney BJGP 2018 · NICE CG173 · Opioids Aware)
Analgesia supports activity and does not replace it. Use a regular trial at an adequate dose for a defined period, then stop. Doses are per BNF. For neuropathic arm pain that persists, NICE CG173 offers a choice of amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin. Use of these drugs for radicular pain is off-label for amitriptyline and duloxetine. Strong opioids are not a routine option. If a longer-term opioid is ever considered, Opioids Aware (FPM, updated 2026) sets an ideal ceiling of ≤50 mg/day oral morphine equivalent and advises specialist input above 90 mg/day.
Step 1 — NSAID (if suitable) ± paracetamol

Oral NSAID (e.g. ibuprofen or naproxen), dose per BNF, taken regularly for a short defined period

  • Lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Take with food
  • Gastroprotection (PPI) if at GI risk: older age, ulcer history, anticoagulant/antiplatelet/SSRI/steroid
  • Avoid or use cautiously in CKD, heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, NSAID-sensitive asthma, and in pregnancy (avoid in the third trimester)
  • Topical NSAID is an option for localised muscular pain with lower systemic risk
  • Paracetamol can be used if NSAIDs are unsuitable, but evidence for paracetamol alone in spinal pain is weak. Set honest expectations
Hannah: well, 48, no contraindications → regular NSAID trial for up to 2 weeks with review. Ask what she has actually been taking.
Step 2 — Neuropathic agent for persistent radicular pain

Amitriptyline or duloxetine (or gabapentin/pregabalin), dose per BNF — NICE CG173 choice

  • Consider for burning, shooting or electric arm pain that persists, or that disturbs sleep, despite Step 1
  • Use one neuropathic drug at a time. If one fails or is not tolerated, switch to another (CG173)
  • Amitriptyline at night can help sleep. Warn about next-day drowsiness and driving
  • Duloxetine is useful if anxiety or depression co-exists. Do not combine with other serotonergic drugs without checking
  • Start low and titrate. Review benefit and harm within a few weeks and stop if there is no meaningful gain
Evidence for any neuropathic agent specifically in cervical radiculopathy is limited and extrapolated. Share that uncertainty.
Step 3 — Weak opioid: short, reviewed, only if needed

Codeine (alone or with paracetamol), dose per BNF, only if NSAIDs are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective

  • Small supply with a stop date and a review. No automatic repeats
  • Warn about constipation, drowsiness and the drug-driving law. Offer a laxative
  • MHRA (Sept 2020): counsel on the risk of dependence and addiction before starting
  • Avoid co-prescribing with benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids: risk of profound sedation and respiratory depression (MHRA)
  • Strong opioids are not a treatment for acute neck pain or radiculopathy in primary care
Opioids Aware (2026): opioids help acute pain in the short term but have little evidence of benefit in long-term pain. Ideal ≤50 mg/day OME, and specialist advice above 90 mg/day.
Avoid / use with great caution
  • Collars: no role in non-specific neck pain or whiplash
  • Benzodiazepines for "spasm": at most a very short course in exceptional cases. There is a dependence risk, and combining them with opioids is dangerous (MHRA March 2020)
  • Gabapentinoids: a CG173 option for neuropathic pain, but Class C / Schedule 3 controlled drugs since April 2019. MHRA warnings cover respiratory depression (gabapentin Oct 2017; pregabalin Feb 2021) and abuse and dependence. Prescribe in small quantities and review
  • Starting strong opioids for a patient requesting "something strong": explain why not and offer the evidence-based alternatives
  • Chronic primary pain (NICE NG193): do not start opioids, gabapentinoids, NSAIDs or paracetamol. An antidepressant can be considered after discussion
Specialist options (secondary care)
  • MRI is arranged when the result would change management: persistent or disabling radiculopathy after a conservative trial, progressive or significant motor deficit, suspected myelopathy, or red flags
  • Image-guided nerve root / epidural injection may give short-term relief of radicular pain in selected patients (spinal or pain service). Cervical injections carry rare but serious risks
  • Surgical decompression (e.g. anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, arthroplasty, foraminotomy) is for progressive neurological deficit, myelopathy, or disabling radiculopathy that has not responded to conservative care
  • Degenerative cervical myelopathy: surgery aims to halt progression. Function lost before surgery may not return, which is why early recognition matters

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Neck pain: a desk job that hurts, a head that won't turn, and a fear of permanent damage
Neck pain and arm tingling rarely threaten life, but they do threaten a person's working day, sleep, driving and sense of control. Hannah's consultation is driven by fear and exhaustion as much as by pain. A plan that ignores her workload, stress and sleep will fail however good the prescription.
💼
Work & the Fit Note

Staying at work with adjustments usually leads to a better recovery than time off. Hannah's job is itself a driver of her pain, so the aim is to change how she works rather than to stop working.

Fit note, if needed: "may be fit for work taking into account the following advice": workstation assessment, regular breaks, amended hours for a short period. Her employer has a duty to assess display screen equipment use. Occupational health if symptoms persist.

🚗
Driving & DVLA

Safe driving requires turning the head to check mirrors, blind spots and junctions. If pain or stiffness stops her doing that, or if sedating medication (amitriptyline, codeine, gabapentinoids) impairs her, she should not drive until it improves. Driving while impaired by medicines is an offence.

GOV.UK/DVLA: drivers must tell the DVLA about a medical condition that affects their ability to drive safely (for example, myelopathy affecting hand or leg control). Check the condition-specific GOV.UK guidance, as bus and lorry (Group 2) licence holders have different rules. Document the advice given.

👨‍👩‍👧
Family & Home

Carrying shopping, lifting children or grandchildren and housework can all flare the pain. Family members may encourage rest "to protect the neck", which is well meant but slows recovery.

Suggest pacing, sharing lifting for a short time and keeping active together. Include any caring responsibilities in the plan.

😴
Sleep & Stress

Stress raises muscle tension, and poor sleep lowers pain tolerance. The two often start before the neck pain and keep it going afterwards. Hannah describes both.

Offer practical sleep advice. Check mood with PHQ-9/GAD-7 if indicated. Offer NHS Talking Therapies self-referral for anxiety or depression, and signpost workplace support (EAP). Amitriptyline or duloxetine can be chosen with sleep or mood in mind.

🧠
Health Anxiety & Control

"Trapped nerve doing damage" is a frightening idea. Anxiety makes people check the symptom repeatedly, avoid movement and seek scans. Each of these tends to make things worse.

Give accurate information, a written safety-net and a named review date. Knowing exactly what would prompt action gives a sense of control, and in practice reduces unscheduled attendance.

⚖️
Whiplash, Claims & Litigation

After a collision, anger, a legal claim and repeated medical assessments can keep attention on the symptoms. Neither dismiss nor inflate them.

Keep objective, dated records of findings and function. Recommend the same active plan regardless of the claim. Screen for post-traumatic stress symptoms after a frightening crash.

🎯 The three pivots that pass this case
1 · Root vs cord
One-arm dermatomal = radiculopathy (self-limiting). Both hands + gait + brisk reflexes = myelopathy → urgent MRI + neurosurgery.
2 · Screen the sinister
Trauma, infection, malignancy (MSCC — NG234/NG12), inflammatory arthritis (RA atlantoaxial), dissection/SAH — exclude before calling it mechanical.
3 · Restraint + reassurance
No routine MRI; no opioids/collar. Reframe the fear, keep active, ergonomics, and safety-net myelopathy.
⛔ Don't miss cervical myelopathy (both hands, gait, brisk reflexes, bladder) · Don't scan routine mechanical neck pain · Don't prescribe opioids or recommend a collar · Don't dismiss genuine radicular symptoms or the patient's fear · Always give a myelopathy safety-net
