📋 Clinical Stem — Vaginal Discharge & STI Screen
A 24-year-old woman with three weeks of altered vaginal discharge and one episode of bleeding after sex, who "just wants some thrush treatment" and is reluctant to be examined
Aisha Rahman, 24, attends asking for thrush treatment. She has had three weeks of increased vaginal discharge, which she describes as different from her usual thrush — more watery, and she's noticed a smell. On direct, sensitive questioning she mentions one episode of light bleeding after sex last week. She has a new partner over the past two months and they have not consistently used condoms; her first cervical screening test, three months ago after her invitation at 24½, was normal (HPV negative). She is keen to be given thrush treatment and leave, and is hesitant about an examination or swabs. She is otherwise well, with no fever or severe pain.
This stem tests the ability to: take a non-judgmental sexual history and risk assessment; recognise that "different from usual thrush" plus odour and a new partner shifts the differential toward BV and STIs rather than candida; offer appropriate testing (self-taken vulvovaginal NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhoea, and a full STI screen) and explain why; recognise post-coital bleeding as a feature that mandates examining the cervix and considering the NICE NG12 cervical cancer pathway if the cervix looks suspicious (not simply reassuring on the basis of a recent normal smear); manage PID risk; and handle partner notification, safeguarding and the patient's reluctance to be examined with sensitivity. The SCA challenge is balancing her wish for a quick fix against the need to test and examine.
Scenario A — Bacterial vaginosis Thin grey discharge, fishy odour, no itch, raised pH. Metronidazole; advise on recurrence triggers (douching, etc.); not an STI but consider STI risk.
Scenario B — Vulvovaginal candidiasis Thick white discharge, vulval itch/soreness, normal pH. Azole treatment; if recurrent (≥4/yr) induction + maintenance and screen for diabetes.
Scenario C — STI (chlamydia/gonorrhoea/trichomonas) New partner, inconsistent condoms, altered discharge ± dysuria/bleeding. NAAT self-swab + full STI screen; treat; partner notification; test of cure where indicated.
Scenario D — Pelvic inflammatory disease Lower abdominal/pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, abnormal bleeding, cervical motion/adnexal tenderness. Treat empirically; exclude ectopic/pregnancy; safety-net; partner treatment.
Scenario E — Cervical red flag Post-coital or intermenstrual bleeding; a friable/irregular cervix on examination. Examine the cervix; urgent referral for suspected cervical cancer (NG12) if suspicious — a recent normal smear does not exclude it.

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you think is causing it — and what's made you feel it's thrush?"
Aisha has self-diagnosed thrush. Engaging her idea lets you gently introduce that the features she describes (odour, watery, different from usual, bleeding after sex) don't all fit thrush — opening the door to testing without making her feel doubted or judged.
😟 Concerns
"Is there anything you've been worried it might be?"
She may be quietly worried about an STI (given the new partner) but reluctant to say so, or embarrassed about examination. Naming the worry, and reassuring on confidentiality and non-judgment, often unlocks an honest history and consent to testing.
🎯 Expectations
"You were hoping for thrush treatment — can I explain why I'd like to check a couple of things first?"
Her expectation is a quick prescription. Naming it lets you offer a better deal: the right treatment based on a self-taken test, addressing the bleeding properly, and protecting her future fertility — framed as being on her side, not obstructing her.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Embarrassment, stigma and the wish to be in and out quickly

Vaginal symptoms and sexual-health questions carry stigma and embarrassment, and many people present hoping for a quick, private fix — "just some thrush cream." A consultation that simply hands over the cream misses BV, STIs, PID and, occasionally, cervical pathology; one that interrogates or judges drives the patient away and damages trust. The work is to create a confidential, non-judgmental, unhurried space, explain why a few questions and a simple (often self-taken) test give the right answer, and respect autonomy if she declines — while being clear about the bleeding that does need looking at.

🔒 Confidentiality

Explicit reassurance about confidentiality (and its limits in safeguarding) is the foundation of an honest sexual history — especially for young people.

"Everything we talk about here is confidential. The only time I'd ever need to share anything is if I were worried about your safety — and I'd talk to you first."
🙅 Reluctance to be examined

Offer the self-taken vulvovaginal swab to lower the barrier; explain when an examination is genuinely needed (the bleeding) and let her retain control.

"For the infection tests you can take the swab yourself — it's just like inserting a tampon. The one thing I would like to look at, because of the bleeding after sex, is your cervix — but we can talk that through and you're always in control."
💬 Non-judgmental partner notification

Framing partner notification as routine and blame-free (and offered via the clinic/GUM) makes it acceptable and protects others.

"If a test is positive, we'd want to let recent partners know so they can be treated too — that can be done completely anonymously through the sexual-health service. It's routine and nobody is blamed."
🩺 Future fertility framing

Linking testing to protecting her future fertility (untreated chlamydia → PID → tubal damage) gives a positive, personal reason to test.

"One reason I'm keen to test rather than guess is that some infections, if missed, can quietly affect fertility later — testing now is the best way to protect that."
1E — Psychosocial context: the person behind the discharge
🧭
Who is Aisha, and what is shaping what she tells you?
A sexual-health history is only as good as the trust in the room. What a patient discloses about partners, bleeding, coercion or previous infections depends on her age, culture, relationship, previous experiences of healthcare and what she fears the answer might mean. Exploring context is not a detour from the clinical task — it is how you get the information that decides the clinical task.
💞
The new relationship

A two-month relationship is often the point when condoms are dropped and when a positive STI result feels most threatening — "does this mean he's been unfaithful?" or "will he think I have?" Many patients avoid testing to avoid that conversation. Explain that chlamydia can be carried silently for months or years, so a positive result rarely tells you who acquired it from whom or when.

"Infections like chlamydia can sit quietly for a long time without symptoms, so a positive test doesn't tell us who had it first. It's about getting you both treated, not about blame."
🕌
Culture, faith and family

For some patients, sex outside marriage or a new relationship carries family, cultural or religious weight, and confidentiality from relatives (including relatives registered at the same practice, or who may see a prescription or letter) is a real concern. Do not assume — ask how she would like results and contact handled (which phone number, whether a message can be left, text vs call).

"How would you like us to get in touch about results? Is it okay to text this number, or would you rather we only call you directly?"
🛡️
Coercion and domestic abuse

Recurrent STIs, requests for repeated emergency contraception, a partner who controls contraception or condom use, or reluctance to be seen alone can signal intimate-partner violence or reproductive coercion. Ask routinely and privately, not only when there is a visible sign. If she discloses, the plan changes — safety, IDVA/local domestic abuse services, documentation that the partner cannot access, and care with partner notification.

"I ask everyone this: does anyone ever pressure you into sex, or stop you using contraception or condoms when you want to?"
🧒
Young people

Under-16s can consent to STI testing and treatment if Gillick competent, and to contraception if the Fraser criteria are met; confidentiality is maintained unless there is a risk of serious harm. Every under-18 needs a proportionate child sexual exploitation assessment (age and age of partner, gifts or money, alcohol or drugs, older "boyfriend", going missing, repeat STIs or emergency contraception). A child under 13 cannot consent to sexual activity in law (Sexual Offences Act 2003) — disclosure of sexual activity or an STI in an under-13 means a safeguarding referral every time.

"You can see me on your own and what we talk about stays private. The only time I'd need to share anything is if I thought you or someone else was at risk of serious harm — and I'd talk to you about it first."
🩺
Previous experiences of examination

Reluctance to be examined may be embarrassment, but it may also reflect previous sexual trauma, vaginismus, FGM, or a painful previous speculum examination. Offering a self-taken swab, a clinician of her preferred gender, a chaperone, a smaller speculum, and explicit permission to stop are trauma-informed defaults, not concessions.

"Some people find examinations difficult for all sorts of reasons, and you don't have to tell me why. You're in control — we can stop at any point, and if today isn't right we can plan another time."
💼
Time, work and access

"I'm in a rush" is often real: an office job, a lunch-break appointment, the difficulty of getting back for results. Make the efficient path the safe path — self-swab and bloods today, results by text, the examination booked at a time that suits her (with a clear reason why it should not wait long), and direct booking or online self-sampling from the local sexual health service where available.

"I know you're short of time. If we do the swab and the blood test now, the results come to your phone — and we'll find a time that works for the examination this week."

🤝 Partner notification & follow-up

Positive STI → partner notification (often via GUM, can be anonymous) is essential to prevent reinfection and onward transmission.

Advise abstinence until both partners treated; arrange test of cure where indicated — chlamydia in pregnancy (no sooner than 3 weeks after treatment); gonorrhoea only in selected cases (BASHH 2025: not routinely needed for anogenital infection treated with ceftriaxone 1 g IM when the isolate is susceptible — needed if susceptibility unknown, pharyngeal infection, an alternative regimen, pregnancy or persistent symptoms, as advised by GUM); persistent symptoms. Retest under-25s with chlamydia for reinfection at about 3 months (NCSP; the BASHH 2026 guideline allows 2–6 months after treatment).

Offer onward referral to sexual-health services for full screening, contact tracing and complex cases.

🚩 The discipline — treat the infection, but examine the bleeding

The trap is to find an infective cause (BV or chlamydia), treat it, and attribute the post-coital bleeding to that — without ever looking at the cervix. Cervical cancer is repeatedly missed this way. For Aisha, the formulation is: characterise and test the discharge (likely BV ± STI given the risk), AND examine the cervix because of the post-coital bleeding — referring urgently under NG12 if it looks suspicious, independent of her recent normal smear.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
She came for thrush cream — acknowledge that, explain why the picture has changed, then offer a plan that is quick, private and hers to control
1
Validate — name the expectation and the hurry

Aisha expects a prescription for thrush and a short appointment. She has had thrush before, so her self-diagnosis is reasonable on its face, and her time pressure is real. Naming both out loud tells her you have heard her before you ask anything of her — and makes it much less likely that the rest of the plan feels like an obstacle course.

"You've had thrush before, so it makes complete sense that you thought this was the same thing — and I can see you're short of time. Let me explain why I don't think this one is thrush, and then we'll find the quickest way to sort it."
2
Explain — share the reasoning in her own terms

Three things she told you do not fit thrush: the discharge is watery rather than thick, there is a smell, and it does not itch. Together they point towards bacterial vaginosis, which needs a different medicine — thrush cream will not work. The new partner and inconsistent condoms mean a sexual-health check is sensible, and the bleeding after sex needs the cervix looked at whatever the swabs show. Keep the explanation to these three plain points; resist a lecture.

"Thrush is usually thick, white and itchy. What you're describing — watery, with a smell and no itch — sounds more like a bacterial imbalance called BV, and thrush cream won't treat that. Because there's a new partner, I'd also like to check for infections that often have no symptoms. And the bleeding after sex is the one thing I don't want to guess about."

Negotiate — something today, and control over the rest

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Much of what prevents the next episode costs nothing and is rarely said. Self-care for BV and candida, safer sex, contraception review and screening are part of the management, not an afterthought — and each one can be tailored to what Aisha has told you.
🚿
Stop douching & perfumed products
Target: water or plain emollient externally only
Mechanism

Lactobacilli keep the vagina acidic (pH ≤4.5) by producing lactic acid; that acidity suppresses the anaerobes that cause BV. Douching, antiseptics, perfumed washes, bubble bath and vaginal deodorants strip lactobacilli and raise pH — the very conditions in which BV recurs. Irritants also cause a contact vulvitis that is easily mistaken for thrush.

Practical

Ask directly — many patients douche because they notice odour, which worsens the cycle. Advise washing the vulva with water or an unperfumed emollient and never washing inside the vagina. Explain that BV is not caused by poor hygiene; over-washing is more often the problem.

BASHH 2012 BV guideline and its BASHH patient information list douching, bubble baths, scented soaps, antiseptics and feminine washes as modifiable BV triggers
🛡️
Condoms & safer sex
Target: condoms with new partners until both tested
Mechanism

Condoms reduce transmission of chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomonas and HIV, and semen raises vaginal pH, so condom use is also associated with fewer BV recurrences. A new relationship is the natural moment both partners test and agree when to stop using condoms.

Practical

Frame it as routine: "many couples both get tested at the start of a relationship." Warn that clindamycin cream and clotrimazole vaginal products can damage latex condoms and diaphragms — use another method during and shortly after treatment (check product information). Free condoms are available through local sexual health services and C-Card schemes for young people.

Abstain (including oral sex) until treatment is complete and partners are treated — the commonest cause of "treatment failure" is reinfection
💊
Contraception review
Target: no pregnancy risk from missed pills; LARC offered
Mechanism

Aisha sometimes misses her pill. Missed pills plus unprotected sex create a pregnancy risk that changes drug choice (no metronidazole 2 g stat, no fluconazole, no doxycycline) and makes ectopic pregnancy part of any pain-and-bleeding differential. Metronidazole and doxycycline are not enzyme inducers and do not reduce the effectiveness of hormonal contraception.

Practical

Do a pregnancy test today. If recent missed pills and unprotected sex put her at risk, apply FSRH missed-pill rules (emergency contraception if indicated; repeat a pregnancy test at least 3 weeks after the last unprotected sex). Offer long-acting reversible contraception — if she chooses a copper IUD, note it is associated with BV, and intrauterine contraception should not be fitted with untreated symptomatic infection or cervicitis.

FSRH: STI test at the time of IUC fitting if at risk; delay fitting if symptomatic until tested and treated
🧪
Regular testing & self-sampling
Target: test with each new partner; retest if positive
Mechanism

Most chlamydia and much gonorrhoea is asymptomatic, so symptoms are a poor guide to infection. Testing at partner change and retesting after a positive result find reinfection before it ascends to cause PID.

Practical

Tell her about free online self-sampling kits from her local sexual health service. If chlamydia is positive, arrange a retest for reinfection at about 3 months (NCSP recommendation for under-25s; the BASHH 2026 guideline allows 2–6 months). Offer HIV and syphilis testing with each full screen; discuss PrEP only if her risk profile indicates it.

NCSP (since 2021) focuses the opportunistic offer on young women and people with a womb or ovaries aged 15–24
🚭
Smoking & alcohol
Target: no alcohol during metronidazole + 48 h after
Mechanism

Smoking is associated with BV, and stopping is one more modifiable factor for recurrence. Metronidazole product information advises avoiding alcohol during treatment and for 48 hours afterwards because of a possible disulfiram-like reaction (flushing, vomiting, palpitations) — the evidence is weak, but it remains the licensed advice.

Practical

Ask about plans for the next week — a birthday or wedding is when patients stop the course early. A vaginal gel option avoids most systemic effects if alcohol or nausea is a barrier. Offer smoking cessation support if she smokes.

Completing the course and avoiding early stopping reduces relapse
🌸
Screening & HPV
Target: attend cervical screening when invited; HPV vaccine complete
Mechanism

Cervical screening detects high-risk HPV and pre-cancer in women without symptoms; it is not a diagnostic test for someone with symptoms. That is why post-coital bleeding needs the cervix examined even after a normal screening result — screening and symptom assessment are separate pathways.

Practical

Reinforce that her normal screening result is good news for the future, but does not answer today's question. Check HPV vaccination status (routine in the UK schools programme). Explain that the cervical examination today is about the bleeding, not a repeat smear.

NICE NG12: a previous normal screening result should not delay referral of a suspicious-looking cervix

7F — Psychosocial impact: relationships, work, identity and trust
🫂
A "simple" discharge that touches relationships, fertility worries and self-image
For many patients the hardest part of this consultation is not the treatment but what the diagnosis seems to say about them — cleanliness, faithfulness, their partner, their future fertility. The GP who treats the infection but leaves those meanings unexamined sends the patient home to worry, argue, or avoid the next test.
💔
Telling a partner

A positive STI result can feel like an accusation. Offer the options for partner notification — telling the partner herself, the clinic contacting partners anonymously, or a joint approach — and be clear that timing of infection usually cannot be determined. If there is any concern about abuse, partner notification is done by the service, not by the patient.

"You don't have to have this conversation alone. The sexual health team can contact your partner without giving your name."
🧼
Shame and "being dirty"

BV and thrush are commonly misunderstood as hygiene problems, which drives more washing and more recurrence. Explicitly de-stigmatise: BV is a change in the balance of normal bacteria, candida is a normal inhabitant that overgrows, and neither reflects cleanliness.

"This isn't about being clean or not — washing more can actually make it worse. It's just the balance of the normal bacteria that has shifted."
🍼
Fertility fears

Mentioning fertility can motivate testing but can also frighten. Put it in proportion: a treated chlamydia infection found early is very unlikely to cause problems; the risk lies in untreated or repeated infection and PID. Offer that perspective proactively if a result is positive.

"Finding it now and treating it is exactly what protects your fertility — it's infections that are missed for a long time that cause problems."
💼
Work and practicalities

This rarely needs time off work or a fit note. The practical barriers are appointments, results and the examination: offer text results, early or late slots, and direct booking with sexual health. Driving: there are no DVLA implications for these conditions or for the standard treatments.

"You won't need any time off for this. Let's make the follow-up fit around work — results by text, and an appointment time that suits you."
🔐
Confidentiality and records

Worries about who can see the record — parents on the same practice list, a partner with access to her phone or NHS App — are common and legitimate. Explain what goes in the record, that sexual health clinics keep separate confidential records, and agree safe contact details.

"If you'd prefer, the sexual health clinic can do the tests, and their records are kept separately from your GP notes."
🔁
Recurrence and frustration

Recurrent BV or thrush erodes quality of life, sex and confidence, and patients often cycle through pharmacy treatments. Validate the frustration, confirm the diagnosis properly, and offer a plan (triggers, route switch, suppression with specialist advice) rather than another identical prescription.

"If this keeps coming back, it deserves a proper plan — not just another box of the same thing."

Ectopic pregnancy and severe PID/tubo-ovarian abscess can evolve over hours. Missed pills plus a new partner make pregnancy possible, so the ectopic safety-net is not theoretical for Aisha.
🟠 Urgent — bleeding and PID symptoms
"Please come back within a day or two if the bleeding after sex happens again, if you get bleeding between periods, or if you develop pain deep inside during sex — and please don't cancel the appointment to look at your cervix, even if the discharge clears up."
Treating the discharge often resolves the symptom that brought her in, which is exactly when the examination for post-coital bleeding gets dropped. The phrase decouples the two.

🚫 Danger zones in management
✗
Thrush cream for an odorous, non-itchy discharge→ The features point to BV (or trichomonas). Check pH and treat the cause; candida usually has normal pH and itch.
✗
Post-coital bleeding attributed to the infection→ Examine the cervix. A cervix consistent with cancer → NG12 suspected cancer pathway, regardless of screening history.
✗
Fluconazole, doxycycline or metronidazole 2 g without a pregnancy check→ She misses pills. Test first; use topical azole, azithromycin regimen or metronidazole 400 mg BD if pregnant.
✗
PID treated as "just BV" or with doxycycline alone→ Pelvic pain + tenderness = PID until proven otherwise: ceftriaxone 1 g IM + 14 days doxycycline + metronidazole (BASHH 2019), pregnancy test, 72-hour review.
✗
Oral tablets for gonorrhoea in general practice→ Refer to GUM for culture and ceftriaxone 1 g IM (BASHH 2025); resistance is rising.
✗
Treating the patient but not the partner→ Chlamydia, gonorrhoea and trichomonas need partner notification and abstinence advice; BV and candida do not need routine partner treatment.
✗
Missing safeguarding in a young person→ Every under-18 needs a proportionate exploitation assessment; under-13 = safeguarding referral every time (Sexual Offences Act 2003). FGM disclosed by, or visually identified in, an under-18 must be reported to the police within one month (FGM mandatory reporting duty, England and Wales).
✗
Repeated empirical treatment for "recurrent" discharge→ Confirm the diagnosis (Gram stain, culture/speciation, TV NAAT), look for a foreign body or retained tampon, check HbA1c for recurrent candida, and consider lichen sclerosus or dermatitis.
