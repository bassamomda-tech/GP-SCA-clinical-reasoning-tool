📋 Clinical Stem — Perinatal Mental Health
A first-time mother at 7 weeks postnatal, brought by her partner, tearful and exhausted, convinced she is "a terrible mother" — with intrusive thoughts she is frightened to disclose
Priya Sharma, 31, attends with her 7-week-old baby and her partner, ostensibly about the baby's feeding. She is tearful, exhausted and flat. With gentle questioning she describes 4 weeks of persistent low mood, poor sleep even when the baby sleeps, loss of enjoyment, poor appetite, and overwhelming guilt that she is "a terrible mother" and the baby "would be better off without me around." She is breastfeeding and very anxious about taking any medication. When asked carefully and given space, she discloses distressing intrusive thoughts of the baby coming to harm, which horrify her and which she has told no one. She denies any wish to act on them. There is no confusion, grandiosity or loss of touch with reality. She has no past psychiatric history but her mother had "a breakdown" after childbirth.
This stem tests the ability to: distinguish baby blues from postnatal depression and from the emergency of postpartum psychosis; conduct a thorough, sensitive risk assessment (thoughts of self-harm/suicide and thoughts about the baby), and correctly interpret ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts of harm (a feature of postnatal depression/anxiety/OCD — distressing but not psychosis) versus psychotic command/delusional phenomena; explore the guilt and the fear of disclosure; manage treatment in a breastfeeding mother (talking therapy, and sertraline where an antidepressant is needed) without withholding effective care; recognise the family history of probable postpartum psychosis as a risk marker; and arrange appropriate support (health visitor, perinatal mental health team) with a clear safety-net. The SCA challenge is creating enough safety for honest disclosure and responding to the intrusive thoughts without alarm or over-reaction.
Scenario A — Postnatal depression (this stem) Persistent low mood >2 weeks, guilt, anhedonia, impaired function/bonding, ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts, no psychosis. Risk assess; talking therapy ± sertraline; health visitor + perinatal team; safety-net; review.
Scenario B — Baby blues Days 3–10, tearful, labile, anxious, resolving, function preserved. Reassurance, support, watchful waiting; review if persisting beyond 2 weeks.
Scenario C — Postpartum psychosis (EMERGENCY) Rapid onset (often first 2 weeks), mania/confusion, delusions (often about the baby), hallucinations, severe insomnia, behavioural change. Same-day psychiatric assessment; usually Mother & Baby Unit admission; high suicide/infanticide risk.
Scenario D — Perinatal anxiety / OCD / PTSD Marked anxiety, intrusive ego-dystonic thoughts with checking/avoidance (OCD), or re-experiencing after traumatic birth (PTSD). Talking therapy first-line; SSRI if needed; perinatal team if severe.
Scenario E — High-risk antenatal flag Personal/family history of bipolar or postpartum psychosis identified in pregnancy. Pre-emptive perinatal psychiatry referral and a written care plan for the perinatal period.
Key variables to adapt for Timing (blues days 3–10; psychosis often first 2 weeks; PND any point in the first year); severity and function; risk to self and baby; nature of intrusive thoughts (ego-dystonic vs delusional/command); past/family history (bipolar, postpartum psychosis); breastfeeding and medication choice; birth trauma (PTSD); social support, domestic abuse, safeguarding; physical contributors (thyroid, anaemia).

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you make of how you've been feeling — what do you think is going on?"
Priya may believe she is simply failing as a mother, or fear she is "going mad." Surfacing her model lets you reframe this as a common, recognised, treatable illness — not a character flaw — which is itself therapeutic and reduces the shame that fuels non-disclosure.
😟 Concerns
"Is there something you've been frightened might happen if you told someone?"
Her central concern is almost certainly that disclosing her thoughts will lead to her baby being taken away. Naming and addressing this fear directly — explaining that the aim is to support her and keep mother and baby together — is what unlocks honesty and engagement.
🎯 Expectations
"What were you hoping for from coming in — and how do you feel about different kinds of help?"
She may expect to be told to "pull herself together," or fear being pushed onto medication while breastfeeding. Naming her expectations lets you offer a menu (talking therapy, health visitor support, safe medication if needed) and make a shared decision that respects her wishes about breastfeeding.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Guilt, stigma and the fear of losing the baby

Perinatal mental illness is shrouded in guilt and stigma — the cultural expectation that new motherhood is joyful makes women feel they are failing, and the fear that admitting dark thoughts will lead to their baby being removed keeps them silent. That silence is dangerous: it is why perinatal suicide and undiagnosed illness persist. The single most important clinical act is to create a non-judgmental space in which honest disclosure feels safe, to normalise intrusive thoughts and low mood, and to be explicit that the goal is to help her and keep her and her baby well — together.

🤱 "I'm a terrible mother"

The pervasive guilt of PND is a symptom, not a truth. Naming it as part of the illness, and separating her worth from her symptoms, is therapeutic.

"That feeling of being a terrible mother is one of the most common symptoms of this illness — it's the depression talking, not the truth. The fact you're here, worrying about your baby, tells me a lot about the kind of mother you are."
😨 Fear the baby will be taken

This fear silences women. Address it openly: the aim is support, and most women are helped at home with their baby.

"I want to reassure you — telling me how you feel will not lead to your baby being taken away. My job is to help you get well, and the vast majority of mums are supported at home, with their baby, exactly where they should be."
🧠 Normalising intrusive thoughts

Ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts are common and frightening; explaining what they are relieves enormous distress.

"Those horrible thoughts that pop into your head and upset you — lots of parents get them, precisely because you love your baby and your mind is on high alert. Having them does not mean you'll act on them; the distress they cause you is actually reassuring."
🤝 Support & the partner

Involve the partner and health visitor; isolation worsens outcomes. Practical support and sleep matter.

"You don't have to carry this alone. With your agreement I'd like to involve your health visitor and, where helpful, your partner, so you've got proper support around you — and we'll look at practical things like rest too."

🚩 The discipline — the same thought, two very different illnesses

The pivotal clinical judgement is the nature of a thought about harm. An ego-dystonic intrusive thought — unwanted, distressing, recognised as wrong, with no intent and preserved insight — is a feature of postnatal depression/anxiety/OCD and is reassuring in its very distress; it should be normalised and treated, not panicked over. A thought driven by a delusion or command hallucination, or with intent, or with lost insight, is postpartum psychosis — an emergency. For Priya, the formulation is moderate postnatal depression with ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts, no psychosis, current risk low but with a family history of probable postpartum psychosis warranting vigilance.

Step 7
Management — Talking Therapy · Medication & Breastfeeding · Support · Safety-Net
▲collapse
Management is stepped to severity: support and talking therapy for mild–moderate illness, antidepressants (sertraline commonly, even when breastfeeding) where moderate–severe or unresponsive, the health visitor and social support throughout, and a clear, explicit safety-net for escalation. The guiding principle is not to withhold effective treatment from a breastfeeding mother out of unfounded medication fears.
7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Priya has two unspoken expectations — that honesty will cost her the baby, and that treatment will mean stopping breastfeeding. Answer both before offering any plan
1
Validate — name the fear and the wish

Priya expects judgement, and possibly the involvement of social services, because she has admitted to frightening thoughts. She also wants to keep breastfeeding and fears medication will harm the baby through her milk. Both are reasonable given what she knows. Naming them out loud, before discussing treatment, shows that you have heard what she has not quite said, and it takes the edge off her fear.

"I suspect two things might be worrying you: that telling me about those thoughts means someone will take your baby, and that getting help means giving up breastfeeding. Can I answer both of those honestly before we talk about anything else?"
2
Explain — share the reasoning plainly

Ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts are a recognised feature of perinatal depression and anxiety. They do not mean she is dangerous, and on their own they are not a safeguarding trigger. Involving the health visitor and perinatal services is about supporting the family. Breastfeeding does not rule out treatment: psychological therapy is first-line for mild to moderate illness, and where an antidepressant is needed, sertraline passes into breast milk in only small amounts. Leaving depression untreated carries its own risks for mother and baby.

"The thoughts you've described are something many mums with postnatal depression get. They frighten you precisely because you'd never act on them. That's not a reason to worry about your baby's safety; it's a reason to get you well. And you won't have to choose between getting better and breastfeeding."
3
Negotiate — a concrete plan she has chosen

Offer a menu rather than a prescription: talking therapy (with rapid perinatal access), health visitor listening visits, practical support, and sertraline if she wants to add it now or if symptoms don't improve. 7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
NICE CG192 places psychological interventions at the centre of care for mild to moderate perinatal depression and anxiety, and asks that they are started quickly (the guideline's aim is assessment within 2 weeks of referral and psychological intervention within 1 month of that assessment). Practical, social and relational support comes alongside therapy, not instead of it. For Priya, every item below is compatible with breastfeeding and can start this week.
💬
Psychological therapy (CBT / IPT)
Target: referral today; perinatal priority access
Mechanism

CBT targets the thinking patterns behind the guilt and hopelessness ("I'm a terrible mother") and the way intrusive thoughts are interpreted. Interpersonal therapy works on role transition — the move into motherhood — and on relationships and support. For intrusive harm thoughts, exposure-based CBT reduces the checking and avoidance that keep them going.

Practical

NHS Talking Therapies services in England prioritise pregnant and postnatal women, and many accept self-referral. Where symptoms are subthreshold to moderate, facilitated self-help is an option; for moderate to severe depression, high-intensity CBT (or IPT) is appropriate. Check that the service can see her with the baby present, or that childcare is possible.

NICE CG192: offer psychological intervention for mild to moderate perinatal depression and anxiety
🏠
Health visitor partnership
Target: HV contacted within days, with consent
Mechanism

The health visitor sees mother and baby at home. She can offer listening visits, repeat the EPDS, watch how feeding and bonding are going, and pick up deterioration earlier than a GP appointment would. Continuity reduces isolation and builds a trusting relationship that makes disclosure easier.

Practical

With Priya's consent, phone or task the health visitor the same day, share the risk formulation and the crisis plan, and agree who reviews what and when. Explain to Priya that this is support, not surveillance, and say it explicitly, because she fears exactly the opposite.

Joint GP–HV working is a core part of perinatal care pathways
😴
Protecting sleep
Target: one protected block of sleep most nights
Mechanism

Severe sleep loss worsens mood, anxiety and intrusive thoughts. In women at risk of bipolar disorder or postpartum psychosis, sleep disruption is a recognised trigger for relapse. Insomnia even when the baby is asleep is a symptom to track, and a sudden reduced need for sleep is a warning sign of mania.

Practical

Plan with the couple: the partner takes one night feed (expressed milk or formula top-up if she chooses) so she gets a 4–5 hour block. Share night duties at weekends and make use of nap opportunities. Avoid sedative hypnotics while breastfeeding or co-sleeping unless a specialist advises them. Give safe-sleep advice for the baby.

Protected sleep is a standard relapse-prevention step in perinatal psychiatry
🤱
Mother–infant relationship support
Target: bonding assessed and supported at each review
Mechanism

Depression can blunt a mother's responsiveness to her baby, and she may then read the baby's behaviour as rejection, which deepens the guilt. Interventions that focus on the relationship (such as video-interaction guidance or parent–infant psychotherapy, where available) help her see the baby's cues and her own competence.

Practical

Point out specific moments of connection in the room ("she's watching your face"). The health visitor or local parent–infant service can offer structured work. Baby massage and parent–baby groups offer contact with other parents, but only if she feels able to go.

NICE CG192 recommends considering extra help with the mother–baby relationship where there are difficulties
🫂
Peer and voluntary support
Target: one peer contact signposted today
Mechanism

Hearing other mothers describe the same thoughts and guilt is powerfully normalising, and it reduces the shame that drives silence. Peer support also eases isolation, a recognised risk factor, especially for first-time mothers living far from family.

Practical

Signpost to national perinatal charities (for example PANDAS Foundation, and Action on Postpartum Psychosis for women with a family history of postpartum psychosis) and to local peer groups. Include the partner: charities also run support for fathers and partners.

Peer support is an adjunct that improves engagement; it does not replace treatment
🍎
Physical recovery and the basics
Target: FBC/ferritin and TFTs checked; pain treated
Mechanism

Postpartum iron deficiency, postpartum thyroiditis, unrelieved pain and poor nutrition all cause fatigue and low mood and make depression harder to treat. Gentle physical activity has a modest antidepressant effect and gives the day some structure.

Practical

Check FBC, ferritin and TFTs; treat any deficiency. Ask about perineal or wound pain and about continence. Encourage regular meals and fluids (especially while breastfeeding), and short daily walks with the pram, framed as something for her rather than another task to fail at.

Treating physical contributors often lifts energy within weeks

Valproate — do not use in women and girls of childbearing potential for mental illness
  • NICE CG192: do not offer valproate for acute or long-term treatment of a mental health problem in women and girls of childbearing potential, unless other options are ineffective or not tolerated and the pregnancy prevention programme is in place.
  • Babies exposed in utero have around a 10% risk of major congenital malformations and a 30–40% risk of neurodevelopmental disorders (figures used in MHRA and NICE materials).
  • MHRA (from 31 January 2024): valproate must not be started in anyone, male or female, under 55 unless two specialists independently consider and document that no other effective or tolerated treatment exists, or that the reproductive risks do not apply. Lithium — specialist decisions only
  • Lithium in pregnancy carries a small increased risk of fetal cardiac malformations. Stopping it, especially abruptly, sharply raises the risk of bipolar relapse. Whether to continue is a specialist decision made with the woman, ideally before conception.
  • NICE CG192: if lithium is continued in pregnancy, check plasma levels every 4 weeks, then weekly from 36 weeks, and within 24 hours of childbirth; make sure she drinks enough fluid; plan birth in an obstetric unit with fluid balance monitored.
  • Breastfeeding: NICE CG192 advises women taking lithium not to breastfeed, because lithium passes into milk and can reach toxic levels in the infant.
  • GP role: flag every woman of childbearing potential on lithium for preconception advice; never stop it without specialist input; recognise toxicity (vomiting, diarrhoea, tremor, confusion) as an emergency, especially around birth and with dehydration.
SSRIs in pregnancy — the balanced conversation
  • Most SSRIs have not been linked with a major overall increase in malformations. UKTIS monographs give the current evidence for each drug and should be used for individual counselling. Paroxetine has been associated with cardiac malformations in some studies and is generally not a first choice to start in pregnancy.
  • Late-pregnancy exposure is linked with transient neonatal adaptation symptoms (jitteriness, feeding difficulty) and a small increased absolute risk of persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn. Tell the maternity team so the baby can be observed after birth.
  • MHRA Drug Safety Update (January 2021): SSRI/SNRI use in the month before delivery may cause a small (less than 2-fold) increase in the risk of postpartum haemorrhage. Take this into account alongside other bleeding risks; it is not a reason to stop an effective antidepressant.
  • Untreated depression in pregnancy is associated with poorer self-care, preterm birth and postnatal depression. The risk of doing nothing belongs in the same conversation.
SSRIs in breastfeeding — what the SPS guidance says
  • The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service lists sertraline and paroxetine as the SSRIs of choice during breastfeeding. Both have shorter half-lives and pass into milk in smaller amounts than other SSRIs.
  • Fluoxetine (long half-life, active metabolite) and citalopram produce higher infant levels; they are generally not first choices to start while breastfeeding, although a woman already well on one should not be switched without advice.
  • Ask about the baby's age and health: premature, low-birth-weight or unwell infants need more caution. Advise the mother to watch for irritability, poor feeding, excessive sleepiness or poor weight gain, and to report these promptly.
  • If she started an SSRI in pregnancy and wants to breastfeed, continuing the same drug usually avoids a second exposure for the baby.

7G — Psychosocial impact: identity, relationship, work, driving and safeguarding
🫂
Perinatal illness — the mother who feels she is failing at the one thing she was "supposed" to find natural
Perinatal mental illness strikes at identity, at the couple's relationship and at the family's sense of safety, all at once and at a time of extreme exhaustion. The mother often feels ashamed of being unwell when she is "meant to be happy". The partner may feel helpless or frightened, and the wider family may offer advice that deepens the guilt. A plan that only treats symptoms, without addressing these pressures, is likely to fail.
🪞
Identity and the "good mother" ideal

Priya expected motherhood to feel natural and joyful. When it doesn't, she concludes she is defective. Social media and well-meaning relatives reinforce the ideal. Separating the illness from her identity, and explaining that bonding often grows gradually, eases the self-blame that keeps depression going.

"Lots of mums don't feel an instant rush of love, especially when they're unwell — it often grows as you recover. What I can see is a mum who is worried sick about doing right by her baby."
💑
The partner and the relationship

Partners commonly feel shut out, frightened or resentful, and they are at increased risk of depression themselves. Involving him (with Priya's consent) in the explanation, the practical plan and the crisis plan makes him an ally and an early-warning system. It is also a chance to ask how he is coping. Remember that domestic abuse must still be asked about with Priya alone.

"You've clearly been worried about her. The most helpful things you can do are protect some sleep for her, keep her company, and call for help straight away if she seems very different or talks about not being safe. And how are you doing?"
💼
Maternity leave, work and money

Worry about returning to work, reduced income on statutory maternity pay, or self-employed income stopping can all add to depression. If she is not fit to return when leave ends, a fit note can be issued. Signposting to benefits and financial advice (Citizens Advice, health visitor links) is part of management, not an extra.

"Let's not add work to your worries right now — if you're not well enough when your leave ends, we can sort a fit note and a phased return. Is money a worry at the moment?"
🚗
Driving and the DVLA

Mild to moderate depression without significant impairment does not usually need DVLA notification. DVLA guidance says that severe depression with significant memory or concentration problems, agitation, behavioural disturbance or suicidal thoughts means she must not drive and must notify the DVLA, and with an acute psychotic illness (including postpartum psychosis) she must not drive and must notify the DVLA. Sedating medication and extreme sleep deprivation also impair driving with a baby in the car.

"At the moment you're safe to drive as long as you feel alert — but if you're ever so exhausted you can't concentrate, or you're having thoughts of harming yourself, please don't drive, and we'd need to talk about telling the DVLA."
🛡️
Safeguarding — support, not punishment

Safeguarding is about the welfare of the baby, any other children and the mother as an adult at risk. Ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts with no intent, and a mother who is caring well for her baby, do not in themselves trigger a referral to children's social care. A referral is needed when the baby's safety or care is compromised, when there is intent or psychosis, when there is domestic abuse with children in the household, or when she cannot care for the baby. If you are unsure, discuss with the practice safeguarding lead, and be open with the family where this is safe.

"My job is to keep you and your baby well together. If I were ever worried about her safety, I'd tell you honestly and we'd get extra support in — the aim is always help, not taking her away."
🌍
Culture, language and extended family

Cultural expectations about the postnatal period, the role of grandparents, and stigma around mental illness vary widely and can help or hinder recovery. Use a professional interpreter when needed, never the partner or a relative, for mental health and risk questions. Ask what support the extended family can offer, and whether anyone is making things harder.

"In some families there's a lot of support after a baby, and in others it can feel like a lot of pressure. What's it like in your family? Is there anyone who makes you feel better — or worse?"

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — postpartum psychosis or rapid change
"Very occasionally, mums become unwell in a different way — suddenly not sleeping but not feeling tired, feeling unusually high or sped up, confused, having strange ideas or hearing things. Because of what happened to your mum, I want you and your partner to know these signs. If any of them happen, that's an emergency: call the crisis line, 111 or 999, or go to A&E the same day. Don't wait for an appointment."
Postpartum psychosis can develop and worsen within hours, and the partner is often first to notice. NICE CG192 expects assessment within 4 hours of referral. Document that the partner was included, with consent.
🟠 Urgent — suicidal thoughts or a change in thoughts about the baby
"If the thought that your baby would be better off without you becomes stronger, or you start making any plans to harm yourself, or the thoughts about the baby change so that they start to feel like something you might act on — contact us or the crisis team straight away, the same day, day or night. You won't be judged, and you won't be in trouble."
MBRRACE-UK red flags — new thoughts or acts of violent self-harm, and new and persistent feelings of incompetence as a mother or estrangement from the infant — need urgent senior psychiatric assessment. The safety-net must be specific about what counts as a change.
🟡 Medication — the first weeks on an SSRI
"In the first couple of weeks on sertraline some people feel more restless or anxious before they feel better. If you feel much worse, more agitated, or have new thoughts of harming yourself, contact us promptly rather than waiting for your review. And if the baby seems unusually sleepy or isn't feeding well, let us or your health visitor know."
Early agitation and emerging suicidal thinking are recognised with antidepressants. Infant adverse effects are uncommon with sertraline, but monitoring them reassures the mother and is good practice.

⛔ Danger zone — common pitfalls in perinatal mental health
✗
Treating ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts as a psychosis or safeguarding emergency→ Explore intent, insight and distress. If the thoughts are unwanted, resisted and horrifying to her, normalise them and treat the depression or OCD.
✗
Missing early postpartum psychosis because it fluctuates or she seems "fine" today→ Ask about sleep need, elation, confusion and odd beliefs; take the partner's account seriously; refer the same day if you have any doubt.
✗
Starting an antidepressant without screening for bipolar disorder→ Ask about previous highs and the family history; refer to perinatal psychiatry if bipolar is suspected.
✗
Telling a mother to stop breastfeeding, or withholding treatment, because of medication fears→ Use SPS guidance: sertraline is commonly used; the risks of untreated depression go in the same conversation.
✗
Stopping lithium, valproate or antipsychotics abruptly when a woman becomes pregnant→ Continue and get urgent specialist advice; relapse risk after abrupt withdrawal is high.
✗
Asking about domestic abuse in front of the partner→ Ask only when she is alone; plan an opportunity if you can't do it today.
✗
Accepting "better off without me" at face value, or accepting a denial of suicidal thoughts at face value→ Explore passive ideation directly: plans, intent, method, protective factors — and revisit it at every review.
✗
Forgetting the physical contributors→ Check FBC, ferritin and TFTs; treat pain and anaemia.
