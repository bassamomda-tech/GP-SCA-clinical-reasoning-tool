📋 Clinical Stem — Psychosis & Schizophrenia
A 21-year-old student brought by his worried mother — withdrawn for months, now convinced his housemates are spying on him and hearing voices commenting on him
Daniel Osei, 21, a university student, is brought in by his mother, who is frightened by how he has changed. Over 4–5 months he has become increasingly withdrawn, stopped attending lectures and seeing friends, and neglected his self-care. In the last few weeks he has become convinced his housemates are spying on him and have "bugged" his room, and he hears two voices commenting on what he is doing. He is guarded and suspicious in the consultation, sleeps poorly, and has been smoking high-potency cannabis daily. There is a family history of schizophrenia in an uncle. He has had fleeting thoughts that he might be "better off dead" but denies any plan. He has no fever or confusion and is physically well.
This stem tests the ability to: recognise a first episode of psychosis (positive symptoms — persecutory delusions, third-person auditory hallucinations — on a background of months of negative symptoms/decline), and to assess it sensitively in a guarded patient; to assess RISK thoroughly (suicide/self-harm, command hallucinations, self-neglect, vulnerability, risk to/from others, safeguarding); to exclude organic and drug causes (cannabis, delirium, organic disease) with history, examination and baseline investigations; to refer URGENTLY to the community mental health team / Early Intervention in Psychosis service rather than starting antipsychotics alone in primary care; to engage and support the family with consent; and to understand the longer-term GP roles (physical-health monitoring, relapse prevention). The SCA challenge is building enough rapport with a suspicious young man to assess risk and facilitate referral, while supporting a frightened parent and being honest within the limits of confidentiality.
Scenario A — First-episode psychosis (this stem) Young person, positive + negative symptoms, cannabis use, family history. Risk assessment; exclude organic/drug causes; urgent EIP/CMHT referral; engage family (consent); don't start antipsychotics alone; safety-net.
Scenario B — Acute risk / crisis Command hallucinations to harm self/others, active suicidality, severe self-neglect, or refusal endangering safety. Crisis team / urgent psychiatric assessment; consider Mental Health Act.
Scenario C — Organic psychosis / delirium Older patient, acute onset, fluctuating, clouded consciousness, fever, focal signs. Treat as delirium — find and treat the cause; admit/medical work-up, not a psychiatric label.
Scenario D — Drug-induced psychosis Stimulants/cannabis/withdrawal. Manage substance use; may resolve, but new psychosis still warrants assessment; comorbid SMI common.
Scenario E — Established schizophrenia (GP role) Known patient on antipsychotics: physical-health monitoring, relapse signs, adherence, clozapine bloods, comorbidity, carer support, shared-care.

The history has to do several hard things at once: build enough trust with a suspicious young man to explore his experiences; characterise the psychotic symptoms (positive and the often-missed negative); conduct a thorough risk assessment; and screen for the organic and drug causes that must not be mislabelled as schizophrenia. Rapport is not a nicety here — without it, Daniel discloses nothing and the risk assessment fails.
🎓 SCA framing — gentle, non-confronting, curious
"Thanks for coming in — I can see this is hard. I'm not here to judge or to catch you out; I just want to understand what life's been like for you lately. Can you tell me how things have been?"
Don't challenge delusions or argue with them; explore them with curiosity ("that sounds frightening — tell me more about it"). A non-confronting, validating stance is what allows a guarded, paranoid patient to talk, and is the gateway to assessing risk.

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you make of what's been happening to you — what do you think is going on?"
Daniel may have little insight, or a frightening explanation (the housemates are real to him). Understanding his model lets you meet him where he is — neither colluding with nor flatly contradicting the delusion — and frame help in terms he can accept (stress, sleep, the distress of the voices).
😟 Concerns
"What's been the hardest or most frightening part of all this for you?"
His concern may be fear of the voices, of being watched, or of being "mad"/sectioned. Naming it builds trust, reduces threat, and lets you offer help framed around reducing his distress rather than labelling him.
🎯 Expectations
"What were you hoping might come from today — and what would feel like help to you?"
He may not want help, or fear hospital. Naming this lets you explain the supportive, non-coercive role of the specialist team, address fears about being sectioned, and negotiate engagement — central to a successful, consensual referral.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Fear, stigma, a frightened family, and the chance to intervene early

First-episode psychosis usually strikes a young person and their family at once — frightening, stigmatised, and often misread for months as "stress", drugs or laziness. The patient may be terrified, mistrustful, and lack insight; the family is frequently the one raising the alarm and carrying the distress. The consultation matters enormously because earlier treatment improves outcomes (shorter DUP). Success rests on a calm, non-judgmental, hopeful stance: engaging the young person without confrontation, taking risk seriously, supporting the family within the bounds of confidentiality, and getting specialist help moving quickly.

🤝 Engagement over confrontation

Don't argue with delusions; build trust around shared goals (sleep, less distress).

"I can't fully know what you're experiencing, but I can see it's distressing and exhausting. Whatever's behind it, I'd like to help you feel safer and sleep better — can we work on that together?"
👪 The frightened family

Support the mother; seek consent to involve her; signpost carer support.

"I can see how worried you are, and bringing him in was the right thing. With Daniel's permission I'd like to keep you involved — and there's support for families going through this too."
😨 Fear of being "sectioned"

Address the fear of hospital/coercion honestly.

"Most people get help without ever going into hospital — the team I want to involve work with you in the community. My aim is to support you, not to take control away from you."
🌱 Hope & early help

Convey realistic hope — early treatment helps.

"Getting help early genuinely makes a difference to how things go. There are effective treatments and a specialist team for exactly this — you don't have to manage it alone."
1E — Psychosocial context: the person behind the psychosis
🧭
Daniel's world: the student, the son, the cannabis, and the fear of what a diagnosis will mean
The history is not complete when you know the symptoms. You also need to know where Daniel lives and with whom, what he is using and why, who supports him, what he fears, and what he stands to lose. These questions shape both risk and engagement. A young man who is isolated in a shared house with people he believes are spying on him is in a different position from one who can go home to his mother tonight. Ask about each area gently, and in an order he can tolerate.
🏠
Where and with whom he lives

Daniel lives in a shared student house with the housemates he now believes have bugged his room. That matters for risk in both directions. He may feel he has to defend himself, and his housemates may be frightened or hostile. He may also stop eating or sleeping there, or leave suddenly. Ask where he will sleep tonight, and whether staying with his mother for now is possible and acceptable to both of them.

"Where are you staying at the moment? Do you feel safe there? Would going back to your mum's for a while help, or would that make things harder?"
🌿
Cannabis: what it does for him

Daily high-potency cannabis is both a risk factor and, for many young people, a form of self-medication for anxiety, poor sleep or the voices. If you start with a lecture he will disengage. Ask what it does for him, how much he uses, and whether he has noticed any link with how he feels. That gives you the motivational groundwork for a later brief intervention and referral to drug services.

"A lot of people find cannabis helps them switch off or sleep. What does it do for you? Have you noticed whether the voices or the worries change when you smoke more or less?"
🎓
Studies, money and the future

He has stopped attending lectures, so his course, funding and student accommodation may all be at risk. Loss of role is one of the most damaging consequences of a first episode, and returning to education or work is a named recovery goal in NICE CG178. Early contact with the university's student support or disability service, with his consent, can stop him from being withdrawn from the course by default.

"Your course matters. With your permission, I can write something for the university so they understand you've been unwell. That can protect your place while you get help."
👪
Family history and family dynamics

An uncle with schizophrenia raises Daniel's genetic risk. It may also shape what the family fears: "he's going the same way". Explore what they saw happen to the uncle. That experience, whether it was admission, detention or recovery, will colour how they react to the word "psychosis". High expressed emotion at home (criticism, hostility, over-involvement) predicts relapse, so family intervention is part of the treatment, not an optional extra.

"You mentioned your uncle. What happened for him? I ask because families who've seen someone go through this often carry that worry, and it helps me to know."
🌍
Culture, faith and trust in services

How someone understands unusual experiences depends on culture and faith. Young Black men in the UK are also more likely to reach mental health services through crisis, police or compulsory routes, and they and their families may reasonably mistrust services. Ask how Daniel and his family make sense of what is happening, and whether faith or community leaders are part of their support. Show from the start that you want him to have a say in his own care.

"People understand experiences like these in different ways, through faith, stress or family beliefs. How do you and your family make sense of it? I want the help we arrange to fit you."
🔐
Confidentiality and his mother's role

Daniel is an adult, so his information belongs to him. You can always listen to his mother and take a collateral history without breaching confidentiality. Sharing his information with her needs his consent, unless there is a serious risk to him or others that makes disclosure necessary and proportionate. It often helps to see him alone for part of the consultation, then agree with him what can be shared.

"I'd like to speak with you on your own for a few minutes. Then we can agree together what, if anything, you're happy for me to share with your mum."

🚩 Recognise and refer — don't over-label or under-treat

The GP's job is not to diagnose schizophrenia but to recognise psychosis, exclude organic/drug causes, assess risk, and get specialist help quickly. For Daniel the formulation is a likely first episode of psychosis with heavy cannabis use and a family history, current self-harm risk low but present, no organic red flags — requiring urgent EIP referral, a safety plan, family support with consent, and physical-health baseline, but NOT a GP-initiated antipsychotic or a premature schizophrenia label.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Daniel came in expecting to be judged, disbelieved or sectioned. His mother expects a diagnosis and a quick fix. Deal with both expectations before you set out the plan
1
Validate: name the fear out loud

Daniel's unspoken expectation is that telling a doctor about the voices will lead to hospital or detention. His mother may expect you to "make him take tablets" or to confirm schizophrenia today. If you leave either expectation unspoken, it will undermine the plan. Name them first, calmly and without defensiveness. His challenge, "you're going to get me sectioned", is not hostility. It shows you his main fear.

"I think you might be worried that talking to me today means being taken into hospital. I want to be honest with you: that isn't what I'm planning. From what you've told me, you're not in immediate danger. The help I have in mind is in the community, with you involved in every decision."
2
Explain: share your reasoning in his terms

You do not need him to accept the word "psychosis" to agree a plan. Frame it around what he does experience: exhaustion, fear, poor sleep, the voices being distressing, and his studies slipping. Explain that a specialist team for young people (Early Intervention in Psychosis) can see him quickly. Explain that getting help early changes how things go, and that no decision about medication will be made without him.

"Whatever is behind it, you're not sleeping, you're frightened, and your course is slipping away. There's a team that works with young people going through exactly this. They see people quickly, and the earlier they're involved, the better things tend to go. Nobody will make you take medication. It's a conversation, and you'd have a say."
3
Negotiate: agree something he can say yes to today

Offer concrete steps he can accept now: a phone referral to the EIP team today, blood tests to "check your body is OK", help with sleep, a named person to call, and agreement about what his mother can be told. Ask him to choose where he stays tonight. Offer a review within days, not weeks. When he chooses something himself, he is more likely to engage later.

"Can we agree three things today? I'll phone the team this afternoon so you're seen quickly. We'll do some blood tests to check your physical health. And you'll have a number to ring, day or night, if things feel worse. Which of those feels OK to you?"
Key principle: Engagement comes first, and the referral comes from engagement. A GP who "wins" the argument about whether the housemates are really spying loses the patient. A GP who agrees a shared goal (sleep, safety, less fear) gets the referral accepted. For his mother, the honest message is: "The specialists will work out the diagnosis over time. My job today is to make him safe and get him seen quickly, and to make sure you're supported too."
7B — Why treatment matters: goals tailored to this patient
2 weeks
NHS England EIP Access and Waiting Time Standard: at least 60% of people with first-episode psychosis should start a NICE-recommended package of care within 2 weeks of referral. Refer the same day, by phone.
15–20 yrs

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
NICE CG178 recommends offering an oral antipsychotic together with psychological interventions (family intervention and individual CBT) for first-episode psychosis. If someone declines medication, still offer the psychological treatments. Most of the interventions below are delivered by the EIP team. The GP's role is to know they exist, recommend them with conviction, and support them over time. For people on antipsychotics, NICE CG178 also recommends a combined healthy eating and physical activity programme and help to stop smoking.
🧠
CBT for psychosis (CBTp)
Target: ≥16 planned sessions, one-to-one
Mechanism

CBTp does not try to argue a person out of their beliefs. It helps them link thoughts, feelings and behaviour to their experiences, look at other explanations, reduce distress, and develop coping strategies for voices and paranoia. It follows a formulation built with the person. It also covers relapse prevention.

Practical

Delivered by the EIP or CMHT psychology team. It can start before, with or without medication. Tell Daniel it is a talking therapy "about what's been happening and how to make it less frightening", not about proving him wrong. Ask about it at every review and encourage him to finish the course.

NICE CG178: offer CBTp to everyone with first-episode or established psychosis, alongside medication
👨‍👩‍👦
Family intervention
Target: 10 planned sessions over 3 months–1 year
Mechanism

Family intervention reduces high expressed emotion (criticism, hostility, emotional over-involvement), which is a well-established predictor of relapse. It also teaches the family about the illness and early warning signs, and improves communication and problem-solving in the household. It supports carers, who are often exhausted and frightened.

Practical

Offer it to families who live with, or are in close contact with, the person (NICE CG178). Include Daniel where possible. His mother is the obvious partner. With consent, refer through EIP. Tell her about carer support (Rethink Mental Illness, local carers' centre) and her right to a carer's assessment.

NICE CG178: recommended for all families of people with psychosis in close contact. Reduces relapse and admission
🌿
Cannabis and substance use
Target: stop, or reduce as a first step; drug service if dependent
Mechanism

High-THC ("skunk") cannabis is associated with a higher risk of developing psychosis, with a dose–response relationship. In the multicentre EU-GEI case–control study (Di Forti et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2019), daily use of high-potency cannabis carried roughly five times the odds of psychotic disorder compared with never using it. Continued use after a first episode is linked to more relapse and admission. Stimulants and alcohol also worsen psychosis.

Practical

Take a non-judgemental, motivational approach. Ask about quantity, potency, route and function. Give a brief intervention at every contact. Refer to local drug services, or to the dual-diagnosis pathway where available. EIP teams usually include substance work. A positive drug screen does not rule out a primary psychotic illness.

Reducing cannabis is the most important change Daniel can make himself
🥗
Healthy eating, activity & smoking cessation
Target: from the start of any antipsychotic, not after weight has been gained
Mechanism

Antipsychotic-induced weight gain is often fastest in the first weeks to months of treatment, especially with olanzapine and clozapine. It is easier to prevent than to reverse. People with psychosis have high smoking rates, and smoking drives much of the cardiovascular part of the mortality gap.

Practical

Offer a combined healthy eating and physical activity programme to people starting antipsychotics (NICE CG178). Offer help to stop smoking with behavioural support and pharmacotherapy. Tell the mental health team first if the person takes clozapine or olanzapine, because tobacco smoke speeds their metabolism (see clozapine card). Use the Lester Positive Cardiometabolic Health Resource (2023 update) to decide when to act on weight, glucose, lipids and blood pressure.

Prevention is easier than reversal: weigh at every early review
💼
Education and employment (IPS)
Target: stay on the course or make a supported return
Mechanism

Having a role (study, work) protects against relapse, social drift and poverty. Individual Placement and Support (IPS) is "place then train": fast, supported entry into real jobs or courses, with ongoing support. It has better outcomes than traditional stepwise vocational rehab.

Practical

NICE CG178 recommends offering supported employment programmes to people who want to find or return to work. Many EIP teams include an IPS worker or education and employment support. With consent, contact the university's disability or wellbeing service early to arrange interruption of studies, extensions or Disabled Students' Allowance, rather than let him be withdrawn for non-attendance.

Returning to study is a recovery goal, not a reward for recovery
😴
Sleep, routine & relapse signature
Target: written relapse-prevention and crisis plan shared with carer
Mechanism

Sleep disruption is both a trigger and an early warning sign of relapse. Each person has a fairly consistent "relapse signature", a sequence of early changes such as poor sleep, withdrawal, irritability and suspiciousness. These often appear a few weeks before florid symptoms return. Noticing them early creates a window to act before crisis.

Practical

Encourage regular sleep and wake times, less caffeine and fewer late-night screens, and no cannabis as a sleep aid. Once he is engaged, record his early warning signs, what helps, and who to call, in a plan that he, his mother, EIP and the practice all hold. Code the crisis plan in the GP record.

Acting on the relapse signature early can prevent crisis and admission
🎨
Arts therapies & peer support
Target: considered by the specialist team, especially for negative symptoms
Mechanism

Negative symptoms (apathy, withdrawal, flattened affect) respond less well to antipsychotics than positive symptoms, but often limit function the most. Arts therapies and peer support give people ways to express themselves, connect with others and rebuild social confidence without relying on talking alone.

Practical

NICE CG178 advises considering arts therapies for people with psychosis, particularly to relieve negative symptoms. They are delivered by HCPC-registered arts therapists through secondary care. Signpost to peer-support and recovery-college courses, which many mental health trusts run.

Negative symptoms: the part of the illness that most affects function

Children and young people — NICE CG155
  • If a child or young person under 18 has suspected psychosis, refer urgently to a specialist mental health service: CAMHS, or EIP where the local pathway accepts under-18s (the EIP standard covers people from age 14). Do not wait for diagnostic certainty.
  • CG155 advises that antipsychotic medication should not be started in primary care for a first presentation of sustained psychotic symptoms unless done in consultation with a consultant psychiatrist with training in child and adolescent mental health.
  • Do not offer antipsychotics to young people with transient or attenuated symptoms (at-risk mental state).
  • Psychological treatment (CBTp, family intervention) is offered with medication. Monitoring is similar to adults, using age-appropriate growth and weight charts.
  • Think about safeguarding, education (school or college liaison) and the parents' role. Young people aged 16–17 are presumed to have capacity to consent to treatment.
Primary-care prescribing traps with antipsychotics
  • QT-prolonging combinations: check before adding macrolides, some antidepressants (for example citalopram), ondansetron or methadone to a QT-prolonging antipsychotic. Correct low potassium and magnesium.
  • Clozapine not visible on the GP record: it is usually supplied by the trust. Record it as a hospital-issued drug so interaction alerts work and out-of-hours clinicians can see it.
  • Smoking change: stopping smoking (including a switch to vaping), or starting again, changes clozapine and olanzapine levels. Contact the team before a quit attempt.
  • Anticholinergic load: procyclidine, tricyclics and antihistamines add to constipation, confusion and cognitive effects.
  • Valproate: sometimes used in schizoaffective disorder or bipolar. It must not be started in anyone under 55 unless two specialists independently document the justification (MHRA, January 2024).
  • Benzodiazepines: short-term only, if at all. Avoid long-term prescribing for sleep or anxiety in psychosis.

7G — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: study, driving, family, identity and the law
🫂
Psychosis: the student who fears for his course, the son who fears his uncle's path, and the mother who fears being shut out
A diagnosis of psychosis affects the whole of a young person's life: education, independence, driving, relationships, how they see themselves, and sometimes their liberty. Most people with a first episode make a good or partial recovery, and the GP is often the one professional who stays with them for decades. Deal with these areas early and practically. It builds the trust that later relapse prevention depends on.
🎓
Study, work and money

Encourage him to stay connected with his course. With consent, contact the university's wellbeing or disability service early. An interruption of studies, extensions or Disabled Students' Allowance can protect his place and funding. Fit notes can support a gradual return to work for people who are employed. IPS or employment support through EIP helps people get back into study or work. Signpost benefits advice if he has lost income (Universal Credit, PIP).

"Would it help if I wrote to the university? I don't need to give them details, just enough to protect your place while you're getting help."
🚗
Driving and the DVLA

Tell the patient not to drive while acutely unwell with psychosis. The licence holder must notify the DVLA of a psychotic illness. Group 1 (car): relicensing is usually possible once the person has been well and stable for at least 3 months, is adhering to treatment and has no impairing side effects, with a supportive specialist report where needed. Group 2 (bus/lorry): much stricter. Relicensing needs a longer period of stability than Group 1, with insight, adherence, the minimum effective dose and a consultant psychiatrist's report. Check the current DVLA guidance for the required period. If a patient keeps driving against advice, follow GMC confidentiality guidance on disclosure to the DVLA.

"While you're unwell, it isn't safe to drive, and the law says the DVLA needs to know. Once you've been well and settled on treatment for a few months, most people can get their car licence back."
👪
Family and carers

NICE CG178 advises offering carers an assessment of their own needs, written information about psychosis and services, and a carer-focused education and support programme. Make clear that his mother can always give you information. What you share back depends on Daniel's consent. Ask him regularly, because consent can change as trust grows. Identify her as a carer on the practice record and consider her own health, since carers' own mental health often suffers.

"You're allowed to tell me anything you're worried about, any time. What I can tell you depends on what Daniel agrees to. I'll keep asking him, and you'll have support in your own right."
🪞
Identity, stigma and hope

Labels such as "schizophrenic" or "psychotic" carry heavy stigma, and a young person may start to see themselves only through the diagnosis. Use person-first language ("a person experiencing psychosis"). Be accurate about the diagnosis: the formal diagnosis is made by specialists over time. Offer realistic hope: recovery is common, especially with early intervention. Peer support and recovery colleges help people rebuild a sense of who they are beyond the illness.

"This is something that's happening to you. It isn't who you are. Plenty of people go through an episode like this and go on to finish their studies, work and have families."
⚖️
The Mental Health Act: what a GP needs to know

Compulsory assessment is a last resort, used when someone with a mental disorder needs assessment or treatment for their own health or safety or to protect others, and will not accept it voluntarily. It is usually coordinated by an Approved Mental Health Professional (AMHP) with two doctors, one approved under section 12. Section 2: assessment, up to 28 days. Section 3: treatment. Section 136: police power to take someone to a place of safety from anywhere except a private dwelling (Policing and Crime Act 2017 amendment). Section 135: a warrant to enter premises. A GP who knows the patient is often asked to be one of the two medical recommendations. The Mental Health Act 2025 amends the 1983 Act (for example, it raises the detention threshold to risk of serious harm and shortens some treatment-order periods), but its changes are being brought in gradually. Check which provisions are in force locally.

"Being detained in hospital is rare, and it's only used when someone is at serious risk and can't accept help any other way. That isn't where we are. The help I'm arranging is voluntary, and you're part of it."
💞
Relationships, sexual health and contraception

Antipsychotic effects on prolactin and sexual function can quietly damage relationships and adherence. Ask about them directly. For women of childbearing potential, discuss contraception and plans for pregnancy before any antipsychotic or mood stabiliser, and follow the valproate Pregnancy Prevention Programme strictly. For men on valproate, give the MHRA 2024 contraception advice. Remember the vulnerability to exploitation (financial, sexual, drug-related) during acute illness, and safeguard where needed.

"Some of these medicines can affect sex drive or sexual function. People often feel awkward mentioning it, but it's important, and there's usually something we can change."

For anyone starting an antipsychotic, NICE CG178 sets baseline checks, then weight weekly for the first 6 weeks, then at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually. 7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases + medico-legal rationale

⚠ Three scenario-specific safety-nets — adapt the wording, keep the content

🔴 Emergency — suicide risk, command hallucinations, harm to others
"If the thoughts about being better off dead get stronger, if you start thinking about how you'd do it, or if the voices tell you to hurt yourself or anyone else, that's an emergency. Don't wait for an appointment. Ring the crisis line on this card, which is open day and night, or call 999, or go to A&E. Mum, the same goes for you: if you're worried he's not safe, you can make that call. You won't be overreacting."
The risk of suicide is substantially raised in psychosis, particularly early in the illness and after discharge. Risk changes quickly, so a single "low risk" judgement is not enough. The safety-net needs to go to the carer as well, and be written down, because the patient's insight and memory may be impaired. Document the risk assessment, the plan, the crisis numbers and who holds them.
🧪 Physical — organic causes and medication emergencies
"If you develop a high temperature, become confused or very drowsy, have a fit, get a bad headache or stiff neck, or your muscles go very stiff with a fever, that could be a physical problem rather than your mental health. Go to A&E or ring 999. If you're later started on medication and your neck, jaw or eyes go into spasm, get seen the same day."
Encephalitis (including autoimmune), delirium and neuroleptic malignant syndrome can all be mislabelled as "just psychosis". NMS (fever, rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, raised CK) needs the antipsychotic stopped and emergency admission. Acute dystonia is frightening and very likely to put the patient off treatment if it is not managed quickly.
🟠 Urgent — no contact from the team, or things slipping
"The team should contact you within the next few days. If you haven't heard anything by Thursday, or if you're sleeping even less, feel more watched, or can't face going back to the house, ring the surgery and ask for me. I'd rather you called too early than too late. I'll also phone you on Wednesday to check in."
Referrals get lost, and paranoid patients often don't answer calls from unknown numbers. The GP owns the patient until the specialist team has made contact. A planned check-in call and a named clinician make it more likely he will stay engaged. Record the referral method, the date and the follow-up plan.
What to document — and why it protects the patient and you
Risk assessment: suicidal ideation (passive or active, plan, intent), command hallucinations, harm to or from others, self-neglect, vulnerability, safeguarding of dependants. State the formulation and why you chose that level of urgency.
Referral: EIP contacted by phone on [date/time], name of the person spoken to, agreed timescale. Written referral sent. Plan to chase if there is no contact.
Consent and confidentiality: what Daniel agreed can be shared with his mother. Collateral history taken. Any disclosure made without consent, and its justification (serious risk).
Organic screen: examination findings (conscious level, temperature, neurology), bloods, urine drug screen, and ECG where indicated. Results reviewed and actioned.
Crisis plan: written safety plan given to the patient and carer, with crisis line numbers, 999/A&E advice, and means restriction discussed.
Driving: advised not to drive while unwell, and told of the duty to notify the DVLA. Documented at each relevant review.

⛔ Danger zones — common errors and the fix
Starting an antipsychotic yourself "to settle him"
NICE CG178: not in primary care for a first presentation unless in consultation with a consultant psychiatrist. Refer urgently and do the baseline instead.
Routine letter referral for a first episode
Phone EIP the same day. The standard is treatment within 2 weeks. Use the crisis team or duty psychiatrist if risk, distress or lack of insight need same-day assessment.
"It's just the cannabis — stop smoking and see"
Substance use and primary psychosis often co-exist. A positive drug screen does not rule out a psychotic disorder. Refer and address cannabis in parallel.
Arguing with the delusion
Explore with curiosity and empathise with the distress. Agree shared goals (sleep, safety, studies) without colluding.
Missing organic psychosis
Check temperature, conscious level, cognition and neurology. Acute onset, fluctuation, seizures, focal signs or older age point to a medical cause → same-day medical assessment.
Clozapine sore throat or constipation treated as minor
Fever or sore throat → same-day FBC. Constipation → active treatment and a low threshold for emergency assessment (MHRA, October 2017). Smoking change → check levels (MHRA 2020).
Leaving physical health to the mental health team
SMI register, annual checks, QRISK3, statins and diabetes care are primary care's job. A 15–20 year mortality gap is not someone else's problem.
Starting or continuing valproate without checks
MHRA 2024: no new starts under 55 without two specialists. Pregnancy Prevention Programme for women. Contraception advice for men.
Forgetting driving
Advise no driving while unwell, and the duty to tell the DVLA. Group 1 relicensing usually after 3 months of stability. Group 2 is much stricter.
