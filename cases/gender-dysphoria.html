📋 Clinical Stem — Gender Dysphoria
A 24-year-old trans man, anxious about being dismissed, asking for a referral to a gender clinic — and quietly disclosing low mood, self-harm and that he's started buying testosterone online
Sam Okoro, 24 (he/him), assigned female at birth, attends asking to be referred to a gender clinic. He has felt a persistent mismatch between his gender identity and his body for years, with significant distress, and has socially transitioned. He's braced to be questioned or refused. As trust builds he discloses low mood, episodes of self-harm, and — hesitantly — that he has recently started buying testosterone online because the waiting list is "years long" and he couldn't wait. He has no regular contact with mental-health services. He is registered as female and is overdue cervical screening. He fears being judged, misgendered, or told he needs to "prove" anything.
This stem tests the ability to deliver respectful, affirming, person-centred care: using the correct name and pronouns; understanding the GP's role is to refer directly to a Gender Identity Clinic on request (without gatekeeping, demanding a mental-health assessment first, or pathologising the identity); to proactively assess and support mental health and suicide/self-harm risk (driven by minority stress, not by being trans); to respond safely to self-medication with online testosterone — recognising the harm-reduction issues, considering specialist advice and (per GMC guidance) the possibility of bridging prescribing and shared care, and arranging monitoring — rather than simply refusing involvement; to address general healthcare and anatomy-based screening (a trans man with a cervix still needs cervical screening); and to signpost support during the long wait. The SCA challenge is building trust with someone braced for rejection, and balancing affirming care with safety.
Scenario A — Referral + risk + self-medication (this stem) Affirming care; direct GIC referral without gatekeeping; assess/treat mood & self-harm; harm reduction + consider bridging/shared care & monitoring for online testosterone; anatomy-based screening; signpost support.
Scenario B — Straightforward referral request Person wants a GIC referral; refer directly, support general & mental health, signpost peer support, manage the wait.
Scenario C — Shared care on established treatment Person on specialist-initiated hormones; GP monitors per shared-care protocol (bloods, BP, bone/CV health), continues general care.
Scenario D — Mental-health crisis Active suicidality/self-harm → urgent mental-health assessment & safety plan, alongside affirming support.
Scenario E — Young person / family Child/adolescent with gender incongruence → specialist young-people's pathway, holistic/multidisciplinary, family involvement, safeguarding; avoid primary-care-initiated interventions.
Key variables to adapt for Adult vs young person; referral (don't gatekeep) vs shared care vs crisis; mental-health/suicide risk; self-medication & bridging/harm reduction; anatomy-based screening & general health; hormone monitoring (bone/CV); social support & minority stress; respectful language throughout.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Minority stress, mistrust of healthcare, and the GP as ally

Many trans people approach healthcare expecting to be misgendered, questioned, gatekept or dismissed — and these experiences, alongside discrimination and isolation (minority stress), are the main drivers of the high rates of distress, self-harm and suicide in this group. The single most powerful clinical act is to be a respectful, affirming ally: use the right name and pronouns, make the referral without gatekeeping, support mental health and general health proactively, respond to self-medication with harm reduction rather than refusal, and keep the person supported through long waits. Trust, once earned, is protective.

🗣️ Respectful language

Correct name/pronouns; ask, don't assume.

"I want to make sure I get this right — can you tell me the name and pronouns you'd like me to use? I'll update your records too."
🚪 No gatekeeping

Commit to the referral up front.

"You don't need to convince me of anything — I'll refer you to the gender clinic today. My job is to support you, not to put hurdles in the way."
🛟 Harm reduction on hormones

Engage, don't refuse, around online testosterone.

"I understand why you couldn't wait years. Rather than you doing this alone and unmonitored, let's talk about keeping you safe — I can seek specialist advice about a bridging prescription and arrange some blood tests."
🧠 Mental health matters

Address distress proactively and compassionately.

"The distress and self-harm you've described are real and important — and they're often about how the world treats people, not about who you are. Let's get you some support for that too."
1E — Psychosocial context: the person behind the referral
🫂
Sam's life outside the consulting room — support, safety, work, money and the long wait
A referral letter can be written in five minutes, but the waiting list for an adult Gender Dysphoria Clinic (GDC) is measured in years. What decides how Sam gets through that wait is his social context: who supports him, whether he is safe at home and at work, whether he can afford to keep buying hormones online, and whether he has anyone to talk to. Ask about these areas with curiosity, not as a checklist, and ask permission before exploring the more personal ones. Each one leads to something you can act on.
👨‍👩‍👧
Family and relationships

Family acceptance protects mental health, and rejection is one of the strongest associations with poor outcomes in trans people. Sam describes "variable" family support. Find out who knows, who is supportive and whether anyone is hostile. A partner may be a key source of support, or the relationship may be under strain.

Never share information about Sam's gender history with family members without his consent. That applies even when a relative phones the practice asking about him.

"Who in your life knows how you feel, and how have they responded? Is there anyone you can lean on when things get hard?"
💼
Work, study and discrimination

Gender reassignment is a protected characteristic under the Equality Act 2010. It covers anyone proposing to undergo, undergoing or having undergone a process of changing their sex, and does not require any medical treatment. Workplace harassment, being outed, and problems with facilities or dress codes are common sources of stress.

A fit note is appropriate if Sam's mental health is affecting his ability to work. Occupational health and Acas guidance can help with reasonable workplace adjustments.

"How are things at work? Has anyone made it difficult for you because of your transition?"
💷
Money, housing and online sourcing

Buying hormones online costs money. Some people take on debt, ration their doses or stop suddenly when they run out. Housing insecurity is also more common in trans people, particularly young adults who have left an unsupportive family home.

Ask how Sam pays for the testosterone and what he would do if he could no longer afford it. Financial pressure can push someone towards riskier sources or, rarely, towards exploitative situations. Treat any hint of that as a safeguarding question.

"Buying testosterone online must be expensive. How are you managing that, and are things okay with where you're living?"
🌐
Community, peer support and online spaces

Peer and community groups (local LGBTQ+ centres, trans-led support groups and national helplines) reduce isolation, which is a key driver of distress. Online communities can be a real source of connection. They can also be where unregulated hormone suppliers and unverified dosing advice circulate.

Ask where Sam gets his information and help him weigh it up. Don't dismiss it.

"Where have you found support or information so far? Some online advice is good and some isn't, so I'm happy to go through anything you've read."
🧩
Co-occurring conditions

Depression, anxiety, eating disorders and autism or other neurodivergence are more common in people with gender dysphoria than in the general population. The Cass Review (2024) emphasised a holistic assessment of these alongside gender-related distress. Their presence is a reason to offer extra support. It is not a reason to delay or refuse the GDC referral.

Asking about them respectfully also helps the specialist service plan his assessment.

"Alongside the referral, I'd like to understand anything else that affects how you're feeling, like anxiety, eating or sleep, so I can support you with those too."
🛡️
Safety: abuse, harassment and hate crime

Trans people experience high rates of harassment, domestic abuse and hate crime, and often don't report it. Ask directly and privately. Domestic abuse pathways, MARAC and adult safeguarding apply exactly as for any other patient. Hate crime can be reported to the police or through third-party reporting services.

"Some people I see have been hurt or threatened because of who they are. Has anything like that happened to you, at home or anywhere else?"

Changing name and titleAn adult can change their name and title on the GP record on request. A Gender Recognition Certificate (GRC) is not needed, and neither is a deed poll for the practice record.

Whether or not a GRC is held, disclosing trans status without consent breaches confidentiality under GMC guidance and UK GDPR.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Sam expects to be refused or questioned, and he wants testosterone. Meet the first expectation in full, and be honest and supportive about the second.
1
Validate: name both expectations

Sam came for a referral, expecting a fight. He may also hope you will take over prescribing his testosterone. The first can be met completely and straight away. The second needs an honest conversation. Name both so he doesn't feel you are side-stepping the one that matters most to him right now.

"You came in for a referral and I'm doing that today, no conditions. I also get the sense you're hoping I can help with the testosterone itself. That's a fair thing to ask, so let's talk it through properly."
2
Explain: what a GP can and can't do

Gender-affirming hormones for adults are normally started by the specialist GDC and then shared with the GP under a shared-care agreement. GMC advice says a GP may issue a bridging prescription in narrow circumstances, but is not obliged to. Those circumstances are: the person is already self-medicating from an unregulated source (or is highly likely to), it is intended to reduce a risk of self-harm or suicide, gender-specialist advice has been sought, and the lowest acceptable dose is used. The RCGP (2024) says initiating hormones before specialist assessment is not part of the core GP role unless the GP has extended expertise. Explain this openly, without hiding behind it.

"Usually the gender clinic starts hormones and then I take over prescribing with their support. Because you're already taking testosterone and things have been hard, I'm going to ask a gender specialist today what the safest option is, including whether I can prescribe for you in the meantime."
3
Negotiate: what Sam leaves with today

Whatever happens with prescribing, Sam leaves with a lot: the referral sent, blood tests and a blood pressure check, harm-reduction advice, a safety plan, mental-health support, a cervical screening plan he feels comfortable with, and a named follow-up date. Monitoring does not depend on whether you prescribe.

"Today: the referral goes off, I'll check your bloods and blood pressure so we know the testosterone is safe, we'll make a plan for the difficult days, and I'll see you again in a week. You're not doing this on your own any more."
Key principle: The referral is a right, not a reward. Monitoring is harm reduction, not approval of the online source. A bridging prescription is a decision for the individual GP, made after specialist advice. Keep these three separate, so that declining one never means withholding the others.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Most of what helps Sam during the wait isn't a prescription. It is psychological support, a safety plan, connection with others, practical help with social transition, and looking after his physical health. NICE NG222 (depression) and NG225 (self-harm) apply to Sam exactly as they would to any other adult.
🛟
Collaborative safety plan
Target: written plan today; reviewed at every contact
Mechanism

A safety plan works out, in advance and with the person, their warning signs, coping strategies, people and places that help, professional contacts, and how to make the environment safer. It gives structure to a crisis when thinking is hardest. NICE NG225 favours this kind of needs-based, collaborative approach over risk-scoring.

Practical

Write it together and give Sam a copy (paper or phone). Include his own coping strategies, trusted people, the crisis team or NHS 111 (mental health option), Samaritans 116 123, and 999 or A&E for immediate danger. Discuss safer storage of medicines and prescribe limited quantities if you start an antidepressant. Ask about self-harm wound care.

NICE NG225: needs-based assessment and safety planning; not risk prediction tools
🧠
Psychological therapy
Target: referral or self-referral this week
Mechanism

Evidence-based talking therapies (CBT, behavioural activation, counselling) treat depression and anxiety whatever their cause. For Sam they also build coping with minority stress. Therapy should support him. It should never aim to change or suppress his gender identity, which would be a conversion practice.

Practical

NHS Talking Therapies accepts self-referral. With recent self-harm and moderate to severe symptoms, consider a referral to community mental-health services. Some areas have LGBTQ+-affirming counselling services. Therapy is never a precondition for the GDC referral.

NICE NG222: psychological therapy as first-line option for less severe depression; combined with SSRI for more severe
🤝
Peer and community support
Target: one named group or contact before next review
Mechanism

Minority stress theory suggests that social connection and community belonging buffer the effect of discrimination on mental health. Isolation during the wait is one of the main things that makes it harmful.

Practical

Signpost local LGBTQ+ centres, trans-led peer groups, national LGBT+ helplines and the local GDC's patient information. Most GDC websites list support organisations. Check anything you give out is current, and let Sam choose.

Social support consistently associated with lower depression and suicidality in trans cohorts (observational evidence)
🪪
Practical support with social transition
Target: records updated today if Sam wants
Mechanism

Being called by the wrong name or title, or being sent letters that out someone, adds to distress every time it happens. Getting the practice's own systems right is a quick, concrete way to reduce harm.

Practical

Update name, title and pronouns, and add an alert so reception use them. Discuss whether and when to change the gender marker, and explain the effects on NHS number, screening and waiting lists first. Record the organs present with consent. Don't make assumptions about what Sam wants.

Equality Act 2010: services must not discriminate on the basis of gender reassignment
🫁
Smoking, alcohol, weight and exercise
Target: smoke-free; alcohol within guidance
Mechanism

Smoking adds to the higher haematocrit and cardiovascular risk that testosterone can cause, and to the VTE risk of oestrogen. Alcohol and drug use are often ways of coping with distress and make self-harm risk worse. Exercise helps mood but can be difficult for trans people who find changing rooms unsafe.

Practical

Offer stop-smoking support (NICE NG209). Take an alcohol history using AUDIT-C. Suggest ways to exercise that feel safe for Sam, such as home workouts, running, or inclusive sports groups.

Smoking cessation is the most important modifiable CV and thrombotic risk factor on hormone therapy
👕
Chest binding: safer practice
Target: no binding with bandages or tape; regular breaks
Mechanism

Many trans men bind their chest to reduce dysphoria. Binding too tightly or for too long can cause pain, skin damage, shortness of breath and rib discomfort. Improvised binders (elastic bandages, duct tape) carry the highest risk.

Practical

Ask without judgement. Advise a purpose-made binder of the right size, taking it off to sleep and exercise, taking breaks, and never using bandages or tape. Assess and treat any skin or musculoskeletal problems. Binding helps Sam's wellbeing, so support safe binding rather than discouraging it.

Harm reduction supports wellbeing while reducing physical complications (observational evidence)
7D — Prescribing guide: who initiates what (NHS England service specifications · GMC · RCGP · BMA · UK legislation)
In gender care, what matters most for the GP is who is responsible for prescribing. Specialist services initiate gender-affirming hormones. The GP prescribes and monitors under shared care, handles any bridging decision within strict limits, and treats co-occurring conditions exactly as for anyone else. For under-18s, the legal and commissioning rules have changed a great deal since the Cass Review (April 2024). The GP's role there is referral, support and safeguarding, never prescribing.

If you are considering a bridging prescription: the checklist
  • GDC referral already sent; this is an adult (18+)
  • Already self-medicating from an unregulated source (or highly likely to), and bridging is intended to reduce risk of self-harm or suicide (GMC conditions)
  • Advice sought from a gender specialist or experienced colleague, with the reply or the attempt documented
  • Lowest acceptable dose, in a regulated preparation, with the product and dose agreed on specialist advice (dose per specialist / BNF)
  • Fertility preservation discussed before prescribing, and documented
  • Baseline bloods and BP done, and a monitoring schedule agreed
  • Check your ICB or local policy (many areas have one) and that you feel competent. If not, say so honestly and keep monitoring
If you decide not to bridge: what you still do
  • Explain your reasoning honestly, and don't frame it as a judgement of Sam
  • Offer the same monitoring bloods, BP and review schedule you would offer if you were prescribing
  • Harm-reduction advice: sterile single-use equipment, needle exchange, safe sharps disposal, no sharing, rotate injection sites, never double a missed dose, bring the product in so it can be checked
  • Offer BBV testing and hepatitis B vaccination if equipment may have been shared
  • Pass on the specialist advice when it arrives, and review the decision if Sam's risk changes
  • Document everything: the product, the dose, the advice given, the bloods and the plan

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, family, identity and the wait
🫂
Gender dysphoria and the wait: identity, safety, relationships and being believed
For many trans people, the hardest part of gender dysphoria is not the dysphoria itself. It is the years spent waiting for care, and what happens in the meantime: at work, at home, in public and in healthcare settings. The GP is often the only clinician who sees the whole of that picture.
💼
Work and education

The Equality Act 2010 protects against discrimination and harassment because of gender reassignment, with no requirement for medical treatment. If Sam's mood affects his work, a fit note is appropriate. Use neutral wording that does not disclose his gender history unless he agrees ("low mood and anxiety" rather than a gender-related diagnosis). Suggest adjustments such as time off for GDC appointments.

"Would it help if the fit note just says 'anxiety and low mood'? You decide what your employer knows."
🚗
Driving and DVLA

Gender dysphoria and gender-affirming hormones do not need to be reported to the DVLA. The mental-health rules apply as usual. DVLA guidance (Assessing fitness to drive) says that severe depression or anxiety with significant memory or concentration problems, agitation, behavioural disturbance or suicidal thoughts means the person must not drive and must tell the DVLA. Discuss this sensitively. Changing the name and gender on a licence is an administrative process, not a medical one.

"Nothing about being trans or taking hormones affects your licence. If your mood gets bad enough that you can't concentrate, or you're having thoughts of ending your life, you shouldn't drive until that improves."
👨‍👩‍👧
Family and relationships

Relatives may be supportive, confused or hostile, and a partner may need support too. Offer information, but never share Sam's gender history or medical details with them without his consent. If a relative contacts the practice, confirm nothing without Sam's consent.

"If it would help, you could bring someone you trust to an appointment. That's entirely your choice, and I won't share anything without your permission."
🪞
Identity, body image and intimate examinations

Cervical screening, chest examination and some blood tests can cause significant dysphoria. Use Sam's own words for his body, explain why each examination is needed, offer choices (a smaller speculum, a clinician he chooses, a chaperone, stopping at any point) and self-sampling where it is available. Testosterone can cause vaginal atrophy. A short course of topical vaginal oestrogen before screening may help, but agree this with Sam and his GDC or specialist.

"I know this test might be really difficult for you. We can go at your pace, use whatever words you prefer, and stop whenever you want."
⏳
The wait: hope and anger

The Levy review found that more than 40,000 adults were waiting for a first appointment (March 2025), and some clinics estimated waits of over a decade for new referrals. NHS England accepted the recommendations: a single national waiting list was introduced in 2026, self-referral is no longer accepted, and from October 2026 GP referrals go through one national referral service. Anger about this is reasonable. Acknowledge it honestly, don't offer false timescales, and plan the wait actively rather than leaving Sam to wait passively.

"I'd be angry too. I can't make the list shorter, but I can make sure you're looked after properly while you're on it."
🏥
Trust in healthcare

Many trans people have had healthcare experiences where every symptom was put down to their gender identity or hormones (sometimes called "trans broken arm syndrome"), or where they were refused care. Investigate chest pain, headache or abdominal pain exactly as you would for anyone else, and take account of anatomy and hormones where they are relevant.

"Whatever you come in with, I'll look into it properly, the same as I would for anyone."

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases + medico-legal rationale

⚠ Three scenario-specific safety-nets: say these, then write them down

🔴 Mental-health crisis
"If you feel you might act on thoughts of ending your life, or you've hurt yourself badly, call 999 or go to A&E. If you're struggling but not in immediate danger, call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option, or ring Samaritans on 116 123 at any time. And please tell us. I'd much rather hear from you early."
Recent self-harm, depression and being in a population with high rates of suicidal thoughts put Sam at risk. A written safety plan with crisis numbers, limited medicine quantities and a fixed 1-week review is the standard of care (NICE NG225, NG222). Document the plan and that it was given.
🩸 Hormone-related emergencies
"If you get a painful swollen calf, chest pain or sudden breathlessness, that needs checking the same day. Call 999 if it's severe. If an injection site becomes hot, red and swollen, or you get a fever, contact us that day. And if you get headaches, flushing or feel unusually tired, let me know, because it can mean your blood has become too thick."
VTE (a particular risk with oestrogen, and with testosterone when haematocrit is high), polycythaemia and injection-site infection are the main acute harms. Unregulated products and doses make these more likely. Document the harm-reduction advice.
🟠 Safeguarding and harm from others
"If anyone is threatening or hurting you, at home or anywhere else, or pressuring you in exchange for money or hormones, please tell me. There's help available and you won't be judged."
Domestic abuse, hate crime and exploitation are under-reported. Adult safeguarding procedures apply as for any patient. For under-18s, the NHS England guidance to primary care (2026) covers unregulated hormone supply and consideration of safeguarding referral.

Referral: "Referred to adult GDC on patient request; no preconditions; copy offered." This shows the referral was made without gatekeeping.

Hormones: product, strength, dose, route, source; specialist advice sought (date, who); bridging decision and reasons; monitoring plan. This is what GMC advice expects.

🚫 Danger zones
✗
Requiring a mental-health assessment before referral→ Adult GDCs accept referral on request. Mental-health needs are treated alongside the referral, not as a gate before it.
✗
Prescribing testosterone on the spot without specialist advice or a fertility discussion→ GMC conditions require specialist advice and lowest acceptable dose. The RCGP (2024) says initiation is outside the core GP role. Fertility must be discussed first.
✗
Refusing to monitor because the hormones are "not prescribed"→ Monitoring is harm reduction. Take bloods and BP, and document, whatever you decide about prescribing.
✗
Using a risk score to decide Sam is "low risk"→ NICE NG225: don't use risk tools to predict suicide or decide on care. Do a needs-based assessment and write a safety plan.
✗
Assuming screening will happen automatically→ Invitations follow the gender marker. Arrange by anatomy: cervical opt-in, and breast or AAA requests where relevant.
✗
Referring a young person directly to the CYP Gender Service, or supporting unregulated hormone supply→ Since September 2024 referrals come via NHS paediatrics or CAMHS. No GP prescribing or shared care with unregulated providers for under-18s (NHS England guidance).
✗
Disclosing gender history without consent→ This breaches confidentiality, and it may be a criminal offence under GRA 2004 s.22 if a GRC has been applied for or is held. Check consent for letters, fit notes and conversations with relatives.

🤝 1 — The GP's role

Affirming care: correct name/pronouns; non-judgmental. Refer directly to a Gender Identity Clinic on request — no gatekeeping, no mandatory mental-health pre-assessment. Support mental health (high suicide/self-harm risk from minority stress) and general health through long waits. Don't pathologise identity or use conversion practices.

🛟 2 — Safety & general health

Self-medication: harm reduction + monitoring always (haematocrit/BP/LFTs/lipids on testosterone); seek specialist advice; bridging only within GMC conditions and GP competence (RCGP 2024); shared care once the GDC is involved. Anatomy-based screening: trans man with a cervix needs cervical screening; tailor screening to anatomy not recorded gender. Young people: refer via NHS paediatrics/CAMHS to the CYP Gender Service; puberty blockers only within the PATHWAYS trial; no GP prescribing or shared care with unregulated providers; family support/safeguarding. Signpost peer support; provide continuity.
