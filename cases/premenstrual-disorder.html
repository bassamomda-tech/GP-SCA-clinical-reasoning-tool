It's the timing that defines itPremenstrual disorders are defined by the cyclical, luteal-phase pattern: symptoms appear in the 1–2 weeks before menstruation and resolve within a few days of bleeding starting, with a symptom-free week. The character of symptoms matters less than the timing
PMS vs PMDDPremenstrual syndrome (PMS) = distressing physical/psychological symptoms in the luteal phase. PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is the severe form with prominent mood symptoms (marked irritability, depression, anxiety, lability) causing significant impairment — a recognised psychiatric diagnosis
Diary = the diagnosisDiagnosis rests on a prospective symptom diary over at least 2 menstrual cycles (e.g. the DRSP) — confirming the luteal-phase pattern with a symptom-free follicular phase. Retrospective recall is unreliable and over-diagnoses
Rule out the mimicThe key differential is an underlying mood disorder (depression, anxiety, bipolar) with PREMENSTRUAL EXACERBATION — here symptoms are present all month and worsen premenstrually. The diary distinguishes a symptom-free week (PMDD) from year-round symptoms (mood disorder)
Ask about suicidePMDD carries a significantly increased risk of suicidal thoughts and self-harm, concentrated in the luteal phase. A risk assessment is mandatory — these symptoms can be severe and dangerous even though they remit each month
SSRI works fast hereSSRIs are highly effective for PMDD and can be taken continuously OR only in the luteal phase (they work within days for premenstrual symptoms, unlike in depression). First-line pharmacological options alongside a combined pill
Ovulation suppressionSuppressing ovulation helps: a combined oral contraceptive (continuous/extended, drospirenone-containing often preferred) is a first-line hormonal option; severe refractory cases may need GnRH analogues (with add-back HRT) under specialist care
Stepped & specialistStepwise: lifestyle/CBT → SSRI and/or COCP → higher-dose/continuous → GnRH analogues → (rarely) surgery. Severe, refractory or diagnostically uncertain cases warrant gynaecology / a specialist premenstrual-disorder service
📋 Clinical Stem — Premenstrual Disorder (PMDD)
A 34-year-old woman who feels "like a different person — almost unhinged" for a week or two before every period, with rage, despair and fleeting thoughts she'd be better off gone, then is "completely fine again"
Leila Hassan, 34, describes a pattern she's lived with for years but never named: for about 10–14 days before each period she becomes intensely irritable, tearful and anxious, snaps at her partner and children, feels hopeless, and has had fleeting thoughts that her family "would be better off without me" — then within a day or two of her period starting it lifts and she feels "completely normal, like myself again." She functions well the rest of the month. She is frightened by the intensity and the dark thoughts, ashamed of how she behaves toward her family, and wonders if she is "going mad" or bipolar. She has no symptoms in the week after her period. She is not on hormonal contraception and is otherwise well.
This stem tests the ability to: recognise the cyclical, luteal-phase pattern with a symptom-free follicular phase as premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) rather than a primary mood disorder; distinguish it from an underlying depression/anxiety/bipolar disorder with premenstrual exacerbation (symptoms all month, worse premenstrually) — using a prospective 2-cycle symptom diary to confirm; perform a mandatory risk assessment for the luteal-phase suicidal thoughts and validate the experience without pathologising her as a "bad mother"; explain the diagnosis and the stepped management (lifestyle/CBT, SSRI continuous or luteal, combined pill/ovulation suppression, GnRH analogues for refractory cases under specialist care); and know when to involve gynaecology/specialist services. The SCA challenge is taking the dark thoughts seriously and confirming the diagnosis with a diary, while relieving her shame and fear of being "mad".
Scenario A — PMDD (this stem) Severe luteal mood symptoms with a symptom-free week and impairment ± luteal suicidal thoughts. Confirm with a 2-cycle diary; risk assess; SSRI (continuous/luteal) ± COCP; lifestyle/CBT; review.
Scenario B — Mood disorder with premenstrual exacerbation (the mimic) Depression/anxiety present all month, worse premenstrually; no symptom-free week on the diary. Treat the underlying disorder; SSRI continuously; mental-health input.
Scenario C — PMS (non-severe) Luteal physical/psychological symptoms that are distressing but not severely impairing. Lifestyle, simple measures, consider COCP/SSRI if needed.
Scenario D — Bipolar disorder History of elevated/manic episodes; premenstrual worsening can occur. Caution with antidepressants; mental-health/specialist input; do not miss bipolarity.
Scenario E — Severe / refractory PMDD Failed first-line treatments, profound impairment, high risk. Specialist premenstrual-disorder service; GnRH analogues with add-back HRT; rarely surgical options.
Key variables to adapt for The luteal pattern + symptom-free week (PMDD) vs all-month symptoms (mood disorder); severity/impairment (PMS vs PMDD); suicide/self-harm risk in the luteal phase; the prospective diary; bipolar history (antidepressant caution); contraceptive needs/desires; previous treatments; perimenopause overlap; the shame and relationship impact.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Shame, dismissal, and a real illness hidden as "just hormones"

Premenstrual disorders sit at a painful intersection of stigma: women are told it's "just PMS" and dismissed, while privately they experience symptoms severe enough to threaten relationships and, at the extreme, life. Many carry deep shame about how they behave during the luteal phase and blame themselves. The transformative clinical act is to take it seriously — to name PMDD as a genuine, hormone-linked condition with effective treatments, to separate the woman's worth from her premenstrual symptoms, and to assess the very real luteal-phase risk that the cyclical remission can hide.

🙎 "Am I a bad mother?"

Attribute the behaviour to a treatable condition; separate it from her character.

"The way you are with your family in those two weeks isn't who you are — it's a symptom of a hormone-driven condition. That you feel so awful about it tells me how much you love them. We can treat this."
🧠 "Am I going mad / bipolar?"

Reassure and differentiate clearly from bipolar disorder.

"You're not going mad. Because it comes and goes with your cycle and you're completely well in between, this is premenstrual dysphoric disorder rather than bipolar — and that distinction matters, because it's very treatable."
🆘 Taking the dark thoughts seriously

The remitting nature doesn't reduce the risk; assess and safety-plan.

"I'm really glad you told me about those thoughts — they're part of how severe this can get, and I take them seriously even though they lift after your period. Let's make a plan for the worst days and get you treatment so they ease."
🚫 Undoing dismissal

Counter the "just PMS" message she may have internalised.

"This isn't something to just put up with or be embarrassed about. It's a recognised condition, and there are treatments that genuinely work — you deserve to feel like yourself all month."

🚩 The symptom-free week is the whole game

The defining discrimination is whether there is a genuine symptom-free follicular phase. If yes (Leila), the diagnosis is a premenstrual disorder — PMDD given the severity, mood predominance, impairment and luteal suicidal thoughts. If symptoms persist throughout the month, it is a primary mood disorder with premenstrual exacerbation, treated as such. The prospective diary makes the call. For Leila: likely PMDD, with a mandatory ongoing risk plan and bipolar screened out.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Leila hopes for a quick fix and fears she'll be dismissed as "hormonal" or labelled bipolar. Name both, then agree a plan
1
Validate: name what she came for

Leila wants an explanation, to hear that she isn't "going mad", and something that works before her next bad fortnight. She may also expect a blood test for "hormone levels", or fear being told to "just put up with it". Say these expectations out loud before you explain anything. Otherwise asking for a diary can sound like being fobbed off.

"I suspect you came wanting two things: an answer to what's happening, and something that helps before your next bad couple of weeks. I think we can do both today, and I want you to leave with a plan, not just a leaflet."
2
Explain: timing is the diagnosis, and there's no blood test

Hormone levels are normal in PMS and PMDD. The problem is the brain's sensitivity to the normal rise and fall of hormones, so a blood test cannot confirm it. What confirms it is the pattern: symptoms before the period, gone by the end of it, and a week that is genuinely clear. A daily diary kept over 2 cycles shows this and also rules out a depression that runs all month (RCOG GTG 48).

"Your hormone levels are almost certainly normal. What's different is how strongly your brain reacts to the normal monthly changes. That's why a blood test won't show it, but a daily diary will. It also gives us a baseline, so we can see whether treatment is really working."
3
Negotiate: the diary comes first, but safety does not wait

The diary does not have to delay help. Keep her safe and start the things that cannot harm her today: direct questions about the suicidal thoughts, a written luteal-phase safety plan with crisis numbers, lifestyle changes, a CBT or self-help referral, and information. RCOG GTG 48's standard is diary first (2 cycles), then drug treatment. Starting an SSRI before the diary is complete is a departure from that standard. Consider it only when the severity of her symptoms, or her suicidal thoughts, mean that waiting 2 cycles is not reasonable. If you do, first screen for bipolar disorder, remember that a year-round depression has not yet been excluded, keep the diary going, review within 1 week (NICE NG222), and record the reason. Seek same-day mental-health advice instead if there is any intent or plan.

"I'd like you to start the diary tonight. Alongside it, we'll make a plan for your worst days, and I'll refer you for CBT, which really does help with this. Usually we'd wait for two months of diary before starting a medicine. Because the dark thoughts are so hard on those days, we can talk about starting one sooner. If we do, I'll see you again within a week."
Key principle: RCOG GTG 48 asks for a prospective diary over 2 cycles before treatment starts, and that is the standard to explain. The diary exists to get the diagnosis right. It is never a reason to leave risk unmanaged or to send her away with nothing. An SSRI before the diary is complete is an exception for severe symptoms or suicidal thoughts, not a routine choice. Whatever you decide about starting a drug early, the luteal suicidal thoughts are dealt with today, not after the diary: ask directly, agree a written safety plan, give crisis numbers (999 or A&E if in immediate danger; NHS 111 mental-health option; Samaritans 116 123), and book follow-up.

≤10 mg/day

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
RCOG GTG 48 treats lifestyle measures and CBT as first-line options, not a warm-up before the "real" treatment. They are safe to start on the day she is seen, can run alongside the diary, and still help after medication is stopped. Each card names what it changes and what to aim for.
🧠
Cognitive Behavioural Therapy
Target: NHS Talking Therapies self-referral this week
Mechanism

CBT does not change the hormonal trigger. It changes how the luteal thoughts ("I'm a terrible mother", "they'd be better off without me") are read and acted on. It builds coping plans for the predictable bad days and reduces the shame spiral that follows an outburst.

Practical

Give her the local NHS Talking Therapies self-referral route. Group, guided self-help and individual formats all exist. Tell the service about the premenstrual pattern and the luteal thoughts so they can adapt the work to it. It combines well with an SSRI or COC.

RCOG GTG 48: CBT should be considered routinely as a treatment option for severe PMS
📔
Symptom Diary + Psychoeducation
Target: daily DRSP (or app) for 2 cycles
Mechanism

Seeing the pattern on paper turns an unpredictable "different person" into a predictable, time-limited phase. That helps her plan, eases self-blame, and helps her partner understand. It is also the outcome measure for every later step.

Practical

Paper DRSP or a cycle-tracking app that records symptom severity each day. Rate every day, including the good ones, because the good days prove the symptom-free week. Mark the days bleeding starts. Bring it to each review.

RCOG GTG 48: prospective recording over 2 cycles before treatment
🏃‍♀️
Regular Aerobic Exercise
Target: most days, not just during bad weeks
Mechanism

Aerobic exercise improves mood, sleep and stress reactivity. Its benefit on premenstrual symptoms builds over the whole cycle, not on the bad day itself.

Practical

Choose something that fits around two children: brisk walks on the school run, a home workout, or a regular class booked for the week before her period so it happens anyway. Aim for consistency, not intensity.

RCOG GTG 48 lists exercise among first-line measures; evidence quality is modest but harm is minimal
😴
Sleep, Caffeine, Alcohol
Target: protect sleep in the luteal phase; alcohol-free on the worst days
Mechanism

Luteal insomnia amplifies irritability and hopelessness. Alcohol lifts mood briefly, then worsens it and removes inhibitions. That matters because impulsive self-harm is more likely under the influence.

Practical

Keep regular sleep and wake times. Avoid caffeine after midday in the luteal week. Keep no alcohol at home on the diary's worst days, and put this in the safety plan. Smaller, regular meals can help if cravings and energy dips drive irritability.

Low-risk measures endorsed by RCOG GTG 48 as part of first-line care
🗓️
Luteal-Phase Planning
Target: diary-guided plan for demanding events
Mechanism

Predictable symptoms can be planned around. Cutting avoidable stress in the known window lowers the chance of the conflict that leaves her ashamed.

Practical

Schedule difficult conversations, deadlines and big family events in the follicular phase where she can. Agree a "time-out" signal with her partner for flashpoints. Share the plan with a trusted person.

Clinical teaching: fewer luteal flashpoints means less guilt after them
🌿
Supplements: Honest Framing
Target: informed choice, safe doses
Mechanism

Agnus castus (chasteberry) has some trial support, but safety data are limited. Calcium and vitamin D have some supportive evidence. Vitamin B6 is listed by RCOG, but high doses cause neuropathy.

Practical

If she prefers a non-prescription option, suggest a product with the UK Traditional Herbal Registration (THR) mark. Keep B6 within NHS limits. Avoid St John's wort if an SSRI or COC is likely. Do not let a supplement delay effective treatment for PMDD with luteal suicidal thoughts.

RCOG GTG 48: limited evidence; B6 neuropathy warning at high doses
7D — Prescribing guide: RCOG Green-top 48 treatment ladder
RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 48, Management of Premenstrual Syndrome (2016; published in BJOG 2017), is still the current UK guideline, and its treatment ladder applies in primary care. First line is lifestyle and CBT, a drospirenone-containing COC and/or a low-dose SSRI (continuous or luteal phase). Second line is a higher-dose SSRI, or transdermal oestradiol with progestogen protection. Third line is a GnRH analogue with add-back HRT, and surgery is the last resort. Choosing the SSRI regimen: continuous or luteal?
  • Luteal phase only (day 15 to menses): suits regular cycles, purely premenstrual symptoms, and women who want less drug exposure or fewer sexual side effects.
  • Continuous: suits irregular cycles, co-existing anxiety or depression, symptoms starting early in the luteal phase, or poor response to luteal dosing.
  • SSRIs act within days on premenstrual symptoms, unlike in depression, which is why the luteal regimen works. RCOG GTG 48 supports either regimen.
  • Stopping: luteal dosing stops each cycle at menses. After continuous use, taper the dose rather than stopping abruptly.
  • Dose per BNF. Citalopram and escitalopram have maximum doses because of QT prolongation (MHRA Drug Safety Update, Dec 2011).
Choosing a COC: drospirenone, and how to take it
  • RCOG GTG 48: drospirenone-containing COCs may be effective and should be considered first-line pharmaceutical options. Emerging data favour continuous rather than cyclical use, because the hormone-free interval can bring symptoms back.
  • Continuous or tailored regimens are outside the product licence but are supported by the FSRH guideline Combined Hormonal Contraception (2019, amended 2023; now published by CoSRH). Explain this and record it.
  • Before prescribing: UKMEC 2025 check (migraine with aura, VTE history, smoking at 35 or over, BMI, BP), BP, BMI, and a drug interaction check.
  • Discuss VTE honestly: the absolute risk is small, but it is higher than with levonorgestrel COCs (MHRA 2014).
  • If she has had a progestogen-driven mood reaction in the past, review after 3 months. Some women do worse on any hormonal method.

7F — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: family, work, driving & identity
🫂
PMDD: two weeks a month as "someone else", and the family, job and self-image that absorb the damage

The illness is cyclical, but the consequences add up. Relationships are strained by repeated luteal conflict, performance at work dips in a predictable pattern, and a woman's sense of herself as a good mother erodes. Naming these impacts, and planning for them, is part of treatment.

👨‍👩‍👧
Family, Partner and Children

Luteal irritability lands on the people closest to her. Partners may start to see her as the problem, and children notice the pattern. After each episode comes guilt, which feeds the hopelessness in the next cycle.

With her consent, involving her partner turns the pattern into something they face together, with a shared plan for the difficult days. Children can be given simple, age-appropriate explanations. If there is any concern about the children's welfare or safety at home, follow local safeguarding procedures.

"Would it help if your partner came to the next appointment? When the people at home understand this is a treatable condition, they can help you through the difficult days instead of it turning into an argument."
🏠
Safety at Home: Domestic Abuse

"Irritability" and "relationship conflict" can sometimes be the visible part of an abusive relationship. PMDD does not cause abuse, and abuse is never the victim's fault, even when she blames her own behaviour.

Ask safely, when she is alone: "Do you ever feel frightened of your partner?" If she says yes, follow local domestic abuse pathways and think about any children in the household.

"I ask everyone this: do you feel safe at home? Has anyone ever hurt you or made you feel afraid?"
💼
Work and Study

Concentration, energy and patience dip in a predictable window. Some women take repeated short absences that are recorded as unrelated minor illnesses.

A fit note is rarely needed. Reasonable adjustments help more: flexible deadlines, the option to work from home on the worst days, and moving demanding tasks to the follicular phase. Severe, long-term PMDD may meet the Equality Act definition of disability, so explain this to her rather than deciding it for her.

"Would it help to have a letter that explains this is a medical condition with a predictable pattern? Many employers can make small changes around those two weeks."
🚗
Driving and the DVLA

DVLA guidance does not name PMS or PMDD. Check the current DVLA guidance on depression and anxiety: it says that someone with severe symptoms, including significant memory or concentration problems, agitation, behavioural disturbance or suicidal thoughts, must not drive and must tell the DVLA. People with milder symptoms without those features do not need to tell the DVLA.

Discuss this with Leila and record the discussion. Tell her not to drive whenever she has suicidal thoughts, agitation or poor concentration, and that if her symptoms meet the DVLA description she must tell the DVLA. If you are unsure whether she meets it, check the DVLA guidance or ask the DVLA medical advisers rather than deciding for her. Also warn that some SSRIs cause drowsiness at first.

"On any day when you're having thoughts about not being here, or feel agitated or can't concentrate, please don't drive. Get a lift or use other transport. The DVLA has rules for when mood symptoms mean you need to tell them, so let's look at those together."
🪞
Identity and Shame

Many women describe two selves: the capable woman of the follicular weeks and the "monster" of the luteal ones. Years of being told it's "just PMS" can leave deep shame and a fear of being "mad".

A diagnosis with a name, a mechanism and treatment is itself a psychological intervention. Peer support from patient organisations (e.g. the National Association for Premenstrual Syndrome, or IAPMD) reduces isolation.

"The person you are in your good weeks is you. What happens in the bad weeks is the condition acting on your brain. Your guilt shows how much you care. It doesn't show that you're a bad mother."
🤰
Fertility, Pregnancy and Future Plans

Treatment choices depend on family plans. A COC and ovulation suppression cannot be used while trying to conceive. SSRIs need a discussion before conception.

A history of PMDD is worth flagging for the perinatal period. Ask about mood at booking and after the birth, because women with hormone-sensitive mood symptoms may be more vulnerable then.

"Are you thinking about more children in the next year or two? That changes which treatment makes most sense, and it's something we'd plan around together."

7I — Safety-netting: exact phrases, RAG marking and danger zones

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases: use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency: the thoughts become a plan
"If the thoughts about not being here ever become stronger than passing thoughts — if you start planning, or feel you might act on them — that's an emergency, even if you know it will lift after your period. Ring 999 or go to A&E if you're in immediate danger. Otherwise call NHS 111 and choose the mental-health option, or call the Samaritans on 116 123 any time, day or night. And please tell us the same day."
Luteal suicidality can escalate within days, then lift with the period, and that pattern can hide how dangerous it is. The crisis route has to work at 2am on day 26 of her cycle, not only during surgery hours. NICE NG225 asks for a safety plan built with the person, not a risk score.
💊 After starting an SSRI
"In the first couple of weeks some people feel more restless or anxious, or have darker thoughts before things get better. If that happens, don't wait for your appointment. Call us the same day. And if you ever feel unusually high, wired, or you stop needing sleep, stop the tablets and ring us."
Early activation and suicidal thoughts are recognised risks in the first weeks, especially under 25 (MHRA). A switch to mania would suggest undiagnosed bipolar disorder (NICE CG185).
🟠 On the combined pill
"Very rarely this pill causes a blood clot. If you get a painful swollen calf, chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or a sudden severe headache or problems with your vision or speech, get urgent help. Call 999 if it's chest pain or stroke symptoms. And if your headaches change or you start getting warning signs before them, like zigzag lights, tell us, because that changes whether this pill is safe for you."
Drospirenone COCs carry a higher VTE risk than levonorgestrel COCs (MHRA 2014). New migraine with aura makes a combined pill UKMEC 4, so patients need to know to report it.
Why safety-netting matters beyond clinical care
Record the risk conversation as a formulation: what the thoughts are, their frequency and timing in the cycle, plans or intent, protective factors, and the safety plan agreed. Do not record a risk category (NICE NG225).
Record the bipolar screen before any SSRI: "no history of elevated mood or reduced need for sleep; no family history of bipolar disorder".
Off-label use: "SSRI for PMDD, off-label, discussed and agreed" and "continuous COC regimen, off-label, discussed".
COC: record the UKMEC 2025 check, BP, BMI, smoking status, migraine history, and that the VTE risk was discussed.
Crisis numbers given (NHS 111 mental-health option, Samaritans 116 123, 999) and the review date booked.

🚫 8 Danger zones
✗
Not asking about suicide because "it goes away"→ Ask directly every time. The risk is real in the luteal phase. Safety plan, not a risk score (NICE NG225).
✗
Diagnosing on recall alone→ Prospective diary over 2 cycles (RCOG GTG 48). Recall over-reports.
✗
Missing depression that runs all month→ No symptom-free week means a primary mood disorder. Treat that (NICE NG222).
✗
SSRI without a bipolar screen→ Ask about highs and family history. If present, get psychiatric advice first (NICE CG185).
✗
Ordering "hormone levels" to diagnose→ Hormone levels are normal in PMS. Bloods only to exclude mimics.
✗
COC without a UKMEC or VTE check→ Check UKMEC 2025 (migraine with aura, VTE, smoking at 35+). Discuss the drospirenone VTE trade-off.
✗
Prescribing progesterone for PMS→ Not effective and can provoke symptoms (RCOG GTG 48).
✗
Oestradiol patches treated as contraception→ Not contraceptive. Use the LNG-IUS or a barrier method, and always protect the endometrium.

Premenstrual Disorder (PMDD) — Clinical Decision Framework
RCOG/NICE · Luteal pattern + diary · SSRI/COCP · Risk
▼expand
🔍 1 — Diagnose

Pattern: luteal-phase symptoms resolving with menses + a symptom-free week. PMDD = severe mood-predominant form with impairment. Confirm with a prospective ≥2-cycle diary (DRSP). Differentiate from a mood disorder with premenstrual exacerbation (symptoms all month) and bipolar. Always assess suicide/self-harm risk (luteal phase).

💊 2 — Treat (stepped)

1. Lifestyle + CBT. 2. SSRI — continuous OR luteal-phase (works within days); and/or combined pill (continuous/drospirenone-containing) to suppress ovulation. 3. Escalate (specialist): GnRH analogues + add-back HRT; rarely surgery. Safety plan throughout; review early; specialist/gynae for severe/refractory.
