📋 Clinical Stem — UTI in Women
A 58-year-old post-menopausal woman with a fourth episode of cystitis this year, now frustrated and asking for "a standby antibiotic" — with a passing mention of pink urine
Janet Okafor, 58, attends with two days of burning on passing urine, going more often, and lower tummy discomfort. She says this is "the fourth time this year" and that antibiotics from the pharmacy or out-of-hours keep sorting it but it keeps coming back. She is post-menopausal (last period ~6 years ago), not sexually active currently, and otherwise well — no fever, no loin pain, no vomiting. When asked, she mentions the urine looked "a bit pink" on one occasion last month even after the antibiotics had finished. She would like "a few antibiotics to keep at home so I can start them whenever it flares."
This stem tests the ability to: confirm an uncomplicated lower UTI from focused symptoms (and know when a dipstick adds nothing); recognise this as recurrent UTI and shift from repeated reactive antibiotics to a structured plan (self-care, behavioural measures, vaginal oestrogen for a post-menopausal woman, and prophylaxis only after review); and — critically — not miss the red flag of visible haematuria persisting after UTI treatment in a woman over 45, which mandates a NICE NG12 2-week-wait bladder cancer referral. The SCA challenge is handling the request for standby/back-pocket antibiotics thoughtfully (antimicrobial stewardship) while still leaving Janet with a plan that addresses why it keeps recurring.
Scenario A — Uncomplicated lower UTI Young, non-pregnant woman, classic dysuria/frequency, no vaginal symptoms, no systemic features. Treat empirically (nitrofurantoin 3 days); self-care; safety-net for systemic features; no culture needed unless fails.
Scenario B — Recurrent UTI (this stem) ≥3 in a year. Confirm episodes (cultures where possible), review behavioural/self-care measures, vaginal oestrogen if post-menopausal, consider single-dose post-coital or continuous prophylaxis; exclude haematuria red flag and incomplete emptying.
Scenario C — Pyelonephritis Loin pain, fever/rigors, nausea/vomiting, systemically unwell. Send culture; broader/longer antibiotic (e.g. cefalexin 7–10 days); admit if septic, vomiting, pregnant or not improving.
Scenario D — UTI in pregnancy Always treat (symptomatic) and screen/treat asymptomatic bacteriuria; always culture; avoid trimethoprim in 1st trimester and nitrofurantoin at term; low threshold for review (pyelonephritis and pre-term birth risk).
Scenario E — "It's not a UTI" Dysuria with vaginal discharge/itch → thrush, BV or STI; suprapubic pain + discharge + deep dyspareunia → PID; sterile pyuria → consider chlamydia, TB, or non-infective cause. Dysuria is not always a UTI.
Key variables to adapt for Age (no dipstick >65; asymptomatic bacteriuria common); menopausal status (vaginal oestrogen for recurrence); pregnancy (always treat/culture, drug choice); systemic features (upper vs lower tract); haematuria (visible 45+ = 2WW; persistent non-visible 60+ with dysuria/raised WCC = 2WW); catheter (don't dipstick; treat only symptomatic); recurrence pattern; sexual history (STI mimic; post-coital trigger); comorbidity (diabetes, immunosuppression, stones — complicated UTI).

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Recurrent UTI — the grind of "here we go again"

Recurrent UTIs are genuinely wearing: the pain, the disrupted sleep and work, the repeated trips for antibiotics, and the sense that nothing is fixing the underlying problem. That fatigue is what drives the standby-antibiotic request. A consultation that simply says "no" to that request — without offering a real plan to reduce recurrence — leaves the patient feeling unheard. The work here is to validate the frustration, treat today's episode, and put a structured prevention plan (and the necessary red-flag check) in its place.

🌸 Post-menopausal vaginal atrophy

After the menopause, falling oestrogen thins the vaginal and urethral tissues and changes the vaginal flora, which is a major and treatable driver of recurrent UTI. Vaginal oestrogen is one of the most effective preventive measures and is often overlooked.

"After the menopause the tissues down below get thinner and that makes infections more likely. A small amount of oestrogen cream or pessary used vaginally can really cut down how often these happen — it works locally and barely gets into the bloodstream."
💊 Antibiotic stewardship vs the standby request

Back-pocket antibiotics feel empowering but risk resistance and treating non-UTI symptoms. Offer instead: a clear self-care plan, a prevention strategy, and — if episodes are culture-confirmed and frequent — a properly set-up self-start or prophylaxis plan with review.

"I understand wanting something ready at home. Rather than antibiotics you start blindly, let me set up a proper prevention plan — and if we do go for standby antibiotics, I'd want to base it on what's actually grown on a urine test, so we hit the right bug."
🩸 The blood — careful framing

The haematuria must be raised, but without alarm. Frame the referral as thoroughness given her age and the fact the blood outlasted the infection.

"One thing I don't want to overlook is that the urine was still pink after the antibiotics finished. In most people it's nothing serious, but at your age it's exactly the situation where I'd want a specialist to take a quick look to be on the safe side."

⚠️ Dipstick — use it correctly

Under 65, clear symptoms: a dipstick adds little — treat on symptoms. Nitrite and/or leucocyte positivity supports UTI; a fully negative dipstick in someone with few symptoms makes UTI less likely.

Do NOT dipstick over-65s or catheterised patients: asymptomatic bacteriuria is common, and a positive stick does not mean infection — diagnose on new urinary/systemic symptoms.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Janet wants a stash of antibiotics — acknowledge it, share your reasoning, then agree a plan that actually reduces the infections
1
Validate — name the request and the frustration

Janet's request is reasonable from where she sits: four infections, four courses, and each time the antibiotic "worked". What she is really asking for is control — not having to chase a prescription while in pain. Naming both the request and the frustration before responding stops the consultation turning into a bargaining match over a prescription.

"Four infections in a year is miserable, and I can see why having antibiotics at home feels like the answer — you'd be in control rather than chasing a prescription each time. Let me tell you what I'm thinking, because I want to get you to fewer infections, not just faster treatment."
2
Explain — why a blind stash is not the best tool yet

Several episodes were never cultured, so we do not know which bug she has or what it is sensitive to. Repeated courses breed resistance, and a self-started antibiotic can mask the one thing that must not be masked: the blood. NICE NG112 sets out a prevention ladder — behavioural measures, vaginal oestrogen after the menopause, then single-dose or non-antibiotic options, with daily antibiotic prophylaxis last. A standby supply is not a rung on that ladder for most women; if ever used, it should rest on culture results and a clear plan.

"Only a couple of your infections were tested, so I don't know which germ keeps coming back or which antibiotic actually suits it. If I hand you a supply now, we're guessing — and the next one might not work. There are also things that stop these happening in the first place, and after the menopause one of them works particularly well."
3
Negotiate — give her something concrete today

Janet must leave with more than a refusal: treatment for today's infection with a urine sample sent first; vaginal oestrogen started; a written self-care and prevention plan; the fast-track referral for the blood explained calmly; and a named route back if she gets symptoms again ("ring and say 'recurrent UTI plan' — we'll see you and send a sample the same day"). Offer to revisit a culture-guided standby or prophylaxis plan once the referral and prevention steps are in place.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
NICE NG112 puts behavioural and personal hygiene measures on the first rung of recurrent UTI prevention, and NG109 makes self-care part of every acute episode. Be honest about the strength of the evidence: fluids and self-care are sensible and low-risk; cranberry and D-mannose have limited or uncertain evidence and NG109 notes there is no evidence to support cranberry products or urine alkalinising agents for treating an acute lower UTI.
💧
Fluids & Not Delaying Voiding
Target: pale-straw urine through the day; void when the urge comes
Mechanism

Regular urine flow dilutes and flushes bacteria that ascend the urethra before they can establish in the bladder. Holding on for long periods and concentrated urine both favour bacterial growth.

Practical

Drink enough to avoid dehydration (NG109 / NG112 self-care advice). Many women with frequency or leakage cut their fluid intake — explore this, because it can feed the cycle. Spread drinks through the day and ease off in the two hours before bed if nocturia is a problem.

NG112 rung 1 — low cost, low risk; evidence for increased water intake mainly in premenopausal women
🚽
Complete Emptying & Bowels
Target: soft daily stool; no significant post-void residual
Mechanism

Residual urine is a reservoir for bacteria. Constipation raises pelvic pressure, impairs bladder emptying and increases perineal bacterial load. Prolapse can also cause incomplete emptying after the menopause.

Practical

Ask about straining, a feeling of incomplete emptying, a vaginal lump or dragging. Treat constipation (fibre, fluids, laxative if needed). Consider a bladder scan for post-void residual if emptying is in doubt; "double voiding" (wait a minute, then try again) helps some women.

Named reversible drivers in the NG112 work-up — fix before prophylaxis
🌸
Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause
Target: vaginal oestrogen started; moisturiser for comfort
Mechanism

Oestrogen loss thins the vaginal and urethral lining, raises vaginal pH and depletes protective lactobacilli, allowing gut-derived uropathogens to colonise the vaginal introitus and periurethral area.

Practical

This card is the non-drug half: vaginal moisturisers and lubricants help comfort and are the first step after breast cancer (NG23). The effective prevention step is vaginal oestrogen itself (see 7D and 7F). Oral or systemic HRT does not reduce UTI risk and should not be offered for that purpose (NG112).

Vaginal oestrogen reduced recurrent UTI in postmenopausal women in the NG112 evidence review
❤️
Sex-Related Triggers
Target: identify and remove the trigger
Mechanism

Intercourse massages periurethral bacteria into the bladder. Spermicides disrupt the vaginal flora and favour E. coli colonisation; a diaphragm can also impede emptying.

Practical

Janet is not currently sexually active, so this is for completeness — but ask, because circumstances change and a post-coital pattern changes the plan. Advice: pass urine after sex, avoid spermicide-containing contraception, use lubricant if dryness. If a clear post-coital pattern persists, NG112 allows single-dose antibiotic prophylaxis at the trigger.

A clear trigger unlocks single-dose prophylaxis — far less antibiotic than nightly dosing
🍒
Cranberry & D-Mannose — Honest Advice
Target: informed choice, not false hope
Mechanism

Cranberry proanthocyanidins and D-mannose are thought to reduce the adhesion of E. coli fimbriae to the bladder lining.

Practical

NG112 describes the evidence for prevention as limited or uncertain; some women choose to try them, and that is reasonable if they understand this. They are not a treatment for an active infection (NG109). Cranberry products can be sugary, and there are case reports of interaction with warfarin — check before endorsing.

Patient choice — not a substitute for vaginal oestrogen or a culture
🩺
Acute-Episode Self-Care
Target: symptom relief within days; know when to escalate
Mechanism

Lower UTI symptoms are driven by bladder-wall inflammation. Analgesia and hydration relieve symptoms while the antibiotic — or, in mild cases, the body — clears the infection.

Practical

Paracetamol, or ibuprofen if suitable (NG109). Keep a simple symptom diary for each episode — date, symptoms, whether a sample was sent and the result — so the next consultation starts with evidence rather than memory. Record any visible blood and when it happened relative to the antibiotic.

🫂
Recurrent UTI — a "minor" infection that quietly reorganises a woman's life
Each episode is short, but the pattern is not. 🚗
Driving and travel

Uncomplicated UTI has no DVLA notification requirement. The practical issue is urgency on journeys and not driving while feverish, drowsy or unwell. In older or frail women, a UTI-related delirium is a reason not to drive until fully recovered — that is general fitness-to-drive advice, not a notifiable condition.

Being told recurrent infections relate to menopause can feel like an unwelcome label of ageing, and the word "hormones" may alarm women who have heard mixed messages about HRT. Nitrofurantoin does not treat pyelonephritis. The 48-hour reassessment rule comes from NG109 (lower UTI) and NG111 (pyelonephritis). A woman with fever and loin pain on a 3-day bladder antibiotic is under-treated twice over — wrong drug and wrong duration.
🟣 Don't lose the thread — blood in the urine
"I've sent the fast-track referral today. You should hear within two weeks — if you haven't heard in a week, ring us and we'll chase it. And if you see blood again, even once the infection has gone, let us know."

"After the menopause the tissues around the bladder get thinner and lose their natural protection, so germs from the skin get in more easily. That's why this keeps happening — and it's also why there's something we can do about it."

💊 Vaginal oestrogen
"It's a tiny dose used locally — not HRT tablets. It barely reaches the bloodstream, and it helps the tissues protect themselves again. Every night for two weeks, then twice a week."

Danger zones — where recurrent UTI consultations go wrong
"Blood in the urine with a UTI — just re-treat"
Visible haematuria that persists or recurs after successful UTI treatment at ≥45 is an urgent suspected cancer referral (NG12). A clear dipstick later does not cancel it
A stash of antibiotics for "next time"
Culture first, work through the NG112 prevention ladder, and review. A self-start supply, if ever used, rests on culture results and usually specialist advice
Nitrofurantoin for fever and loin pain
Pyelonephritis needs an NG111 regimen (e.g. cefalexin 7–10 days) and an MSU. Nitrofurantoin does not reach the kidney
Dipsticking an 80-year-old with delirium
UKHSA 2025: no dipstick at ≥65 or with a catheter. Diagnose on new urinary symptoms and systemic signs; look for other causes of delirium
Nightly antibiotics on unconfirmed "UTIs"
Confirm with cultures, fix reversibles, offer vaginal oestrogen (postmenopausal), then methenamine or single-dose prophylaxis before daily antibiotics — each with a review date
Trimethoprim in early pregnancy; 3 days in pregnancy
Pregnancy: MSU, immediate 7-day course — nitrofurantoin first (avoid at term), amoxicillin if susceptible or cefalexin (NG109)
Ignoring vaginal discharge
Dysuria with discharge or itch is vaginitis or an STI until shown otherwise — examine, swab, test, and don't reflex-prescribe for UTI
Offering HRT tablets to prevent UTIs
NG112: do not offer systemic HRT to reduce recurrent UTI. Vaginal oestrogen is the evidence-based option
Referral made, never tracked
Log urgent referrals, confirm the appointment, and give the patient a way to chase it — the haematuria safety-net is the one most often lost
