📋 Clinical Stem — The Limping Child
A 13-year-old boy with a few weeks of "knee" pain and a limp, now worse, who is overweight and walks with his foot turned out — and whose knee examines completely normally
Kyle Morrison, 13, is brought by his mother with several weeks of right knee and thigh pain and a limp that has worsened over the past week, now making PE difficult. There was no clear injury. He is overweight for his age. He walks with an antalgic gait and the right leg appears externally rotated. When you examine the knee it is entirely normal — no swelling, full painless range. He is otherwise well, afebrile, with no rash, bruising, weight loss or night sweats, and no recent illness. His mother is frustrated that "it's just his knee" and wants a referral to physiotherapy.
This stem tests the single most important principle of the limping child — that the hip refers to the knee — and the recognition of slipped upper femoral epiphysis (SUFE): an overweight adolescent around puberty, with knee/thigh pain, an externally rotated leg, restricted internal rotation of the hip, and a normal knee. It requires the candidate to examine the HIP despite knee-presenting pain, to recognise SUFE as needing urgent orthopaedic referral and non-weight-bearing (to prevent avascular necrosis), and NOT to refer to physiotherapy or reassure as a knee problem. It also tests the broader age-based framework and the exclusion of the emergencies (septic arthritis, and the NG12 leukaemia red flags) and safeguarding. The SCA challenge is to redirect a parent fixated on "the knee" toward urgent hip assessment, and to convey the seriousness without alarm.
Scenario A — SUFE (this stem) Overweight adolescent, knee/thigh pain, externally rotated leg, restricted hip internal rotation, normal knee. Same-day orthopaedic assessment, non-weight-bearing, X-ray (AP + frog-lateral); avoid AVN; check the other hip.
Scenario B — Septic arthritis (EMERGENCY, any age) Won't weight-bear/move joint, fever, systemically unwell, raised inflammatory markers (Kocher). Same-day admission; aspiration + IV antibiotics.
Scenario C — Transient synovitis (3–10y) Well child, post-viral, low/no fever, improving; diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out sepsis. Provisional diagnosis only if aged 3–9, well, afebrile, weight-bearing, limp <48 h. Rest, analgesia, safety-net, review at 48 h.
Scenario D — Perthes' (3–10y) Insidious limp/hip-knee pain, restricted hip movement, often well. Orthopaedic referral; X-ray (avascular necrosis of the femoral head).
Scenario E — Red flags: leukaemia / DDH / NAI Persistent/unexplained bone pain or pallor/fatigue/fever/lymphadenopathy/bruising → very urgent FBC within 48 h; petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment (NICE NG12). Infant: DDH (asymmetry, limited abduction). Any: non-accidental injury — inconsistent history/other injuries.
Key variables to adapt for Age (DDH/septic/toddler's # → transient synovitis/Perthes' → SUFE); weight-bearing & systemic features (septic); hip refers to knee; SUFE in the overweight adolescent; persistent bone pain + systemic features (leukaemia, NG12); safeguarding/NAI; growing pains as exclusion; bilateral/other-hip involvement.

1C — ICE
💭 Ideas
"What do you think is causing it — and what were you hoping we'd do?"
Kyle's mother thinks it's a knee problem for physiotherapy. Surfacing this lets you explain, respectfully, why the hip must be examined and why a referral to physio would be the wrong move if this is SUFE — replacing her plan with a better one she understands.
😟 Concerns
"Has anything about it worried you, beyond the pain and the limp?"
She may worry about him missing school/sport, or (unspoken) something serious. Naming it lets you reassure appropriately while being honest that some hip conditions need urgent attention.
🎯 Expectations
"Let me explain what I've found and what needs to happen next."
She expects a physio referral. If SUFE is found, you must reframe to urgent hospital assessment and non-weight-bearing — clearly and calmly, so she understands the change of plan and its importance.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The anxious parent, the "obvious" knee, and the danger of premature reassurance

A limping child frightens parents and pulls the clinician toward the site of the complaint. The limping child is a classic exam — and real-life — trap precisely because the dangerous diagnoses hide: SUFE behind "knee pain", septic arthritis behind a "viral limp", leukaemia behind "growing pains". The safe consultation resists premature reassurance, reasons by age, always examines the joint above and below (the hip for knee pain), screens the emergencies and red flags, and reframes the parent's expectations toward the right — sometimes urgent — pathway with clarity and care.

🦵 "It's just his knee"

Explain why the hip matters, without dismissing.

"You're right that the pain's in his knee — but in growing children the hip often sends its pain to the knee, so checking the hip is exactly how we avoid missing something important."
🏥 Reframing to urgent care

If SUFE, explain the change of plan calmly.

"From examining his hip, I'm concerned about a condition where the top of the thigh bone slips. It needs the hospital to see him urgently today, and he shouldn't put weight on that leg in the meantime — treating it quickly protects the hip."
🚫 Not physiotherapy

Explain why physio would be wrong here.

"I know you were hoping for physio, but if this is what I think, exercises could make it worse — this needs an orthopaedic surgeon, not physiotherapy, and quickly."
🛡️ Honest reassurance

Balance seriousness with calm.

"This is treatable, and we've caught it at the right time by checking properly. I'll arrange everything now and explain each step so you know what's happening."

Her request is reasonable on the information she has: the pain is in the knee, Kyle is sporty-ish, and physiotherapy is what friends were offered for knee pain. She is frustrated that it has dragged on for weeks. Acknowledging the frustration and her logic before changing the plan stops the change from feeling like a dismissal — and it gives Kyle permission to hear the explanation without feeling blamed for his weight or for "making a fuss".

"You came in hoping for physio for his knee, and I can completely see why — that's where he feels it, and it's been going on for weeks now. I'm glad you brought him, because I've found something important that changes what we need to do."
2
Explain — the hip sends pain to the knee, and the growth plate has slipped

Use one clear picture: the ball at the top of the thigh bone sits on a growth plate, like a scoop of ice cream on a cone, and in some teenagers during a growth spurt the ball starts to slide off. The nerves that supply the hip also supply the skin around the knee, so the brain reads it as knee pain. The knee exam is normal; the hip exam is not — his hip will not turn inwards and his leg rolls out. Explain why physiotherapy is wrong: exercise and weight-bearing load the slipping growth plate. Explain why speed matters: a slip that becomes unstable risks cutting off the blood supply to the ball of the hip (avascular necrosis), which is permanent.

"When I examined his knee, it was completely normal. But when I turned his hip inwards it was stiff and sore, and his leg rolls outwards. That pattern makes me think the growth plate at the top of his thigh bone has slipped — it's called a slipped upper femoral epiphysis. Hip problems often show up as knee pain, which is why it's been so confusing."
3
Negotiate — offer a concrete, practical plan today

The non-negotiables are same-day orthopaedic assessment and no weight on the right leg until he is seen. What is negotiable is how you make that happen: you phone the on-call orthopaedic team, write the referral letter, advise on how he travels (carried or by wheelchair to the car, crutches only if he is already confident and told not to put the foot down), and you help with the school and work fallout. Offer analgesia now. Tell her what to expect at hospital — X-rays, likely admission and an operation to hold the hip with a screw, usually soon after diagnosis.

"Here's what I'd like to do: I'll ring the orthopaedic team at the hospital now so they're expecting him today. Until they've seen him he mustn't put any weight on that right leg — not even to hop to the car if you can avoid it. I'll give him something for the pain and a letter to take. Does that feel doable, and what would make getting there today easier?"
Key principle: Unlike most "expectation" consultations, the plan here is not up for negotiation — delay can cost Kyle his hip. The skill is to be firm on the what (same day, non-weight-bearing) and flexible on the how (transport, analgesia, school, siblings, parental work), so the family leaves feeling looked after rather than overruled. The same structure works for the other limp scenarios: for a well 5-year-old with probable transient synovitis the expectation might be "an X-ray to be sure" — validate it, explain why a watchful 48-hour review is safer and more informative, and negotiate a clear review time and return criteria.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
In the limping child, "non-drug management" is mostly about protecting the joint and the child while the right team takes over. The key interventions differ sharply by diagnosis: strict non-weight-bearing for suspected SUFE, rest-then-return for transient synovitis, maintaining range and low-impact activity in Perthes', movement and physiotherapy in JIA, and — for all of them — school adjustments, family support and, later, weight management where relevant.
🦯
Strict Non-Weight-Bearing (Suspected SUFE)
Target: no weight through the leg until orthopaedics have seen him
Mechanism

In SUFE the femoral head slips posteriorly and medially on the weakened proximal femoral physis. Loading through the leg drives further slip; a stable slip that becomes unstable (the child cannot bear weight even with crutches) carries a much higher risk of avascular necrosis because the retinacular vessels supplying the head can be torn or kinked.

Practical

From the consulting room: wheelchair to the car if available, no hopping or walking "just to the door". Crutches only if he can already use them safely with the right foot off the ground. Hospitals will usually admit and fix promptly. Do not send him for a routine outpatient X-ray and home to wait.

Suspected SUFE → urgent same-day orthopaedic assessment; non-weight-bearing until seen (Am Fam Physician 2017 SCFE review)
🛋️
Rest Then Graded Return (Transient Synovitis)
Target: normal walking within about a week; review at 48 h
Mechanism

Transient synovitis is a self-limiting, usually post-viral, sterile inflammation of the hip synovium with a small effusion. Rest reduces intra-articular pressure and pain; anti-inflammatory analgesia may shorten symptoms. The natural history is improvement over days — which is exactly why failure to improve is itself a red flag.

Practical

Let the child choose their level of activity; avoid sport and trampolining until walking normally. Keep them off nursery or school only while they cannot walk comfortably. Give a written sheet with the return criteria and the 48-hour review time. If not fully resolved by around a week, or it recurs, reassess and consider imaging for Perthes'.

Provisional diagnosis only in well, afebrile, weight-bearing 3–9-year-olds with limp <48 h — review at 48 h
🏊
Maintain Range, Reduce Impact (Perthes')
Target: keep hip abduction and rotation; low-impact activity
Mechanism

Perthes' is idiopathic avascular necrosis of the femoral epiphysis followed by fragmentation and slow re-ossification over years. Outcome depends on keeping the femoral head "contained" in the acetabulum and spherical as it remodels; stiffness (especially loss of abduction) and a younger age at onset influence prognosis — younger children generally do better.

Practical

Management is led by paediatric orthopaedics: observation, physiotherapy to keep range, activity modification (swimming and cycling rather than running and jumping), and surgery for some older children. The GP role is recognition, prompt referral, analgesia, school letters and supporting a family through a condition that can last several years.

Referral route: X-ray the hip and refer to paediatric orthopaedics (Am Fam Physician 2023 limp review)
🏫
School, PE & an Individual Healthcare Plan
Target: minimal missed school; safe access to lessons and exams
Mechanism

After SUFE fixation or during Perthes' or JIA, children face weeks to months on crutches or with restricted activity. Missed school and exclusion from PE and friendships drive low mood and social withdrawal — particularly for a teenager who may already feel self-conscious about his weight.

Practical

Schools in England have a statutory duty to support pupils with medical conditions (DfE, Supporting pupils at school with medical conditions). Ask for an individual healthcare plan: lift pass, leaving lessons early to avoid crowded corridors, a buddy to carry bags, ground-floor rooms, and an adapted PE role (scoring, coaching) rather than exclusion. Exam access arrangements if exams fall during recovery.

Keeps the young person connected to school and peers — the strongest protection against secondary low mood
🥗
Family-Based Weight Management (Later, Not Today)
Target: raise at a follow-up, with consent, as a family plan
Mechanism

Obesity increases the shear force across the proximal femoral physis during the pubertal growth spurt and is the strongest modifiable risk factor for SUFE, including slips of the other hip. Weight also affects anaesthetic risk and later hip health. But raising weight on the day of an urgent diagnosis risks shame and disengagement.

Practical

NICE NG246 (2025) favours respectful, non-stigmatising conversations and multicomponent, family-based interventions for children and young people living with obesity (diet, activity, behaviour change), tailored to the family. Plot BMI centile, ask permission, and focus on the whole family's habits and on activities Kyle can do while recovering (upper-body work, swimming once the surgeon allows).

NICE NG246: tailored, family-based, non-judgemental approach for children and young people
🤸
Physiotherapy — When It Is and Isn't Right
Target: physio after diagnosis, never instead of it
Mechanism

Physiotherapy is central after SUFE fixation (gait retraining, strengthening, graded return), in Perthes' (range of movement), in JIA (joint protection, strength, function) and in Osgood-Schlatter (load management and strengthening). It is harmful as a first step in undiagnosed hip pathology because it delays diagnosis and loads a slipping physis.

Practical

Say it plainly to the parent who asked for physio: "Physio will very likely be part of his recovery — after the surgeons have made the hip safe." For an adolescent with typical Osgood-Schlatter and a normal hip exam, activity modification and strengthening are first-line, with physiotherapy if not settling (see the Reasoning GP Osgood-Schlatter page).

Reframes the parent's request as "yes, later" rather than "no"
7D — Prescribing guide: analgesia and fever in the limping child (NICE NG143 · BNF for Children · MHRA)
There is no disease-modifying drug the GP gives for a limp. Prescribing is about safe symptom relief while the diagnosis is secured — and about not obscuring the red flags. Paracetamol and ibuprofen at weight- or age-appropriate doses per BNF for Children are the mainstays. Definitive treatment (IV antibiotics for septic arthritis, surgical fixation for SUFE, disease-modifying drugs for JIA) belongs to secondary care.
Step 1 — Paracetamol or ibuprofen

Dose per BNF for Children (by age/weight)

  • Ibuprofen is a sensible first choice for probable transient synovitis — its anti-inflammatory action may shorten symptoms (Kermond et al., Ann Emerg Med 2002: RCT of 36 children, median symptom duration 2 vs 4.5 days with placebo)
  • Paracetamol if NSAIDs are unsuitable (e.g. dehydration, NSAID-sensitive asthma, chickenpox — see BNF for Children cautions)
  • Check what has already been given today before advising the next dose
  • Weight-based dosing: record the child's weight
Pain that settles with ibuprofen does not rule out septic arthritis or SUFE — analgesia can make a sick joint look better
Step 2 — If there is fever (NICE NG143)

Treat distress, not the number

  • NG143: use paracetamol or ibuprofen for a febrile child who appears distressed, not simply to lower the temperature
  • Do not give both at the same time; consider alternating only if distress persists or recurs before the next dose is due
  • Antipyretics do not prevent febrile seizures
  • A febrile child who won't weight-bear or use a limb has an amber NG143 feature, and NG143 asks you to consider septic arthritis/osteomyelitis → same-day assessment for septic arthritis/osteomyelitis
If the child looks septic, arrange emergency transfer and follow NICE NG254 (suspected sepsis in under 16s) and local policy — analgesia and antipyretics are not the priority
What not to prescribe
  • Codeine under 12: contraindicated for pain (MHRA Drug Safety Update, July 2013); for 12–18-year-olds only for acute moderate pain not relieved by paracetamol or ibuprofen, short course
  • Aspirin under 16: avoid (Reye's syndrome — BNF for Children)
  • Oral antibiotics "just in case" for a hot joint or febrile limp — send for assessment instead; antibiotics before cultures can obscure the diagnosis
  • Oral steroids for an undiagnosed swollen joint — can mask infection and leukaemia; JIA steroids are specialist-directed

7G — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: school, sport, family, identity and driving
🫂
The limping child — a teenager on crutches, a parent who "should have known", and a family reorganised overnight
A diagnosis like SUFE turns an irritating knee ache into an emergency admission and an operation. For Kyle it means crutches, missed school and sport, and — if handled clumsily — a sense that his body and his weight are to blame. For his mother it may mean guilt ("I thought it was just his knee"), time off work and a scramble to organise other children. Addressing these explicitly is part of good management and strongly influences how well the family engages with follow-up.
🏫
School & Exams

Post-operative crutches mean stairs, crowded corridors and heavy bags become hazards. Missed lessons accumulate quickly. A letter to school and a request for an individual healthcare plan (DfE statutory guidance on supporting pupils with medical conditions) turns an ad-hoc problem into an organised one.

"Would it help if I wrote to school explaining what's happened and what he'll need — like a lift pass and leaving lessons a few minutes early — so you don't have to argue for it?"
⚽
Sport, Friends & Identity

For many adolescents, sport is where friendships happen. Being told "no PE" for months can feel like exclusion. Offer a role (scorer, coach's assistant) and a realistic timeline set by the surgeon, and name the good news: most children return to full activity after a stable slip is fixed.

"Kyle, the surgeons will tell you when you can play again — it's usually months, not years. In the meantime, is there a way you can still be part of the team?"
⚖️
Weight, Shame & Stigma

Kyle may already be teased about his weight. Linking his hip directly to his size in the first conversation risks shame and disengagement. NICE NG246 emphasises respectful, non-stigmatising language and family-based approaches. Deal with the hip today; ask permission to talk about weight at follow-up, framed as health and the other hip, not blame.

"At a later appointment, would it be OK to talk as a family about things that can help protect his other hip — like activity and food? No one's to blame for this."
👩‍👦
Parental Guilt & Family Logistics

"Should I have brought him sooner?" is a common unspoken worry. Knee-presenting SUFE is frequently missed by clinicians too. Acknowledge that and move to the practical: time off work, siblings, transport, parking, how long the admission might be.

"Lots of people — doctors included — are caught out because it feels like the knee. You've done the right thing. What do you need to sort out today to get him to the hospital?"
🚗
Driving & Transport

Kyle is too young to drive, so the DVLA question does not arise for him — but transport does: he should be taken by car or wheelchair, not walk or cycle. For an older adolescent who drives (17+) and has hip surgery, the DVLA does not set a fixed no-driving period after limb surgery; the driver must be able to control the car safely, including an emergency stop, and should follow the surgeon's advice and check with their insurer.

"Please drive him there rather than letting him walk or get the bus — every step on that leg matters until the surgeons have seen him."
🛡️
Safeguarding Without Accusation

In other variants — a toddler with a fracture, a non-mobile infant with bruising, a story that changes — NICE CG89 requires you to consider maltreatment. The skill is to be open and non-accusatory while following the local pathway: discuss with the safeguarding lead or on-call paediatrician and document the explanation given verbatim.

"With any injury in a young child we always make sure we fully understand how it happened, and sometimes that means the children's team taking a look too. It's a routine part of keeping children safe."

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases + RAG marking + danger zones

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Suspected SUFE — before and after the hospital
"Until the orthopaedic team have seen him today, Kyle mustn't put any weight on his right leg. If he suddenly can't move the leg at all, or the pain becomes much worse, call 999 rather than driving. And after this is all sorted — if he ever gets pain in the other hip, groin, thigh or knee, even if it seems minor, please bring him in the same day and tell whoever sees him that he's had a slipped hip before."
A stable slip can become unstable with further loading, sharply raising the risk of avascular necrosis. Contralateral slips are common and present the same deceptive way. Document the non-weight-bearing advice, who you spoke to in orthopaedics, and the time.
🩺 Transient synovitis — the 48-hour contract
"I think this is most likely an irritable hip after his cold, which usually settles in a few days. I'd like to see him again in 48 hours. But if he gets a temperature, stops putting weight on the leg, seems unwell or more sleepy, or the pain is getting worse rather than better — don't wait for that appointment; bring him back or go to A&E the same day."
The accepted primary-care rule permits a provisional diagnosis only in a well, afebrile, weight-bearing child aged 3–9 with a limp under 48 hours — and only with review at 48 hours. The safety-net is what makes watchful waiting safe; record the specific triggers given.
🟣 NG12 — the persistent or night-time pain
"Most children's aches are nothing serious. But if the pain keeps going, wakes him at night, or he seems pale, tired, bruises easily, gets lumps in his neck or armpits, or keeps getting fevers — I want to see him and do a blood test within a couple of days, not weeks."
NICE NG12: any one of these features warrants a very urgent FBC within 48 hours; unexplained bone pain or swelling warrants consideration of a very urgent X-ray. "Growing pains" should not be labelled until these are absent and the examination is normal.

💭 The hip–knee link
"The nerves from the hip also supply the knee, so a hip problem often feels like knee pain. That's why I always check the hip when a child has knee pain."

"I'm worried that the growth plate at the top of his thigh bone has slipped. It's treatable, and it's best treated quickly — which is why I want him seen today."

"Think of the top of the thigh bone like a scoop of ice cream on a cone. Standing on it pushes the scoop further off. Keeping weight off holds it where it is until they fix it."

🚫 Danger zones
✗
Examining only the knee→ Knee or thigh pain in a child requires a hip examination, especially internal rotation. A normal knee exam with knee pain is a clue, not reassurance.
✗
Routine X-ray and home for suspected SUFE→ Acute limp over 9 with painful or restricted hip movement needs urgent same-day assessment. Phone orthopaedics; keep non-weight-bearing.
✗
Calling it transient synovitis outside the criteria→ Under 3, over 9, febrile, unwell, non-weight-bearing, or limp over 48 h: not a primary-care transient synovitis diagnosis. Send for same-day assessment.
✗
Waiting for bloods in a febrile child who won't weight-bear→ Kocher criteria are a hospital tool. In primary care, fever plus non-weight-bearing (an amber NG143 feature in under-5s that should prompt you to consider septic arthritis/osteomyelitis) means same-day referral.
✗
"Growing pains" with night pain or systemic features→ NICE NG12: persistent or unexplained bone pain alone warrants a very urgent FBC; add X-ray within 48 h for unexplained bone pain or swelling.
✗
Oral antibiotics or steroids "to see if it helps"→ Both can mask septic arthritis or leukaemia and compromise cultures or diagnosis. Refer instead.
✗
Codeine for a child under 12→ Contraindicated for pain (MHRA 2013). Escalating analgesic need means reassessment.
✗
Ignoring an injury that doesn't fit→ NICE CG89: a fracture with an absent or unsuitable explanation (or bruising in a child who is not independently mobile) means suspect maltreatment and follow the local safeguarding pathway.

👶 1 — Differential by age

0–3y: DDH, septic arthritis, toddler's fracture, NAI. 3–10y: transient synovitis, septic arthritis, Perthes'. 10–16y: SUFE, septic arthritis, Perthes'. Any age: septic arthritis, malignancy (leukaemia), osteomyelitis, JIA, NAI. Always examine the hip in knee pain (SUFE/Perthes' refer to the knee).

🚦 2 — Triage & act

🔴 Same-day: won't weight-bear + fever/unwell → septic arthritis (admit, aspirate, IV abx; Kocher); bone pain + pallor/bruising/lymphadenopathy/fever → very urgent FBC within 48 h (NG12; petechiae/hepatosplenomegaly → immediate); acute limp under 3 → same-day paediatrics; suspected NAI → safeguarding (NICE CG89); suspected SUFE → same-day orthopaedics, non-weight-bearing, AP + frog-lateral X-ray. 🟠 Urgent specialist: Perthes', DDH, JIA (BSPAR ≤4 weeks). 🟢 Routine: transient synovitis (exclusion; well child; rest/analgesia/safety-net/review). Don't over-label "growing pains".
