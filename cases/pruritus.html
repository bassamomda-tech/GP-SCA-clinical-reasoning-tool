📋 Clinical Stem — Itch (Pruritus)
A 61-year-old man with 3 months of relentless generalised itch and NO rash, drenching night sweats and weight loss, who thinks his "skin's just dry"
Frank Adeyemi, 61, has had 3 months of relentless, generalised itching that keeps him awake, with no rash — only scratch marks. He assumes his skin is just dry and wants "a strong cream or antihistamine". On questioning he has had drenching night sweats (changing the sheets), has lost about 6kg without trying, and feels more tired than usual. He has no jaundice that he's noticed, normal bowels, takes no regular medications, and there are no affected household contacts. On examination there is no primary rash, but there is some firm, non-tender lymphadenopathy in his neck and axillae. He's keen to leave with something to stop the itch.
This stem tests the central reasoning in pruritus — the rash vs no rash fork — and recognition that generalised itch WITHOUT a primary rash, accompanied by B-symptoms (drenching night sweats, unintentional weight loss, fatigue) and lymphadenopathy, is a red-flag presentation for haematological malignancy (lymphoma) requiring examination of nodes/spleen, blood tests (FBC, film, LDH, ESR) and an urgent suspected-cancer (NICE NG12) referral — NOT simply an emollient and antihistamine. It also tests the broader systemic screen for itch-without-rash (liver, renal, thyroid, iron, polycythaemia, diabetes), the dermatological causes when a rash IS present (notably scabies — contacts, burrows, night itch), drug causes, and symptomatic relief. The SCA challenge is converting the patient's "just dry skin / give me a cream" request into appropriate urgent investigation, sensitively.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The "trivial" symptom that can be the first sign of cancer

Itch is dismissed — by patients as dry skin, by clinicians as a minor dermatological nuisance. Yet generalised itch without a rash is a recognised harbinger of systemic disease, including lymphoma, and can precede other symptoms by months. The skilled consultation holds two things together: genuine, prompt relief of a miserable, sleep-destroying symptom, and the discipline to ask "is there a rash?" and to screen for the systemic causes and B-symptoms — so that a request for "a strong cream" is met with both comfort and, where indicated, a life-saving work-up.

🔎 Reframing "dry skin"

Explain why this itch needs investigating.

"Dry skin is common and I'll treat the itch — but dry skin doesn't usually cause night sweats and weight loss. Those make me want to check what's going on underneath, to be safe."
⚖️ Thorough, not alarming

Pitch the cancer-pathway work-up proportionately.

"Most of the time these tests are reassuring. Given your symptoms, the responsible thing is to examine you and do some blood tests quickly rather than just hand over a cream."
😴 The misery of itch

Acknowledge the impact and treat it now.

"Itch like this is exhausting and ruins your sleep — that matters, and I'll give you something to ease it tonight while we sort out the cause."
🤝 Holding uncertainty

Be honest while supportive.

"I can't tell you the cause yet, but I'd rather check properly than miss something. Whatever it is, we'll work through it together."

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate

1
Validate — name the expectation and the misery

Frank came for a cream. Three months of sleep-destroying itch is genuinely miserable, and his request is reasonable. If you start with "I'm worried about cancer", he hears that his problem has been hijacked. If you start by naming his request and the toll the itch is taking, he hears that you are on his side — which makes the next step easier to accept.

"You came in hoping for something strong to stop the itch — and after three months of being kept awake, I completely understand why. I am going to give you something for it today."
2
Explain — share the reasoning behind the tests

The clinical reasoning is simple and can be shared openly: dry skin does not cause drenching night sweats, 6kg of weight loss or enlarged glands. Itch without a rash is sometimes the skin's way of signalling something happening inside the body. Most causes are treatable (iron, thyroid, liver, kidney), but the glands mean the blood specialists need to see him quickly.

"Here's what's making me think carefully: dry skin doesn't usually cause soaking night sweats, weight loss and swollen glands. Together those mean I want to do blood tests today and ask the specialists to see you quickly, to find out what's driving the itch."

He leaves with two things: a symptom plan (emollient as a soap substitute and moisturiser, a cooling menthol cream, lukewarm short showers, nails short, and — only if sleep remains very disturbed — a short course of a sedating antihistamine at night with a driving warning) and a cause plan (bloods today, a suspected cancer pathway referral, a named date to discuss results, and a safety-net).

"So — for tonight, a moisturiser to use instead of soap and a cooling cream for the itchiest areas. For the bigger question, blood tests today and a fast-track referral. I'll ring you with the results myself. Does that sound like a plan you can go with?"
Key principle: In pruritus, "treat the symptom" and "pursue the cause" are not competing agendas. The failing consultation does only one of them — either a cream and goodbye (missing lymphoma), or an anxious cancer referral with nothing for the itch (leaving the patient sleepless and resentful). The passing consultation delivers both, in that order: relief first, reasoning second, plan third.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
General skin-care measures are the foundation for every cause of itch — including Frank's, while the cause is being found. Dry skin itches more whatever the underlying disease, and scratching damages the barrier and feeds the cycle. These measures are low-risk and should be explained carefully rather than dismissed as "just moisturise".
🧴
Emollients & Soap Substitute
Target: liberal, frequent use — ideally 2–3+ times daily
Mechanism

Xerosis lowers the itch threshold: a disrupted stratum corneum lets irritants in and water out, and exposed nerve endings fire more readily. Emollients restore the lipid barrier and reduce transepidermal water loss. Soap and foaming washes strip lipids, so washing with an emollient instead removes a daily trigger.

Practical

Choose one the patient will actually use (a lighter cream by day, a greasier one at night). Smooth on in the direction of hair growth; apply after a bath while skin is damp. Use a pump or spatula rather than fingers in the tub. Fire safety (MHRA): all emollients — paraffin-containing or paraffin-free — soak into clothing and bedding and make them burn readily; warn smokers and anyone near open flames, and advise frequent washing of fabrics.

Mainstay of treatment for itch of any cause
🚿
Bathing & Temperature
Target: short, lukewarm showers; cool bedroom
Mechanism

Heat and vasodilatation intensify itch; hot water also removes skin lipids. Itch is typically worse at night when skin temperature rises under bedding. A clear pattern of itch within minutes of water contact (aquagenic itch) is a clue to polycythaemia vera and should prompt an FBC rather than just advice.

Practical

Keep showers short and lukewarm; pat dry rather than rub; moisturise immediately. Keep the bedroom cool, use light cotton bedding, and avoid overheating (electric blankets, heavy duvets). A cool damp flannel or cooling gel pack on an itchy patch can abort a scratching episode.

Simple, zero-cost — reduces night-time itch and skin damage
✋
Breaking the Scratch–Itch Cycle
Target: fewer scratching episodes; no new excoriations
Mechanism

Scratching gives brief relief by pain-mediated inhibition of itch signalling, but it damages the epidermis, releases inflammatory mediators and sensitises nerves — so itch returns stronger. Over time this produces lichenification (lichen simplex) and nodular prurigo, which itch in their own right.

Practical

Keep nails short; wear cotton gloves at night if scratching during sleep. Substitute: press, pinch or apply a cold pack instead of scratching. Habit-reversal techniques (noticing the urge, a competing hand action) help chronic scratchers. Treat any infected excoriations promptly.

Habit reversal is a recognised behavioural approach in chronic itch and eczema
👕
Clothing & Irritant Avoidance
Target: remove avoidable triggers
Mechanism

Wool and rough synthetic fibres mechanically trigger itch; fragranced products, biological detergents and fabric conditioners act as irritants on a damaged barrier. Occlusive or tight clothing traps heat and sweat.

Practical

Loose cotton or silk-type clothing next to the skin; non-biological, fragrance-free detergent with an extra rinse; avoid bubble baths, perfumed wipes and "medicated" antiseptic washes. Review any topical products the patient has been self-applying — some (e.g. topical antihistamines, local anaesthetics) can themselves sensitise.

Prevents contact dermatitis layering on top of the original itch
😴
Sleep
Target: sleep restored as itch comes under control
Mechanism

Itch peaks at night (circadian changes in skin temperature, cortisol and barrier function), and sleep loss lowers the itch threshold and worsens mood — a second vicious cycle alongside scratching. Improving sleep often reduces reported itch intensity.

Practical

Emollient and cooling measures just before bed; cool, dark room; cotton gloves; no screens in bed. A short course of a sedating antihistamine at night can be considered if sleep stays badly disturbed despite these measures — it helps by sedation rather than by switching off itch, and is best avoided in older or frail people.

Sleep-directed advice addresses the most disabling part of chronic itch
🧠
Psychological Support
Target: mood screened; support offered if distressed
Mechanism

Chronic itch and depression/anxiety are linked in both directions: stress amplifies itch perception and scratching, and relentless itch causes low mood, irritability and, in some, suicidal thoughts. Waiting for suspected-cancer results adds acute anxiety on top.

Practical

Ask about mood (PHQ-9/GAD-7 where useful); offer NHS Talking Therapies self-referral for persistent distress; name the waiting period as hard. If there is a fixed belief of infestation without evidence (delusional infestation), take the distress seriously, avoid arguing about the belief, and involve dermatology/psychiatry (psychodermatology services where available).

Treating distress improves quality of life independent of itch severity
7D — Prescribing guide: treat the cause, then a stepped approach to the symptom
There is no single "anti-itch" drug. Standard practice puts the underlying cause and general skin care first; topical cooling agents next; and antihistamines only selectively — non-sedating ones where itch is histamine-driven (urticaria), and a sedating one at night short-term only if sleep remains disturbed. Cause-specific drugs (cholestatic, renal, polycythaemia, scabies) follow the relevant specialist or national guidance. Doses are "per BNF" unless a named guideline states them.
Step 1 — Skin care + topical (everyone)

Emollient as moisturiser AND soap substitute ± topical cooling agent

  • Emollient liberally and frequently; bath/shower emollient or cream wash instead of soap
  • Menthol-containing cream or emollient (per local formulary / BNF) gives a cooling counter-sensation, useful at night
  • Crotamiton cream is an option for itch (including residual post-scabies itch) — dose per BNF
  • Topical steroid only where there is an inflammatory dermatosis (eczema, lichen simplex) — not for itch without a rash
Avoid topical antihistamines and topical local anaesthetics — risk of contact sensitisation, little benefit.
Step 2 — Antihistamines (selectively)

Non-sedating by day if histamine-driven; sedating at night only short-term if sleep is disturbed

  • Non-sedating (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine): helpful in urticaria and other histamine-mediated itch; little effect on most systemic (cholestatic, uraemic, lymphoma) itch
  • Sedating (chlorphenamine, hydroxyzine, promethazine): benefit is mainly sedation — consider a short trial at night if sleep remains poor despite Step 1
  • Warn: drowsiness can carry over into the next morning — do not drive if affected
Older people: avoid sedating antihistamines — falls, confusion, urinary retention, constipation and cumulative anticholinergic burden. Hydroxyzine: avoid in the elderly (MHRA DSU April 2015).
Step 3 — Cause-specific / specialist

Match the drug to the mechanism — usually with specialist input

  • Cholestatic: colestyramine first-line (BSG/UK-PBC 2018); rifampicin, naltrexone, sertraline are hepatology-led
  • CKD-associated: optimise dialysis/renal care; gabapentinoids at renal dose are renal-team decisions; difelikefalin (TA890) in-centre haemodialysis only
  • Lymphoma: itch usually settles with treatment of the lymphoma
  • Chronic itch of unknown origin: phototherapy, SSRIs/mirtazapine or gabapentinoids — dermatology-led
Gabapentin and pregabalin are Schedule 3 controlled drugs (since April 2019) and accumulate in CKD — not a primary-care first step for itch.

7G — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Itch — the invisible symptom that steals sleep, patience and, sometimes, hides something serious
Chronic itch can harm quality of life as much as chronic pain, but it gets little sympathy: nobody can see it, people assume it is trivial, and the scratching itself is embarrassing. For Frank the burden is doubled — months of broken sleep, and now the shock of being told his "dry skin" needs a fast-track referral. The GP who addresses the work, the driving, the family and the fear alongside the tests is treating the person, not the blood results.
💼
Work & Concentration

Months of broken sleep cause daytime exhaustion, poor concentration and irritability. Visible scratching in meetings or with customers is embarrassing. Upcoming hospital appointments and possible biopsies will need time off.

A fit note is rarely needed for itch alone, but reasonable adjustments (flexible start times, time for appointments) may help — and if lymphoma is confirmed, cancer counts as a disability under the Equality Act 2010 from diagnosis.

"How is the lack of sleep affecting you at work? If you need time for the hospital appointments, I can help with a letter."
🚗
Driving & Sedating Medicines

Itch itself does not need to be reported to the DVLA. The driving risk is the treatment: sedating antihistamines impair reaction times, often into the next morning, and it is an offence to drive when impaired by any medicine. Severe sleep deprivation is itself a driving hazard.

If a serious diagnosis follows, driving advice is given for that condition and its treatment at the time — not assumed now.

"If you take the night-time tablet and feel at all drowsy or slowed down the next morning, please don't drive — it's not safe and it's against the law. The same goes if you're exhausted from lack of sleep."
👨‍👩‍👧
Family & What to Tell Them

Night-time scratching and soaked sheets disturb a partner's sleep too. Once a fast-track referral is mentioned, patients often struggle with whether and what to tell family. For scabies the family issue is practical — everyone must be treated together — and emotional, because of misplaced stigma.

"Would it help to bring someone with you to the results appointment? Some people find it easier not to carry the waiting on their own."
🪞
Identity, Stigma & Shame

Scratch marks, "dirty" connotations of scabies, and the assumption that itch is "all in the mind" all cause shame. Scabies is caught through close skin contact, not poor hygiene — saying this explicitly lifts stigma. Excoriations and lichenified skin can also change how patients see themselves.

"Scabies has nothing to do with how clean you are — it spreads through close skin contact, and anyone can catch it."
😟
Fear & the Waiting Period

The time between referral and diagnosis is often the most anxious part of a cancer journey. Uncertainty, catastrophising and searching online at night (when the itch keeps them awake anyway) can all make it worse. A named results date and a named person to contact are therapeutic.

"The waiting is often the hardest bit. I'll call you myself with the blood results, and here's how to reach us if you're worried before the hospital appointment."
🧠
Mood, Sleep & Delusional Infestation

Chronic itch is linked with depression and anxiety, and severe cases with suicidal thoughts — ask directly if mood is low. A small group of patients hold a fixed belief of infestation despite no evidence, often bringing "samples"; they need empathy, not confrontation, and psychodermatology input.

"Itch that goes on for months can really wear people down. How has your mood been? Have you had any thoughts that life isn't worth living?"

Lost referrals and unactioned results are a recognised cause of delayed cancer diagnosis. Giving the patient a role in checking ("if you haven't heard in a week") builds in a second safety layer.
🟠 Symptoms that should bring you back sooner

✗
Not examining nodes, liver and spleen→ The lymphadenopathy is what triggers the NG12 referral. No examination, no diagnosis.
✗
Waiting for bloods before referring→ Normal bloods do not exclude lymphoma. With unexplained nodes plus B-symptoms, refer on clinical grounds and send bloods in parallel.
✗
Sedating antihistamine to an older person or a driver without warning→ Falls, confusion, anticholinergic burden, next-day impairment. Avoid in the elderly; short-term and with driving advice otherwise.
✗
Scabies treated in one person only→ Every close contact treated the same day, two applications 7 days apart, 60°C laundry on day 1 — otherwise reinfestation looks like treatment failure.
✗
Pregnancy itch reassured as "normal"→ Itch of palms/soles, especially later in pregnancy, needs prompt maternity contact for bile acids and LFTs — same day if fetal movements are reduced (RCOG 2022 ICP).
✗
Missing jaundice or aquagenic itch→ Jaundice with pale stools/dark urine means obstruction until proven otherwise; itch after bathing means check FBC for polycythaemia.
✗
No results or referral follow-up plan→ Name who reviews results and when; ask the patient to call if the hospital hasn't been in touch within a week.

🔀 1 — The fork
Itch WITH a rash

Dermatological: eczema, scabies (burrows/night/contacts → permethrin to all), urticaria, lichen planus, dermatitis herpetiformis, xerosis. Treat the skin condition.

Itch WITHOUT a rash

Systemic: screen FBC/film, LDH/ESR, ferritin, U&E, LFTs, TFTs, glucose. Causes: liver/cholestasis, CKD, thyroid, iron deficiency, polycythaemia (aquagenic), diabetes, lymphoma.

🚩 2 — Red flags & relief

🟣 Itch + night sweats/weight loss/fever/lymphadenopathy → examine nodes/spleen, FBC/film/LDH, urgent NG12 (lymphoma). Jaundice/pale stools/dark urine → cholestasis. Pregnancy (palms/soles) → bile acids + obstetrics. Relief: treat the cause + emollients, avoid soap/heat, cooling agents, sedating antihistamine at night only short-term (avoid in the elderly), break the scratch cycle, address sleep/mood.

1 · Rash or no rash
