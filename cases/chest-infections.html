Bronchitis vs pneumoniaAcute bronchitis (cough ± sputum, no focal chest signs, systemically well) is usually viral and self-limiting — antibiotics rarely help. Pneumonia (focal chest signs, fever, tachypnoea, hypoxia, systemic upset) is a different, potentially serious diagnosis. Examination separates them
CRB-65 stratifies CAPIn community-acquired pneumonia, CRB-65 (Confusion, Respiratory rate ≥30, BP <90/≤60, age ≥65) informs place of care (NICE NG250, 2025): score 1 — primary care with safety-netting, virtual ward, SDEC, hospital at home or hospital; score 2 or more — consider hospital referral; higher scores carry a high mortality risk. Always combine with clinical judgement and oxygen saturations
Stewardship: don't reflex-prescribeMost acute coughs/bronchitis are viral. NICE supports no antibiotic or a back-up (delayed) prescription for uncomplicated cases, with clear safety-netting — reducing resistance. Reserve immediate antibiotics for pneumonia, systemic illness or high-risk patients
Spot sepsis earlyA chest infection can be the source of sepsis: confusion, RR ≥22, hypotension, tachycardia, hypoxia, mottling, very high/low temperature, reduced urine output. Use a sepsis screen / NEWS2 (NICE NG253, 2025 — replaced NG51; high-risk community features include new confusion, RR ≥25, HR >130); sepsis is a time-critical emergency — admit and treat without delay
COPD exacerbations differIn COPD, an infective exacerbation (increased breathlessness, sputum volume/purulence) is managed with bronchodilators ± steroids ± antibiotics (per sputum purulence/criteria); have a lower threshold and a rescue-pack/self-management plan. Watch for type 2 respiratory failure
Persistent cough → think cancer/TBA cough/chest symptoms not resolving (e.g. >3 weeks, or pneumonia not clearing), especially in a smoker 40+, with haemoptysis, weight loss or persistent signs, needs a chest X-ray and the NICE NG12 lung-cancer pathway — and consider TB (cough >3 weeks, weight loss, night sweats, risk factors)
Follow up pneumoniaArrange a follow-up chest X-ray (~6 weeks) after pneumonia in smokers and those over 50 to ensure resolution and exclude an underlying lung cancer obstructing the airway. Safety-net for non-resolving or recurrent same-site infection
Protect the vulnerableThe elderly, frail, immunosuppressed, those with comorbidity/lung disease and the very young decompensate fastest and present atypically (confusion, falls, no fever). Lower the threshold to examine, investigate and admit; offer vaccination (flu, pneumococcal, COVID)
📋 Clinical Stem — Chest Infections
A 72-year-old man with 5 days of cough, fever and breathlessness, now confused and unsteady, whose daughter is worried he's "gone downhill fast"
Brian Côté, 72, is brought in by his daughter with 5 days of productive cough, fever and increasing breathlessness, and since yesterday he has become confused and unsteady on his feet — "not himself at all". He has COPD (ex-smoker, 40 pack-years) and type 2 diabetes. On arrival he looks unwell: respiratory rate 28, oxygen saturation 90% on air, heart rate 112, blood pressure 104/62, temperature 38.6°C, and he is disorientated to time and place with focal crackles at the right base. His daughter expected "just some antibiotics". He has not been eating or drinking much and his urine output is reduced.
This stem tests the ability to: distinguish a serious lower respiratory tract infection / community-acquired pneumonia from simple bronchitis; recognise that this patient is not a "just antibiotics" case but has features of sepsis and significant CAP (confusion, RR ≥22/≥30, hypoxia, tachycardia, borderline BP, reduced urine output, comorbidity) — to calculate CRB-65 and use a sepsis/NEWS2 assessment and arrange urgent admission rather than an outpatient prescription; to recognise atypical presentation in the elderly (confusion/falls); to manage antibiotic stewardship appropriately in milder cases but treat seriously here; and to safety-net and follow up (resolution CXR, NG12 if non-resolving). The SCA challenge is conveying to a worried daughter (who expected a prescription) why admission is needed, calmly and clearly.
Scenario A — CAP with sepsis (this stem) Elderly, comorbid, confused, hypoxic, tachypnoeic, tachycardic, borderline BP. CRB-65 high; sepsis features → urgent admission, oxygen, IV antibiotics/fluids per local sepsis pathway. NOT outpatient antibiotics.
Scenario B — Acute bronchitis (well adult) Cough ± sputum, no focal signs, systemically well. Viral; no/delayed antibiotic; self-care; safety-net. Stewardship.
Scenario C — CAP, low severity (CRB-65 0) Focal signs, fever, but well and stable. Home with oral antibiotics (amoxicillin first-line per local), safety-net, review.
Scenario D — Infective COPD exacerbation Increased breathlessness/sputum volume & purulence. Bronchodilators ± oral steroids ± antibiotics (purulence/criteria); rescue pack; watch for CO2 retention/type 2 failure.
Scenario E — Persistent/atypical Cough >3 weeks, haemoptysis, weight loss, non-resolving pneumonia, smoker 40+ → CXR + NG12 lung-cancer pathway; consider TB (cough >3 weeks, night sweats, risk factors).

Her concern is the rapid change and the confusion. Naming it validates her for bringing him and lets you align: that confusion is exactly why he needs urgent assessment.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Expectations, stewardship, and the deteriorating older patient

Chest infections sit at two poles of the same consultation skill. At one pole is the well adult who wants antibiotics for a viral cough — where the art is to explain stewardship and safety-net without leaving them feeling fobbed off. At the other is the frail, comorbid older patient whose "chest infection" is actually pneumonia and sepsis, presenting atypically with confusion — where the art is to recognise severity behind a family's modest expectation and act decisively. Both require reading severity accurately and communicating the plan with clarity and empathy.

🏥 Reframing to admission

Explain clearly why home antibiotics aren't safe here.

"I know hospital wasn't what you expected, but his confusion and low oxygen tell me his body's under real strain. Treating this properly — with oxygen, fluids and antibiotics into a vein — needs to happen in hospital, today."
💊 Stewardship (milder cases)

For the well patient, explain why antibiotics may not help.

"Most chest infections like this are viral, so antibiotics often don't help and can cause side effects. I'll give you a 'just-in-case' prescription to use only if you're not improving, and tell you exactly what to watch for."
👪 Supporting the family

Acknowledge their worry and role.

"You did exactly the right thing bringing him in — you know him best, and 'not himself' is an important sign. Let's get him the help he needs."
🛡️ Prevention

Vaccination and self-management for at-risk patients.

"Once he's through this, we'll make sure his flu, pneumonia and COVID jabs are up to date and his COPD plan is solid — that reduces the chance of this happening again."

7A — Address the expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
The expectation runs in both directions — "just antibiotics" for the septic patient, and "surely I need antibiotics" for the viral cough. Handle both the same way.
1
Validate — name the expectation and the worry behind it

Brian's daughter came expecting a prescription and now faces an ambulance. Her father hates hospitals, and she half-blames herself for not coming sooner. Naming what she expected, and why that was a reasonable thing to expect, stops the next part of the conversation feeling like a correction. For the well patient with bronchitis the same step applies in reverse: they want antibiotics because they feel dreadful, have lost sleep, and have been told before that "it's gone to your chest".

"You came in expecting some antibiotics, and I completely understand why — that's what usually happens with a chest infection. And you were absolutely right to bring him in today."
2
Explain — share the reasoning in the patient's terms

Link the plan to the things the family has already noticed. For Brian: the confusion, the breathing, the low oxygen and the reduced urine are the reasons, and each can be named plainly. For bronchitis: most acute coughs are viral, antibiotics make little difference to how long the cough lasts, and they cause side effects (diarrhoea, thrush, rash) and resistance. NICE NG120 notes an acute cough is usually self-limiting and can last 3 to 4 weeks.

"The confusion and his oxygen level tell me the infection is affecting his whole body, not just his chest. Tablets at home take a day or two to work and can't give him oxygen or fluids. In hospital they can do all three straight away."
3
Negotiate — give them something concrete

Leave nothing vague. For Brian: you are calling the ambulance, starting oxygen, phoning ahead, sending a letter with his medicines and observations, and planning his follow-up after discharge. For the well patient: a clear self-care plan, a back-up prescription if appropriate (NICE NG120 / NG250 point-of-care CRP 20–100 mg/L), specific warning signs and a time frame to return. The family should leave knowing exactly what happens next.

"Here's what's happening now: I'm giving him oxygen, calling the ambulance and phoning the hospital so they're ready for him. You can go with him. Once he's home again, I'll see him, check his chest has cleared on an X-ray and sort out his vaccines."
Key principle: In chest infection the evidence is on your side at both ends — antibiotics rarely help viral bronchitis, and oral antibiotics are not enough for sepsis. The skill is to disagree with the expectation without disagreeing with the person. A family who feels heard accepts admission; a patient who feels heard accepts a back-up prescription.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + target
Most people with a lower respiratory tract infection are managed at home with no antibiotic at all, so self-care is the treatment rather than a courtesy. For people recovering from pneumonia, NICE NG250 asks you to explain the expected recovery timeline. For everyone, prevention (vaccination, smoking cessation, COPD self-management) reduces the next episode.
💧
Fluids, Rest and Fever Control
Target: pale urine; regular paracetamol if febrile/uncomfortable
Mechanism

Fever, tachypnoea and poor intake increase insensible fluid loss. Dehydration worsens tachycardia, reduces renal perfusion (AKI risk, especially on ACE inhibitors, diuretics or metformin) and thickens secretions. Paracetamol reduces fever and pleuritic discomfort, allowing better intake and sleep.

Practical

Small frequent drinks; paracetamol per BNF (adjust in low body weight or liver disease). Avoid NSAIDs in dehydration, CKD or on anticoagulants. In the older patient, a family member checking intake and urine output is a practical safety measure — reduced urine is a sepsis warning sign.

Poor intake and reduced urine output are recognised sepsis risk features (NICE NG253)
🍯
Self-Care for Acute Cough
Target: symptom relief while the cough runs its 3–4-week course
Mechanism

Viral bronchitis inflames the airway epithelium; cough continues while it repairs, often for 3 to 4 weeks. Honey soothes the cough reflex in adults and children over 1 year. Some over-the-counter cough medicines (for example, those containing pelargonium, guaifenesin or dextromethorphan) have limited evidence, as NICE NG120 notes.

Practical

Honey in warm water; no honey under 1 year (infant botulism). Explain that cough medicines are unlikely to help much and are not suitable for young children. Pharmacist advice is appropriate. Back-up prescription only where NG120 criteria are met.

NICE NG120: self-care and no antibiotic for people not systemically very unwell or at higher risk of complications
🚭
Smoking Cessation
Target: stop completely; offer referral at every contact
Mechanism

Smoking impairs mucociliary clearance and alveolar macrophage function, raising the risk of pneumonia and pneumococcal disease. In COPD, stopping smoking is the only intervention shown to slow the rate of lung function decline. A recent infection is a "teachable moment" when motivation is higher.

Practical

Very brief advice (Ask–Advise–Act), then referral to local stop-smoking services: behavioural support plus varenicline, combination NRT or a nicotine vape, per NICE NG209. Brian is an ex-smoker — confirm he has stayed stopped and check household exposure.

NICE NG209: behavioural support plus pharmacotherapy gives the best quit rates
💉
Vaccination
Target: flu annually; pneumococcal; COVID and RSV per current eligibility
Mechanism

Influenza is a major trigger for secondary bacterial pneumonia and COPD exacerbation; pneumococcus is the commonest bacterial cause of CAP. Vaccination reduces infection and severity in older and at-risk people.

Practical

Check the record after recovery: seasonal flu; pneumococcal vaccine (the UK programme has replaced PPV23 with PCV20 for adults 65+ and at-risk groups, 2025–26 roll-out); COVID-19 per the current seasonal offer; RSV for eligible older adults. Follow the UKHSA Green Book for timing and eligibility — these change each season.

UKHSA Green Book: annual flu and one-off pneumococcal vaccination for those aged 65+ and clinical risk groups
🫁
COPD Self-Management and Pulmonary Rehabilitation
Target: written plan; rehab offered after hospitalised exacerbation
Mechanism

Early recognition of an exacerbation (more breathless, sputum volume or colour change) and early treatment shorten recovery. Pulmonary rehabilitation improves exercise capacity and quality of life and reduces readmission after an exacerbation.

Practical

NICE NG115: individualised self-management plan; consider a rescue pack of oral corticosteroids and antibiotics for people who understand how to use it, with advice to tell the practice when they start it. Offer pulmonary rehabilitation within 4 weeks of discharge after a hospitalised exacerbation. Check inhaler technique at every review.

NICE NG115: pulmonary rehabilitation for people recently hospitalised with an exacerbation
🧍
Graded Return to Activity
Target: gradual return over weeks; expect fatigue up to 3 months after pneumonia
Mechanism

Older adults lose muscle mass rapidly during acute illness and bed rest, increasing falls and loss of independence. Gradual mobilisation preserves function; overexertion in the first weeks worsens fatigue.

Practical

Short walks, sitting out of bed, building daily. Community physiotherapy or reablement after discharge for frail patients. Explain the NG250 timeline so a lingering cough at week 4 is not read as treatment failure — while making clear what would be (new fever, worsening breathlessness).

NICE NG250: tell people how long symptoms are expected to last after pneumonia

Common prescribing errors in chest infection
  • Oral antibiotic and home for a patient who meets sepsis high-risk criteria — the error this case is built around
  • Routine 7-day (or longer) courses for adult CAP where NG250 recommends 5 days
  • Antibiotics for acute bronchitis in a well patient "to be safe" — no benefit, real harm
  • Clarithromycin prescribed with simvastatin or atorvastatin without pausing the statin; or with other QT-prolonging drugs
  • Doxycycline in pregnancy or in children under 12
  • Fluoroquinolones used as routine alternatives — MHRA Drug Safety Update (January 2024) restricts systemic fluoroquinolones to situations where other recommended antibiotics are inappropriate
Point-of-care CRP — how to use it (NICE NG250)
  • Use it only when, after clinical assessment, pneumonia has not been diagnosed and it is not clear whether to prescribe
  • CRP <20 mg/L: do not routinely offer an antibiotic
  • CRP 20–100 mg/L: consider a back-up (delayed) prescription
  • CRP >100 mg/L: offer an antibiotic
  • CRP never overrides severity — a septic patient is admitted whatever the number, and Brian does not need a finger-prick test to decide

7G — Psychosocial impact: family, independence, work and driving
🫂
Chest infections — the older man who "went downhill fast", the carer who feels guilty, and the worker who wants antibiotics so they can get back to work
An episode of pneumonia in an older person is often the moment a family first sees their parent as frail. Recovery takes weeks to months, confidence and independence can take a knock, and a hospital admission can be frightening. At the other end of the spectrum, the working adult with a viral cough often wants antibiotics because they cannot afford more time off. Both sit behind the clinical question and both shape whether the plan is accepted.
👪
The Carer's Guilt and Fear

Brian's daughter wonders whether she should have come sooner, and the confusion reminds her of her mother's last illness. Unaddressed, guilt turns into anger or resistance to the plan. Naming it and absolving her is part of the treatment.

Offer her a role: go with him, bring his medicines and his COPD plan, tell the ward what he is normally like — her description of his baseline helps staff judge delirium.

"You haven't left this too late — you noticed something changed and you acted. That's exactly what we need families to do. The most helpful thing now is to go with him and tell them what he's normally like."
🏠
Hospital Aversion and Independence

"He hates hospitals" usually means fear of not coming out, loss of control, or a bad previous experience. Exploring it briefly lets you address the real fear. Hospital-at-home and virtual wards are genuine options for selected moderate cases (NG250 lists them for CRB65 1) — but not for a septic, hypoxic patient.

After discharge, a short period of reablement or community therapy protects independence.

"What worries him most about hospital? … Going in today is about getting him well enough to come home sooner, not about taking his independence away."
🧠
Delirium and Recovery of Cognition

New confusion in infection is delirium until proven otherwise. It usually improves as the infection is treated, but can take days to weeks to settle in older adults, and a delirium episode can unmask an underlying cognitive decline. Families find this distressing and need it explained.

At follow-up, check whether cognition has returned to baseline; if not, consider a cognitive assessment once fully recovered.

"The muddled thinking is the infection affecting his brain. It usually clears as he gets better, though it can take a little while. If he's still not himself once he's over this, we'll look into it properly."
💼
Work, Fit Notes and the Antibiotic Request

The working adult with bronchitis often asks for antibiotics because they need to be back at work. Acknowledge the pressure honestly: antibiotics will not shorten the cough meaningfully. People can self-certify for the first 7 days of absence; a fit note can follow if needed. Masking and staying off while febrile reduces spread to colleagues.

"I know you need to get back to work. The honest answer is antibiotics won't get you back any quicker for a cough like this — rest, fluids and paracetamol will. If you're off for longer than a week, I can do a fit note."
🚗
Driving

There is no specific DVLA notification requirement for a chest infection or pneumonia. The general duty applies: do not drive while feeling unwell, feverish, excessively fatigued or drowsy, or while confused. Driving safety is also relevant after delirium — advise not driving until fully back to normal. Recurrent hypoxia or cognitive impairment would raise separate DVLA questions.

"Please don't drive until you feel completely back to normal — being unwell and tired slows your reactions, and that's the same as driving over the limit in terms of risk."
🪞
Identity, Frailty and Future Planning

For many older people a pneumonia admission is a turning point. It is an appropriate moment, once recovered, to review COPD care, medicines, falls risk and — where the patient wishes — future care preferences. Framed positively, this is about staying well at home rather than planning for decline.

"Now you're through it, let's make sure you're as well protected as possible — vaccines, your inhalers, and a plan for what to do early if your chest flares up again."

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — sepsis / severe pneumonia
"If at any point you become very breathless, can't finish a sentence, become confused or very drowsy, your lips look blue, your skin looks mottled or very pale, or you haven't passed urine all day — call 999. Don't wait to see if it settles, and don't wait for the surgery to open."
These are the lay equivalents of the NICE NG253 high-risk sepsis features. Giving them in plain words, and saying "999" rather than "come back", is what makes the safety-net usable at 2 am.
💊 Treated at home — not improving
"You should feel a bit better within two or three days of starting the antibiotic, even though the cough will last weeks. If you're no better by then, or worse at any time, contact us the same day so we can check you again — and sooner if you're getting more breathless."
Failure to improve at 48–72 hours is the trigger for reassessment of diagnosis, organism and place of care. Stating the time frame turns a vague "come back if worse" into a measurable plan.
🟠 Viral cough — no or back-up antibiotic
"This cough usually lasts about three weeks. Use the back-up prescription only if you get a high temperature that won't settle, you feel much more unwell, or you're getting breathless. 💊 2 — Treat & follow up

Bronchitis: usually viral — no/delayed antibiotic + safety-net. CAP: severity-guided antibiotics per NICE NG250 (amoxicillin 500 mg TDS × 5 days first-line low-severity; doxycycline or clarithromycin if penicillin-allergic; POC CRP <20 / 20–100 / >100 mg/L where the decision is unclear); admit if severe/septic. COPD exacerbation: bronchodilators ± steroids ± antibiotics (purulence); rescue pack; controlled O2. Persistent/non-resolving (smoker 40+, haemoptysis, weight loss) → CXR + NG12 ± TB. Resolution CXR in smokers/over-50s; vaccination & cessation; protect the vulnerable.
