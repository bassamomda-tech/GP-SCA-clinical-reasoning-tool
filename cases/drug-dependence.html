📋 Clinical Stem — Drug Dependence
A 41-year-old woman on long-term codeine for back pain, now taking far more than prescribed and buying extra online, frightened she's "addicted" and that the doctor will stop her tablets
Dawn Fletcher, 41, has taken codeine for chronic low back pain for several years. She now takes well above the prescribed dose, has started buying extra co-codamol online, and gets anxious, sweaty and unwell if she runs out — taking more for the withdrawal than the pain. She feels ashamed, is frightened she's "an addict", and is terrified the doctor will simply stop her tablets and leave her in pain and withdrawal. Her mood is low, she sleeps poorly, and she also drinks a bottle of wine most evenings. She has two school-age children. She has come, hesitantly, because a friend nearly died of an overdose and it scared her.
This stem tests the ability to: recognise iatrogenic opioid dependence (escalating dose, supplementing from non-prescribed sources, withdrawal symptoms, taking the drug for withdrawal rather than analgesia) sensitively and without stigma; to distinguish dependence from addiction and to use non-judgmental, person-first language; to assess the whole person (mood/self-harm, co-use of alcohol — relevant to overdose risk and to abrupt-withdrawal seizure risk, blood-borne virus and social/safeguarding assessment given dependent children); to plan a SUPPORTED, GRADUAL withdrawal rather than abrupt stopping, with shared decision-making and the door kept open; to provide harm reduction (overdose/naloxone advice, especially with alcohol co-use), and to involve specialist drug services; and to address the chronic pain appropriately (NICE NG59 advises not offering opioids for chronic low back pain). The SCA challenge is engaging a frightened, ashamed patient, reassuring her she won't be abandoned or simply "cut off", and converting fear into a collaborative plan.
Scenario A — Prescribed opioid dependence (this stem) Iatrogenic; escalating + non-prescribed supplementation; withdrawal-driven use; alcohol co-use; dependent children. Non-judgmental assessment; supported gradual taper; harm reduction/naloxone; treat mood/pain; safeguarding; specialist support.
Scenario B — Benzodiazepine/z-drug or gabapentinoid dependence Long-term use, tolerance, withdrawal. Gradual tapered withdrawal (never abrupt — seizure risk for benzodiazepines); avoid co-prescribing with opioids.
Scenario C — Illicit opioid use / OST Heroin use, injecting. Harm reduction (needle exchange, naloxone, BBV testing/vaccination), opioid substitution (methadone/buprenorphine), specialist drug service.
Scenario D — Overdose / emergency Reduced consciousness, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression → 999 + naloxone + airway/breathing support; alcohol/benzodiazepine co-ingestion worsens risk.
Scenario E — Stimulants / polydrug / pregnancy Cocaine/amphetamine (cardiac/mental-health risks), polydrug use, or dependence in pregnancy — specialist, multidisciplinary, never abrupt withdrawal.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Shame, fear of abandonment, and the trap of iatrogenic dependence

Drug dependence — especially when it began with a doctor's prescription — is steeped in shame, secrecy and a fear of being judged or "cut off". That fear is dangerous: it drives concealment, supplementation from unsafe sources, and avoidance of help. The single most powerful clinical move is non-judgmental, person-first engagement that distinguishes dependence from moral failing, reassures the patient they won't be abandoned, and builds a collaborative, harm-reduction-informed plan. Relapse is part of recovery, not a reason to withdraw care.

🙇 Reducing shame

Reframe dependence accurately and kindly.

"This isn't a moral failing — your body has become dependent on a medicine you were prescribed. That happens to lots of people, and we can help you through it."
🤝 "I won't cut you off"

Directly address the abandonment fear.

"I'm not going to stop your tablets suddenly and leave you struggling. We'll reduce slowly, together, at a pace you can manage — and I'll keep supporting you whatever happens."
🛟 Harm reduction first

Safety doesn't depend on stopping.

"While we work on this, let's keep you safe — I'd like to talk about overdose, especially mixing with alcohol, and give you naloxone, a medicine that can reverse an overdose."
👪 The children & the future

Frame help as protective, sensitively.

"Getting you well is good for you and your kids. I may need to involve some support around the family — not to take anything away, but to make sure everyone's okay and you've got help."

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Dawn expects to be judged, cut off and reported — name that fear, share your reasoning, then agree a plan she can live with
1
Validate — name the fear and the courage

Dawn has rehearsed the worst outcome: the tablets stopped today, a label of "addict" on her record, and social services at the door. She also half-hopes for the opposite — that the doctor will simply prescribe the extra so she no longer has to buy it online. Naming both expectations out loud, before any plan, stops her bracing for a fight and signals that you are on her side.

"I think you came in expecting me to stop your tablets, maybe tell you off, and perhaps involve social services. None of that is how this works. You've done exactly the right thing by coming, and I want to help you get back in control — safely."
2
Explain — why slow, why one supply, why safety first

She needs three pieces of reasoning. First, stopping suddenly would make her unwell and push her back to buying online, so reduction is gradual. Second, buying co-codamol online is dangerous for reasons she may not know: the paracetamol in every tablet can silently damage the liver, especially with wine, and counterfeit tablets can contain far stronger synthetic opioids. Third, the codeine is not treating her back pain well — NICE NG59 advises against opioids for chronic low back pain — so the aim is better pain control by other means, not simply less medication.

"Your body has got used to codeine, so stopping suddenly would make you feel awful — we won't do that. What worries me more is the online tablets: each one has paracetamol, which can hurt your liver, especially with wine, and you can't be sure what's really in them. So step one is getting everything from one safe supply, then bringing it down slowly."
3
Negotiate — give her something concrete today

She should leave with: an agreed prescribed dose from one prescriber, dispensed in small instalments; a take-home naloxone kit and a plan for who knows where it is; blood tests (LFTs, BBV offer); a referral or self-referral route to the local drug and alcohol service; a first step on the alcohol; help for her mood and sleep; and a review within a week. Safeguarding is discussed openly as support for the family, not as a threat.

"Here's what I suggest for today: I'll prescribe what you need from one place, a week at a time, so you don't need to buy any online. I'll give you naloxone to keep at home. I'll check your liver, and put you in touch with the drug and alcohol team — they're supportive, not punitive. Then I'll see you next week and we'll plan the first small reduction together. How does that sound?"
Key principle: Engagement is the treatment. People are far safer in treatment than out of it, so the first consultation succeeds if Dawn comes back next week — not if her dose falls today. Refusing all prescribing and refusing to acknowledge her online supply are both unsafe: the first pushes her further into unregulated tablets, the second leaves the paracetamol and overdose risks unmanaged. Do not, however, simply prescribe whatever she reports taking — verify, cap at safe limits, and involve the specialist service when intake is high or control is lost.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Most of what keeps Dawn alive and gets her well is not a prescription. Harm reduction (naloxone, never mixing, never using alone), structured psychosocial support (NICE CG51 for drug misuse; NHS Talking Therapies for mood), a safe alcohol plan, and an active, exercise-led approach to her back pain (NICE NG59) do the heavy lifting. The taper works only when these are in place.
🧯
Take-Home Naloxone & an Overdose Plan
Target: kit at home today; household trained
Mechanism

Naloxone is a competitive opioid antagonist that displaces opioids from receptors and restores breathing within minutes. It is shorter-acting than most opioids, so re-sedation can occur — the person must never be left alone and 999 is always called.

Practical

Supply an intramuscular (Prenoxad) or intranasal (Nyxoid) kit with training — for Dawn and for whoever is at home. Drug services have been able to supply naloxone without a prescription since 2015, extended to all formulations including nasal by the Human Medicines (Amendment) Regulations 2019; 2024 regulations widened supply to more services and professionals. Replace kits used, lost or out of date.

OHID/Orange Book: offer take-home naloxone to everyone at risk of opioid overdose and their families

Never Mix, Never Alone, Know Your Supply
Target: no alcohol within hours of opioid doses; no online tablets
Mechanism

Opioids, alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs and gabapentinoids act synergistically on respiratory drive; most drug-poisoning deaths involve more than one depressant. Unregulated tablets may contain nitazenes, which can be many times more potent than the drug they imitate, and may need repeated naloxone doses.

Practical

Agree specific rules: no wine on the same evening as codeine while the alcohol plan starts; no tablets from anyone else; tell someone at home when she takes her dose; keep the naloxone visible. If she does use from an unknown source — a small test amount, never alone. Store all medicines locked away from the children.

OHID National Patient Safety Alert (NatPSA/2023/009/OHID, July 2023): potent synthetic opioids implicated in heroin overdoses and deaths — ensure naloxone access and overdose awareness

Alcohol — Assess, Then Reduce Safely
Target: AUDIT + SADQ now; towards ≤14 units/week
Mechanism

About 9–10 units nightly adds to opioid respiratory depression, worsens sleep architecture and mood, and increases paracetamol hepatotoxicity. If she is alcohol-dependent, abrupt cessation risks withdrawal seizures — the same "never stop suddenly" rule applies as for benzodiazepines.

Practical

AUDIT; if dependence is possible, SADQ and withdrawal history. Non-dependent harmful drinking → brief intervention and a stepped reduction with drink-free days. Dependent → planned assisted withdrawal with the specialist service (NICE CG115 thresholds, as in the alcohol protocol) with thiamine. Tackle alcohol alongside the opioid, not "later".

NICE CG115: brief intervention for harmful drinking; assessment and assisted withdrawal for dependence

Active Back-Pain Self-Management
Target: exercise programme + pacing; pain education
Mechanism

Long-term opioids give little functional benefit in chronic low back pain and can cause opioid-induced hyperalgesia; as the dose falls, pain often stays the same or improves. Graded activity rebuilds tolerance to movement and reduces fear-avoidance.

Practical

Group or individual exercise programme via physiotherapy; manual therapy only as part of a package with exercise; support return to work. NICE withdrew its NG59 psychological therapy and combined physical–psychological programme recommendations in July 2026 — her mood and distress are supported through NHS Talking Therapies in their own right. Resources such as Live Well with Pain run alongside the taper.

NICE NG59: exercise first; do not offer opioids for chronic low back pain

Psychosocial Support, Mood & Sleep
Target: keyworker or regular GP contact; PHQ-9/GAD-7 tracked
Mechanism

Low mood, anxiety and insomnia are both causes and consequences of opioid and alcohol use, and withdrawal amplifies them temporarily. Motivational interviewing strengthens her own reasons for change; structured support improves retention and reduces relapse.

Practical

NHS Talking Therapies (self-referral) for depression/anxiety; CBT-I principles for sleep rather than a hypnotic; drug and alcohol service keyworking; mutual aid (SMART Recovery, Narcotics Anonymous) offered with facilitated access. Contingency management and behavioural couples therapy are CG51 options within specialist services.

NICE CG51: offer brief motivational interventions and facilitate access to mutual aid; structured psychosocial interventions in specialist care

Family, Recovery Capital & Early Help
Target: support plan for the household within weeks
Mechanism

Stable housing, income, relationships and meaningful activity ("recovery capital") predict sustained recovery. Parental substance use affects children through inconsistent care, secrecy and household risk — support for the parent is support for the children.

Practical

Explore who else is at home and who can help; offer early help or family support services; school nurse or health visitor liaison where appropriate; social prescribing for debt, benefits and activity. Follow local safeguarding procedures if there is risk of significant harm — openly, with Dawn wherever safe to do so.

7D — Prescribing guide: Orange Book 2017 · NICE TA114 / CG52 / TA115 · NG215 · Opioids Aware
The right prescribing plan depends on which dependence this is. Prescribed or over-the-counter opioid dependence without loss of control can often be managed in primary care with a stabilise-then-taper plan (NICE NG215; Opioids Aware). Loss of control, high intake, illicit use or injecting needs the specialist drug service, where opioid substitution treatment (OST) with methadone or buprenorphine (NICE TA114) is the evidence-based option. The UK clinical guidelines ("Orange Book", 2017) remain the national reference for drug-misuse prescribing; check for local shared-care arrangements before initiating anything.
Step 1 — Stabilise (Dawn, week 0–2)

One prescriber · one pharmacy · a verified, capped daily dose

  • Calculate the real daily codeine and paracetamol intake (prescribed + bought), and her oral morphine equivalent
  • Never prescribe paracetamol above the BNF maximum; codeine's BNF maximum is 240 mg/day — if her true intake exceeds what can be prescribed safely, do not try to match it: seek drug-service advice (buprenorphine substitution is a specialist option for codeine dependence)
  • Consider switching co-codamol to a codeine-only preparation so the taper does not drag paracetamol with it; dose per BNF
  • Dispense in instalments (weekly, or more often if risk is high); no early scripts; record an agreement
Stabilising first removes the online supply, the paracetamol excess and the chaos — the taper then starts from a known, honest baseline.
Step 2 — Taper (weeks to months)

About 10% of the original dose every 2–4 weeks; smaller steps at the end

  • Agreed with Dawn; she sets the pace (NICE NG215: shared decision, patient-led, flexible)
  • Hold at a step for a hard week or life event — do not reverse more than one step
  • Pre-warn: withdrawal (sweats, yawning, cramps, diarrhoea, restlessness, anxiety) peaks within days of a cut and settles — it is not the back pain returning
  • Symptomatic relief per BNF (e.g. loperamide for diarrhoea, an antiemetic); avoid adding benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids
  • Review at least every 2–4 weeks; weekly at first
Months, not weeks, is success. The opioid-prescribing protocol sets the same 10%-every-2–4-weeks pace, within the Opioids Aware range of 5–10% every 2–6 weeks.
Step 3 — Escalate to the drug service when…

Loss of control, high intake, or the taper keeps failing

  • Continued online or illicit buying despite a prescribed supply
  • Intake above safe prescribable limits; injecting; heroin or other illicit opioids
  • Repeated failed tapers; polydrug use; pregnancy; significant mental illness
  • Specialist options: OST maintenance with buprenorphine or methadone (TA114), a planned detox (CG52), then relapse prevention with naltrexone for the motivated and opioid-free (TA115)
Maintenance OST is a legitimate long-term treatment, not a failure. Stay involved as her GP throughout.

7G — Psychosocial impact: work, driving, family, identity
🫂

Dependence rarely looks like the stereotype. Dawn is a working mother whose dependence began with a legitimate prescription. What she fears most is not withdrawal but exposure: being labelled, losing her children, losing her licence and her job. 👪
Children and Safeguarding

Parental substance use is a recognised risk factor, but most parents in treatment keep their children. Safeguarding is proportionate: it asks whether the children are safe and supported now, and what help the family needs. Early help and honest partnership are the usual route; a referral to children's social care is made when there is reasonable concern of significant harm, following local procedures and Working Together to Safeguard Children (2026).

Practical risks to address today: medicines stored locked away; who looks after the children if she is unwell; no driving them while impaired.

"Coming for help is the most protective thing you could do for your children. My job is to make sure you and they have support. If I ever need to involve anyone else, I'll tell you first and explain why."
🚗
Driving and DVLA

Driving while impaired by any drug, prescribed or not, is an offence; the drug-driving law gives a medical defence only if a prescribed medicine is taken as directed and the driver is not impaired. Persistent misuse of or dependence on opioids and other drugs is a notifiable condition for DVLA purposes (non-prescribed use, or doses above BNF limits, count as misuse or dependence for licensing): the patient must tell the DVLA, and the licence is usually refused or revoked for a minimum period of stability (longer for Group 2 lorry and bus licences). People fully compliant with a supervised methadone or buprenorphine programme may be licensed after favourable assessment, usually with annual review.

"I need to talk about driving. Please don't drive on any day you feel drowsy or have been drinking. Because of the dependence, the DVLA rules may also apply to you — let's look at them together so you know where you stand."
💼
Work, Money and the Online Habit

Buying co-codamol online costs money and time and carries the constant fear of being found out. Sick days for withdrawal or hangovers may be putting her job at risk. A fit note can say "may be fit for work" with adjustments during a difficult phase; the reason can be worded with her agreement. Debt advice and benefits checks via social prescribing reduce pressure that drives relapse.

"How has work been through all this? If there's a hard patch while we reduce, I can help with a fit note — and we can agree together what it says."
🪞
Identity, Shame and Stigma

"Addict" is a word Dawn applies to herself as a verdict. Person-first language (a person with dependence, not "an addict"), accurate explanation of iatrogenic dependence, and acknowledgement of the courage it took to attend reduce shame — and shame reduction measurably improves engagement. Coding and records should be accurate but not pejorative.

"You're not 'an addict' — you're someone whose body has become dependent on a medicine that was prescribed for you. That's common, and it's treatable."
🍷
Relationships and the Evening Wine

The nightly bottle may be her only "switch-off", or a shared habit with a partner. Cutting down can strain the relationship if the partner is not on board. Inviting a trusted person into the plan (with consent) helps with naloxone, supervision of medicines and support on hard evenings. Screen sensitively for domestic abuse, which is common and changes the plan.

"Is there someone at home you trust who could know about the plan? It helps to have someone who knows where the naloxone is and can support you on tough evenings."
🔁
Relapse, Hope and Keeping the Door Open

Relapse is common and is part of recovery, not a reason to stop care. The dangerous moment after a lapse is shame-driven disappearance — or returning to an old dose after a break, when tolerance has fallen. An explicit agreement that she can come back at any point, without judgement, is a safety intervention in its own right.

"If you slip up — buy tablets again, or have a bad week — please come and tell me. You won't be in trouble, and the plan won't be torn up. We'll just adjust it."

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — overdose
"If you — or anyone — can't be woken, is breathing very slowly or making snoring or gurgling sounds, or has tiny pupils or blue lips, that's an overdose. Call 999 straight away, give the naloxone, put them on their side, and stay with them — the naloxone can wear off before the drugs do. Make sure someone at home knows where the kit is."
Most opioid overdoses happen with someone nearby. Naloxone plus a 999 call turns a witnessed overdose into a survivable one. Documenting the kit supplied, the training given and who else was trained is both good care and good medicolegal practice.
💊 Same day — paracetamol, seizures, crisis
"If you've taken more co-codamol than the packet allows, even spread over a day or two, please tell us the same day or go to A&E — paracetamol can damage your liver before you feel ill. If you ever have a fit, severe confusion or shaking after cutting down alcohol, call 999. And if you feel you can't keep yourself safe, call us, NHS 111 (mental health option) or 999."
Staggered paracetamol overdose is easy to miss because the patient feels well for the first day or two. Alcohol withdrawal seizures and suicidal crises are the other same-day dangers in Dawn's profile.
🟠 Come back soon — the taper is slipping
"If you find yourself buying tablets again, running out early, or drinking more to cope, come back and tell me — you won't be in trouble. And if you ever have a break from codeine, even a few days, don't go back to your old amount: your body can't handle it any more."
Shame-driven disappearance after a lapse is the commonest way engagement is lost. The lost-tolerance warning targets the highest-risk moment for overdose: resuming a previous dose after a break.
