📋 Clinical Stem — Multimorbidity & Polypharmacy
An 82-year-old woman on 14 medicines, increasingly frail with falls and dizziness, brought by her daughter for a "medication review" — who quietly says she'd rather take fewer tablets
Edith Hargreaves, 82, attends with her daughter for a medication review. She takes 14 medicines for hypertension, type 2 diabetes, atrial fibrillation, osteoporosis, depression, an overactive bladder, reflux, osteoarthritis and high cholesterol. Over the past months she has become more frail, with two falls, light-headedness on standing, daytime drowsiness and some new forgetfulness. Her HbA1c is "very well controlled" and her blood pressure runs low (she feels dizzy). She finds the tablets a burden, doesn't always take them all, and tells you quietly she'd "rather not be on so many". Her daughter worries about the falls and the forgetfulness. Edith's priority, she says, is to "stay on my feet and keep my marbles".
This stem tests the ability to conduct a person-centred structured medication review in multimorbidity (NICE NG56): starting with "what matters to you" (Edith wants to avoid falls and preserve cognition, and take fewer tablets); weighing benefit vs treatment burden and harm for each medicine in THIS frail, older person; identifying the medicines contributing to her presentation — postural hypotension from over-treated BP, hypoglycaemia risk from over-tight diabetes control, anticholinergic burden (the bladder antimuscarinic ± antidepressant) driving falls/confusion, and the time-to-benefit problem with preventives (statin, bisphosphonate) in frailty; recognising high-risk combinations and monitoring needs (anticoagulant for AF — stroke prevention to keep); and deprescribing deliberately, one change at a time, by shared decision, with monitoring and safety-net. The SCA challenge is balancing safe deprescribing with not stopping the medicines that genuinely matter (e.g. anticoagulation), and doing it as a collaboration with Edith and her daughter.

Scenario B — Conflicting guidelines Single-disease guidelines pulling in opposite directions (e.g. NSAID for OA vs CKD/AF/anticoagulation). Individualise; prioritise what matters; avoid harm.

💭 Ideas
"What do you feel the tablets are doing for you — which ones do you value, and which feel pointless?"
Edith has views on her own medicines and has informally stopped some. Surfacing this respects her expertise in her own life, reveals adherence reality, and identifies the medicines she'd most welcome stopping — a collaborative starting point.
😟 Concerns
"What worries you most — the falls, your memory, or the number of tablets?"
Her concerns (and her daughter's about falls/memory) align with the clinical priorities. Naming them lets you target the review at what's frightening them and frame deprescribing as the solution, not a loss.
🎯 Expectations
"What would a good outcome from today look like for you?"
She wants fewer tablets and to stay well on her feet and mentally sharp. Naming this lets you agree a realistic, staged plan (a few changes now, review, more later) rather than overhauling everything at once.
1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The person behind the pill list — burden, autonomy and "more is not better"

Polypharmacy accumulates condition by condition, guideline by guideline, until an older person is carrying a daily load of tablets, monitoring and appointments that can itself cause falls, confusion, hospital admissions and misery — while some of those medicines no longer help, or never could in the time she has left. The most valuable thing a GP can do is step back, ask what matters to the person, weigh each medicine's benefit against its burden and harm in their life now, and deprescribe deliberately and collaboratively — recognising that, in multimorbidity and frailty, fewer, well-chosen medicines is often better care.

⚖️ Fewer can be better

Reframe deprescribing as good care, not giving up.

"Taking fewer tablets isn't us giving up on you — for some of these, stopping them will actually help your balance and your mind, which is exactly what you want."
🤝 Her expertise

Treat her as the expert in her own life.

"You know your body and your days better than anyone — tell me which tablets feel worth it and which don't, and we'll start there together."
🛡️ Keeping what matters

Reassure that the important ones stay.

"Some medicines are genuinely protecting you — like the blood-thinner for your heart rhythm, which prevents strokes — so we'll keep those. It's the ones causing more harm than good we'll tackle."
👪 Involving the daughter

Include the carer with Edith's consent.

"With your agreement, it helps to have your daughter involved — she sees the falls and can help keep an eye as we make changes."

Understanding Edith's day-to-day life tells you which medicines she actually takes, which ones she can safely manage, who notices when something goes wrong, and what "success" means to her. 🧓
Independence & living at home

Edith lives at home with some help. Two falls and a fear of a third threaten the thing she values most: staying in her own house. Ask who helps with shopping, cleaning and personal care, whether she has a pendant alarm, and whether she has had a "long lie" on the floor after a fall. A long lie is a marker of high risk and changes the urgency of the falls referral.

"If you did fall again, how would you get help — could you get up on your own, or reach a phone or an alarm?"
👩‍👧
The daughter as carer

Her daughter is anxious and increasingly involved. She may be taking on medicines support, lifts to appointments and night-time worry. Carers are entitled to a carer's assessment from the local authority (Care Act 2014), and the practice should code her as a carer. Involve her with Edith's consent, but keep Edith at the centre. The daughter's view ("Mum should take everything the doctor gave her") may be different from Edith's ("I'd rather take fewer").

"How are you coping yourself? Looking after someone you love is a lot — there's support available for you too."
💊
How the medicines are actually taken

Ask Edith to bring every box and bottle, including over-the-counter products and herbal remedies (a "brown bag" review). Many older people keep old stock, take ibuprofen from the supermarket on top of prescribed naproxen, or use St John's wort or ginkgo, which interact with anticoagulants and antidepressants. Check who fills any dosette box, whether she can open blister packs and bottles, and whether poor eyesight or arthritis in her hands gets in the way.

"Could you show me how you take them in a normal day — morning to bedtime? And is there anything you buy yourself from the chemist or health shop?"
🧠
Fear of dementia & loss of self

"Keep my marbles" is a statement of fear. New forgetfulness in someone on an antimuscarinic may be partly reversible once the drug is stopped. That is a hopeful message, but don't over-promise: also plan a proper cognitive assessment once the medicines have been rationalised. Ask directly what she fears. Many older people have watched a spouse or friend decline with dementia.

"You mentioned your memory — is there something in particular you're worried it might be?"
⚖️
Autonomy, capacity & future wishes

Edith has capacity to make her own decisions; assume capacity unless there is evidence otherwise (Mental Capacity Act 2005). Her wish to take fewer tablets is legitimate even if a guideline would add more. This is also a natural time to ask whether she has thought about a Lasting Power of Attorney or advance care planning, especially if her cognition is changing. Raise it gently and don't force it into this appointment.

"Some people like to plan ahead and write down what matters to them for the future — is that something you'd like to talk about, now or another time?"
😔
Mood, loneliness & meaning

She is on an antidepressant. Is her mood now good, and was it prescribed after a bereavement years ago? Loneliness and low mood reduce motivation for exercise and self-care and increase falls risk. Social prescribing (lunch clubs, befriending, community strength and balance classes) can treat several problems at once, and is often more welcome than another tablet.

"How's your mood been lately — and how much do you get out and see people these days?"

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Edith wants "fewer tablets" and her daughter wants "no more falls". Both are right, and both can be met with a staged plan they agree to
1
Validate — name both expectations out loud

Edith expects to come away on fewer tablets. She may also secretly expect to be told off for missing doses. Her daughter may expect more to be done: a scan, a referral or a new tablet for memory. Say both expectations back to them before explaining anything. This stops either of them feeling overruled and shows you have heard the quiet comment Edith made about wanting fewer tablets.

"Edith, I heard you say you'd rather be on fewer tablets. That's a completely reasonable thing to want, and I think we can do it. And I can see you're worried about the falls and her memory," (to the daughter) "which is exactly what I want to tackle too."
2
Explain — link the tablets to the symptoms

Explain in plain language that some of her current medicines are probably contributing to the dizziness, falls, drowsiness and forgetfulness. Her blood pressure is now lower than it needs to be at 82, especially on standing. Her sugar is tighter than is safe and the gliclazide can cause lows. The bladder tablet (oxybutynin) is known to affect memory and balance in older people. So in her case, fewer tablets is not a compromise. It is the treatment. Also explain that some tablets are genuinely protective and will stay, especially the blood thinner for her heart rhythm.

"Some of your tablets were right when they were started, but your body has changed since then. A few are now pushing your blood pressure and sugar too low, and one of them can make memory and balance worse. Easing those should help the very things you're worried about. Others, like your blood thinner, are protecting you from a stroke, so those stay."
3
Negotiate — agree the first one or two changes today

Don't overhaul 14 medicines in one appointment. Agree the first one or two changes, the ones with the most harm and least benefit (e.g. stop oxybutynin and stop gliclazide, or stop naproxen). Book the lying and standing BP recheck and bloods. Give a written list of what has changed and what to watch for, and set a date to decide on the next step. Explain that the plan will continue over several reviews. This matches her wish for fewer tablets, deals with the daughter's safety concern, and keeps each change traceable.

"Today let's stop two tablets, the bladder one and the sugar tablet that can cause lows. I'll write down exactly what's changed. In two to four weeks we'll check your blood pressure lying and standing, see how you feel, and then decide on the next one or two together."
Key principle: NICE NG56 frames the review around the person's goals and a shared plan, and NICE NG197 (shared decision making, 2021) asks us to discuss benefits, risks and the option of doing nothing in terms the person understands. Edith's expectation (fewer tablets) and the clinical priorities (fewer drug-related falls and less confusion) point the same way. Say this explicitly. Patients often assume that deprescribing is about cost or about "giving up on them".

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored target
Deprescribing works best alongside measures that treat the same problems without a tablet. NICE NG249 (falls, 2025) recommends a multifactorial falls assessment and intervention for older people who have fallen, including strength and balance exercise, home hazard assessment and a medication review focused on falls-risk medicines. Non-drug measures also stop symptoms returning once a medicine is withdrawn (e.g. bladder training after stopping oxybutynin).
🏋️
Strength & balance programme
Target: progressive programme, ideally supervised, sustained for months
Mechanism

Sarcopenia and poor balance are core parts of frailty. Progressive resistance and balance training improve lower-limb strength, gait speed and the ability to recover from a trip. This is the intervention with the strongest evidence for preventing falls in community-dwelling older people.

Practical for Edith

Refer to the community falls service or physiotherapy (NICE NG249). Many areas run evidence-based programmes such as FaME or Otago. Programmes need to be progressive and sustained; one-off advice leaflets don't prevent falls. Encourage walking with a suitable aid, and teach her how to get up from the floor.

Reduces falls; also improves confidence, mood and independence
🧍
Postural hypotension measures
Target: no symptomatic drop on standing
Mechanism

Ageing blunts the baroreflex. Vasodilators, diuretics and dehydration reduce preload. Together these cause a drop in cerebral perfusion when she stands up. Counter-manoeuvres and adequate fluids help the body cope while the antihypertensives are being reduced.

Practical for Edith

Sit on the edge of the bed for a minute before standing. Clench calves and buttocks before walking. Avoid hot baths and large carbohydrate meals before activity. Maintain fluid intake unless she has a fluid restriction. Get up at night with a light on. Review whether the diuretic is making her rush to the toilet at night, a double hazard alongside her overactive bladder.

Cuts dizziness while antihypertensives are being reduced
🚽
Bladder training & continence advice
Target: bladder training for at least 6 weeks (NICE NG123)
Mechanism

Bladder training gradually lengthens the intervals between voids and retrains detrusor control. Stopping the anticholinergic is safer if the symptoms it treated are managed another way. Otherwise urgency returns and she rushes to the toilet, which is itself a common cause of falls.

Practical for Edith

NICE NG123 recommends bladder training for at least 6 weeks as first line for overactive bladder. Advise moderating caffeine and fizzy drinks, avoiding large volumes of fluid late in the evening, and treating constipation. Consider a continence service referral, a commode or urinal at night, and a clear, lit route to the toilet.

Allows the oxybutynin to be stopped without urgency and falls returning
🏡
Home hazard assessment (OT)
Target: home assessment and modifications
Mechanism

Environmental hazards (rugs, poor lighting, stairs, a bath without rails) combine with drug effects to cause falls. An occupational therapist identifies and changes these and assesses her function and safety.

Practical for Edith

OT home visit, grab rails, night lights, a raised toilet seat if needed, and a pendant or telecare alarm (especially if she has had a long lie). Check vision (cataracts, varifocals on stairs) and footwear. These are simple, low-cost measures that she and her daughter usually welcome.

Reduces falls in people at high risk when combined with other measures
🍲
Nutrition, hydration & bone health
Target: stable weight; adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D
Mechanism

Weight loss and low protein intake worsen sarcopenia and frailty. Poor intake also changes drug handling: renal function falls, creatinine drops with muscle loss (so eGFR looks falsely reassuring), and hypoglycaemia risk rises. Dehydration drives postural hypotension and AKI.

Practical for Edith

Weigh her at each review (weight is also needed for Cockcroft–Gault). Screen for malnutrition, e.g. with MUST. Encourage regular meals with protein. If her dietary calcium is adequate, a vitamin D supplement alone may replace a large chewable calcium/vitamin D tablet she dislikes. Check with bone-health guidance, as she is on a bisphosphonate.

Supports strength and makes renal dosing more accurate
🤝
Social prescribing & carer support
Target: one social contact or activity she values each week
Mechanism

Loneliness, low mood and inactivity feed frailty. A social prescribing link worker can connect Edith to community groups, exercise classes and befriending. They can also help her daughter access a carer's assessment and respite.

Practical for Edith

Refer to the PCN social prescribing link worker. Code the daughter as a carer and signpost her to the local carers' centre and a Care Act 2014 carer's assessment. If mood stays low once medicines are adjusted, psychological therapy (NHS Talking Therapies is open to older adults) may be more acceptable than an increased antidepressant dose.

Addresses mood, isolation and carer strain, which are the drivers of burden

7G — Psychosocial impact: independence, driving, family, identity and care settings
🫂
Multimorbidity: when looking after your health becomes a part-time job
Fourteen medicines, several clinics, blood tests, eye screening, foot checks and the nurse's diabetes review add up to a "treatment burden" that takes up time, energy and dignity. NICE NG56 asks us to reduce that burden where we can. The impact reaches into how Edith sees herself, how she gets about, and what her daughter's life now looks like.
🚗
Driving & the DVLA

Ask whether Edith still drives. Drivers must renew their licence at 70 and every 3 years after that, declaring fitness themselves. Falls from postural hypotension, hypoglycaemia on a sulfonylurea, sedation, and cognitive change can all affect safe driving. Follow DVLA "Assessing fitness to drive" guidance on hypoglycaemia, and remind her that it is illegal to drive if impaired by medicines. Stopping gliclazide and the anticholinergic may make driving safer. If cognition is a concern, discuss it openly and document.

"Do you still drive? Some of these tablets can affect alertness and blood sugar, so I want to make sure you're safe behind the wheel."
👩‍👧
The daughter-carer relationship

The daughter's anxiety may lead her to take over ("I'll do Mum's tablets now"). This can protect Edith but can also erode her autonomy. Agree roles with Edith's consent. Offer the daughter a copy of the medicines plan and a named contact. Signpost a carer's assessment (Care Act 2014) and local carers' support. Carer burnout is a common reason older people move into care homes.

"Would it help if I wrote the changes down for both of you, so you're working from the same list?"
🪪
Identity: "patient" or person?

Being defined by a pill list is demoralising. Edith was a mother, a worker and a neighbour before she was "on 14 medicines". Framing the review around what she wants to do (gardening, the grandchildren, church, getting to the shops) restores agency and gives practical measures of success that mean more than a blood pressure reading.

"What would you like to be able to do in six months' time that's hard at the moment?"
📦
Practical burden: packs, deliveries & appointments

Multi-compartment compliance aids ("blister packs") are not a default solution. NICE NG67 (medicines support in social care) and the Royal Pharmaceutical Society prefer original packs with a proper assessment of need, because compliance aids can't hold some medicines and hide which tablet is which. Consider synchronising repeat dates, once-daily regimens, a community pharmacy New Medicine Service or delivery, and combining reviews into one longer appointment instead of several short ones.

"Would it be easier if all your tablets were due on the same day each month, and we did your reviews together in one visit?"
🏥
If Edith moves into a care home

A move to a care home is a high-risk transition for medication errors. NICE SC1 (managing medicines in care homes) covers medicines reconciliation on admission, care-home medication reviews, homely remedies and covert administration (which needs a best-interests decision under the Mental Capacity Act). Enhanced Health in Care Homes arrangements provide a named clinical lead and structured medication reviews for residents.

"If you ever need more support at home or move somewhere with care, we'll make sure your medicines list goes with you and is checked."
🕰️

Talking about time-to-benefit means gently acknowledging that time is limited. Many older people welcome this honesty: "I don't want pills for 10 years from now, I want to enjoy now." Others want everything continued. Both are valid choices under NICE NG197. Record her preferences so future clinicians don't restart what she chose to stop.

"Some tablets work over many years and others help you now. Which matters more to you at this stage of life?"

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases, marking rows and danger zones

⚠ Three scenario-specific safety-nets: use these (almost) verbatim

🔴 Emergency — head injury on a blood thinner, or signs of bleeding
"Because you're on a blood thinner, if you fall and hit your head, even if you feel fine, you need to be seen at A&E the same day, and tell them you take apixaban. Also call 999 or go to A&E if you vomit blood, pass black or bloody stools, or suddenly get a bad headache, weakness in an arm or leg, or trouble speaking."
Anticoagulated older people with head injuries need prompt assessment. NICE NG232 (head injury, 2023) advises considering a CT head scan within 8 hours for people on anticoagulants even when they have no other indication for imaging, so same-day emergency assessment is the right safety-net. The falls are why this safety-net matters so much for Edith. Document it at every review.
💊 After stopping or reducing medicines
"Over the next few weeks, if you get very thirsty or are passing a lot of water, your bladder becomes much harder to control, your heartburn comes back and doesn't settle, or your mood dips, ring the surgery. None of these is an emergency, but it tells us whether you still need that medicine. Please don't restart any old tablets from the drawer without checking with us first."
Each withdrawn medicine has a predictable "return of symptoms" pattern. Giving this in writing, and telling the pharmacy, prevents unsupervised restarting of old stock. Ask the family to return unused medicines to the pharmacy.
🟠 Intercurrent illness (sick days) and new falls
"If you have vomiting, diarrhoea or a fever and can't keep fluids down, pause the ramipril, the water tablet and the metformin (I've marked them on your list) and ring us. Restart them once you've been eating and drinking normally for a day or two. Keep taking your blood thinner. If you have another fall or feel faint, let us know the same week, even if you're not hurt."
Sick-day guidance (Think Kidneys) reduces AKI risk from ACE inhibitors, diuretics and metformin. A clearly marked written list avoids the dangerous mistake of stopping the anticoagulant. Recurrent falls change the priorities of the plan.

🧭 1 — Approach (NG56)

Start with "what matters to you". Structured medication review per drug: indication valid? effective? wanted? safe (interactions/monitoring/renal)? duplicated? evidence in THIS person? 📉 2 — Deprescribe & keep

Target: falls/confusion drivers (over-treated BP/postural drop, ACB, sedatives, hypo risk), high-risk combos (triple whammy → AKI; opioid+benzo), low-value preventives. Relax over-tight HbA1c/BP. Keep high-value (anticoagulation in AF). How: shared decision, one change at a time, taper where needed (steroids/benzos/beta-blockers), monitor, simplify, safety-net, review; coordinate (pharmacist, falls, frailty MDT, continuity GP).
