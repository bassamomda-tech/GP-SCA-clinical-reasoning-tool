📋 Clinical Stem — Glaucoma
A 62-year-old man told by his optician he has "raised pressure and possible glaucoma", anxious about going blind, who has heard nothing since and isn't sure he needs the drops
Samuel Boateng, 62, attends after a routine eye test where the optician said he had "high pressure in the eyes and signs of early glaucoma" and referred him to the eye clinic. He has noticed no change in his vision and feels well. His father went blind "from his eyes" in old age. He is African-Caribbean, short-sighted, and has type 2 diabetes. He is worried about losing his sight but also unconvinced he needs treatment "for something that isn't bothering me", and he is a taxi driver concerned about his licence. He asks what glaucoma actually is, whether he'll go blind, and whether he really has to use eye drops for the rest of his life.
This stem tests the ability to: explain chronic open-angle glaucoma and ocular hypertension in plain language — a silent, usually asymptomatic disease detected on screening, diagnosed on the triad of intraocular pressure, optic disc and visual fields; recognise the patient's risk factors (age, family history, African-Caribbean ethnicity, myopia, diabetes); understand the GP's role (supporting the ophthalmology-led pathway, reinforcing adherence, recognising the acute angle-closure emergency, family screening advice and DVLA implications); address the adherence challenge of a silent disease and the "why treat something I can't feel" belief; and handle the patient's fear of blindness and his driving/licensing concerns. It also tests recognition of acute angle-closure glaucoma as a distinct, sight-threatening emergency.
Scenario A — Chronic open-angle glaucoma / OHT (this stem) Asymptomatic, found on optometry; risk factors present. Explain the disease and the triad; support ophthalmology-led treatment (prostaglandin drops/SLT); reinforce adherence; family screening; DVLA.
Scenario B — Acute angle-closure glaucoma (EMERGENCY) Sudden severe painful red eye, blurred vision with haloes, headache, nausea/vomiting, fixed mid-dilated pupil, hard eye. Same-day emergency ophthalmology; reduce IOP; do not delay.
Scenario C — Normal-tension glaucoma Glaucomatous disc/field changes with IOP in the "normal" range; still needs ophthalmology and IOP lowering; consider vascular factors.
Scenario D — Drug-precipitated angle-closure risk Predisposed (hypermetropic, shallow chamber) patient started on a pupil-dilating drug (some antidepressants/antihistamines/antimuscarinics) → acute closure. Recognise and warn.
Scenario E — Poor adherence / progressing disease Known POAG with worsening fields due to missed drops or side-effect intolerance. Explore barriers; simplify regimen; ophthalmology review; consider SLT/surgery.
Key variables to adapt for Open-angle (silent, chronic) vs angle-closure (acute emergency) vs normal-tension; risk factors (age, family history, ethnicity, myopia/hypermetropia, diabetes); the diagnostic triad (IOP, disc, fields); first-line treatment (prostaglandin drops / SLT); adherence to a silent disease; precipitating drugs; DVLA/occupation; family screening; pregnancy/comorbidity drug cautions (beta-blocker drops in asthma).

💭 Ideas
"What's your understanding of what glaucoma is?"
Samuel may think glaucoma always causes symptoms, or conflate it with his father's blindness. Establishing his model lets you correct misconceptions, explain the silent nature, and connect treatment to protecting his remaining sight — the foundation for adherence.
😟 Concerns
"Is it the worry about going blind like your father — or the driving — that's on your mind most?"
His twin concerns — blindness and his taxi licence — are both legitimate. Addressing the fear honestly (most treated glaucoma does NOT lead to blindness) and being straight about the DVLA process respects him and improves engagement.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Treating a disease you can't feel — and the fear of a parent's blindness

Glaucoma asks something difficult of patients: use eye drops every day, for life, for a condition that causes no symptoms — to prevent a loss they cannot yet perceive. That paradox, plus the emotional weight of a parent who went blind and the practical fear for a driving livelihood, sits at the heart of this consultation. The clinical task is less about prescribing (ophthalmology leads that) and more about understanding: making the silent disease real, framing the drops as protecting the sight he has, and being honest and supportive about blindness risk and the DVLA.

👁️ Fear of blindness

Honest reassurance: detected and treated glaucoma usually preserves useful vision for life; blindness mainly occurs when it's caught late or untreated.

"I understand the fear, especially with your dad. The good news is that because yours has been caught early and we treat it, most people keep good vision for the rest of their life. The danger is mainly when it's missed or left untreated."
💧 Why treat the symptomless

Reframe the drops as protecting existing vision, not curing a symptom.

"The damage glaucoma does can't be reversed — so the whole point is to protect the vision you have now, before you'd ever notice a problem. That's why the drops matter even though nothing feels wrong."
🚕 The driving conversation

Be straight about DVLA notification and the field standard, framed supportively.

"Because you drive for a living, we do need to be open about the DVLA — if glaucoma affects both eyes you have to tell them, and there's a vision standard. Many people with treated glaucoma keep driving; let's find out exactly where you stand."
👨‍👩‍👧 Family screening

Empower him to protect his relatives.

"Because glaucoma runs in families, it's worth telling your brothers, sisters and children to get their eyes checked — picking it up early in them is the best protection, just like it was for you."
1E — Psychosocial context: the person behind the pressure reading
🫂
Samuel — a driver whose living depends on his eyes, a son who watched his father go blind, a man asked to treat something he cannot feel

Before any plan is made, find out what Samuel's life looks like and what this diagnosis threatens. The same optometry letter means something very different to a retired man who rarely drives and to a self-employed taxi driver with a licence to protect. These questions are not "extra" — each one changes what you say later about driving, drops, cost and family.

🚕
Work and income

Is he self-employed or on an app platform? How many hours a day, and does he drive at night? Taxi and private hire licences are issued by the local licensing authority, and most authorities apply the DVLA Group 2 medical standard to their drivers (Department for Transport best-practice guidance). A glaucoma diagnosis may therefore mean a medical declaration to the council as well as any DVLA duty. Knowing this early lets you plan the conversation instead of springing it on him at the end.

"Tell me a bit about the taxi work — are you licensed with the council, and do they ask you for a medical?"
👴
His father's story

What does he actually know about his father's blindness? Was it glaucoma that was never treated, or diagnosed late? Many people in Samuel's position assume glaucoma always ends in blindness because that is the only version they have seen. The story is also a lever: it explains his fear, and it is a powerful reason to take the drops once he sees that his father probably did not have the chance of early detection that he now has.

"You mentioned your dad lost his sight. Do you know what happened — was it picked up, was he treated?"
🧠
Health beliefs and trust

"It isn't bothering me" is a reasonable belief for most of medicine — pain and symptoms are how people judge illness. Explore where his scepticism comes from: past experiences with the NHS, friends who "used drops and went blind anyway", beliefs about long-term medicines, or concern about side effects. Some Black African and Caribbean patients report mistrust rooted in real experiences of healthcare; name it without assuming it.

"Some people feel uneasy about using a medicine every day for life. What are your thoughts about that?"
🩸
Living with diabetes already

He already takes metformin, attends diabetic eye screening and has a diabetes review. Adding drops and a new clinic can feel like "one more thing". Ask how he manages his current medicines and appointments — that routine is the scaffold you will attach the drops to. Diabetic eye screening photographs look at the retina for diabetic retinopathy; it is not a glaucoma check, and he needs to understand that the glaucoma clinic is separate.

"How do you get on with your diabetes tablets and check-ups? We might be able to fit the drops into the same routine."
👨‍👩‍👧‍👦
Family around him

Who lives with him, and who might help with drops if dexterity or memory become an issue later? Does he have brothers, sisters or adult children aged 40 or over? They are entitled to a free NHS sight test because of his diagnosis, and telling them is one of the most useful things he can do. Some patients feel guilty about "passing it on" — frame it as protection, not blame.

"Do you have brothers, sisters or grown-up children? Glaucoma runs in families, and there's something simple we can do to protect them."
😟
Mood and worry

A threat to sight and livelihood at the same time can cause significant anxiety, poor sleep and low mood — and some people cope by avoidance, which in glaucoma means missed appointments. Ask how he has been since the optician's visit. Anxiety that is acknowledged and addressed is far less likely to turn into non-attendance.

"Since the optician told you, how has it been playing on your mind? Some people find it's all they can think about."

🚩 Two diseases sharing a name

"Glaucoma" spans a silent chronic disease that you treat for decades to protect vision, and an acute emergency that blinds within hours. The discipline is to know which you're dealing with and to safety-net the other. For Samuel, the diagnosis is the chronic open-angle spectrum (POAG/OHT) found on screening, in a high-risk man — needing ophthalmology-led IOP-lowering treatment, lifelong adherence, monitoring, family screening and DVLA attention — while he leaves knowing the warning signs of the acute, painful type.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Samuel half-hopes to be told he doesn't need treatment — acknowledge that, share the reasoning, then agree a plan he owns
1
Validate — name the expectation

Samuel's expectation is mixed: he wants to understand the diagnosis, he fears blindness, and part of him wants permission to skip lifelong drops for an eye that feels normal. He may also expect the GP to "sort out" the eye problem today. Name the ambivalence out loud; people are more willing to reconsider a belief once it has been heard rather than corrected.

"It sounds like part of you is frightened about your sight, and part of you is thinking — my eyes feel fine, why would I use drops every day for the rest of my life? That's a completely reasonable question, and I'd rather we talk it through than you just take my word for it."
2
Explain — the silent disease and the evidence

The key fact is that glaucoma damage is painless, affects side vision first and is irreversible — so the only time to protect sight is before the patient notices a problem. Lowering the pressure slows damage: in large trials, treating raised pressure roughly halved the chance of developing glaucoma over five years (OHTS), and each 1 mmHg of pressure lowering reduced the risk of progression by about 10% (EMGT). The eye clinic, not the GP, confirms the diagnosis and chooses treatment — often a one-off laser rather than drops.

"Glaucoma steals side vision so gradually that the brain fills in the gaps — by the time people notice, a lot has gone, and it doesn't come back. Treatment is about protecting what you've got. And it may not even be drops: the eye clinic now usually offers a quick laser first, which for many people means no drops for years."
3
Negotiate — offer something concrete today

He should leave with: confirmation that the referral has been received (and what to do if he has not heard within a few weeks), a clear picture of what the clinic will do, honest information about driving and his taxi licence, the free sight test message for his family, and the acute angle-closure warning signs. Agree who does what. If he later decides against treatment, respect it — but make sure the eye team knows and that pressure checks continue.

"Here's what I suggest: I'll check your referral has reached the eye clinic today. When they see you, they'll confirm whether it's glaucoma or just high pressure and talk to you about laser or drops. Whatever you decide with them, I'll help you make it work around your driving. Does that sound fair?"
Key principle: In glaucoma the GP rarely starts or changes IOP-lowering treatment — NICE NG81 places diagnosis and treatment decisions with eye services. The GP's leverage is understanding: a patient who understands why the disease is silent, and who feels his driving and family concerns were taken seriously, is the patient who turns up to clinic and uses the drops.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
No diet or lifestyle change is proven to stop glaucoma — lowering IOP is the only treatment shown to slow it. Non-drug management here means the laser that NICE now puts first, the practical skills that make drops work, and the systems that stop people being lost to follow-up. Most of it is where the GP and community pharmacist add real value.
🔦
Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT)
Target: offered first-line by eye services (NICE NG81, 2022)
Mechanism

A low-energy laser is applied 360° around the trabecular meshwork — the eye's drainage channel. It triggers a healing response that improves aqueous outflow and lowers IOP without cutting or scarring. It is done in clinic under anaesthetic drops in a few minutes.

Evidence

In the UK LiGHT trial (Lancet 2019), SLT first-line gave target IOP comparable to drops, 74.2% of patients were drop-free at 3 years, and fewer needed glaucoma surgery. NICE NG81 (January 2022 update) found SLT very likely to be more cost-effective than drops and now recommends offering it as initial treatment for newly diagnosed COAG and for OHT with IOP ≥24 mmHg.

For Samuel

Removes the daily-adherence problem that worries him most. Tell him (as NG81 advises) that SLT may delay or reduce, but not remove, the need for drops, and may need repeating. Drops may be used while he waits for SLT. After laser, don't drive until vision is clear — the eye team will advise.

Adherence built in — one procedure, not daily drops
💧
Drop technique & punctal occlusion
Target: one drop in, eye closed, inner corner pressed ~2 min
Mechanism

A single drop is more than the eye can hold; the excess drains through the tear duct into the nose, where it is absorbed into the bloodstream without first-pass metabolism. Closing the eye and pressing on the inner corner (punctal occlusion) keeps more drug in the eye and reduces systemic absorption — which matters most for beta-blocker drops.

Evidence

Studies watching patients instil drops consistently find that many miss the eye, touch the bottle tip on the eye or lashes, or use several drops at once. NICE NG81 recommends checking drop technique as part of support for adherence.

For Samuel

Ask him to show you (or the pharmacist) with a bottle of artificial tears. Wash hands, tilt head back, pull lower lid into a pocket, one drop without touching, close eye, press inner corner for about two minutes, wipe excess. If more than one drop type: at least five minutes apart.

More drug in the eye, less in the heart and lungs
⏰
Adherence systems
Target: drops on every scheduled day; bottles collected on time
Mechanism

Glaucoma drops give no feedback: missing them feels exactly the same as taking them. Habits are built by attaching the drop to an existing daily cue, not by willpower. Many patients also stop because of side effects they never report.

Evidence

Poor adherence and non-persistence with glaucoma drops are common and are associated with progression. NICE NG81 advises taking account of factors affecting adherence (for example cognitive impairment and side effects) and offering preservative-free drops to people with preservative allergy or symptomatic ocular surface disease.

For Samuel

Evening prostaglandin drops linked to his evening metformin or brushing teeth; phone alarm; bottle kept by his toothbrush, not in the car. Ask non-judgementally how many days a week the drops go in. Drop aids from the pharmacist if dexterity changes. Check repeat intervals against expected use.

The single biggest modifiable driver of outcome on drops
📅
Never lost to follow-up
Target: every eye-service appointment attended or rebooked
Mechanism

Glaucoma progression is only visible on repeated fields, disc imaging and IOP measurement. A patient who misses reviews can lose field for years unnoticed, even if he is using drops.

Evidence

Delayed or cancelled follow-up in glaucoma has been recognised as a cause of avoidable sight loss in UK hospital eye services. NICE NG81 sets monitoring intervals by risk, and NICE QS180 (serious eye disorders) includes reassessment within the recommended interval.

For Samuel

Confirm today that the referral has arrived. Ask him to tell the practice if he has not heard within the timeframe the optometrist gave, or if an appointment is cancelled and not rebooked. If a clinic letter says "did not attend", contact him.

Monitoring is what detects progression before he does
👓
Regular sight tests & family screening
Target: relatives aged 40+ tested at the interval their optometrist advises
Mechanism

Early COAG has no symptoms, so it is found only by checking pressure, the optic disc and the visual field. First-degree relatives carry a higher risk, and the risk is greater still in people of Black African or Caribbean family origin, in whom glaucoma tends to start earlier and progress faster.

Evidence

In England, people aged 40 or over whose parent, sibling or child has glaucoma are entitled to a free NHS sight test; people with diabetes or glaucoma and everyone aged 60 or over are also eligible. In Scotland NHS eye examinations are free for everyone.

For Samuel

He is eligible himself (aged 62, diabetes, glaucoma). Suggest he tells his siblings and adult children aged 40+ to book, and to mention his diagnosis to the optometrist so pressure and field tests are included.

Cheapest glaucoma intervention in the whole pathway
🩺
General health & medicines review
Target: drug list checked at every review; diabetes managed
Mechanism

The GP controls several things that affect the eye indirectly: steroids by any route can raise IOP in susceptible people; beta-blocker drops interact with oral rate-limiting drugs; pupil-dilating and topiramate prescriptions matter for angle-closure risk; diabetes brings its own retinal and neovascular risks.

Evidence

BNF and SmPC cautions (topical beta-blockers, topiramate, corticosteroids). No lifestyle intervention (diet, supplements, exercise) has been shown to slow COAG, so avoid over-promising — general health advice is for general health.

For Samuel

Keep his diabetes reviews going; flag "glaucoma — on eye drops" prominently in the record so every prescriber sees it; ask the eye team before any steroid eye drops; list eye drops in his medication record (patients often forget them when asked).

Prevents GP-caused pressure rises and drug interactions
7D — Prescribing guide: the NICE NG81 (2022) treatment ladder — specialist-led, GP-continued
NICE NG81 (published 2017, last updated January 2022) changed first-line treatment from drops to laser. Diagnosis, the decision to treat and every treatment change belong to eye services (hospital or accredited community glaucoma service). The GP usually continues the prescription, recognises systemic harm, and should not start, stop or swap IOP-lowering drops without the eye team — except stopping immediately if a side effect is dangerous, and telling the eye team the same day.
Step 1 — 360° SLT (first-line)

Newly diagnosed COAG, or OHT with IOP ≥24 mmHg

  • NG81 (2022): offer 360° selective laser trabeculoplasty as initial treatment
  • Explain it can delay the need for drops and reduce, but not remove, the chance they are needed
  • Effect may wear off; a repeat procedure may be offered
  • Not suitable for everyone (for example some secondary glaucomas, or where the angle cannot be treated) — the eye team decides
OHT with IOP below 24 mmHg: not routinely treated — monitored by eye services at risk-based intervals (NG81).
Step 2 — Generic prostaglandin analogue

Latanoprost (generic) — dose per BNF, once daily in the evening

  • NG81: generic PGA while waiting for SLT, if SLT is unsuitable or declined, or if more IOP lowering is needed after SLT
  • Once daily — the simplest regimen; few systemic effects
  • Local effects: red eye, permanent iris darkening (mainly mixed-colour irises), longer darker lashes, periorbital skin darkening and fat loss
  • Preservative-free formulation if preservative allergy or significant ocular surface disease (NG81)
Warn about iris colour change before starting, especially if only one eye is treated.
Step 3 — Other classes / combinations

Beta-blocker, carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, alpha-2 agonist, or fixed combinations — dose per BNF

  • Used when PGA is not tolerated, not enough, or contraindicated
  • Timolol: contraindicated (SmPC) in asthma or a history of asthma, severe COPD, sinus bradycardia, sick sinus syndrome, second- or third-degree heart block without a pacemaker, overt heart failure
  • Fixed combinations reduce bottle numbers — several contain timolol without it being obvious from the brand name
  • Brimonidine: contraindicated under 2 years; not recommended under 12 (SmPC)
Always read the ingredients of combination drops in a wheezy or bradycardic patient.
Step 4 — Surgery (specialist decision)
  • Trabeculectomy, tube drainage devices or minimally invasive glaucoma surgery for advanced disease or progression despite treatment
  • Close post-operative follow-up by the eye team
  • A painful, red or sticky operated eye — even years later — needs same-day eye assessment: late bleb infection threatens sight
  • After surgery some patients need no drops in the operated eye; keep the medication record accurate from clinic letters
Acute angle closure — not this ladder (same-day emergency)
  • Phone the on-call ophthalmologist / eye casualty and arrange same-day assessment — do not wait and see, and do not book a routine referral; ED if eye services cannot be reached
  • While waiting: lie the patient flat, face up; give analgesia and an antiemetic; never use dilating drops
  • Pilocarpine drops and oral acetazolamide only on eye-casualty advice, if available and not contraindicated — doses per BNF; acetazolamide is a sulfonamide (check allergy, renal function, potassium)
  • Advise the patient not to drive; eye services then treat both eyes (laser iridotomy or lens extraction)

7G — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: work, driving, family & identity
🫂
Glaucoma — the driver who fears the letter from the DVLA, the son who fears his father's fate, the patient asked to trust a treatment he cannot feel
🚗
Driving and the DVLA

Car and motorcycle (Group 1) licence holders must tell the DVLA if they have glaucoma in both eyes, or in one eye if they only have sight in that eye or another condition affects the other eye; glaucoma in one eye alone need not be reported if they meet the eyesight standard. Bus and lorry (Group 2) drivers must report glaucoma even in one eye. The DVLA may arrange a binocular field test. The Group 1 field standard is a horizontal field of at least 120°, extending at least 50° left and right, with no significant defect within 20° of fixation above or below the horizontal meridian (DVLA Assessing fitness to drive). Many people with treated early glaucoma meet the standard.

The duty to notify is the driver's; the GP advises, documents, and follows GMC confidentiality guidance if a patient who is unsafe will not stop driving.

"It's your legal responsibility to tell the DVLA if both eyes are affected — the eye clinic will confirm that. They'll usually arrange a field test, and lots of people with early glaucoma pass it and keep driving."
🚕
Taxi licence and livelihood

Taxi and private hire licences come from the local council, and most councils apply the stricter DVLA Group 2 medical standard (Department for Transport guidance), so Samuel should check his licensing authority's medical requirements once the diagnosis is confirmed. Being honest now protects him: driving without declaring a notifiable condition can invalidate insurance. If he eventually cannot meet the standard, discuss income, Access to Work and benefits advice early rather than at crisis point.

"Because you're licensed as a taxi driver, the council may have its own medical rules as well as the DVLA. Once the eye clinic confirms things, let's check exactly what they need so there are no surprises."
👁️
Identity and fear of blindness

For many patients the word "glaucoma" means "going blind". Samuel's father makes that fear concrete. Honest reassurance is that most people whose glaucoma is found early and treated keep useful sight for life; sight loss is mostly seen when it is detected late, progresses despite treatment, or treatment and monitoring lapse. If sight does deteriorate later, low-vision services, the Certificate of Vision Impairment and charities such as Glaucoma UK and RNIB offer support.

"Most people in your position — found early, treated, and keeping their check-ups — keep good vision for the rest of their lives. The people who lose sight are mostly those who were never picked up, or who stop treatment."
👨‍👩‍👧
Family

Telling relatives can feel like spreading bad news. Frame it as something he can do for them: a free NHS sight test from age 40 for parents, siblings and children of someone with glaucoma (England; eye examinations are free for everyone in Scotland). African-Caribbean family origin raises their risk further, and glaucoma in Black patients often starts younger — so the message matters for his adult children.

"You can do for your children what nobody could do for your dad — get it picked up early. From 40 the test is free; ask them to tell the optician you have glaucoma."
💷
Cost and access

Cost is rarely a barrier for Samuel: in England prescriptions are free from age 60, and diabetes treated with medication qualifies for a medical exemption certificate. His sight tests are free on several grounds. Practical barriers matter more — time off work for clinic visits (lost fares), travel after dilating drops or laser, and fitting the drops around shifts.

"The drops won't cost you anything. What might be harder is getting to appointments without losing a day's fares — shall we think about how to plan those?"
🧍
Living with a lifelong label

Some people become hypervigilant, checking their vision constantly; others avoid thinking about it and drift from follow-up. Both are understandable. Offer information from reliable sources (NHS, Glaucoma UK), invite questions at future reviews, and look out for anxiety or low mood — particularly if fields worsen or driving is threatened.

"It's a lot to take in on one day. Glaucoma UK have good information and a helpline — and you can always bring questions back to me."

Missing the timolol in a combination drop→ New wheeze, COPD exacerbation, bradycardia or falls in a patient on glaucoma drops: read every bottle's ingredients; contact the eye team that week.
✗
Stopping or swapping drops without the eye team→ Stop immediately only if the side effect is dangerous, and tell the eye team the same day. Otherwise agree the alternative with them first.
✗
Getting the DVLA rule wrong→ Group 1: notify if both eyes affected (or one eye if it is their only seeing eye, or another condition affects the other eye). Group 2: notify even if one eye. Taxi licences: check the council — most apply Group 2 standards.
✗
Refusing anticholinergics to someone with treated open-angle glaucoma→ The warning is about angle closure. Take care in known narrow angles without laser; warn about symptoms.
✗
Forgetting topiramate→ Warn about sudden blurred vision or eye pain in the first weeks; if it happens, stop and send the same day; Yellow Card.
✗
Prescribing steroid eye drops for a red eye→ Can raise IOP and worsen herpes simplex keratitis — ophthalmology only.
✗
Never mentioning family→ "Relatives aged 40 and over can have a free NHS sight test — ask them to mention your glaucoma."

🚦 1 — Which glaucoma?
Acute angle-closure (EMERGENCY)

Sudden painful red eye, haloes, blurred vision, headache, vomiting, fixed mid-dilated pupil, hard eye → same-day emergency ophthalmology.

Chronic open-angle / OHT

Silent; found on optometry; diagnosed on triad (IOP + disc + fields). Risk: age, family history, African-Caribbean, myopia, diabetes. Routine ophthalmology pathway.

💧 2 — Manage

Lower IOP (ophthalmology-led): 360° SLT first-line for new COAG and OHT ≥24 mmHg (NG81, 2022); generic prostaglandin drops while waiting, if SLT unsuitable/declined, or for extra lowering; add other classes/surgery to reach target (avoid beta-blocker drops in asthma). GP role: adherence support (the key to outcome), DVLA notification + field standard, advise family screening, diabetes/medication review, avoid pupil-dilating precipitants in people with narrow angles (not an issue in treated open-angle glaucoma), safety-net the acute emergency.
