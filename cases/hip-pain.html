📋 Clinical Stem — Hip Pain
A 68-year-old woman with months of deep groin pain and a worsening limp, struggling on stairs and to put on her shoes, asking whether she "needs a new hip"
Margaret Doyle, 68, has 6 months of gradually worsening right groin pain that radiates to the front of her thigh and occasionally the knee. It is worse with walking and weight-bearing, eases with rest, and she now limps, struggles on stairs, and finds it hard to put on her right shoe and sock. She has short-lived morning stiffness. There is no history of trauma, no fever, no rest or night pain that wakes her, no weight loss, and she is otherwise well aside from well-controlled hypertension. She is frustrated at slowing down and asks directly whether she needs "a new hip". On examination she has an antalgic gait and restricted, painful internal rotation of the right hip.
This stem tests the ability to: localise hip pain by site (groin/anterior = true hip joint; lateral = GTPS; buttock = referred spine) and recognise the classic clinical picture of hip osteoarthritis — activity-related groin pain referring to thigh/knee, restricted painful internal rotation, functional difficulty (stairs, shoes/socks) — diagnosing it clinically per NICE NG226 without mandatory imaging; exclude the red flags (septic arthritis, fracture, malignancy, avascular necrosis, polymyalgia) by history and examination; remember that hip pathology refers to the knee; manage OA conservatively first (education, exercise/physiotherapy, weight, analgesia by the topical-first ladder) and explain the role and threshold for joint-replacement referral honestly; and address the patient's specific question about surgery with shared decision-making. The SCA challenge is meeting the "do I need a new hip?" question with a staged, evidence-based plan rather than a premature yes/no.
Scenario A — Hip osteoarthritis (this stem) Groin pain, restricted internal rotation, functional loss. Clinical diagnosis (NG226); conservative first (exercise/physio, weight, analgesia ladder); arthroplasty referral when QoL/function significantly limited despite optimised conservative care.
Scenario B — Greater trochanteric pain syndrome Lateral hip pain, tender over trochanter, worse lying on it/stairs, internal rotation preserved. Load management, physiotherapy, relative rest, ± injection; not the joint.
Scenario C — Referred from spine Buttock/posterior pain ± radiation, normal hip exam, lumbar signs. Treat as back/radicular pain; screen for cauda equina symptoms (emergency); examine the spine.
Scenario D — Red-flag hip Septic (hot, fever, won't move — emergency); fracture (fall, can't weight-bear, short/externally rotated leg); malignancy (night/rest pain, weight loss, cancer history → NG12); avascular necrosis (steroids/alcohol, groin pain, normal early X-ray → MRI).
Scenario E — Paediatric hip / inflammatory Child with limp (DDH, transient synovitis vs septic, Perthes', SUFE — knee/thigh pain); or inflammatory arthritis / PMR (bilateral girdle pain, stiffness, raised inflammatory markers).
Key variables to adapt for Pain location (groin/lateral/buttock); true joint vs soft tissue vs referred; OA clinical diagnosis; red flags (septic, fracture, cancer, AVN, PMR); referral to the knee; age (paediatric differentials, fragility fracture); function/QoL for arthroplasty threshold; analgesia ladder and comorbidity; the "do I need surgery?" question.

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 Independence, ageing, and the "new hip" question

For an older adult, hip pain is rarely just about the joint — it is about independence, the fear of "slowing down", and a worry about a big operation. Many arrive having already decided they either need, or dread, a hip replacement. The consultation succeeds by taking the functional loss seriously, giving an honest account of the staged pathway (effective conservative care first, surgery when justified), and anchoring decisions in what the person wants to keep doing — rather than in an X-ray.

🚶 Independence & goals

Anchor the plan in what she wants to keep doing.

"What matters is keeping you doing the things you value — walking the dog, the stairs, getting dressed easily. Let's aim the treatment at those, and measure progress by them."
🔧 The staged pathway

Explain conservative-first honestly.

"For most people we get good relief with exercises, managing weight and the right painkillers. If that isn't enough and it's really limiting your life, a hip replacement is a very effective operation — but it's a step we take when it's truly needed."
🏋️ Exercise isn't harmful

Counter the fear that movement damages the joint.

"It's natural to worry that using it makes it worse, but the right exercise actually strengthens the muscles around the joint and reduces pain — keeping moving is part of the treatment, not against it."
😟 Fear of surgery

Address worry about a big operation honestly.

"If we do get to a replacement, it's one of the most successful operations there is for pain and function — and you'd be fully involved in deciding when the time is right for you."

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
"Do I need a new hip?" — don't answer yes or no; answer with the pathway and when she would reach each step
1
Validate — name the expectation

Margaret has come expecting a referral for hip replacement, and possibly an X-ray "to see how bad it is". Her reasoning is sound: she has classic hip OA and real functional loss. Saying so first stops her feeling fobbed off when the plan starts with exercise rather than a surgeon.

"You've clearly thought about this, and you're right that this is arthritis in the hip joint. A replacement is a real option for you at some point, so it's a fair question. Let me explain how we decide when that point is."
2
Explain — the reasoning, in her terms

NICE NG226 says to consider referral for joint replacement when symptoms substantially affect quality of life and non-surgical management has been ineffective or is unsuitable. The decision is based on clinical assessment, not scoring tools or X-ray grade, and age, smoking, comorbidity and BMI are not reasons to exclude someone. NICE QS87 (statement 7) expects non-surgical core treatments for at least 3 months before any referral for consideration of joint surgery. The core treatments work for many people, and they also help whoever does go on to surgery, because stronger muscles make recovery easier.

"We decide on surgery by how much the hip is limiting your life once the simpler treatments have had a fair go, not by how the X-ray looks. The exercises aren't a hoop to jump through. They often help enough on their own, and if you do end up having the operation, being stronger beforehand makes the recovery quicker."
3
Negotiate — something concrete today, and a date

She leaves with physiotherapy (or self-referral details), a pain-relief plan, practical aids, and a booked review with a clear rule: if the hip is still substantially limiting her life despite this, the referral is made. NG226 itself sets no fixed trial period, but QS87 expects at least 3 months of core treatment first. If she already has severe limitation and non-surgical treatment clearly does not suit her, an earlier referral is a clinical judgement to document.

"Here's the deal I'd suggest: physio and exercises starting now, the right painkiller, and some help around the house. I'll see you again in about 6–8 weeks to see how it's going, and we'll make the decision at around three months: if it's still stopping you doing the things that matter, I'll refer you to the orthopaedic team, and you and the surgeon decide on the operation together. Does that feel fair?"
Key principle: The failure modes are opposites: "yes, I'll refer you" before any core treatment, or "you just have to live with it". Both lose marks and both let the patient down. The pathway answer ("not yet, and here's exactly what would make it yes") respects her question, is honest about surgery, and keeps her in charge of the timing.

7C — Non-medication management: the NG226 core — mechanism + evidence + target
📘 Education & self-management
Explain OA and its course; tailored advice; encourage activity and pacing; support devices (walking aid, raised seat/shoe-horn) and footwear where helpful. Reassure that exercise is protective, not harmful.
🏋️ Therapeutic exercise
The cornerstone. Muscle strengthening and aerobic exercise, ideally physiotherapy-guided; improves pain and function. Maintain long-term.
⚖️ Weight management
Where overweight, weight loss reduces load and improves symptoms; offer support.
NICE NG226 makes therapeutic exercise and, where relevant, weight management the core treatment for everyone with OA, whatever the joint, age or severity. Drugs are adjuncts to these, never a replacement. Manual therapy is only for hip or knee OA, and only alongside exercise. Walking aids support independent mobility. NG226 also lists treatments not to offer. Knowing that list saves patients money and false hope.
🏋️
Therapeutic Exercise (Core — NG226)
Target: most days, long term; physio-led programme to start
Mechanism

Strengthening the hip abductors, extensors and quadriceps improves joint control and shock absorption. Aerobic activity raises the pain threshold and improves general fitness, mood and sleep. Range-of-motion work slows the loss of internal rotation and flexion that makes shoes and socks so hard. Pain relief comes from better muscle function and less sensitisation, not from "repairing" cartilage.

Practical

Refer to MSK physiotherapy, or give self-referral details where available, for a tailored programme combining local strengthening with general aerobic exercise. Group programmes such as ESCAPE-pain (hip and knee) are available in many areas. Warn that exercise may ache at first and takes weeks to help. An ache that settles within a day is acceptable. Cycling, swimming and water-based exercise suit painful weight-bearing.

NICE NG226: offer tailored therapeutic exercise to all people with OA
⚖️
Weight Management
Target: if overweight, a goal she chooses; 10% likely better than 5%
Mechanism

Load across the hip during walking and stair-climbing is several times body weight, so modest weight loss cuts joint load disproportionately. Adipose tissue is also metabolically active and contributes to low-grade inflammation and pain sensitivity.

Practical

Weigh her and ask permission to discuss it. Advise that weight loss improves pain, function and quality of life. Help her choose a realistic goal, and signpost to local weight-management services (e.g. NHS Digital Weight Management Programme if eligible). Weight is also part of the pre-operative conversation, but it is never a gate to referral.

NICE NG226: any loss helps; 10% of body weight likely better than 5%
🦯
Walking Aids, Aids & Adaptations
Target: safe independent mobility at home and outside
Mechanism

A walking stick held in the hand opposite the painful hip shifts load away from the joint and reduces abductor effort through each stride. Long-handled shoe horns, sock aids, raised toilet seats and chair raisers reduce the deep hip flexion that OA makes painful. Rails reduce fall risk.

Practical

Show her how to use a stick in the opposite hand and set it to wrist height. Refer to occupational therapy or social care for a home assessment if stairs, bathing or dressing are difficult. Suitable cushioned footwear helps. For Margaret, a sock aid and long shoe horn address her most frustrating limitation today.

NICE NG226: consider walking aids for lower-limb OA
📘
Information & Self-Management
Target: she can explain her condition and her plan back to you
Mechanism

Beliefs drive behaviour. The "bone on bone, worn out" story leads to fear of movement, inactivity, weakness and more pain. An accurate account (OA is common, variable, not inevitably progressive, and movement helps) builds self-efficacy and makes the exercise plan more likely to stick.

Practical

Give tailored verbal and written information at diagnosis and at follow-up (e.g. NHS and Versus Arthritis hip OA resources). Agree functional goals. Explain pacing: spread activity through the day rather than "boom and bust". Explain that flares happen and are not signs of damage. Ask about sleep and mood, which amplify pain.

NICE QS87: tailored information and self-management support
🤲
Manual Therapy (Adjunct Only)
Target: only alongside an exercise programme
Mechanism

Mobilisation, manipulation and soft-tissue techniques can give short-term improvements in pain and range of movement. That can make it easier to engage with exercise, but it does not replace it.

Practical

If her physiotherapist offers hands-on treatment, it should sit within an active programme. Paying for repeated passive treatment on its own is not supported by the evidence. Explain this gently if she mentions private manipulation.

NICE NG226: consider manual therapy for hip/knee OA only with exercise
🚫
What Not to Spend Money On
Target: redirect effort to exercise and weight
Evidence

NG226 says do not offer glucosamine, intra-articular hyaluronan, acupuncture or dry needling, or electrotherapy (including TENS, ultrasound and interferential) for OA. Arthroscopic lavage and debridement are also not offered. Many patients have already bought supplements or a TENS machine.

Practical

Ask what she has tried and bought, without judgement. Explain the evidence, and suggest putting the money and effort towards something that works, such as a gym or pool membership, an exercise class or a good pair of shoes. If she finds something harmless helpful, she can keep it alongside, not instead of, the core treatment.

NICE NG226 "do not offer" list — a frequent SCA and review point
7D — Prescribing guide: NICE NG226 analgesia ladder (adjuncts to the core) · surgery
NICE NG226 (2022) positions every drug as an adjunct to exercise and weight management, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. For the knee, a topical NSAID is offered first. For the hip, NG226 says only to consider a topical NSAID. It is a deep joint, so many patients go on to an oral NSAID with a PPI. Paracetamol and weak opioids are not for routine use. Strong opioids are not offered for OA at all. Doses per BNF.
Step 1 — Topical NSAID (Consider for the Hip)

Ibuprofen or diclofenac gel — dose per BNF

  • NG226: offer for knee OA; consider for OA of other joints, including the hip
  • Minimal systemic absorption, so a sensible first try in an older patient with hypertension or other comorbidity
  • Apply over the groin/anterior hip regularly for a fair trial of 2–4 weeks, then judge it by function
  • If no useful effect, don't persist. Move to Step 2 if suitable
Be honest: evidence for topical NSAIDs is strongest for superficial joints such as the knee. Margaret should know it may not reach the hip well.
Step 2 — Oral NSAID + PPI

e.g. naproxen or ibuprofen, with omeprazole or lansoprazole — choice and dose per BNF

  • NG226: when topical treatment is ineffective or unsuitable, consider an oral NSAID at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time, and offer a PPI alongside
  • Take account of GI, liver, renal and cardiovascular risk, age, and current medication before starting
  • For Margaret: check her antihypertensives (ACEi/ARB + diuretic = "triple whammy"), baseline U&E/eGFR and BP; recheck BP at review
  • Intermittent use around flares or activity is often better than continuous use
Avoid or seek alternatives with previous GI bleed/active ulcer, heart failure, significantly reduced eGFR, or anticoagulation (see drug cards).
Step 3 — Intra-articular Corticosteroid

Usually image-guided for the hip — via MSK interface / orthopaedics / radiology

  • NG226: consider when other drug treatments are ineffective or unsuitable, or to support therapeutic exercise
  • Explain relief is short-term, typically 2–10 weeks (NG226). Use the window to exercise
  • Transient rise in blood glucose in diabetes; small infection risk
  • If replacement is likely soon, discuss timing with the orthopaedic team. Many units prefer no hip injection in the months before surgery (local policy)
Unlike the knee, the hip is not a joint GPs routinely inject blind — refer for a guided injection.
Paracetamol & Weak Opioids — Not Routinely (NICE NG226)
  • NG226: do not offer paracetamol or weak opioids routinely for OA, unless they are used infrequently for short-term pain relief and all other pharmacological treatments are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective
  • This is a change from older practice, when paracetamol was the default first step. The evidence showed little benefit for OA pain
  • If Margaret already takes paracetamol and finds it helps occasionally, discuss it rather than stopping it abruptly. It should not be the plan
  • Weak opioids (codeine, co-codamol) cause constipation, drowsiness, falls and dependence (MHRA Drug Safety Update, September 2020, on dependence and addiction) with limited benefit
Strong Opioids — Do Not Offer for OA (NICE NG226)
  • NG226: do not offer strong opioids to manage osteoarthritis. Risks outweigh benefits
  • If someone arrives already on regular opioids: review functional benefit. If it isn't documented, agree a slow, supported taper rather than another repeat
  • Where opioids are being prescribed for any reason, Opioids Aware (2026) advises ideally no more than 50 mg/day oral morphine equivalent, with specialist advice before exceeding 90 mg/day
  • When pain is uncontrolled despite optimised core treatment, the answer is referral (injection or surgical opinion), not escalating opioids

7G — Psychosocial impact: independence, driving, family & identity
🫂
Hip OA — the woman who is "slowing down" and afraid of what comes next

For Margaret the hip is a question about independence: can she still look after herself, her home and the people she cares for? The management plan works best when it is built around those things.

🏠
Independence & Home

Stairs, bathing, dressing and getting in and out of low chairs and cars are where hip OA bites first. Losing them feels like the start of losing independence. Practical aids and an occupational therapy assessment can restore a surprising amount quickly, and show her the plan is doing something today.

"Let's start with the things that frustrate you most at home. A sock aid, a long shoe horn and a raised seat can make a real difference this week while the exercises build up."
🚗
Driving and the DVLA

Hip OA itself rarely needs DVLA notification, but she must be able to control the car safely, including an emergency stop. After a hip replacement, the GOV.UK surgery-and-driving guidance says the DVLA needs to be told only if she is still unable to drive 3 months after the operation. She should also tell her insurer. The surgical team advise when she can return to driving. Many people resume at around 6 weeks, but it is individual.

"Right now, if you can get in and out and do an emergency stop comfortably, you're fine to drive. If you have the operation, the team will tell you when it's safe. You'd only need to tell the DVLA if you still couldn't drive three months afterwards."
👨‍👩‍👧
Family & Caring Roles

Many women of Margaret's age are carers, whether for a partner, grandchildren or a parent. Pain that limits lifting, walking or driving threatens that role and can bring guilt. Planning for any operation (who helps at home for the first weeks) is easier if it starts early. A carer's assessment may be needed if she is the main carer.

"Is there anyone who relies on you day to day? If we do head towards an operation, it helps to plan early for the first few weeks afterwards."
🪞
Identity & "Getting Old"

"Slowing down" often means feeling old before her time. Framing OA as a common, manageable condition where movement helps, rather than a sign of inevitable decline, protects mood and motivation. Low mood and poor sleep amplify pain, so ask about them and treat them in their own right.

"Arthritis in the hip is very common and it doesn't mean the rest of you is wearing out. Lots of people your age get back to walking the dog and gardening with the right plan, and some with a new hip."
😟
Fear of Surgery (and Fear of Being Refused)

Patients often hold two fears at once: dreading an operation, and fearing they will be told they are "too old" or "too heavy" for one. NG226 is clear that age, sex, smoking, comorbidity and BMI are not reasons to exclude someone from referral. They are factors in the risk conversation with the surgeon.

"Nobody will turn you down for a referral just because of your age or weight. Those are things you'd talk through with the surgeon when weighing up the risks, and the decision would be yours."
🤸
Falls, Confidence & Social World

A painful limp and fear of falling shrink a person's world: fewer trips out, less socialising, more isolation. Strength and balance work reduces falls as well as pain. Social prescribing, community exercise groups and ESCAPE-pain-type classes treat the loneliness as well as the joint.

"Have you found yourself going out less because of the hip? Some of the group exercise classes are as much about getting out and meeting people as the exercise. Would that appeal?"

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases + medico-legal rationale

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — fall or hot hip
"If you have a fall and then can't put weight on the leg, or the leg looks shorter or turned out, call 999 or go straight to A&E. Don't wait for us. The same goes if the hip suddenly becomes hot, swollen and extremely painful, especially with a fever or feeling shivery and unwell. That could be a fracture or an infection, and both need treating the same day."
Occult hip fracture after a fall and septic arthritis are the time-critical diagnoses hidden behind "my arthritic hip". NICE CG124 recommends MRI when a fracture is suspected despite negative X-rays, so a normal plain film does not end the assessment. Septic arthritis destroys a joint within days.
💊 Medication harms
"While you're on the anti-inflammatory, stop it and call us if you get indigestion or stomach pain, black or tarry stools, vomiting blood, swollen ankles or you pass much less urine. If you're ill with vomiting or diarrhoea, stop it until you're better."
GI bleeding and acute kidney injury are the preventable harms of NSAIDs in older people, especially with ACE inhibitors/ARBs and diuretics. A specific, rehearsed phrase makes it far more likely the patient acts.
🟠 Change in the pattern
"Arthritis pain usually comes with activity and eases with rest. If it changes, so it's there all the time, or it wakes you every night, or you lose weight without trying, or feel generally unwell, come back sooner. Those aren't typical of arthritis and I'd want to look again."
Rest and night pain, weight loss and systemic features suggest malignancy (bone metastasis, myeloma — NG12), avascular necrosis or inflammatory disease. The first consultation sets the threshold for return.

🛑 Declining strong painkillers
"Strong painkillers aren't recommended for arthritis now. They don't help people walk further and they cause falls. If we can't control it safely, the next step is the specialist, not a stronger tablet."
🏋️ Hurt isn't harm
"Exercise may ache a bit to begin with, but it isn't wearing the joint out. The stronger the muscles, the less strain on the hip."
📋 Safety-net
"If you fall and can't put weight on it, or the hip goes hot and swollen with a fever, that's the same day, via 999 or A&E. If the pain starts waking you every night or you lose weight, come back sooner."
Danger zones
Reflex X-ray "to see how bad it is"
Typical OA at 45+ is diagnosed clinically (NG226). X-ray grade correlates poorly with symptoms and doesn't decide referral.
Starting co-codamol as the first step
NG226: paracetamol and weak opioids not routinely. Core treatment first, then topical/oral NSAID + PPI.
Oral NSAID with no PPI and no BP/renal check
NG226: lowest dose, shortest time, with a PPI. Check the ACEi/ARB + diuretic combination in a hypertensive patient.
Refusing referral because of age, BMI or an Oxford score
NG226: refer on quality of life after non-surgical care; don't exclude for age, smoking, comorbidity or BMI; don't use scoring tools to decide.
Reassured by a normal X-ray after a fall
Suspected fracture with negative X-ray → MRI (CT if MRI not within 24 h) — NICE CG124.
"Wear and tear" with night pain and weight loss
Not OA until proven otherwise: imaging and bloods; myeloma bloods at 60+ with persistent bone pain or unexplained fracture (NG12).
Treating lateral pain as hip OA
Lateral, tender trochanter, preserved IR = GTPS: load management and physiotherapy, not joint replacement.

🚩 2 — Red flags · 💊 3 — Manage

Red flags: hot hip + fever (septic → admit); fall + can't weight-bear (fracture → X-ray ± MRI); rest/night pain + weight loss/cancer history (malignancy → NG12); steroids/alcohol + normal X-ray (AVN → MRI); girdle pain + stiffness + ↑ESR/CRP (PMR). OA management (NG226): education, therapeutic exercise (cornerstone), weight; topical NSAID considered first then oral NSAID at lowest dose for shortest time with a PPI (no routine paracetamol/weak opioids; no strong opioids or glucosamine). Arthroplasty by quality of life/function despite optimised care, not X-ray grade.
