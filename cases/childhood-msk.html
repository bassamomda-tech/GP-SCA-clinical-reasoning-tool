📋 Clinical Stem — Childhood MSK Presentations
A 6-year-old brought with months of intermittent leg pains that her mother has been told are "growing pains" — but who now has a morning limp, a swollen knee, and looks pale and tired
Maya Patel, 6, is brought by her mother with several months of intermittent leg pains. A previous clinician called them "growing pains". But over the past few weeks the pattern has changed: she now limps in the mornings, complains of a stiff, swollen right knee that's better as the day goes on, and has been more tired and pale, off her food. There's no clear injury. Her mother is reassured it's "just growing pains" but is worried by the limp and the tiredness. On examination Maya has a swollen, warm right knee with reduced movement, an antalgic morning gait, and looks pale with a couple of unexplained bruises. She is afebrile today.
This stem tests structured reasoning about childhood MSK complaints: recognising that this is NOT "growing pains" (which are bilateral, in the legs not joints, evening/night only, never cause a daytime limp or joint swelling, with a normal examination) but a pattern of persistent joint swelling with morning stiffness/gelling — suggestive of juvenile idiopathic arthritis — while ALSO recognising the systemic red flags (pallor, fatigue, anorexia, unexplained bruising) that mandate a very urgent FBC (NICE NG12: within 48 hours — arranged the same day here because of the cluster of features) and early paediatric involvement to exclude leukaemia; applying the pGALS screen and a focused joint examination; reasoning through the broader differential (septic arthritis, reactive arthritis, malignancy, mechanical/hypermobility, SUFE/Perthes' with the hip-refers-to-knee rule); and referring appropriately (paediatric rheumatology for JIA with uveitis screening; urgent paediatrics/haematology for the red flags). The SCA challenge is to gently dislodge the inherited "growing pains" reassurance and act on a changed, concerning pattern.
Scenario A — "Growing pains" that aren't (this stem) Persistent swollen joint + morning stiffness (JIA pattern) + systemic red flags (pallor/fatigue/bruising → leukaemia). pGALS; very urgent FBC (NG12 ≤48 h — same day here) + same-day paediatric discussion; paediatric rheumatology; don't accept "growing pains".
Scenario B — True growing pains Bilateral leg (not joint) pain, evening/night, normal by day, normal exam, no limp. Reassure, explain, safety-net; review if the pattern changes.
Scenario C — Septic arthritis (emergency) Hot, swollen joint, won't weight-bear, fever, unwell → same-day hospital assessment (aspiration and IV antibiotics are hospital decisions; Kocher criteria are a hospital teaching aid, not a reason to wait).
Scenario D — JIA Joint swelling/stiffness >6 weeks under 16; limp, gelling; refer paediatric rheumatology; uveitis screening; early treatment.
Scenario E — Mechanical / hypermobility / sports Activity-related aches, clicks, recurrent sprains (hypermobility — Beighton; physio/strengthening); acute sprain/apophysitis (Osgood-Schlatter)/fracture/growth-plate injury; SUFE/Perthes' (hip refers to knee).

1D — Psychosocial context
🫂 The reassuring label, the worried parent's instinct, and the cancer that hides in "aches"

"Growing pains" is one of the most over-applied reassurances in paediatrics — comforting, usually right, and occasionally a label that lets juvenile arthritis or childhood leukaemia hide in plain sight for months. The safe clinician treats every childhood MSK complaint as a question to be answered, not a label to be inherited: testing it against the specific benign pattern, screening the systemic red flags, examining properly (pGALS), and trusting a parent's instinct that "something has changed" — because in children, that instinct is frequently the first and best sign that this is not benign.

🔎 Respecting the instinct

Validate the parent's sense that it's changed.

"You know Maya better than anyone, and you're right that this has changed — the swollen knee and the tiredness aren't part of simple growing pains, so let's look properly."
📏 The growing-pains rule

Explain the specific pattern.

"Growing pains are aches in both legs, in the evening, with a completely normal child by day. A swollen joint and a morning limp don't fit, which is why I want to investigate."
⚖️ Thorough, not frightening

Pitch the urgent tests proportionately.

"Most of the time these checks are reassuring. Given the tiredness and bruising, the responsible thing is an urgent blood test and a specialist review — being careful, not assuming the worst."
👁️ Why JIA matters

Explain the eye link if arthritis is likely.

"If this is a type of childhood arthritis, it's very treatable — and it's important to catch early because it can affect the eyes silently, which the specialists screen for."

🧭 The rule to carry into every consultation

"Growing pains" is not a disease with a test; it is a clinical label that is safe only when every feature fits, the examination (including pGALS and a systemic look) is normal, and there are no NICE NG12 features. Blood tests are not routinely needed for a classic pattern with a normal examination — but the label should be provisional, the family should be told what would change it, and a clinician who inherits the label should re-test it rather than repeat it. Maya's pattern fails on site, laterality, timing, function, examination and systemic features.

7A — Address the parent's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Mrs Patel half-expects to hear "it's still growing pains" and to go home with advice and Calpol. The safe plan is a blood test today and paediatric involvement — so name her expectation, explain what has changed, and agree the plan together
1
Validate — the old label was reasonable then, and her instinct is right now

Mrs Patel was told "growing pains" by a clinician she trusted, and at the time the pattern may genuinely have fitted. She is torn between that reassurance and her own sense that something has changed. Contradicting the previous doctor bluntly makes her feel foolish and can make her defensive; instead, frame it as the pattern having moved on. Saying out loud that her instinct was correct is both kind and clinically important: parental concern that "something is different" is a recognised signal in children and is exactly why she came.

"It sounds like you were given a reasonable explanation at the time — growing pains are very common. But you've noticed things have changed, and you're right: a swollen knee, a limp in the mornings and being so tired aren't part of growing pains. I'm really glad you brought her back."
2
Explain — two things to check, in the right order

Explain in plain language that the knee looks inflamed, and that the commonest cause of a knee that stays swollen and stiff for weeks in a young child is a form of childhood arthritis, which is treatable. Then explain that the tiredness, paleness and bruises need a blood count first, because the blood count checks the "factory" in the bones that makes blood cells, and rarely a problem there can cause bone and joint pain. If she asks directly whether this could be leukaemia, answer honestly: it is one of the things the test looks for, most children with these tests turn out not to have it, and that is exactly why it is being done quickly. Avoid both false reassurance and a list of frightening diagnoses.

"Her knee looks inflamed — the most likely reason is a type of childhood arthritis, which the children's joint team treat very well. But because she's pale, tired and has some bruises, I want to check her blood count today first. That tells us the blood is being made normally. If you want to ask me anything directly about what it could show, please do — I'll be honest with you."
3
Negotiate — firm on what and when, flexible on how

The non-negotiables are a full blood count and film arranged today (NICE NG12 sets a 48-hour maximum; the cluster of pallor, fatigue, bruising and a swollen joint justifies doing it the same day), a same-day conversation with the paediatric team, and a clear route to paediatric rheumatology if the blood count is reassuring. The negotiable parts are how you make it bearable: numbing cream before the blood test, where it happens (practice phlebotomy, the local children's unit or the paediatric assessment unit — whichever the paediatric team advise), who phones whom with the result, and what to tell school. Offer paracetamol for comfort and explain why ibuprofen can wait until the platelet count is known.

"Here's what I'd like to do: a blood test today — we can use numbing cream so it's less scary for Maya — and I'll speak to the children's doctors at the hospital this afternoon. I'll ring you myself with the result. Does that work for you, and is there anything that would make today easier?"
Key principle: The expectation to be managed is reassurance, not a treatment request. The skill is to replace an inherited label with a plan without undermining the family's trust in medicine — respect the previous clinician, credit the parent's observation, and give a concrete timeline. The same structure works in reverse for Scenario B (true growing pains): the parent may expect blood tests or an X-ray "to be sure" — validate the worry, explain why a normal examination and a classic pattern are more informative than a normal blood test, and agree what would change the plan.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
For most childhood MSK problems, the non-drug plan is the treatment: explanation, keeping the child active, targeted physiotherapy, and school adjustments. For Maya, the immediate non-drug priorities are practical comfort and keeping her moving while the blood count and specialist assessment happen; the longer-term priorities depend on the diagnosis.
🛁
Comfort While Waiting (Maya)
Target: comfortable nights and mornings until the diagnosis is secured
Mechanism

Inflammatory joint stiffness ("gelling") is worst after rest and eases with gentle movement and warmth, which improve synovial fluid movement and muscle relaxation around the joint. Forcing a painful joint or complete rest both make stiffness worse.

Practical

A warm bath or shower on waking; gentle play-based movement before school; let Maya choose her activity level; avoid trampolines and contact games until she has been assessed. Paracetamol for comfort. Keep a simple diary of morning stiffness, swelling, night pain, temperature and new bruises — it helps the specialist and flags deterioration.

Keeps her functioning without masking red flags; the diary turns the parent's instinct into data
🌙
True Growing Pains — Explanation First (Scenario B)
Target: confident parents, uninterrupted sleep, normal daytime activity
Mechanism

The cause of growing pains is uncertain — they are not caused by growth itself, and lower pain thresholds, muscle fatigue after activity and hypermobility have all been proposed. The condition is benign and self-limiting, usually fading over months to a few years.

Practical

Explain the pattern and why the examination is reassuring. Massage, warmth and gentle calf and hamstring stretching at bedtime are low-risk, and small trials suggest stretching may reduce episodes — evidence is limited. Occasional simple analgesia on bad nights. Crucially, give the family the list of features that would change the label (limp, swelling, daytime or one-sided pain, fever, pallor, bruising, weight loss) and offer review if they appear.

A clear explanation is the treatment — and the safety-net keeps the label provisional
🤸
Hypermobility — Strength, Control, Pacing
Target: stays active in sport and play with fewer sprains and less fatigue
Mechanism

In generalised joint hypermobility, lax ligaments place more demand on muscles to stabilise joints; poor proprioception and deconditioning increase pain and injury. Strengthening and neuromuscular control training improve joint stability.

Practical

Reassure that hypermobility is common and usually benign in childhood. Physiotherapy for core and lower-limb strengthening, balance and proprioception, with graded activity rather than avoidance; supportive footwear or insoles for symptomatic flat feet. Encourage regular activity (swimming, cycling, dance with a strength focus). Consider hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or another heritable disorder if there is skin fragility, easy bruising, recurrent dislocations or a relevant family history.

Evidence for physiotherapy in childhood hypermobility is modest but consistent with the mechanism; avoidance of activity worsens outcomes
🦵
JIA — Physiotherapy & Occupational Therapy (Specialist-Led)
Target: full joint range, normal muscle bulk, normal growth and gait
Mechanism

Persistent synovitis causes pain-inhibited muscle wasting (the quadriceps waste fast around an inflamed knee), flexion contractures, and local growth disturbance — an inflamed knee can overgrow and cause a leg-length difference. Drug treatment controls inflammation; physiotherapy restores and protects range and strength.

Practical

The paediatric rheumatology multidisciplinary team provides physiotherapy, hydrotherapy, occupational therapy and splints where needed. The GP's role is recognition, timely referral, supporting adherence, and making sure school and the family understand that activity is encouraged, not forbidden.

Early specialist care and physiotherapy protect joints; referral within BSPAR timescales is the GP's key contribution
⚽
Sports Injuries & Apophysitis — Load, Not Rest
Target: symptoms settle while the child stays active
Mechanism

Growing children have weaker growth plates and apophyses than tendons, so repetitive traction causes apophysitis (Osgood-Schlatter at the tibial tuberosity, Sever's at the heel, Sinding-Larsen-Johansson at the lower patella), and injuries that would cause a sprain in an adult can cause a growth-plate (Salter-Harris) fracture.

Practical

Apophysitis: relative rest from the aggravating activity, ice and simple analgesia after sport, quadriceps and hamstring stretching and strengthening, and physiotherapy if not settling — consistent with the practice Osgood-Schlatter protocol. Tenderness over a growth plate after injury, or inability to weight-bear, needs an X-ray. Pain away from the apophysis, night pain or a mass is NICE NG12 territory, not overuse.

Practice protocol: Osgood-Schlatter is managed with load modification and strengthening, not prolonged rest
🏫
School, PE & an Individual Healthcare Plan
Target: full attendance with sensible adjustments
Mechanism

Morning stiffness makes the school run and the first lessons hardest; fatigue and hospital appointments cause missed learning; exclusion from PE and play drives social isolation and low mood.

Practical

Schools in England have a statutory duty to support pupils with medical conditions (DfE, Supporting pupils at school with medical conditions). If JIA is confirmed, the specialist team and school can agree an individual healthcare plan: a later or gentler start to the day, time to move between lessons, adapted PE rather than exclusion, a place to keep medication, and planned catch-up after appointments.

Keeps the child connected to school and friends — protective for mood and development

🫂
Childhood MSK — the wait for a blood result, a label that changes, and a family that reorganises around a child's joints
For Maya's family, today moves the story from "growing pains" to "we need to check her blood". Whatever the result, the family carry the fear of the worst possibility until they hear it, and a diagnosis of JIA brings years of hospital visits, eye checks, medicines and injections. Naming these effects, and helping with the practical ones, is part of management and shapes how well the family engage with care.

The Wait & the Fear of Cancer

The hours between a blood test and its result are when families search the internet. A named person who will phone, a time, and a plan for either result reduce that anxiety far more than generic reassurance. Don't promise a normal result.

The Child's Voice & Needles

A 6-year-old understands more than adults expect and is often frightened by what she overhears. Talk to Maya directly in simple words; offer topical anaesthetic cream and distraction for blood tests. School, Play & Friendships

Morning stiffness makes her late and grumpy; a limp gets noticed by other children; PE exclusion isolates her. A letter to school, and an individual healthcare plan once a diagnosis is made (DfE statutory guidance), turns ad-hoc arguments into an organised plan.

👨‍👩‍👧

Parents may feel guilty that they accepted "growing pains" for months, and worry about time off work for repeated appointments. Siblings get less attention. Acknowledge the guilt directly and signpost practical help: employer flexibility, carer's leave, and charities such as Arthritis UK (formerly Versus Arthritis) and CCLG: The Children & Young People's Cancer Association for families affected by childhood arthritis or cancer.

🚗
Driving & Transport

Maya is far too young for the DVLA question to arise; the practical issue is transport to repeated hospital appointments (hospital travel-cost support may apply for families on certain benefits). For an adolescent with JIA who is learning to drive, drivers must tell the DVLA about a condition that affects their ability to drive safely, or if they need adapted controls — check the gov.uk guidance on arthritis and driving. "Getting to all the appointments can be tough — let us know if transport or time off becomes a problem and we'll see what support is available."
🛡️
Safeguarding Without Accusation

Bruises in a child always need an explanation. Here, pallor and fatigue point to a medical cause, and a normal blood count must not be taken to mean the bruises are non-accidental without a proper assessment. In other scenarios — a fracture with an unsuitable explanation, or bruising in a child who is not independently mobile — NICE CG89 requires you to suspect maltreatment and follow the local pathway (Working Together to Safeguard Children 2026).

"With any bruises in a child we always make sure we understand how they happened — and in Maya's case the blood test also helps us check that her blood is clotting and being made normally."

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🌙 True growing pains — keeping the label provisional
"Everything I've examined today is normal, and the pattern fits growing pains, which are harmless and settle with time. But if she starts limping, a joint swells, the pain is only ever in one leg, it's there in the morning or through the day, it wakes her every night, or she's pale, tired, bruising easily or losing weight — bring her back, because that's not growing pains and I'd want to look again."
Growing pains are a diagnosis of exclusion; the safety-net lists exactly the features that break the pattern, including the NICE NG12 leukaemia and bone-sarcoma triggers.
👁️ JIA — the silent eye and the flare
"Arthritis in children can inflame the inside of the eye without any pain or redness, which is why the eye checks matter even when she seems fine — please never miss one. If her eye becomes red, painful, sensitive to light or her vision seems blurry, get her seen the same day. JIA uveitis is usually asymptomatic until damage is done (RCOphth/BSPAR screening guideline); immunosuppressed children with fever need prompt assessment.

🧭 1 — Is it benign?

Growing pains = bilateral, legs (not joints), evening/night, normal by day, no limp, normal exam. Anything else → investigate. Use pGALS (gait/arms/legs/spine). The hip refers to the knee (examine the hip in knee pain — SUFE/Perthes'). Hypermobility (Beighton) and sports injuries are common benign causes.

🚩 2 — Exclude the serious

🔴 Septic arthritis (hot joint, won't move, fever → same-day hospital; Kocher criteria are a hospital aid, not a rule). 🟣 Leukaemia/bone tumour (persistent bone/joint pain + pallor/bruising/fatigue/fever/weight loss/lymphadenopathy → very urgent FBC within 48 h, NG12; petechiae/hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment; unexplained bone pain/swelling → consider very urgent direct-access X-ray). JIA (joint swelling/stiffness >6 weeks → paediatric rheumatology + uveitis screening). SUFE (knee pain, examine hip → non-weight-bearing + same-day orthopaedics) / Perthes' (hip X-ray + orthopaedics). Consider NAI/safeguarding (NICE CG89). Safety-net; don't over-label "growing pains".
