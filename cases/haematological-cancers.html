The vague-symptom cancersHaematological cancers present with non-specific symptoms — fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, recurrent infection, bruising/bleeding, bone pain, lumps — easily attributed to benign causes. A high index of suspicion and a simple FBC are the GP's most powerful tools
🟣 Very urgent FBC for leukaemiaNICE NG12: consider a VERY URGENT full blood count (within 48 hours) to assess for leukaemia in adults with any of: pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, unexplained persistent or recurrent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, unexplained bruising, bleeding or petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly. Children/young people: unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment; the other features (plus persistent or unexplained bone pain) → very urgent FBC. A count or film reported as acute leukaemia → immediate referral (NICE-endorsed Macmillan rapid referral guide). Don't sit on suspicious bloods
Lymphoma = nodes + B-symptomsConsider Hodgkin/non-Hodgkin lymphoma with unexplained lymphadenopathy (persistent, >6 weeks, painless, >1–2cm), hepatosplenomegaly, or B-symptoms (fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss). Itch and alcohol-induced node pain are classic clues. FBC, film, LDH, ESR + urgent referral/imaging
Myeloma = CRAB + the testsSuspect myeloma in those 60+ with persistent bone pain (esp. back), pathological fracture, recurrent infection, or the CRAB features (hyperCalcaemia, Renal impairment, Anaemia, Bone lesions). NICE NG12 (myeloma recommendations amended April 2025): in people 60+ with persistent bone pain (particularly back pain) or an unexplained fracture, offer FBC, calcium, ESR or plasma viscosity, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains together (urine Bence-Jones if free light chains are unavailable); suspected cancer pathway referral if the results suggest myeloma
Read the FBC properlyCytopenias (anaemia, low platelets, low/abnormal white cells), a raised or very low WCC, or blasts/abnormal cells on the film point to marrow disease. Pancytopenia, blasts, or a markedly abnormal count need urgent haematology — request a blood film and discuss with haematology
🔴 Neutropenic sepsis = emergencyA patient with (or being treated for) haematological cancer who is unwell/febrile may have neutropenic sepsis — a life-threatening emergency. Do not delay: refer immediately to hospital (usually via the 24-hour acute oncology hotline or 999) for empirical IV antibiotics given immediately on arrival (NICE CG151); suspect it in anyone unwell within 30 days of systemic anticancer treatment (NICE NG253); don't wait for the neutrophil count. Also: tumour lysis, hyperviscosity, cord compression, hypercalcaemia
Persistent > transientMany features (lymphadenopathy after infection, mild fatigue) are benign and transient. It is PERSISTENCE, progression, the combination of features, and the abnormal blood count that should trigger referral — safety-net explicitly and re-check rather than falsely reassure
Children: act fastIn children/young people, unexplained petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment; pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, persistent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, persistent or unexplained bone pain, unexplained bruising or bleeding → very urgent FBC (within 48 h); unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly → very urgent referral for ?lymphoma (NICE NG12) — leukaemia is the commonest childhood cancer (see Childhood Limp pathway)
📋 Clinical Stem — Haematological Cancers
A 67-year-old man with weeks of fatigue, easy bruising, recurrent chest infections and "feeling washed out", whose wife says he looks pale and has lost weight
Raymond Clarke, 67, attends feeling increasingly "washed out" over 6 weeks, with fatigue, breathlessness on the stairs, and several bruises he can't account for. He's had two chest infections in two months. His wife, with him, says he looks pale and has lost weight, and is worried. He's had some drenching night sweats. There's no obvious bleeding, no localising symptoms, and he assumed it was "just getting older". On examination he is pale, has scattered bruising and a few small petechiae, and you can feel a firm spleen tip and some small cervical lymph nodes. He hopes you'll "give him a tonic" and send him on his way.
This stem tests the ability to recognise the non-specific presentation of a haematological cancer — unexplained fatigue, pallor, easy bruising/petechiae, recurrent infection, weight loss, night sweats, hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy — as a constellation pointing to marrow disease (leukaemia) rather than "just ageing"; to act on the NICE NG12 guidance with a VERY URGENT full blood count (within 48 hours) and a blood film, plus the relevant screen (LDH/ESR, calcium, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains if myeloma suspected); to recognise and not miss neutropenic sepsis or other haematological emergencies; to make the appropriate urgent/2WW haematology referral on abnormal results or strong suspicion; and to communicate uncertainty and the need for urgent investigation to a patient who wants reassurance. The SCA challenge is converting a "give me a tonic" consultation into urgent, appropriate investigation, sensitively, without either falsely reassuring or causing undue alarm.

Scenario B — Lymphoma Persistent painless lymphadenopathy + B-symptoms (fever/night sweats/weight loss) ± itch/hepatosplenomegaly. FBC/film/LDH/ESR; urgent referral/imaging (NG12).

1E — Psychosocial context: the person behind the constellation
🫂 "Just getting older", the tonic, and the cancer that whispers

Haematological cancers rarely announce themselves; they whisper through fatigue, the odd infection, a bruise, a little weight loss — symptoms patients and clinicians readily ascribe to ageing, stress or a virus. That is exactly why they are missed. The protective discipline is to assemble the constellation, take persistence seriously, and reach for the humble, powerful full blood count — communicating the need for urgent investigation with honesty and care, so a patient seeking a tonic leaves with the right test rather than false reassurance.

🩸 The power of the FBC

Frame the blood test as the sensible, decisive step.

"A simple blood test tells us a huge amount here — it checks your blood cells and can pick up problems early. I'd like to do it urgently, within a day or two."
⚖️ Thorough, not frightening

Pitch the urgency proportionately.

"Most of the time these tests are reassuring. But the combination of things you've described means the careful thing is to check quickly rather than wait."
👫 Listening to the family

Validate the wife's concern.

"Your wife's noticed you're pale and have lost weight — that's important, and it's part of why I want to look into this properly."
🤝 Holding uncertainty

Be honest while supportive.

"I can't tell you the cause until the tests are back, but I'd rather check thoroughly than miss something. We'll go through the results together quickly."
🧓 "Not wanting to make a fuss"

Many older men minimise symptoms and present only when a partner insists. Raymond's hope for a tonic is partly a hope that nothing is wrong, and partly a wish not to be a burden. If you take the constellation seriously without making him feel foolish for coming, he is more likely to turn up for the blood test and to come back if things change.

"You've done exactly the right thing by coming in — and your wife was right to push you. Let's get this checked properly."

Watch for the patient who agrees in the room and then doesn't attend for bloods — book the test before he leaves and agree how you will contact him with the result.

😨 The word "cancer"

Some patients ask outright; others are frightened of the word being said. A patient who doesn't understand why they are being fast-tracked may not prioritise the test or the appointment. Honesty, pitched gently and without false precision, is both kinder and safer.

"One of the things I need to rule out is a problem in the bone marrow, and that can include a type of blood cancer. Most people I test don't have one — but I'd never want to miss it."

Ask how much he wants to know. Offer to include his wife in the conversation. Give written information about the fast-track pathway.

🧾 Practicalities that get in the way

A very urgent blood test is only useful if it happens. Transport, caring responsibilities, work shifts and fear can all delay it. Older couples may depend on each other for driving and caring, so the "well" partner's health matters too.

"Can you get to the surgery for a blood test today or first thing tomorrow? Is there anything that would stop you?"

Use same-day phlebotomy where possible; a home visit for bloods if he is housebound; make sure the practice has an up-to-date phone number.

🔁 The patient who has "been before"

Haematological cancers — myeloma above all — often come after several consultations for apparently separate problems. If Raymond's record shows a chest infection last month and back pain before that, those are part of the pattern, not just past history. Reviewing the record is part of the history.

"I can see you were in a few weeks ago with a chest infection. I'd like to put all of this together rather than look at each thing on its own."

Re-attendance for a vague complaint is a recognised prompt to step back and consider the whole picture, and to do a full blood count.

7A — Address the patient's expectation first: validate → explain → negotiate
🤝
Raymond wants a tonic or iron tablets and to "save everyone the fuss". Acknowledge the wish, explain why a blood test has to come first, then agree a plan he can say yes to
1
Validate — name the expectation and the hope behind it

Raymond's request is reasonable on its surface: he feels run down, he is 67, and a tonic or iron sounds harmless. Underneath it is a hope that nothing is seriously wrong, and a reluctance to be a nuisance. If you name both before disagreeing, he won't feel dismissed, and his wife will see that her worry has been heard too.

"I can hear you'd like something to perk you up, and I understand why — six weeks of feeling like this is miserable. And I can see your wife has been worried. Can I explain what I think we should do first?"
2
Explain — why a tonic or iron would be the wrong first step

No tonic treats this picture. Iron without a blood count could hide a clue and delay the answer, and it would do nothing for low platelets or infections. Tiredness, bruising and small spots on the skin, two chest infections, weight loss, sweats and an enlarged spleen, all together, need a full blood count and a blood film within 48 hours (NICE NG12). Explain what the test looks for in plain language, and say that the likely next steps will depend on the result.

"Each of these on its own is common. But together — the tiredness, the bruises, the infections, the weight loss and what I can feel in your tummy — they make me want to check your blood cells properly, and quickly. If I gave you iron now, it might even muddy the picture."
3
Negotiate — a concrete, time-bound plan

Offer something concrete today: the blood test booked before he leaves (today if the practice can, otherwise first thing tomorrow), a named person who will act on the result, a phone call the same day it's back, and a clear rule for what to do if he becomes feverish or unwell in the meantime. Ask what would make it hard for him to do this, and remove the obstacle.

"Here's what I'd like: a blood test today, which I'll look at myself as soon as it's back. I'll ring you either way. If it shows something that needs a specialist, I'll arrange that the same day. And if you get a temperature or feel suddenly worse before then, don't wait for me — go to A&E. Does that sound manageable?"
Key principle: in suspected haematological cancer, the "treatment" the GP gives today is speed: a very urgent count, a film, and a result that someone owns. Declining the tonic is not the difficult part. The difficult part is doing it in a way that keeps Raymond engaged, so that he has the test, answers the phone, and comes back if he gets worse.

7C — Non-medication management: mechanism + evidence + tailored advice
Before diagnosis, the GP's "management" is organisational: the test, the result, the referral and the safety-net. Once a haematological cancer is confirmed, the specialist team leads the cancer treatment, and much of what keeps the patient safe and well between hospital visits is non-drug care that primary care is well placed to support. For Raymond, the first card applies today; the rest apply once a diagnosis is made.
📋
Result ownership (a "safety-netting system")
Target: every very urgent FBC tracked to action
Mechanism

Delays in haematological cancer often come from process failures rather than missed symptoms: a result filed as "abnormal — see GP routinely", a patient who isn't contacted, or a referral letter that isn't sent. A named clinician and a tracking log close those gaps.

Practical

Mark the request "very urgent — ?leukaemia" with the clinical details so the lab reviews the film. Record the expected result date. Use a practice log or task for suspected-cancer tests and referrals, and check it daily. Confirm the patient's phone number and agree how you'll contact him. If the lab phones an out-of-hours result, it needs to go to someone who can act on it.

NICE NG12 safety-netting: make sure the results of investigations are reviewed and acted on, taking responsibility for this or passing it on as agreed
🧼
Infection prevention during treatment
Target: patient and household know the rules and have a thermometer
Mechanism

Chemotherapy, the disease itself (particularly acute leukaemia, which causes neutropenia, and CLL and myeloma, which impair antibody production) and steroids all reduce the ability to fight infection. Most serious infections come from the patient's own gut and skin flora, so hygiene and early recognition matter more than isolation.

Practical

Hand hygiene; good mouth care; food safety (the haematology team will advise on any specific food restrictions); avoiding people with obvious infections; household members vaccinated (flu, COVID-19). A working thermometer at home and the chemotherapy alert card with the 24-hour hotline number kept on them.

NICE CG151: people having anticancer treatment should get oral and written information about neutropenic sepsis and how to get 24-hour specialist advice
🚶
Physical activity and prehabilitation
Target: stay as active as symptoms and counts allow
Mechanism

Exercise before and during cancer treatment helps preserve muscle mass and fitness, reduces cancer-related fatigue and improves mood and sleep. Fitter patients tolerate intensive treatment better and recover more quickly.

Practical

Walking and gentle resistance work, adjusted to the counts. Avoid contact sports and heavy lifting when platelets are low. In myeloma, avoid high-impact or heavy spinal loading because of lytic lesions, and ask the team about safe exercise. Signpost to Macmillan's physical activity resources and local cancer rehabilitation services.

Exercise is one of the better-evidenced interventions for cancer-related fatigue
🍲
Nutrition and weight
Target: stop further weight loss; dietitian if losing weight
Mechanism

Weight loss is common at diagnosis (a B-symptom) and during treatment, from mucositis, nausea, taste change and low appetite. Malnutrition worsens fatigue, infection risk and treatment tolerance.

Practical

Weigh at each contact. Small frequent meals and nutritional supplements if needed; dietitian referral if weight is falling. Treat mouth problems early. Steroid-containing regimens can raise blood glucose — check it if the patient develops thirst or polyuria.

Early nutritional support helps maintain weight and function through treatment
🧠
Psychological and peer support
Target: support offered at diagnosis and at each transition
Mechanism

Diagnosis, intensive treatment, "watch and wait" for indolent disease, relapse, and the end of treatment are each points of high distress. Anxiety and depression are common and under-recognised, and they affect adherence, sleep and pain.

Practical

Ask directly about mood. Use NHS Talking Therapies (many services have long-term-condition pathways) or hospital psycho-oncology. Signpost to condition-specific charities: Blood Cancer UK, Leukaemia Care, Lymphoma Action, Myeloma UK, Macmillan. For "watch and wait", explain that monitoring is an active and evidence-based strategy, not neglect.

Peer and specialist support improves the experience of care
🗺️
Advance care planning (when appropriate)
Target: wishes recorded before a crisis, not during one
Mechanism

Haematological cancers can deteriorate suddenly (infection, bleeding, relapse), and people with blood cancers are less often referred to palliative care and more likely to die in hospital than people with solid tumours. Early conversations about what matters to the patient allow care to match their wishes.

Practical

Introduce the idea gently once the diagnosis and prognosis are known. Record preferences using ReSPECT or the local equivalent, share them with out-of-hours services, and add the patient to the practice palliative care register when appropriate. Plan jointly with the haematology team, as transfusion and antibiotic decisions often continue until late.

NICE NG142 (end of life care service delivery): identify people approaching the end of life and support advance care planning

7G — Psychosocial impact of the diagnosis: work, driving, family, money and identity
🫂
Blood cancer — the illness that arrives quietly and then takes over the calendar
Haematological cancers range from a CLL that may never need treatment, to an acute leukaemia that means admission within days and weeks of intensive chemotherapy, to myeloma, which is usually treatable but not curable and runs a course of treatment, remission and relapse. Each brings a different kind of uncertainty. The GP is often the one continuous clinician across these phases, and the one who notices how the patient and family are coping.
💼
Work and income

Treatment can mean months off work, and infection risk may rule out some jobs for a time (healthcare, childcare, teaching). Under the Equality Act 2010, cancer counts as a disability from the point of diagnosis, so employers must consider reasonable adjustments. Many patients don't know this.

Fit notes can suggest phased returns or altered duties. Financial strain is common: signpost to Macmillan's benefits advice and grants, and remember that people with cancer in England can apply for a medical exemption certificate for prescription charges.

"Is money or work a worry at the moment? There's practical help available — including free prescriptions and benefits advice — and your employer has a legal duty to make adjustments."
🚗
Driving and the DVLA

Most people with a haematological cancer do not need to tell the DVLA. For a car licence, they must notify it if the illness or treatment affects the brain or nervous system, if their doctor has concerns about fitness to drive, or if medication side effects make driving unsafe. Bus and lorry (Group 2) licence holders must tell the DVLA about leukaemia or lymphoma (gov.uk condition pages). Central nervous system lymphoma carries specific restrictions.

Practical issues matter more often: severe anaemia, sedating opioids, peripheral neuropathy from treatment, and leg weakness from cord compression (which means no driving).

"At the moment you're fine to drive as long as you feel well and alert. If the treatment makes you drowsy, or your hands or feet go numb, let's talk about it — and if you ever drive a bus or lorry, tell me, because the rules are different."
👫
Partner and family

Raymond's wife is frightened and has been carrying the worry alone. Partners of people having intensive treatment become carers overnight: driving to appointments, watching for fever, managing medicines. Their own health often slips. Under the Care Act 2014, carers are entitled to an assessment of their needs by the local authority.

"How are you doing with all this? You've been the one who noticed. Can I make sure you're on our records as Raymond's carer, so we can support you too?"
⏳
"Watch and wait" and living with uncertainty

For CLL, some low-grade lymphomas and smouldering myeloma, the right treatment is often monitoring without treatment. Patients can find this harder than chemotherapy: "I've got cancer and they're doing nothing." Explaining that treating early doesn't improve outcomes for these conditions, and that monitoring is active care, reduces anxiety.

"Watching and waiting isn't doing nothing. For this type, starting treatment before it's needed doesn't help you live longer — it just brings the side effects sooner. The team are keeping a close eye, and so am I."
🪞
Identity, body and intimacy

Hair loss, weight change, steroid-related mood swings, fatigue and loss of physical strength change how people see themselves. Fertility can be affected by treatment in younger patients: fertility preservation should be discussed before treatment starts, which the specialist team arranges. Sexual health and intimacy are rarely raised unless the clinician asks.

"Sometimes treatment changes how people feel about themselves, their body or their relationship. That's common, and it's fine to talk about it here."
🕯️
Relapse, the end of treatment, and end of life

The end of treatment can be unexpectedly hard: hospital contact stops and fear of relapse starts. Myeloma and some leukaemias relapse, and each relapse brings a harder conversation. When the illness is incurable, early palliative input improves symptom control and planning; the practice palliative care register, ReSPECT or an equivalent plan, and anticipatory medicines keep the patient's wishes central.

"Whatever happens with the treatment, I'll still be your GP. If things change, we'll plan together what matters most to you."

7J — Safety-netting: exact phrases + medico-legal rationale

⚠ Three scenario-specific phrases — use these verbatim

🔴 Emergency — infection and bleeding while the count may be low
"Until we have your blood results, and at any point if you're having treatment, a temperature of 38 or above, shivering, feeling suddenly very unwell or confused, or any bleeding that won't stop — from your nose, gums, or in your urine or stools — is an emergency. Don't wait for an appointment and don't take paracetamol and see how it goes. Ring 999 or go to A&E, and tell them your GP is checking you for a blood problem. Once you're under the hospital, ring their 24-hour number on your alert card first."
Raymond has petechiae, bruising and recurrent infections: he may already be neutropenic and thrombocytopenic. NICE CG151 treats suspected neutropenic sepsis as an acute medical emergency, with immediate referral; the fever may be absent or masked. Document the exact advice and that his wife heard it.
🦴 Myeloma and lymphoma complications — spine and calcium
"If you get new back pain — especially pain that's worse lying down, wakes you, or goes round your chest like a band — and particularly if your legs feel weak or numb, or you have trouble passing urine or controlling your bowels, that's an emergency: ring the hospital hotline or go to A&E the same day. The same goes for becoming very thirsty, drowsy, confused or badly constipated — that can be a high calcium level, which is treatable."
Metastatic spinal cord compression and hypercalcaemia are the complications of myeloma and lymphoma that most often present to general practice. NICE NG234 asks for an MRI within 24 hours for suspected MSCC and dexamethasone as soon as possible if there are neurological signs. Hypercalcaemia mimics deterioration or dying but is reversible.
🟠 If the first test is normal — persistence still matters
"If your blood count comes back normal, that's reassuring, but it doesn't explain everything — the glands in your neck, the sweats and the weight loss still need an answer. I'd like to see you again in two weeks either way. If the glands get bigger, or new lumps appear, or the sweats and weight loss carry on, come back sooner and we'll arrange the next tests."
A normal FBC does not exclude lymphoma or myeloma. NICE NG12 lymphoma recommendations are driven by unexplained lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly and associated symptoms, not the blood count. A planned review, rather than "come back if you're worried", is the safeguard.
Why safety-netting matters beyond clinical care
Document the constellation, the NG12 criterion used ("very urgent FBC — ?leukaemia"), and the time the test was requested — this shows the decision was reasoned, not incidental
Record who owns the result and how the patient will be contacted; a result that is filed without action is one of the commonest causes of delayed cancer diagnosis
Record the neutropenic sepsis and bleeding safety-net in the patient's words, that written information was given, and who was present
If the patient declines urgent tests or referral, record the discussion, their understanding of the risk, and the offer to reconsider — and follow them up
On the referral, include the full clinical picture and the results; mark the referral as suspected cancer and tell the patient that is why they are being seen quickly
When prescribing for a patient on haematology treatment, record the interaction check and any advice from the specialist team

⛔ Danger zone — common pitfalls in suspected haematological cancer
✗
Treating each thread separately→ Antibiotics for the chest, iron for the tiredness, reassurance for the bruises. Step back, review the record, and put the constellation together — then do the FBC.
✗
Ordering the FBC but not owning the result→ Name the clinician, set a task, confirm the phone number. A very urgent test that is filed as "see routinely" is worse than useless because it creates false reassurance.
✗
Sending a 2WW letter for blasts on the film→ A count or film suggesting acute leukaemia needs immediate referral (Macmillan rapid referral guide, NICE-endorsed): phone the on-call haematologist the same day.
✗
Checking an FBC in the surgery for a feverish patient on chemotherapy→ Suspected neutropenic sepsis needs immediate hospital referral (CG151) — hotline or 999, no oral antibiotics, no waiting for the count.
✗
Another course of analgesia for back pain in the over-60s→ Persistent bone or back pain, or an unexplained fracture, at 60+ → FBC, calcium, ESR/PV, serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains together (NG12, amended 2025).
✗
Taking a normal FBC as the end of the story→ Lymphoma and myeloma can have a normal count. Unexplained lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly or B-symptoms still need the lymphoma route; bone pain still needs the myeloma screen.
✗
Starting steroids "to see if it helps"→ They can mask lymphoma and leukaemia and spoil the biopsy. Avoid them before diagnosis unless a specialist advises.
✗
Giving a live vaccine, or prescribing clarithromycin, without checking treatment→ Live vaccines are contraindicated during immunosuppressive treatment (Green Book chapter 7); oral targeted drugs have major CYP3A4 interactions. Check the record every time.

🚦 1 — Recognise & investigate

Leukaemia: unexplained bruising/petechiae, pallor, fatigue, recurrent infection, hepatosplenomegaly → very urgent FBC (48h) + film (children: petechiae/hepatosplenomegaly → immediate specialist assessment; other features → very urgent FBC). Lymphoma: persistent painless lymphadenopathy + B-symptoms ± splenomegaly/itch → FBC/film/LDH/ESR + 2WW. Myeloma (60+): bone/back pain, fracture, recurrent infection, CRAB → FBC, calcium, ESR/PV + serum protein electrophoresis + serum FLC together (NG12, 2025); suspected cancer pathway referral if they suggest myeloma.

🔴 2 — Emergencies & referral

Neutropenic sepsis (unwell/febrile + immunocompromised) → immediate admission + IV antibiotics; don't wait for the count. Also cord compression, hypercalcaemia, hyperviscosity, tumour lysis. Markedly abnormal FBC/blasts → same-day haematology. Otherwise 2WW (NG12). Safety-net explicitly; act on persistence not reassurance.
